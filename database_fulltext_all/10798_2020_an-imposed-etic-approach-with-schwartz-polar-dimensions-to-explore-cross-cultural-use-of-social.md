## A B S T R A C T

Cross-cultural studies in Information Systems (IS) have experienced an overreliance on Hofstede’s theory, which has impeded researchers from exploring alternative theoretical lenses. We examine the moderating efects of Schwartz’s Polar Dimensions on users’ perception of Social Network Service, highlighting cultural issues associated with IS privacy, trust, and social capital. We found that the efect of perceived reputation is more powerful in American culture than Chinese culture, though network centrality has a stronger efect on use intention among Chinese users than American users. We also introduce the imposed etic approach, which has been rarely seen in IS research.

## 1. Introduction

It has been noted in the literature that new research is necessary within the Information Systems (IS) discipline to respond to the emerging trends in today’s sociotechnical environment [65]. Because many studies of Social Network Services (SNS) have mainly focused on the idiosyncrasies of the technical artifacts, it is not surprising there is a rising demand for research that conducts a systematic examination of technology usage prediction and social interactions [46,37]. Because of the exponential growth of personal handheld device adoption, the borders among the social contexts of SNS are blurred as users are merging their professional and personal activities [4]. Such socialtechnological phenomena are expected to become more evident as the increasingly robust data network is implemented on a daily basis around the globe [56]. Therefore, reformulating the existing technology to various cultures is important to many Web 2.0 firms, because when domestic subscription hits maturity and adoption saturation, international expansion is often the only way for firms to increase sales. However, entering foreign markets poses great challenges that vary from country to country. For example, Facebook raised billions of dollars upon entering the China and India markets; the former blocks

Facebook out of political concerns, whereas the latter sufers from the lag of infrastructural development [11]. Moreover, previous IS research has established that user-oriented technologies provide numerous human-computer interfaces to facilitate social network interactions. Adapting these interfaces to a user’s cultural background can increase satisfaction, revenue, and market share [82]. Cultural beliefs and activities are integrated into online communication and behaviors on SNS platforms, so that the online context reinforces certain aspects of users cultural values [12].

Cross-cultural challenges occur in all types of companies, especially technology companies, that are going global, evidenced by an abundance of literature on frustrations caused by divergent national laws, regulations, and social norms. Nevertheless, we see unique opportunities and contextualized benefits in conducting cross-cultural analysis in SNS. First, social relationship-oriented assets (e.g., social capital) distinguish SNS development from other types of services or companies. For the first time in history, netizens can ubiquitously post and voraciously consume information about each other and actively explore hidden or new social connections. Coupled with the rapid progress in technological enablers, such as Big Data and Cloud Computing, SNS represents an online critical mass that exceeds the scale of any precedent socializing tools or platforms. Second, SNS can leverage socially embedded resources better than any other businesses. It has changed the behavioral pattern through which social connections sprout and multiply. The “Guanxi” (personal connection in Chinese), as well as its social settings, constitute a crucial aspect in SNS to facilitate productive activity. Third, the manifestations of social ties hinge on the ecological conditions aforded by locally formulated socioeconomic environments. Informal institutions, such as culture, account for the most omnipresent conditions. Hence, culture is an influential factor in the investigation of social needs, motivation, and cognitive perceptions relating to SNS. Lastly, these conditions afect social activities in a so phisticated manner, calling for a diverse body of knowledge and theoretical anchors to enhance our understandings of this subject matter.

In the social media business, Facebook made several attempts and is still trying to enter China, a multi-million user market that has not been tapped because of government restrictions. With significant dificulties and high risks notwithstanding, exploring foreign markets is a constant priority of organizations that have enjoyed great successes domestically. Many services anchored in Web 2.0 technologies have become global practices (e.g., crowdsourcing). The social efects brought by these practices exhibit culturally distinctive characteristics, so that IS researchers consider culture as an important factor afecting individuals’ perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors [111]. For SNS vendors, the biggest hurdle lies in adapting to foreign cultures and ofering de sirable products to indigenous consumers. Specifically, to improve the chance of success, organizations must address two concerns, namely the increasingly global nature of user technologies and the increasingly diversified cultural backgrounds of subscribers. Many lessons have been learned regarding failures due to inadequate or the false assessment of cultural influences on product/service design and strategy implementation. For example, in addition to language translation, the implications of messages broadcasted must be carefully evaluated. The classic social media faux pas by Pepsi’s slogan “Come alive with Pepsi,” which can be interpreted as “bring your dead ancestor back from heaven,” caused an immediate backlash and negative impact on the company’s reputation and sales performance in the Chinese market. Although American SNS service providers have a head start<sup>1</sup>, their followers in many countries are trying to re-create the prosperity or beat the pioneers in their own games (e.g., WeChat and Weibo in China, Line in Japan). It is noteworthy that Facebook, whose founder rigor ously examines the role of culture within his company’s global strategy, has gradually enhanced its presence in several non-Western countries, including South Korea, where Cyworld is losing ground to Facebook due to a series of global and local trends. Case studies have consistently showcased, how valuable it is to wield the power of in-depth knowledge of culture-related phenomena associated with technology use. The de mand for such knowledge as a critical competitive advantage is urgent, because many service providers have achieved domestic adoption sa turation, so future expansion must be global.

National culture has been a crucial driver for context-oriented stu dies in IS. For instance, the global nature of internet “raises questions about the robustness of trust efects across cultures” [45]. Despite the prevalence of cross-cultural studies, an overreliance on Hofstede’s theory in IS research has hindered researchers from exploring alternative theoretical lenses. Prior cross-cultural studies indicated that national cultural scores based on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions may not accurately measure and find significant or desired relationships with variables of interest, largely because cultural distance scores condense culture into a form that may be overly parsimonious and the model-derived cross-level analysis would lead to an ecological fallacy [72]. Moreover, Fischer et al. [31] indicated that Hofstede identified several value dimensions at the country level, but did not find matching dimensions at the individual level. Schwartz proposed values as “desirable trans-situational goals that vary in importance, and serve as guiding principles in the life of a person or other social entity” [126 p. 21], which has been considered central to cultural and cross-cultural research [129]. Schwartz’s value-based structure discriminated dif ferent sets of value constructs at individual and cultural levels, based on separate analyses at each level [86]. Such a structure has been confirmed in many studies undertaken in more than 50 countries (e.g. [127,128],). Furthermore, prior works have established that Schwartz’s value structure is proven to be consistent with empirical findings from studies contrasting the US and China [129]. Hofstede’s dimensions were challenged to be inappropriate when applied to Eastern cultures because “the reliance on Western-based cultural dimensions ignores the existence of indigenous cultural traits that are unique to Asian markets’’ [130, p. 431]. Hofstede conceded this to some extent by adding another dimension, called Confucian dynamism, to his original model as a result of work in China [123].

As such, more studies are needed to adopt an espoused cultural perspective other than Hofstede’s when developing a theoretical fra mework to understand how people with diferent cultural heritages interact with IT artifacts. As Warkentin et al. [105] echoed, novel and extendable studies on cross-cultural IS artifacts give evidence of the ongoing evolution of this cross-cultural IS research stream. Furthermore, Chu et al. [110] conducted a systematic review on cross-cultural research in IS. Their findings suggest that Hofstede’s cultural framework has dominated the area. Given the recent call for additional IS cultural research [105], we introduce the Schwartz’s Polar Dimensions (SPD) in an efort to broaden our understanding of the efect of new cultural constructs from a user’s perspective. SPD, rarely used in IS research, ofers a valuable new perspective on research questions related to cultural phenomena. The IS literature suggests that significant cross-cultural diferences exist in the process of behavioral intention formulation [33,58]. In addition, Srite and Karahanna [93] pointed out that “individuals may identify with national culture to varying degrees. As such, it is inappropriate to assume that one can validly use ecological correlations to substitute for individual correlations.” As the first attempt in utilizing the SPD to investigate cultural diferences pertaining to the use of SNS, our study proposes that contextual factors, including autonomy, conservatism, hierarchy, and mastery, are pillars upholding and highlighting cultural issues related to issues, such as privacy, trust, and social capital, which could contribute to the formulation of behavioral intention to use SNS.

Adopting SPD to study cross-cultural use of SNS is necessary because it attempts to dispose of two fallacies that have long existed among IS research – theoretical fallacy and ecological fallacy. Theoretical fallacy refers to the failure of denying the “Unitarianism” of an overarching theory that is used to investigate cross-cultural issues. Currently, about 99% of the published studies in top IS journals adopt Hofstede’s works as the only theoretical lens. None has raised questions such as “Is there an alternative lens for our research questions?” Coined by Srite and Karahanna [93], ecological fallacy is, to some extent, associated with theoretical fallacy because it springs from the conceptual inconsistency between national cultural stereotypes and individuallevel cultural traits. Hence, it is starkly important to wield the appro priate theory when explaining the intrinsic dynamics of cross-cultural phenomena. Thanks to Srite and Karahanna’s work, IS researchers have embraced the idea of espoused cultural values as shown by their works that combine perspectives from Eastern and Western traditions (e.g., [39]).

Our study exhibits several unique attributes. First, it brings a new view of cultural values solidly into the IS field. Unlike the traditional Hofstede paradigm, this view discriminates diferent sets of cultural constructs at both the country and individual levels [31]. Second, it provides a new perspective to a popular theme that is appealing to both scholarly and industrial audiences in IS – global expansion. Third, it poses nontrivial research questions. Built upon the incremental additions to our understandings of cross-cultural IS issues, our research questions introduce cross-cultural psychology literature and ask: How do American and Chinese subscribers difer with respect to preferences for certain behavior-related variables? Or simply: How does SPD identify the diferent perceptions of SNS use between American and Chinese subscribers? The investigations of these questions test whether SPD has a good fit and reveal psychological/behavioral distinctions between two culturally diferent groups of users. Last, this paper leads to blue ocean ideas, in terms of applying innovative theories to investigate novel research questions and solve strategic globalization problems. Pew Research Center [76] reported that over 36% of cell phone owners use SNS on their mobile devices. Half of the young adults, aged 18–29 years, have SNS apps on their handheld devices. As they will account for the majority of information workforce in the near future, it is not surprising to see a strong demand for more studies of the efects of trust, privacy, and social capital constructs on SNS use intention and how they vary across individuals from diferent countries, instead of investigating the issue within one country [36].

We presupposed that investigating the combination of privacy and trust efects in a cross-cultural study through an innovative cultural lens, may lead to important theoretical discoveries and meaningful managerial implications. The significance of trust vis-à-vis privacy stems from the fact that many service providers initially focused on justifying the return on investment, while issues, such as privacy protection, did not draw the same level of attention. Sequentially, the maturation of SNS mirrors that providers must now adopt, measures to minimize the negative impacts such as reduced customer trust caused by sensitive data leakage and theft. Departing from the investigations of wired SNS, this study aims to empirically validate the appropriateness and robustness of the SPD for IS cross-cultural studies. In particular, we conjecture that the SPD can shed new light on the theoretical groundwork of cultural influence in an emerging IT context such as SNS. To achieve this objective, the SPD cultural dimensions are considered to be moderating factors in this study. For a rigorous analysis across cultural settings, we also propose to employ an imposed etic approach, an established practice in cross-cultural psychology but rarely seen in the IS community, to diferentiate the cultural variances that can be accounted for by the SPD. Our literature review exposed the lack of discussion of how viewpoints are obtained – from the perspective of the subject or the observer. We, therefore, propose the use of an imposed etic approach that aims to 1) recognize the fact that everyone, including researchers, is influenced by personal experience that is influenced by culture; 2) establish a structured approach to acquiring user-specific information about their perceptions; and 3) address translation equivalency.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we will introduce the SPD and explain its appropriateness for IS research based on a comprehensive literature review across diferent disciplines. We then propose our research hypotheses and research model. Next, we will describe the collection of the data used to test our hypotheses, followed by the results of the data analyses. Finally, we present the theoretical and practical implications of our findings, address the lim itations of the study, and discuss opportunities for future research.

## 2. Literature review

When it comes to calculating cultural distance scores, most IS scholars adopt Hofstede's dimensions, [40] which define culture as “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group from another.” However, many leading IS researchers are embracing an individual-level-oriented espoused cultural value system [79,39,53,93] mainly because, although Hofstede’s value system is empirically efective in measuring cultural distance scores at the level of cross-country, it assumes there is no or low variation within a specific country [39]. In other words, Hofstede's theory essentially assumes that people within a society are culturally homogeneous. This theory’s boundary conditions lead to a potential ecological fallacy that poses threats against the goodness of fit between theories applied and target problem domains – country-level lens vs. individual-level technology use. Although it has been noted that espoused cultural values serve as a solution allowing IS researchers to bridge the gaps of practice units, so that the ecological fallacy is mitigated when adopting Hofstede with modifications (e.g. [39],), we argue that finding a theoretical lens that was born with individual-level analysis readiness makes significant contributions to the IS research for two key reasons. First, an experi mental design is made possible to compare two lenses – one was born with cross-country measurement and the other has cross-individual readiness. Second, theoretical diversity emerges. A new way of investigation helps researchers to enhance, or nullify, the validity of the existing one. For example, even after Srite & Karahanna’s work, it is not dificult to see a cross-cultural study that does not fully address the espoused culture issue, and thus, is potentially vulnerable to the ecological fallacy threat (e.g., [46]).

In addition to the notion that diferent theories are best suited to diferent units of practice, it is necessary for scholars to recognize the importance of theoretical diversity. Leidner & Kayworth [57] found that most studies use Hofstede as the sole lens. Our study follows them by looking at a more updated collection of publications in 2018 and confirms this exclusive application of Hofstede's theory. Indeed, we found almost no evidence of a cross-cultural IS study that systematically evaluates an alternative lens, either empirically or conceptually. Most authors who published their works in the AIS Senior Scholar Basket of journals justified their choice of Hofstede, although the limitations of such a theory are explicitly indicated by its popularity [107,39]. Some researchers criticize Hofstede’s model on the grounds that it is rather crude and simplistic, that a survey may not be a suitable way of measuring cultural diferences, and that a study of the subsidiaries of one company may not provide information about entire national cultures [39], but the “Unitarianism” continues. It is ironic that diversity is an iconic image of cross-cultural phenomena, and yet most studies in IS enjoy the uniformity of theoretical perspectives. Not only is it important to acknowledge that Hofstede is not the only animal in the cross-cul tural theory zoo, but also, if not more so, it implements the idea of diversity in our research. Benbasat & Zmud [5] contended that the IS discipline has made significant progress in resolving the learning issue, as reflected through its methodological and theoretical rigor, its methodological and theoretical diversity, and the respect afforded to the discipline’s major journals. As a result of the interdisciplinary nature of IS research, the theories embraced, the methods applied, and the topics addressed by IS scholars are themselves varied, producing the diversity exhibited across the discipline [83]. We further argue that the lack of such diversity with respect to theoretical lenses, correlates with a fallacy limitation that does not enable substantial progress to be made in obtaining valuable discoveries of IS scholarship.

We conducted a comprehensive literature review (see Appendix A) of research in the “high impact” IS and non-IS, and other journals (i.e., AIS Senior Scholar basket journals and others) in which the SPD has been widely used as a valid theoretical foundation that leads to significant findings in multiple disciplines, including social/organizational psychology, public administration, business ethics, management, and others. Some developed conceptual constructs or models that were rooted in SPD [101,62]. Some utilized SPD to obtain understandings of managerial issues among multinational companies [61,25,74,80]. Others integrated Schwarz value items with Hofstede’s work to generate a theoretical framework explaining cross-cultural behaviors [64,102,30]. Our review shows that research that utilized the SPD had been published in high impact journals including Organization Science and Journal of Applied Psychology for the past 20 years. Nevertheless, such an important theory has long been overlooked by IS researchers. Still, at present, very few IS articles have adopted the SPD. The lack of diversified cultural theoretical tools, thus, galvanized us to further examine and validate the SPD for research that investigates IS issues with new perspectives and concepts. The non-IS journals included in the review were selected based on 1) the influential works published in their respective domains; 2) the specialized interest of research that has a strong relevance with this study $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , Journal of Cross-Cultural Psy chology).

Against the backdrop of this dual fallacy, we reviewed studies in the field of cross-cultural psychology and identified interesting potentials of SPD: 1) SPD has a wider scope [72]; 2) some of Hofstede’s dimension (e.g., individualism) are oversimplified, whereas SPD can explain the ethical attributions in countries classified as more individualist [47]; 3) SPD might be more efective in predicting relationships between interested variables [91]; and 4) empirical evidence showed that SPD is a powerful theory, especially in an environment where the influence of the social contract is ubiquitous [72]. The Schwartz approach difers from other cultural models because it provides separate and unique theories and it derives broad values and cultural orientations from the requirements of human societies. Such a unique theory diverges from other cross-cultural frameworks in that the structure of interrelationships among the individual values contributes significantly to our understanding of how values function as an integrated system, which is an important tool accounting for diferences among cultural entities. The SPD, which carries a macro structure to answer many of the calls for meso and micro research by modern social scientists [31], is thereby considered as an appropriate framework in empirical research for a novel and varied methodological analysis of cross-cultural artifacts. As noted above, a glaring advantage of SPD over Hofstede is the intrinsic readiness of measuring individual-level psychological variables. SPD is able to discriminate diferent sets of value constructs at both the individual and country levels, thus allowing investigators to directly examine the degree of similarity or isomorphism between the structures of values in individual-level analyses. Furthermore, adopting SPD in crosscultural IS research introduces diversity to the cross-cultural theory arsenal.

We compared cross-cultural studies, in both IS and non-IS disciplines, to summarize how the two cultural lenses – Hofstede and Schwartz – operate when applied to various contexts. For example, Egri and Ralston [131] used Schwartz's (1994) [126] values model to measure and compare three-generation values obtained at the personal level between the US and China. Their findings suggest personal values changed over generations. They also find that personal values, such a open to change and self-transcendence, exhibit significant diferences between the youth generation in the US and that of China. The authors also stress that they chose Schwartz's because it has been used ex tensively in cross-cultural studies of individual values. Fischer et al. [31] conducted a meta-analysis that discovered the existence of iso morphism, although not a perfect one, among cultural values between country and individual levels using Schwartz's measures. In the IS realm, Ji et al. [46] use Hofstede's [122] work to investigate the user behavior diferences among SNS subscribers from the US, Korea, and China. Although interesting findings are presented, their study is prone to the threat of ecological fallacy warned by Srite and Karahanna [93]. George et al. [114] also adopt Hofstede's work [121]. but they followed Srite and Karahanna [93] to address espoused cultural beliefs in their conceptualization, measurement, and data analysis. Their findings reveal that diferent combinations of cultural and media efects afected the accuracy of deception detection in the process of international communications. However, their work did not specifically address individual-level values, or their connections to the macro level. This. when compared to the studies using Schwartz's, appears to be endemic among cross-cultural IS studies. The implication of this summary is twofold: (1) cross-cultural IS calls for the use of alternative cultural frameworks to assess the goodness of fit between research objectives and culture measurement and (2) Schwartz's framework is a candidate so: lution for those who want to link culture level and individual level in their analysis.

Cultural factors are important because they afect users interpretations of behavioral and social activities associated with technological development and utilization [29]. Significant cultural diferences have been identified in the process of technology acceptance [33,58,93]. In essence, the US (Western) and Chinese (Eastern) cultural factors used within this study are treated as moderating factors to illustrate their unique influential efects on privacy and trust. To gain a better understanding of the cultural variances based on the SPD, we have identified the following contextually salient dimensions in this study: 1) Afective Autonomy – a society that recognizes individuals as autonomous entities who are entitled to pursue their stimulation and hedonism interests and desires; 2) Hierarchy – a society that emphasizes the legitimacy of hierarchical roles and resource allocation; 3) Embeddedness/Conservatism – a society that emphasizes the im portance of social order, obedience, and respect for tradition; and 4) Mastery – a society that emphasizes active mastery of the social environment and individual’s rights to get ahead of other people. These dimensions have been rigorously cross-validated against each other and are capable of capturing more aspects of national cultures, compared with other cross-cultural theories. ## 3. Theoretical background & research model

Developed from a study of global employee values by IBM, Hofstede's culture level dimensions emerged as an outcome of facto analysis based on a worldwide survey. Since then, its subsequent products have become a legacy and the most well-known and widely applied theoretical anchor for cross-cultural studies in numerous disciplines. However, we depart from the Hofstede paradigm and anchor on SPD as the cross-cultural lens because: (1) Schwartz posits that culture emerges from the interaction between individuals acting on the basis of their values, while culture, in turn, feeds back into the values of these same individuals [140]. His position is congruent with our research design, where individual-level culture values are measured, analyzed, and discussed in combination with country-level values. (2) Prior studies of comparing Hofstede and Schwartz show that the latte is a more robust framework in eliciting individual-level values [72]. Furthermore, researchers have utilized Schwartz's culture/individual level model (Appendix D) to conduct conceptual development that led to meaningful discussions in cross-cultural research [138]. (3) Some have empirically tested Schwartz's items in terms of inter-level isomorphism (i.e., the degree of similarity between country and individual levels), whose “findings point to substantial structural similarity” and suggest “there is some isomorphism of values across levels” [31 p. 142].

It is also noteworthy that Hofstede, in his later works, released an individual level framework for cross-cultural comparisons. Nevertheless, we continue with SPD for two important reasons. First, empirical findings provided by Fischer “clearly reject Hofstede's ori ginal argument that the structure of values at the individual and country levels is not at all the same” [31 p. 145]. This study, nevertheless, is collinear with Schwartz and Fischer and posits that country and individual levels share some extent of isomorphism, and thus both are influential in theoretical arguments. The second reason is more of a meta-analytical one. Rai [142] avidly supports the idea of cross-paradigm design in IS research. We, therefore, embrace this idea by lever aging a conceptually distinctive cross-cultural framework to examine and expand the existing boundary conditions that have dominated cross-cultural IS studies. Our literature review (Appendix A) shows that the stream of cross-cultural IS research has almost unanimously ad hered to Hofstede's works without a critical review of alternative an chors.

Culture is a fuzzy concept defining what it comprises remains a formidable challenge [139,141]. The relationship between the country level and individual level is even fuzzier, thus engendering debates regarding what to use in measurement. Nonetheless, some consensus emerged around Schwartz's framework as an operational remedy, mainly because it reveals the existence of isomorphism in the attempts to link country level and individual level values [137,140]. According to Fischer, individual-level cultural values are essentially the “psychological attributes of individuals” [31 p. 136]. It encompasses a wide range of constructs such as personality/cognitive abilities, privacy, trust, and motivation/intention [139]. Furthermore, Schwartz in dicated in his seminal work that he aims to lock down individual cul tural values by identifying a comprehensive set of values that helps in explaining individual diferences in attitudes and behavior, as well as of the populations to which individuals belong [31,143]. Researchers also noticed that the segregation between individual-level interpretations and country-level efects leads to discriminatory and suboptimal decisions that undermine our understandings of cross-cultural phenomena [139]. Intelligence scores, for example, at the individual level may reflect individual (genetic) diferences; however, economic or educational diferences at the culture level may influence diferences between groups of applicants [144]. Schwartz and Fischer also advise not to consider country level as a simple aggregation of individual values without proper justification and analysis [139,143].

Schwartz's cultural dimensions are broad and capable of afecting the values and beliefs of individuals through informal institutions at a macro level [138,140]. This study draws upon Schwartz's work in 2009 to assess the following values that serve as the linking items between country and individual levels: Ambition (a strong drive for success), Daring (the trait of being willing to undertake things that involve risk or danger), Competence/Capable (the quality of being well qualified physically and intellectually), Success (an event that accomplishes its intended purpose), and Family Security (safety for loved ones). According to Schwartz [137], Ambition, Daring, Competent/Capable, and Success work in both Mastery (macro) and Achievement (individual) dimensions. Family Security goes to the Security dimension at the in dividual level. On the other hand, country-level values include Pleasure (gratification of desires), An Exciting Life (stimulating experiences), A Varied Life (filled with challenge, novelty, and change), Social Order (stability of society), Respect for Tradition (preservation of time-honored customs), Wisdom (a mature understanding of life), Authority (the right to lead or command), Social Power (control over others, dominance), Wealth (material possessions, money), and Humility (a disposition to be humble). These values not only represent some of the most significant cultural distances between the US and China, but also demonstrate strong applicability to the research context of this study.

Blau [9] formalized the social exchange theory positing that in dividuals engage in social interaction because they expect to receive some type of reward, and by which form is not necessarily confined to monetary value (e.g., emotional rewards including approval, status, and respect). Wasko and Faraj [106, p 39] further explained that “one po tential way an individual can benefit from active participation is an increased perception that participation enhances his or her personal reputation in the network.” Reputation is an important asset that an individual can leverage to achieve and maintain a desirable status within a collective environment [48]. A consistent finding between virtual interaction and social exchange theory is that building a reputation is a strong motivation for active participation [28]. When virtual teams collaborate within an organizational setting, the chance to improve one’s reputation provides an important motivation for ofering useful advice to others [22]. Although in many situations, reputation leads to direct financial returns, there is evidence indicating reputation possesses unique dimensions that constitute a parallel construct along with economic motivation.

We argue that the positive efect of reputation on the intention of using SNS will be stronger in the culture that exhibits low autonomy than the culture with high autonomy. Schwartz [87] indicates that the person is independent, actively pursue his/her own afectively positive experiences such as pleasure, exciting/varied life, and seeking to express own internal attributes (preferences, traits, and feelings) when such autonomy dimension is high. As a result, reputation, or the value of reputation, is perceived diferently in high autonomous vs. low autonomous cultures. Specifically, high autonomy and strong personal value emphasized culture may drive individuals for short-term gains without much regard for gain in reputation in the society. ## H1: Autonomy negatively moderates the relationship between reputation and use intention of SNS

Social capital refers to “socially embedded resources that are accessed and/or mobilized in purposive action” [60]. Diferences exist among the perceptions of what represents the social capital concept. Some researchers focus on the benefit created and accumulated in a collective setting. Others use the individual setting to conceptualize social capital. We argue that in this research, it is more appropriate to think of social capital as the resources existing in the networked social relationships between social nodes. “Guanxi” is the Chinese word that represents these social connections. The “Guanxi” paradigm, as a heavily studied social capital instantiation, has been the center of research ranging from business to government relations. “Just as physical and human capital facilitate productive activity, social capital does as well” [21, p S101]. Such a view of social capital fully embraces relationship-oriented philosophy, and uses it to guide activities that aim to create and accumulate benefits, which could be social, psychological, emotional, and economical [59].

Nahapiet and Ghoshal [71] posit that “the combination and exchange of information and knowledge would be facilitated when the following criteria are met: individuals are motivated to engage in such activity; there are structural connections between individuals; individuals have the cognitive capability to understand and apply the information/knowledge; and interpersonal relationships have strong and positive characteristics.” Burt [14] and Putnam [78] argue that structural capital is a strong predictor for collective activities, which are evidenced by Krackhardt [54] who pointed out that collective action is relatively easy to achieve when networks are dense and have strong direct ties between members. Other practice-related evidence can be seen in large investments SNS vendors make in media (e.g., TV commercials), and features (e.g., 3rd party apps) that nurture structural capital created through online social networking.

When networks are dense and have strong direct ties among members, collective action is relatively easy to achieve. Prior research has suggested that centrality is an efective measurement of structural capital [106,1]. Centrality is a dependent variable of how many social ties can be created by one individual. The more ties one can have, the stronger centrality that a person possesses. In SNS, such measurement can be reflected by social node’s friend list, ranking of popularity, number of posts and responses generated on a regular basis, and valueadded service subscription. As discussed earlier, autonomous culture is more concerned about personal opinions of others than hierarchic culture. Does such distinction reflect consistently given the existence of centrality? Or if there are many strong social ties, do they predict strong collective activities with/without the influence of autonomy? Based on Schwartz’s work [87], the value oppositions are central to an individual’s self-conception and may motivate a person toward corresponding behavior in terms of costs and benefits. In this framework, behaviors that are consistent with the values of the society and tradition are encouraged and the acts of rejecting these values may lead to social disapproval. It can be argued that in a low autonomous culture, individuals are likely to encourage behaviors that are in line with the societal order, so that there will be a stronger efect of network centrality on the use intention of SNS in the culture that exhibits low au tonomy than the culture with high autonomy. Following the notion suggested by Burt [14] and Putnam [78], we posit that:

H2: Autonomy negatively moderates the relationship between network centrality and use intention of SNS.

In the context of e-vending, trust can be defined as “the subjective probability with which consumers believe that their information is kept private and safe in e-transactions” [41,109,67]. McKnight et al. [69] have identified a comprehensive set of trust antecedents such as privacy and familiarity, the latter of which is a knowledge-based antecedent of trust. Luhmann [63] suggests that “familiarity builds trust because it creates an appropriate context to interpret the behavior of the trusted party.” In SNS, a participant’s familiarity reflects his/her knowledge regarding how the service operates and what procedures are involved within the service. This research extends this notion by establishing that SNS cannot prevail, at least in a sustainable manner, without the user’s belief in service providers who promise not to exploit private information for unauthorized or inappropriate purposes – an expectation of privacy protection.

Moreover, Liu et al. [61] examined the factors afecting internet users’ trust in relevant security technologies (e.g., third-party digital certificate). Among these factors, a user’s perceived privacy protection was found to be significant in afecting a customer’s trust in e-commerce. They proposed a privacy-trust-behavioral intention model that considers an individual’s perceptions of privacy and how it relates to his/her behavioral intention to conduct e-transactions. In such a model, the predictive power of privacy as an antecedent of trust is empirically verified. Smith [147] took a diferent approach to delineate the influential relationship between the regulatory approach and the corporate management of information privacy, corporate cultural value, and individuals’ information privacy concerns, suggesting efects of cultural beliefs in the relationship between privacy and trust.

We investigate the relationship between privacy and trust across individual perceptions to identify cultural variances so that we can attribute these variances to SPD values. Conservatism describes a per son’s psychological status as embedded in a collectivity, finding meaning in life largely through social relationships and identifying with the group. He or she has a cultural emphasis on the maintenance of the status quo and restraint of actions that might disrupt the solidarity of traditional order [87]. Hence, there will be a stronger efect of privacy on trust in the culture that exhibits low conservatism than the culture with high conservatism. Mastery refers to a cultural belief that in dividuals should control and change the social and natural environment through assertive actions (ambition, success daring, and competence) to further personal or group interests [87]. We argue that there will be a stronger efect of privacy on trust in the culture that exhibits weak mastery than the culture with strong mastery. We further hypothesize that:

H3: Conservatism negatively moderates the relationship between privacy and trust.

H4: Mastery negatively moderates the relationship between privacy and trust.

Moreover, IS literature has widely recognized trust as a critical, although not the only, predictor of use intention. For example, Gefen et al. [34] emphasize that trust is a central aspect in many economic transactions because of a deep-seated human need to understand the social surroundings, that is, to identify what, when, why, and how others behave. However, trust is even more crucial in specific transactional environments, such as e-commerce and SNS. Although SNS ofers a unique electronic platform in contrast to e-commerce, the core of SNS consists of personal information that is considered valuable and helpful for users to interact with the virtual social settings. The user must overcome psychological barriers to trustfully participate in SNS activities. In other words, SNS cannot prevail, at least in a sustainable manner, without the user’s belief in service providers who promise not to exploit private information for unauthorized or inappropriate purposes. Inappropriate or undesired personal information leakage leads to a crucial and negative impact against trust, and thereby deters the behavioral intention formalization of SNS use.

With regard to privacy antecedents, Dinev and Hart [26] suggest that social awareness is related to the user’s privacy concerns. Social awareness refers to public interest and passive involvement in social issues that drive regulatory changes intended to address a social entity’s concerns. Values are beliefs linked to emotions [87], therefore, if someone appreciates independence then s/he is likely to get agitated if her/his independence is threatened. Values refer to a desirable goal that motivates action [87]. For those who value social order, justice, and social awareness, they are motivated to pursue these goals. In a hierarchical society, individuals are likely to emphasize on personal privacy through involvements in social issues. They may even be encouraged to take action to preserve privacy. These social entities valuate privacy as an expression of personal dignity that is honored as an important individual right [19]. We argue that a high level of social awareness will lead to close adherence to internet privacy rules and strong sensitivity toward the consequences of compromised privacy protection. Bontempo and Rivero [10] indicated that an individual’s behavior is more closely linked to attitudes that reflect norms in a society of hierarchy. As such, norms, as socially developed codes of conduct that are highly subject to social awareness, outweigh personal perception of values in a hierarchical environment. We, hence, posit that the moderating efect of cultural factors exists when social awareness leads to the perceived importance of SNS privacy. There will be a stronger efect of social awareness on the perception of SNS privacy in the culture that exhibits high hierarchy than the culture with low hierarchy. Following this notion, we hypothesize that:

H5: Hierarchy positively moderates the relationship between social awareness and privacy.

## 4. Research method

A critical concern in cross-cultural research is whether behavior has to be comprehended in its original context, or whether cross-cultural variations can be understood on a common theme [8]. Correspondingly, the field of cross-cultural psychology has introduced two contrast approaches: emic and etic. Emic orientation is culture-specific, whereas etic orientation is culture general or universal [85]. Compared to emic approach, which examines only one culture and studies characteristics that are relative to its internal environment, etic approach studies behaviors from a position outside the system where they occur. Many researchers have contended that to fully reveal the complexity of a behavior, one should only investigate it in its own culture. Nevertheless, even more researchers have argued that doing so is usually impractical. Similar to some methods in ethnography, emic approach avoids the imposition of ideas and value systems from the researcher to people studied. Ideally, researchers should absolutely look at norms, values, and customs through the eyes of members of target cultures. In reality, however, we must deal with the rooted values of our own culture when executing cross-cultural research plans. Hence, an imposed or “pseudo” etic approach was discussed [75]. The goal of its empirical analysis is to move progressively from “imposed” etic to match the emic orientation that is cross-culturally valid [8]. In some literature, this approach is argued to be similar to the “combined etic-emic” approach, which is recommended by Triandis [99] and Hui & Triandis [43].

Berry [6,7] has suggested an iterative approach that illustrates empirical procedures to separate emic and etic orientations in a research methodology. Many cultural studies, including this one, adopted an “imposed” etic approach, in which researchers will scrutinize, reexamine, and revise their methodological tools and conceptions for cultural appropriateness. In Fig. 3, step I identifies culture A. Step II starts with the imposed etic orientation of culture A. Step III discovers another culture B. Step IV compares culture A and B, both with emic orientations. Step V generates valid comparisons. It is important to recognize the assumption that one of the cultures does not require imposition of a priori notions from researchers’ own culture. The major objective of executing such a design is to obtain the conclusion of what comparisons should be made. An alternative explanation lies in Triandis’s [124] recommendation. The researcher begins with constructs that appear to be etic (general) and then develop emic (culturally appropriate) instruments of measurement (e.g., instrument that is designed reflecting American users’ attitudes, norms, and values because SNS and mobile SNS originated in US). It is claimed that with such an instrument a validated construct is obtained and can be used in crosscultural psychology studies. There is empirical research conducted in such a line of tradition [24]. We, therefore, design our methodology around the concept of imposed etic orientation by 1). Developing in struments that are culturally appropriate with the context where SNS use behavior originated (USA). 2). Focusing on the interactions of users, in the US and China, with SNS products. 3). Admitting the serious challenge of imposed etic orientation. Namely, if a behavior can only be understood in its original context, there is no logical basis for com parative studies. We will address this issue in the Limitations section.

## 4.1. Data collection

A pretest was conducted prior to data collection. Doing so allows us to validate and refine the survey instrument. A small sample size of 160 college students was used. On the basis of such pretest and experts reviews of instrument items, we started with survey data collection online. In the first phase, we collected data from a group of students at four public universities in the US. In the second phase, we collected data from universities in China. In the US sample, a total of 322 responses was collected out of which 22 have been dropped because of incomplete and irrelevant responses. The respondents who do not use SNS sites have been removed, because the study is focused on the use of SNS. A total sample of 300 US responses was used for testing the hypothesized model. For the China sample, 14 were dropped because of incomplete and irrelevant responses, resulting in 312 responses. The demographics of the sample are shown in Table 2. We conducted an Ftest to determine if diferent levels of internet experience and SNS experience among the users in two samples had any efect, in general. For internet experience, F (1, 598) = 0.858 with a probability value of 0.355 (> 0.05). For SNS experience, the F (1, 598) = 1.684 with a probability value of 0.195 (> 0.05). We did not find evidence that the variance of two samples is different. As the sample size was quite dif ferent for an internet experience of 4–6 years (i.e., 8 for the US and 65 for China), there was some concern if the results were distorted due to disparity in sample size. Therefore, we conducted Levene’s test for equality of variances and an independent sample t-test to compare the means for these two samples. Levene’s test showed that there was equality of variances (F = 0.521, p = 0.473). The results of a t-test (t = 0.224, p = 0.824) suggested that the means for the US and China for internet experience of 4–6 years were not statistically diferent. We concluded that although the sample size of 8 and 65 for the US and China, respectively, is unequal, we did not have evidence to support that the means are diferent for these two samples. We tested for an SNS experience of 1–3 years (i.e., 42 for US and 92 for China). Therefore, we did not have evidence to believe that unequal sample size may have led to distorted results in our analysis. To enhance comparable validity, we included a question in the US survey asking whether the subjects are international students. Those who answered yes were removed from the data set. This is necessary because many international students study in American colleges and we wanted to focus on local students who display culturally indigenous profiles. We did not, however, do the same for the Chinese survey because local Chinese students account for virtually all of the student body.

In the past, researchers used equivalence indicators to interpret cross-cultural research findings. Construct equivalence, for example, refers to whether a given construct has similar meaning across cultures [103]. According to Mullen [70], metric equivalence refers to the extent to which respondents across cultures rate the measurement scale in the same way. On one hand, the five-point Likert scale is one of the most frequently used scale systems in the world. On the other hand, earlier IS research (e.g. [45],) utilized unified measurement scales in culturally comparative analysis whose metric equivalence did not compromise the validity of research findings. Karahanna et al. [50] suggested that wording and translation are important means of overcoming some biases caused by inconsistency. Translation equivalence requires accurate translation from one language to another, but at the same time should avoid losing the power of information elicitation that comes with the original version of the instrument. Brislin et al. [13] suggested a committee approach that consists of translators who speak both the original and the target language and who are familiar with the target culture. To accomplish this, the English questionnaire was translated into Chinese by a panel of Chinese faculty teaching at US universities. Then, the translations were compared and discussed until an agreement was reached on an optimal translation. In this case, the instrument is subject to both semantic and conceptual verifications to ensure the maximum level of obtaining desired information from respondents. Also, the committee or panel approach increases equivalence between multilingual versions of an instrument and the measures’ cross-cultural validity.

## 4.5. Testing for measurement invariance

Data collected in survey research may generate unreliable results due to measurement biases. When data are gathered from two or more cultural groups, it is important to test for measurement biases before a proper comparison can be done. In cross-cultural research, it should be ascertained that conclusions are based on results obtained on real dif ferences between cultural subsamples rather than measurement scale diferences.

To test our hypotheses, it is necessary to determine that latent constructs were perceived in a similar manner between the US and China respondents [16]). Invariance analyses were carried out to ensure that the US and China respondents would use the same pattern in using the measurement scales. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) is one of the most widely used methods to test for measurement equivalence or invariance [55]. Invariance can be tested using CFA to derive and compare the chi-square and fit statistics of an unconstrained and a series of constrained measurement models [15].

To investigate the appropriateness to compare path coeficients between two groups, we applied the multigroup measurement invariance analysis [94]. Using AMOS, we performed configural, metric, scalar, and factorial invariance analysis to assess if the measurement models are invariant across the US and China samples. Configural invariance is established if the basic model is invariant across groups. No restrictions are imposed on loadings across groups when modeling configural invariance [27]. Results show adequate model fit for the US data $( \chi 2 = 2 3 3 . 2 4 ;$ df = 75; χ2/df = 3.11; RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.915, NFI = 0.911, $\mathrm { G F I } = 0 . 9 0 1 )$ as well as China data (χ2 = 113.00; df = 75; χ2/ df = 1.50; RMSEA = 0.04, CFI = 0.981, NFI = 0.946, GFI = 0.954). Then, we moved from single-group CFA to multiple-group CFA to crossvalidate the model across two groups. Results indicate adequate model fit (χ2 = 346.3; df = 150; χ2/df = 2.31; RMSEA = 0.06, CFI = 0.924, $\mathrm { N N F I } = 0 . 9 3 5 , \mathrm { G F I } = 0 . 9 0 3 )$ , which provided evidence that configure invariance was supported. Next, to test for metric invariance, the factor pattern coeficients were then constrained to be equal. The chi-square diference test shown in Table 7 indicated that the imposition of constraints resulted in statistically significant decreases in the model fit as compared to the unconstrained model, thus suggesting metric invariance. Furthermore, we tested for metric invariance using a comparison between full measurement invariance model and partial invariance or nested models that only constrained individual constructs. As shown by full measurement invariance in Table 7, the value is not significant. We constrained each individual construct to create individual models and found that an item for social awareness construct was problematic. Removing the item showed that the invariance model for social awareness construct was invariant. Other individual constructs in Table 8 showed that they were significant, thus, providing support for metric invariance. Then, we tested for scalar invariance by constraining the intercepts of the invariant factor loadings from the metric invariance model. While com paring with the metric invariance model, it showed a little but not sig nificant increase in chi-square, thereby suggesting an adequate degree of scalar invariance. The CFI index dropped slightly to 0.87, which suggested substantial comparability. Then, we performed a factorial analysis to determine if the US and China samples conceptualize the research constructs in the same way. An unconstrained baseline model was established followed by a fully constrained model. On the basis of results given in Table 9, the fit statistics of two models are comparable indicating the invariance of the unconstrained and constrained models. For further factorial analysis. models that constrained individual research constructs were set up. As shown in Table 9, the fit indices and the changes in chi-squares and the changes in degrees of freedom are not significantly diferent among the models. Next, a theta-delta invariance test was conducted to check that error terms of the groups were noninvariant. As shown in Table 10, Δχ2 was found to be significantly different between the unconstrained and fully constrained models. While testing the partial models, it was determined that privacy, trust, and intention may have been the cause of noninvariance. After the thetadelta invariance test, a latent factor variables invariance test was carried out to see if the variance of the construct between the latent variables was the same for the groups. The results, shown in Table 11 suggest that the variances of the constructs are not significantly different for the groups. These findings suggested that the error terms were diferent for the groups. As a result of these tests, we have no reason to doubt that it is safe to proceed with the study of path coeficients between samples.

## 5. Discussion

This research introduced a new cross-cultural theory for a better understanding of the efect of cultural dimensions in IS, and applied it in a study of SNS. We achieved this objective by integrating the SPD into the IS trust, privacy, and social capital literature. Despite many cross-cultural studies in the IS field, none has sought to investigate an alternative overarching theory other than the Hofstede, which is still evolving and notably important in research though its primacy is gradually declining [105]. Given the paucity of theoretical cultural lenses besides Hofstede’s works in the IS research arena, this study contributes to the growing body of research highlighting the significance of social and institutional influences on the adoption of IS artifacts in a global setting [42]. Non-IS research has embraced SPD as their major cultural theory base because it has performed relatively well across samples and predicted a variety of behavioral traits [129,131–133]. Importantly, the values clusters reflected the values compatibilities and conflicts expected at an individual level [129]. Although Schwartz [127] argued the importance of understanding values as a system rather than as individual values, when trying to determine people’s perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors, research suggests values hierarchies reflect influences at both individual and country levels, which is consistent with Schwartz and Bardi's suggestions [125.129].

We conducted a wide scope of literature review starting from 1990 to 2017 in the field of IS. Among the culture-related IS papers published in the top journals (e.g., high impact basket journals), over 90% used Hofstede’s works as the only cultural theory. Meanwhile, such fields as psychology, management, and sociology embrace a diverse body of theories, where diferent schools of thoughts including Hofstede and Schwartz were applied, compared, and discussed. Another troubling issue is that many of the cross-cultural studies in IS contain the ecological fallacy described by Srite and Karahanna in 2006. This fallacy arises from the use of national stereotypes instead of espoused cultural characteristics. The dual fallacy threat prevents us from obtaining knowledge through cross-validated theoretical lenses. The bottom line, however, is that diversity is a virtue for scholarly works at diferent levels including perspectives (e.g., academic vs. industrial), philoso phies (e.g., interpretivism vs. positivism), and overarching theories. Therefore, our contribution is novel, in that we present the first alternative cross-cultural theory that enables researchers to see the changes within user perception and behavior in the context of SNS by switching SPD values. A major obstacle to the development of comparable approaches in the past might have been the lack of perspective about cultural research development as well as what unique features it con tains when compared to Hofstede. Another issue that our literature review found is the lack of discussion of how viewpoints are obtained – from the perspective of the subject or the observer. We, therefore, propose the use of an imposed etic approach that aims to 1) recognize the fact that everyone, including researchers, is influenced by personal experience that is culture-oriented; 2) establish a structured approach to acquiring user-specific information about their perceptions; and 3) address translation equivalency.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

This paper ofered important contributions in three aspects. First, we aim to bridge the gap by making contributions to the cross-cultural IS literature. Our study demonstrates that foreign SNS penetration should be managed with a strong focus on contextual factors. Doing so brings together the cultural and IS perspectives, as well as country level and individual level values, to enrich online social activities and gen erate insights on user conversion. Second, our study identifies the role of the SPD in terms of user experience. Although not every dimension was found to be significant, the cultural values of the American and Chinese users are measured by a scientific methodology and reliable results are obtained. Third, our study raised further questions about the validity of cross-cultural research approach. Specifically, traditional sociocultural researchers suggested that cross-cultural issues need to be comprehended in its original context, or, cross-cultural variations can be understood on a common theme [8]. We have employed and recommended an imposed etic approach that illustrates iterative empirical procedures to separate emic and etic orientations in our research methodology. This approach reveals valuable insights into investigating cultural-psychological phenomena that are widespread due to the global penetration of IT. On one hand, the imposed etic approach recognizes the challenge of logical basis for comparative studies; on the other hand, it establishes the validity of cross-cultural interpretations by introducing a proxy construct – imposed etic culture that serves as a delegate to emic culture. Along with other validity control procedures, the imposed etic approach provides a new way to enhance the rigor of methodology. While such an approach has long been studied by sociologists, no IS research has been found to discuss its use. Hence, this is the second gap our study fills.

This paper also leverages Schwartz's cultural dimensions to bridge individual-level values with those of macro level, an easily overlooked connection in many cross-cultural studies. We approach this contribution in two ways. First, we establish theoretical validity by drawing upon seminal works in cross-cultural psychology where empirical evidence highlights the superiority of Schwartz in revealing isomorphism between individual and country/culture level interpretations [31,72] Second, to better elicit individual values, we adopted a cross-checking method that contains two stages. In the beginning, the data samples are grouped into two based on country origins (i.e., the US and China), and go through the analysis. We then conduct a second-time analysis based on their responses to Schwartz's values instead of origins, thereby resonating with and mutually verifying the findings from the origin-based data analysis. This method is rare in cross-cultural IS research and should be encouraged in future studies.

The individual-level values embedded in our research design and model are robust indicators of the influences from the cultural level. For example, our findings indicate that Autonomy, as a macro-level value, exerts impacts on the individual and cognitive level perception of reputation, thereby illustrating how diferent impacts of an individuallevel construct have on the endogenous variable (i.e., use intention) under the influence of macro-level cultural values. Along the same lines, network centrality also exhibits significant distinction on the path to an endogenous construct due to the moderating efects of macrolevel cultural values. Moreover, some of the moderating cultural items (e.g., Ambition) operate at both macro and individual levels, thus stressing how country-level analysis can overlap considerably with the individual-level structure, even though the match was not perfect. More importantly, we should address the efects of cultural dimensions by considering the country level and individual level together. As Fischer argued, an interpretation of one level that is isolated from another tends to distort the meanings and understandings of cross-cultural constructs [139]. Maseland and van Hoorn, on the other hand, contend that a culture level construct has implications at lower levels of analysis, affecting the values and beliefs of individuals [138]. In terms of Hofstede's framework. Ng et al. [72] use empirical analysis to demonstrate the incongruence between Hofstede's and Schwartz's in quantifying and measuring cultural distances. Therefore, another contribution of this paper sheds light on the issue that academic communities should consider alternative cultural lenses, instead of unanimously adopting a single one, when they conceive and execute cross-cultural research.

## 5.2. Practical contributions

Moreover, our findings provide evidence that SNS providers need to have a global view of the market and their marketing campaigns should be managed with an emphasis on cultural values in addition to traditional IS success factors, such as the ease of use and usefulness. Tuunanen and Kuo [100] have similarly proposed a value-based prioritization of requirements that led to important discoveries. Our findings, both theoretically and practically, relate to how American and Chinese users have diferent perceptions of SNS use: (1) American users are more sensitive to data leakage news than Chinese users; (2) Support of powerful social figures positively influence SNS penetration success in China, but less so in the US; (3) American users rely more on reputation than their Chinese counterparts; and (4) a large scale of user network and strong ties are considered more efective in acquiring new subscribers in China than the US.

The proposed SPD perspective allows us to take a closer look at how cultural dimensions explain between culture variances. Specifically, the findings suggest that (1) in Eastern type culture, centrality has a stronger efect toward SNS use intention than in Western type culture; (2) opposite to our hypotheses, the efect of perceived reputation on use intention in the US is greater than in China, probably due to the proliferation of engineered reputation that continues to plague Chinese online communities of e-commerce and social media (e.g., the notorious “water army”). For example, Xu et al. [145] and You et al. [146] suggest that reputation manipulation has reached an unprecedented scale in the Chinese e-commerce and social media landscape [145,146]; (3) user trust is significant with use intention in both cultures; (4) in the US sample, the unexplained variance is so obvious that it cannot be determined if privacy has a significantly negative relationship with trust. The Chinese sample, however, indicates a valid influence of privacy to trust; and (5) although social awareness efectively leads to privacy in both cultures, the American data show less stronger efects than the Chinese data. Identifying predictor variables and measuring their correlations in this study, helped us to capture the hidden dynamics that are not covered by previous work. For instance, American users have diferent perceptions of social awareness than Chinese subscribers; hence, their expectations of vendor’s eforts protecting user privacy are diferent from those of Chinese subscribers.

Previous research has indicated that broad cultural categories (e.g., SPD) can be discursively mobilized by organizational members as strategic adaptive resources [81]. Our findings, therefore, ofer practical implications for those who seek to explore foreign markets. In 2014, Facebook launched its recruiting campaigns in China, which was interpreted as a signal that they are preparing to enter the country with 1.5 Billion population. At the same time, Google is seeking a return to China [134]. LinkedIn CEO, Jef Weiner, indicated at an interview that the company is expanding in China and their goal is well aligned with that of the Chinese government. For CEOs and project managers, it is clear that while system characteristics and technology factors help encourage user acceptance and improve user satisfaction, much more needs to be done to ensure strategic success. According to our research, it is important that American service providers, if they want to leverage their first-mover advantages as the US leads in SNS research and development, should lay the foundations and create infrastructures for culturally enticing and inviting social networking activities and develop the trust of subscribers. They should also consider creating connections with powerful social figures for public relation purposes. While these initiatives require commitments and resources, they generate significant returns of investments through building a critical mass of user base and enhancing site stickiness. It has been witnessed that in China, local service providers are outperforming the American first movers in such key aspects as the number of unique visitors and registered members. The same situation also happens in Japan and South Korea. From this study, SNS providers can learn that American and Chinese users establish and accumulate their social connections diferently. For example, Chinese users are likely to exchange profiles, contacts, and basic personal information without having anything to do with mutual interests or common values. The exchange is the means as well as the objective. The same rarely happens for American users, however, who would enjoy sharing opinions and discussing topics that are of their interests and profile exchange is a secondary step that follows.

## 6. Limitations

This paper inevitably has several limitations. First, the caveat of an imposed etic approach is that the value systems and notions of researchers are rooted in and influenced by their cultural background. Such influence is born with the methodology of behavioral research. Second, as the meaning of behavior is context-specific, there is little room for data comparison. In terms of theoretical and methodological implications, emic orientation should be the only principle researchers adhere to. In practice, we shall assume that cultural-psychological instrument items used in diferent cultures can but need not have the same meaning. The inconsistencies can be somehow alleviated by methodological techniques (e.g., the combined emic-etic approach proposed by Triandis [124]). The third limitation is macro. Methodological principles appear as rather rigid standards, yet we realize that these standards rarely can be met in practice. Instead, these standards serve as guidelines and should be pursued as approximately as possible. Another limitation is the disparity issue within sample data. In the categories of Internet Experience (IE), 2.66% of the US users have used the internet for 4–6 years, compared to 20.80% in China. We believe that the distorting efects were under control because: 1) When conducting analysis using only the sample data of more than 6 years IE between the US and China, we can see that the results are aligned with those including both 4–6 years and 6+ years. This indicates that the gaps in the 4–6 years category do not generate critical distortions. 2) The gaps of SNS experience are smaller, thus compensating for the disparity in IE to some degree. 3) Disparity issue within data has long existed in cross-cultural studies. Fortunately, researchers have conducted reliable analysis that tolerates such a limitation. For example, Zhou et al.’s [108] comparative models utilized individualism and indulgence values, which contain obvious subsample disparity issues: high- (N = 315) vs. low-indulgence (N = 99) and high- (N = 328) vs. low-individualism (N = 86). Although we do not find supports for concerns that data disparity leads to major distortions in this study, we recognize that the research design has potential for improvements. Therefore, we call on future researchers to further discern and remedy the potential issue in cross-cultural studies, which shall require rigorous research methodology from the stage of data collection to interpretation, because the multiple facets of culture will somewhat influence the data quality in various ways. Recently, the European Union (EU) has implemented the General Data Protection Regulation, requiring measures to protect citizens within the EU (Nadeau 2018). In the US, the California Consumer Privacy Act was passed in June 2018. Future privacy research in cross-cultural use of SNS should investigate whether regulatory initiatives create greater trust among users in the protection of their personal data.

## 7. Conclusions

In summary, SNS becomes a disruptive platform allowing a crosscultural exchange of ideas, information, and value perceptions. Today, although unique idiosyncrasies remain in each culture, the impacts of “international village” brought by communications technology often obfuscate cultural distinctions. In this sense, it calls for new theoretical lenses to vield implications for sociocultural constructs in the context of daily changing digital world. Drawing on the Schwartz theory, this research has explored new cultural value aspects in the context of SNS. Our study contributes to the increasing focus on alternative theoretical approaches to studying culture in diferent contexts. Our empirical findings demonstrate a suficient level of cross-cultural similarity to make it plausible that we can understand the value concepts vs. decision-making of people in diferent countries. By gaining a rich understanding of the cultural environment, where the influence of social contract is ubiquitous, researchers and practitioners alike can improve the pursuit of greater command of this domain. The important contributions from this rigorous investigation establish the foundation for important future work. Moreover, the findings prove that cross-cultural studies are still valid and necessary in the research area that in. vestigates the nature of international business.
