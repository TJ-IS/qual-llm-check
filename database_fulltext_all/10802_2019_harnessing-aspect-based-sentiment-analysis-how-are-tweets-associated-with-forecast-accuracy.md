# Harnessing Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis: How Are Tweets Associated with Forecast Accuracy?

Shuk Ying Ho<sup>1</sup>, Ka Wai (Stanley) Choi<sup>2</sup>, Fan (Finn) Yang<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup> The Australian National University, Australia, susanna.ho@anu.edu.au <sup>2</sup> The Australian National University, Australia, stanley.choi@anu.edu.au <sup>3</sup> The Australian National University, Australia, fan.yang@anu.edu.au

## Abstract

For firms in the consumer sector of the economy, tweets about service quality reflect consumer satisfaction, which determines firms’ future earnings. Our study responds to anecdotal evidence indicating that analysts have adopted opinion mining to scrutinize Twitter data in order to detect shifts in consumer behavior and make earnings forecasts. If this anecdotal evidence is accurate, certain tweet characteristics may be associated with the accuracy of earnings forecasts for these firms. Our study draws on the literature on consumer satisfaction and firm earnings to identify possible tweet characteristics and hypothesize their associative relationships with analyst forecast accuracy. We use the airline industry as the study context and extract tweets related to airline service quality from publicly available Twitter data and analyst forecast data from the Institutional Brokers Estimate System Academic. We apply content analysis, followed by aspect-based sentiment analysis, to the downloaded tweets. Using regressions, we find that the breadth of coverage and the number of posters are associated positively with forecast accuracy. The valence of tweets differentiates their effects on forecast accuracy: negative tweets enhance forecast accuracy to a greater extent than do positive tweets. We do not detect any association between tweet subjectivity and analyst forecast accuracy. There is a marginal negative association between tweet dispersion and forecast accuracy. We conclude by discussing theoretical and practical contributions.

Keywords: Twitter, Analyst Forecast Accuracy, Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis.

Choon-Ling Sia was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on September 24, 2017 and underwent three rounds of revisions.

## 1 Introduction

For firms in the consumer sector of the economy, tweets about service quality reflect consumer satisfaction about their services, which affects future earnings. Information on consumer satisfaction with a firm’s service is useful to analysts in making earnings forecasts. To elaborate further, although firms issue financial statements and management earnings forecasts, analysts often do not rely solely on these documents when making their predictions of firm earnings; rather, they also consult external sources of information. In addition, they evaluate firm intangibles in predicting earnings (Breton & Taffler, 2001; Luo, 2009; Luo, Homburg, & Wieseke, 2010; Whitwell, Lukas, & Hill, 2007). One important firm intangible is consumer satisfaction about the focal firm’s service. Information about consumer satisfaction “can lead to more reliable valuation of the firm” (Whitwell et al., 2007, p. 86). To estimate consumer satisfaction, analysts rely on offline information such as service investigation reports and expert reviews (Tellis & Johnson, 2007). Because of the popularity of Twitter, people send tweets to share opinions about a firm’s service. <sup>1</sup> Unlike offline information, tweets are consumers’ firsthand opinions; thus, information extracted from tweets is likely to be more reliable and timely and may help analysts predict a firm’s future earnings more effectively than investigation reports and expert reviews.

Our study responds to anecdotal evidence indicating that analysts have adopted opinion mining to scrutinize Twitter data to detect shifts in consumer behavior and to make earnings forecasts. Social media data play an important role in the financial sector. According to an IBM executive report (IBM Global Business Service, 2013, p. 6), 27% of the 124 firm respondents in the finance sector reported analyzing social data. The partnership between Nasdaq and iSentium in developing Nasdaq Analytics Hub to mine tweets also highlights the prosperity of opinion mining of tweets in the finance sector. Nasdaq uses tweets to “create new cues to market moves that investors might otherwise miss or not be able to access” (Ventura, 2017, p. 1). Goldman Sachs Asset Management (GSAM, 2016, p. 1) also mentions that “natural language processing (NLP) enabl[es] us to incorporate textual data in multiple languages…and to gauge sentiment in the text. NLP also allows us to pick up on subtle relationships between companies that might otherwise go unnoticed.” Regarding tweet mining by analysts, Rod Bodkin, the founder and CEO of Think Big Analytics, pointed out that opinion mining of tweets gauges public sentiment to “open new doors for financial analysts” (Bertolucci, 2013, p. 1) and that these opportunities “empower smart analysts who are working on problems…to be a lot more innovative and try out many different things” (p. 1). Seeing these trends, the head of global financial data partnerships at Twitter, Mr. Stephen Morse, remarked:

Financial analysts, traders, and market professionals are using Twitter to stay abreast of the market and make critical decisions.... There are many who take a longer-term view on investments and a more fundamental approach to security valuations—including what’s driving product sales and earnings. (Morse, 2016)

In this paper, we examine the associations between tweet characteristics and analyst forecast accuracy. Intuitively, one may ask whether analysts use technology to mine tweets or whether they manually skim through tweets. Which technique they use may influence our arguments in two ways. First, their choice of technique affects the speed of tweet handling. Technology may enable analysts to consider more recent tweets in their forecasting than does manual skimming. The “speed” factor does not affect our theoretical development; however, the practical challenge is to identify a proper time lag between tweet posting and the release of forecast data. Section 4.3.1 provides post hoc analyses to discuss the effect of time lag on our findings. Second, humans and technology (sentiment analysis tools, in our case) may interpret tweets differently. A computer tool to conduct sentiment analysis may not perfectly align with human judgment. Section 4.3.2 presents the result of how the accuracy of sentiment analysis tools affects our findings.

Despite the application of post hoc analyses, because the techniques of analysts making forecasts are proprietary research, we do not have hard evidence on what analysts exactly do. We thus cannot rule out that our estimates reflect the correlation (rather than causality) between tweet characteristics and analyst forecast accuracy. We therefore urge caution when interpreting our results, which suggest that there is an association (but not necessarily a causal relation) between tweet characteristics and analyst forecast accuracy.

In our study, we follow Luo (2007, 2009) to use airlines in the United States as the context. As a service industry, airlines have direct interactions with passengers who often tweet about airline services while waiting at the airport, providing rich Twitter data for analysis. We obtained tweets posted from January 2012 to November 2016 from a publicly available data set. To process this data set, we developed computer programs in Python and conducted content analysis to extract 245,495 tweets related to aviation. We then conducted aspect-based sentiment analysis to identify how the extracted tweets related to airline service quality and to classify them on the basis of valence and subjectivity. Analyst forecasting data were extracted from the Institutional Brokers’ Estimate System Academic. Using regressions, we found that the breadth of coverage and the number of posters are positively associated with forecast accuracy. The valence of tweets differentiates their effects on forecast accuracy: tweets with negative sentiments are more helpful to analysts than are tweets with positive sentiments. There is a marginal negative association between tweet dispersion and forecast accuracy. We do not detect any significant associative effect between tweet subjectivity and analyst forecast accuracy. Because we control for the availability of offline information and media effects, our results are not driven by concurrent information sources other than Twitter such as traditional media, press articles, web media, management forecasts, and air travel reports. Our results are robust to these variations and the inferences for all hypotheses remain qualitatively equal.

Our study contributes to both theory and practice. Theoretically, this is one of the first studies to integrate two research streams: social media (Twitter, in our case) and analyst forecasts. It identifies associations between tweet characteristics and decision-making in the professional services sector—forecasting of a firm’s earnings by analysts. In contrast to prior research on social media that merely focuses on individuals’ behavior (e.g., Ellison, Vitak, Gray, & Lampe, 2014; Ryan & Xenos, 2011) and firm communication with individuals (e.g., Schniederjans, Cao, & Schniederjans, 2013; Zhang, 2015), our study examines a group of stakeholders—namely, analysts— who have been largely ignored in the information systems literature. In practice, before the advent of Twitter, analysts relied on service investigation reports and expert reviews to infer consumer satisfaction about firms’ services. Analysts were unable to operate at levels of finer detail to reveal fundamental phenomena underlying the coevolution of firms and consumer behavior. This paper asks whether timely nonfinancial information extracted from highly disaggregated tweets can complement firm disclosures and provide useful predictors of firm earnings for analysts. Through empirical tests, we identify associations between tweet characteristics and analyst forecast accuracy. Our findings provide support for anecdotal evidence that analysts are using the opinion mining of tweets to predict firm earnings.

## 2 Background and Hypotheses

## 2.1 Analyst Forecasting and Importance of External Data

Analysts cannot solely rely on management earnings forecasts to prepare their forecasts because, first, management earnings forecasts are not mandatory and thus not all managers choose to issue earnings forecasts and, second, even if forecasts are released, they may be biased. Forecasts are exceptionally inaccurate for firms that suffer a decline in earnings (Jelic, Saadouni, & Briston, 1998). To complicate matters, managers’ accrual-related forecast bias in range forecasts is also affected by managerial opportunism and managers’ fear of litigation (Xu, 2010). In other words, management earnings forecasts are the aggregate outcome of parameters such as the application of various accounting treatments, estimates, and assumptions made by management. Because all these parameters are less observable to analysts, they are restricted in their ability to accurately predict firm earnings, resulting in the problem of low forecast quality.

Forecast accuracy is a key criterion for forecast quality. It is the degree of closeness of an analyst’s predicted value to the actual value of a firm’s earnings. It is a core property to assess the competence of an analyst (Gu & Wu, 2003). Accurate predictions of firm earnings can yield significant profits, and analysts who provide accurate forecasts attract more transient investors as followers (Mintchik, Stuerke, Wang, & Zhang, 2011). Such analysts are therefore more able to influence investors’ decision-making and consequently more strongly affect the market price than those who provide less accurate forecasts (Jackson, 2005). Moreover, these analysts are often rewarded with better professional career outcomes (Hong & Kubik, 2003). As such, analysts have strong incentives to improve their forecast accuracy and, thus, are likely to seek external information in order to improve the accuracy of their forecasts (Gu & Wu, 2003; Hong & Kubik, 2003).

Twitter is a rich data source. Although anecdotal evidence indicates that analysts use the opinion mining of tweets to forecast firm earnings, to our knowledge no research has examined whether tweets are associated with analyst forecast accuracy. Prior research has focused on using tweets as a surrogate to predict product popularity or stock market movement. One widely cited Twitter study is that of Asur and Huberman (2010). They counted the number of tweets related to targeted movies and constructed a linear regression model to predict movies’ box office revenues prior to release. Their predictions achieved an accuracy of 97%, which was higher than the 95% achieved by the Hollywood Stock Exchange Index. Bollen, Mao, and Zeng (2011) used tweets to examine associations between collective moods inferred by tweets and stock market performance. The authors downloaded nine million public tweets dated between February 2008 and December 2008 and used two mood-tracking tools, OpinionFinder and Google-Profile of Mood States, to classify the downloaded tweets into seven-dimensional public moods. Next, they compared the identified collective moods with the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) via a Granger causality analysis under a self-organizing fuzzy neural network. Their results show with high accuracy (87%) that collective moods on Twitter are associated with daily fluctuations in the closing values of the DJIA.

Extending this line of research, we investigate the value of the opinion mining of tweets brought to decision-making in a professional services sector— specifically, analyst forecasting. The rationale for our study is that people’s satisfaction with a firm’s service is reflected in their future purchases, which are associated with the firm’s future earnings (Lambert, 1998; Luo, 2009; Luo & Bhattacharya, 2009). Consumer satisfaction leads to repurchase intentions, positive word-of-mouth, loyalty, and profitable referrals; in contrast, consumer dissatisfaction may result in complaints, negative word-of-mouth, and even customer attrition (Gustafsson, Johnson, & Roos, 2005; Mittal & Kamakura, 2001). Thus, consumer satisfaction information is useful when valuing firms. Analysts can infer consumer satisfaction information from tweet sentiments; thus, tweets are helpful in enhancing analyst forecast accuracy. However, not all tweets are relevant to analysts’ decision-making. Tweets can be submitted by individuals, firms, and media. They can be original or replicate an existing tweet (retweets). They cover various topics. Because only tweets generated by individuals that discuss a firm’s service quality reflect people’s future purchases of the service, we focus on these tweets. Because Twitter has a large user base, these tweets reveal the experiences of many actual buyers over extended periods in natural environments. Their sentiments reflect consumer satisfaction about a firm’s service. More importantly, in the earnings predictions of firms in the consumer sector of the economy, tweets about service quality submitted by individuals are likely to be more useful to analysts than offline information. Because the analyst’s objective is to predict a firm’s sales, it is more crucial to learn how consumers perceive the service rather than to gather experts’ claims about the service (e.g., from service investigation reports and expert reviews). Therefore, tweets about service quality submitted by individuals provide far more valuable information to analysts as approximations of consumer satisfaction than do service investigation reports and expert reviews.

## 2.2 Hypotheses Development

Twitter is a source of information for analysts. When researchers examine the effect of new information on an individual’s judgment, they often use diffusion theory to examine the quantity, valence, and dispersion of information. In most prior studies, “information” implies consumer review (e.g., Dellarocas, Zhang, & Awad, 2007; Liu, 2006; and Trusov, Bucklin, & Pauwels, 2009). For our study, we investigate whether coverage, sentiment, and dispersion of tweets related to a firm’s service are associated with analyst forecast accuracy.

Our study extends the three dimensions—quantity, valence, and dispersion—to fit the context of analyst forecasting, resulting in the dimensions of coverage, sentiment, and dispersion. To elaborate, because we draw on the notion of how tweets reduce information asymmetry for analysts, our data analysis focuses on unique information that adds value to analyst forecasts. Further, “information” in the analyst forecasting literature (e.g., Bradshaw, 2004; Brown, Call, Clement, & Sharp, 2015) implies not only the information per se but also information sources. Thus, we extend “quantity” to tweet coverage by considering unique content (breadth of coverage) and tweet sources (number of posters). Section 2.2.1 discusses how these aspects relate to forecast accuracy in H1 and H2. Regarding sentiment, unlike consumer reviews, which are often subjective, tweets may be subjective or objective. Thus, our study examines both valence and subjectivity—Section 2.2.2 discusses the associations between valence/subjectivity and forecast accuracy in H3 and H4. Finally, Section 2.2.3 examines the association between tweet dispersion and forecast accuracy in H5.

## 2.2.1 Tweet Coverage

Tweet coverage concerns the amount of unique information about consumer satisfaction of a firm’s service extracted from tweets by analysts. We use two variables to operationalize tweet coverage: breadth of coverage and number of posters.

Breadth of coverage is the range of topics discussed in tweets. The “topic” in our context is the quality of service provided by a firm. We argue that when tweets cover many aspects of service quality, analysts are able to better forecast the firm’s earnings. To elaborate, consumers’ concepts of service quality are multidimensional (Stone-Romero & Stone, 1997). Use of a single dimension (such as reliability) to capture consumer satisfaction may yield imprecise results, and dimensions do vary across industries (Stone-Romero & Stone, 1997). The American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI) has identified 10 dimensions that capture the quality of airline services: flight crew courtesy and helpfulness, flight schedule and experience, baggage handling, loyalty programs, seat comfort, in-flight services, call center and ground service, reservation and check-in, food quality, and on-time arrival. These are “critical elements of passenger experience” and are found to influence consumer satisfaction with airline service (ACSI, 2017).

Because consumer satisfaction affects firm earnings (Lambert, 1998) and this information is not reported in financial reports, any incremental information that reveals consumer satisfaction about a firm’s service is useful for analysts seeking to provide a more accurate forecast of the firm’s earnings. Tweets conveying people’s opinions of a firm’s service are a source of useful information, and we thus anticipate that using them will enhance forecast accuracy. When tweets reveal more dimensions of a firm’s service—that is, when the coverage of people’s discussion about the firm’s service is broader—analysts’ knowledge of the firm’s service quality increases. Further, given that consumers’ perception of service quality is multidimensional (Stone-Romero & Stone, 1997), broader coverage helps analysts develop a holistic view of the extent of consumer satisfaction with the firm’s service. Therefore, a positive association is anticipated between breadth of coverage and analyst forecast accuracy. Notably, our variable of interest is not the number of tweets; instead, we are interested in the number of distinct service dimensions covered in tweets. Each piece of distinct information builds on analysts’ ability to accurately estimate a firm’s earnings. Considering these factors together, we hypothesize the following:

H1: Breadth of coverage by tweets about a firm’s service quality is positively associated with analyst forecast accuracy.

Next, we discuss how the number of posters who contribute to tweeting about a firm’s service quality is associated with forecast accuracy. In practice, many Twitter users do not tweet. According to a report by Digital Marketing Ramblings (2017), 44% of Twitter users had never tweeted and 43% of Twitter users who had tweeted had not done so in the year prior to the study. We consider that the number of distinct posters submitting tweets about a subject matter (in our case, a firm’s service quality) is crucial to analyst forecast accuracy. We anticipate a positive association between the number of posters contributing to tweets and analyst forecast accuracy for two reasons.

First, conceptually, each Twitter user is an independent information source. Each experiences a firm’s service in a different situation and uses his or her own perspective to form an opinion about the service. Therefore, when tweets come from many users, this helps analysts develop a holistic view of the extent of consumer satisfaction with the firm’s service. In addition, research on communication has revealed that, when more independent information sources express their opinions on a subject matter, the information obtained is more representative, less biased, and higher in cognitive diversity (Moriarty & Spekman, 1984; Weiss, Lurie, & MacInnis, 2008). Further, when more independent sources contribute to discussions about a subject matter, a collection of all their opinions can produce a more complete picture of how people generally perceive the subject matter. One can also reliably estimate the proportion of people who agree or disagree with the subject matter and assess opinion consistency (Koriat, Adiv, & Schwarz, 2016). Using a similar logic, when only a few posters provide information about a firm’s service quality, their opinions are less representative. Conversely, when more posters contribute to tweeting about a firm’s service quality, the information extracted from these tweets becomes more valuable to analysts. Since posters are independent, the representativeness and cognitive diversity of tweets increase with the number of posters. Analysts can identify a broad set of perspectives contributed by numerous posters and have more complete consideration of how a firm’s service meet or surpass people’s expectations. Further, analysts can reliably estimate the proportion of people who are satisfied or dissatisfied with a firm’s service and assess the consistency of their opinions about them (Koriat et al., 2016). This enables analysts to improve their forecasts.

Second, when tweets are submitted by many posters, these tweets can draw the attention of many other Twitter users, including analysts, because this farreaching word-of-mouth is likely to be more influential for a firm’s earnings prospects. To elaborate, tweets have a 140-character limit; thus, each carries only a brief message. The influence of a tweet does not come entirely from content substantiality; it also comes from how many Twitter users the tweet reaches (Cha, Haddadi, Benevenuto, & Gummadi, 2010). When many Twitter users engage in submitting tweets about a firm’s product, their followers can see the tweets, so they spread rapidly and become more influential. Interestingly, our prediction is similar to empirical findings regarding traditional media, despite traditional media not having a limited content length. Specifically, research has revealed that the number of newspapers reporting an issue is a more important factor in attracting public attention than the substantiality of the newspapers’ content (Kaplanski & Levy, 2010; Mazur & Lee, 1993). Thus, in our research context, when many posters submit tweets about a firm’s service, this exerts a stronger influence on other people’s perceptions; analysts are likely to delve deep to extract useful information from these tweets, and their forecasts tend to be more informed as a result. To summarize, when more posters submit tweets in relation to a firm’s service, analysts are able to make better forecasts. We thus hypothesize the following:

H2: The number of posters of tweets about a firm’s service is positively associated with analyst forecast accuracy.

## 2.2.2 Tweet Sentiment

Sentiment analysis is the automated mining of people’s attitudes toward a subject matter from textual content (Pak & Paroubek, 2010). Textual content includes paragraphs, speeches, and database sources. Many opinion-mining tools can perform sentiment analysis through Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Deep Learning. These tools use lists of taxonomies and lexicons to study word frequencies. They also study sentence structure to deal with semantic issues such as double negation and comparison structure (Khan, Atique, & Thakare, 2015; Pak & Paroubek, 2010; Pang & Lee, 2004). They then categorize textual content per valence or per subjectivity measure. Some tools (including Aylien, in our study) provide confidence scores about each categorization. In our study, we operationalize tweet sentiment with two variables: tweet valence and tweet subjectivity.<sup>2</sup>

First, we discuss tweet valence. In sentiment analysis, a tweet can be used to determine whether the poster holds a positive, neutral, or negative attitude toward the subject matter. Positive tweets reflect tweeters’ good feelings about the services of the focal firm. For example, a positive tweet in our sample is “@SouthwestAir #MAN Southwest Airlines Customer Service rocks! Baltimore flights cancelled but they took care of us. (Mon Dec 23 02:14:34 +0000 2013).” Negative tweets reflect tweeters’ bad feelings about the services of the focal firm. For example, a negative tweet in our sample is “@Delta @DeltaAssist #servicedog is a choice. So rude and said I could leave him at the #airport this is harassment. (Fri Dec 19 00:27:00 +0000 2014).” Neutral tweets convey neither good nor bad feelings on behalf of the poster.

Our study examines the differential effects of tweet valence on forecast accuracy. We anticipate that tweets conveying negative sentiments are more informative and provide more value for analyst forecasting than do those expressing positive sentiments. To elaborate, positive tweets convey people’s favorable attitudes toward a firm’s service, while negative tweets convey people’s unfavorable attitudes toward a firm’s service. Thus, positive tweets imply people’s satisfaction with a firm’s service, and their negative counterparts imply people’s dissatisfaction with the firm’s service. We argue that using negative tweets to infer consumer dissatisfaction is more accurate than using positive tweets to infer consumer satisfaction; thus, negative tweets are more informative for analyst decisionmaking and consequently are more useful in enhancing forecast accuracy than are positive counterparts. The rationale for our expectation is twofold.

The first reason is that the two types of tweet sentiment (i.e., positive and negative) are different in causal attributions (Chen & Lurie, 2013; Sen & Lerman, 2007). Tweets expressing positive sentiments are often attributed to personal experience and mood. Personal experience and mood are subjective, vary from person to person, and are less replicable (Park, Lee, & Han, 2007). Therefore, it is less straightforward to use positive tweets to approximate consumer satisfaction. In contrast, tweets conveying negative sentiments are often attributed to poor service quality or an inefficient shopping process (Chen & Lurie, 2013). These tweets are directly linked to consumer dissatisfaction; therefore, they tend to be informative and diagnostic and can help analysts approximate consumer dissatisfaction.

The second reason is that people are more sensitive to losses than to gains (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), and are therefore more influenced by negative than positive tweets. Thus, the effect of negative tweets on consumer purchasing is more salient than the effect of positive tweets on consumer purchasing. That consumers are more sensitive to negative information than to positive information was empirically proven by Lim and Chung (2011) and Mourdoukoutas and Siomkos (2010). Because tweets with negative sentiments more powerfully draw people’s attention and are perceived as more reliable, analysts consider them to have a stronger influence on people’s future purchases and to be more useful for predicting future sales. Taking all this evidence into account, we hypothesize the following:

H3: Negative tweets have a stronger positive association with analyst forecast accuracy than do positive tweets.

Next, we discuss tweet subjectivity. Subjectivity detection scrutinizes evidentiality in text. It concerns issues such as the information source, whether the information is being presented as a fact or an opinion, and whether writers express their feelings (Barbosa & Feng, 2010; Bravo-Marquez, Mendoza, & Poblete, 2014). In general, tweets are classified as subjective when they present the private state of a poster (e.g., their feelings and emotions) or include opinionated words (e.g., “love,” “awesome,” and “decent”). Subjective tweets are open to greater interpretation based on the poster’s aesthetics (Barbosa & Feng, 2010; Su & Markert, 2008). Conversely, tweets are classified as objective when they provide verifiable facts or quantifiable information (Khan et al., 2015)— in other words, objective tweets present factual material to the audience.

We anticipate that subjective tweets are more useful to analysts in making accurate forecasts than are objective tweets for two reasons. First, subjective tweets contain information beyond simply objective descriptions of service details or statistics that may already be commonly known. They convey a poster’s feelings, perceptions, and opinions about service quality. This information is less observable and less available from external information sources. In contrast, objective tweets convey descriptions of service quality and are transmitted in impersonal ways. These descriptions contain verifiable data that are available from external information sources. For instance, objective tweets that describe flight delays and cancellations do not provide analysts with much new information because the Bureau of Transportation Statistics regularly provides service failure data.

Because more new information reduces information asymmetry, subjective tweets enable analysts to more accurately predict a firm’s earnings than do objective tweets.

More importantly, people’s feelings, perceptions, and opinions about a firm’s service are subjective indicators of their satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) with a firm’s service (Mittal & Kamakura, 2001). As such, subjective tweets are more useful and relevant in the prediction of consumer satisfaction than are objective tweets. Further, subjective tweets carry information that accrues with consumers’ usage of a service. They exert a stronger influence over other people’s perceptions of the firm’s service and future purchases than do objective tweets. Hence, analysts are likely to extract useful information from these tweets and, as a result, their forecasts are expected to have a stronger association with these tweets. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H4: Subjective tweets have a stronger positive association with analyst forecast accuracy than do objective tweets.

## 2.2.3 Tweet Dispersion

Tweet dispersion, which captures the extent to which people disagree about a firm’s service, can have a significant influence on analyst forecasting. In general, prior studies on consumer reviews have found that high degrees of dispersion among online reviews generated by shoppers increases review credibility and, subsequently, leads to more purchases. To elaborate, it has been observed that many firms take advantage of online reviews as a marketing tool to bias consumer purchases (Dellarocas, 2013; Zhu & Zhang, 2010). As a result, some shoppers speculate that a few dominant sources contribute to a substantial portion of positive reviews (Zhang, Li, & Chen, 2012). Therefore, they consider more divergent reviews more credible for making informed purchase decisions (Zhang et al., 2012). The above arguments have been empirically proven by Weathers, Swain, and Grover (2015). The authors extracted secondary data from Amazon to show that consumers perceive that a balance of positive and negative reviews about a product increases review credibility. These findings in the literature on online reviews align with economist predictions that a newsreader who aggregates news from diverse opinions derived from various sources will gain a more accurate assessment of the economic situation than when the news sources are similar (Sarvary & Parker, 1997).

For analyst forecasting, we predict that high dispersion of tweets about a firm’s service quality leads to low analyst forecast accuracy. This prediction is opposite from what the literature on consumer reviews suggests because of one subtle difference between the two research contexts. One purpose of online review sites is to enable individuals to share their opinions about products and help other shoppers make an informed decision (Cheung, Sia, & Kuan, 2012). The intention of these sites to collect feedback for use by other shoppers is clear. Conversely, the key purpose of social media is to connect people. People post on social media for various reasons. Their posts can be “a mixture of fact and opinion, impression and sentiment, founded and unfounded tidbits, experience and even rumour” (Blackshaw & Nazzaro, 2006, p. 4). In other words, people who tweet about a product may simply be voicing their opinions and sharing their personal feelings; they may not have strong intentions to influence other shoppers through their tweets. Therefore, unlike review dispersion, which increases review credibility, tweet dispersion may not necessarily similarly inform analysts about tweet credibility. Indeed, when people voice their opinions and personal feelings in relation to a firm’s service on Twitter and their voices are diverse, it is hard for analysts to precisely assess which opinion is correct and influential to others. Such tweet diversity may also confuse analysts, creating uncertainty about how these people perceive the firm’s service and whether these shoppers will repurchase the firm’s service, which can thus complicate their ability to predict a firm’s future earnings. Based on these considerations, we anticipate that tweet dispersion leads to low analyst forecast accuracy.

H5: Dispersion of tweet valence reduces analyst forecast accuracy.

## 3 Models and Data

## 3.1 Empirical Models

To assess whether tweet characteristics are associated with analyst forecast accuracy, we adopted a regression model similar to that used in the accounting and finance literature (e.g., Amiram, Owens, & Rozenbaum, 2016; Dhaliwal et al., 2012; Merkley, Michaely, & Pacelli, 2017; Lang & Lundholm, 1996; Lihong & Riedl, 2014; Tan, Wang, & Welker, 2011). More specifically, we estimated the following panel regression models with firm- and year-fixed effects:

$$
\begin{array}{l} F E _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta \text { Tweet   variable } (s) _ {i, t - 1} + \sum \beta \\ \text { Controls } _ {i, t} + \sum \beta \text { Year   indicators } _ {t} + \sum \beta \text { Firm } \\ \text { indicators } _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where the subscript i indexes firm and t indexes time. Consistent with the finance literature, the dependent variable, $F E _ { i , t }$ (forecast error), is measured as the difference between the median of analysts’ earnings forecasts and actual quarterly earnings, scaled by the stock price at the beginning of the fiscal quarter. Tweet variable<sub>i,t</sub> is, in turn, (a) Coverage<sub>i,t</sub>, the breadth of coverage (H1); (b) Posters<sub>i,t</sub>, the number of Twitter users who posted about a particular airline (H2); (c) $\mathit { P o s i t i \nu e } _ { i , t } / \mathit { N e u t r a l } _ { i , t } / \mathit { N e g a t i \nu e } _ { i , t } ,$ the number of positive, neutral, or negative tweets that are classified by Aylien’s sentiment analysis into at least valence dimension (H3); (d) $S u b j e c t i \nu e _ { i , \nu } / O b j e c t i \nu e _ { i , t } ,$ the number of subjective or objective tweets that are classified into at least one service dimension (H4); and (e) $S D _ { - } V a l e n c e _ { i , t } ,$ the standard deviation of tweet valence where each positive, neutral, and negative tweet is assigned a value of 1, 0, and −1 for its valence, respectively (H5). $C o n t r o l s _ { i , i }$ <sub>t</sub> include a vector of firm and forecast characteristics as well as other information sources that may affect forecast error. Year indicators<sub>t</sub> capture time-fixed effects such as economy- and industry-wide factors, and Firm indicators<sub>i</sub> capture firm-specific fixed effects that are time invariant. Detailed definitions can be found in Appendix C.

One point worth noting is that controlling for firmfixed effects means much of the variations of the firm variables, especially the variables that do not vary substantially over time, are subsumed by the firm indicators. In other words, including firm indicators in our model biases against locating statistically significant results for our tweet variables and, thus, including the firm indicators provides us with a stronger test than not including them.

The airline industry is a cyclical business (Pearce, 2012). Moreover, individuals often make their travel plans in advance and rely on online information to make detailed plans (Pan & Fesenmaier, 2006). Therefore, tweets may not have an immediate effect on firm finances, and there should therefore be a time lag between the time tweets are posted and analyst forecasting. Inspired by Goh, Heng, and Lin, (2013), who set a time lag in their regression model to examine the relationship between service reviews on social media and consumer behavior, we set a one-month time lag between the month in which tweets were created and the month in which the analyst forecast was issued. We used the Twitter data in the $m ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ month (e.g., January 2010) to predict the accuracy of the forecast issued in the $( m + 1 ) ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ month (e.g., February 2010). The results of the regressions using a one-month time lag are presented in Section 4.2. In addition, we used two other options for time lags (i.e., a three-month lag and a six-month lag) to retest our hypotheses. The results of using a longer time lag between the independent and dependent variables are reported in Section 4.3.1.

All hypotheses have the same dependent variable— analyst forecast error (FE). This is the inverse of forecast accuracy. We followed the literature in finance (e.g., Hilary & Hsu, 2013; Hughes, Liu, & Su, 2008) to measure forecast error as the median forecast of earnings per share minus the actual earnings per share for airline i in fiscal quarter q, then deflated by stock price of airline i at the beginning of the current fiscal quarter. Because the independent variables for H3 (Positive and Negative) and those for H4 (Subjective and Objective) are highly correlated, to avoid multicollinearity, we ran panel regression models separately. All regressions contained the same control variables, except that we included the number of neutral tweets for the regressions that test H3.

We included four sets of control variables. First, we controlled for basic firm characteristics such as firm size (lnSize), financial leverage (Leverage), and a firm’s growth prospect (BM). Large firms generally have a better information environment because large firms tend to disclose information more frequently and have more stable earnings than do smaller firms, leading to high forecast accuracy (Barron, Kim, Lim, & Stevens, 1998; Lang & Lundholm, 1996). Firms with a higher level of debt are inherently riskier and therefore less predictable (Barth, Kasznik, & McNichols, 2001). Market-to-book is used as a proxy for growth prospect to control for the difference in difficulty in valuing high- versus low-growth firms (Dechow & Sloan, 1997).

Second, we controlled for the effect of firm performance on forecast accuracy. Well-performing firms tend to disclose more information while poorperforming firms tend to do the opposite (Kothari, Shu, & Wysocki, 2009); analyst forecast can therefore be affected (Hwang, Jan, & Basu, 1996). We controlled for the effect of firm performance on analyst forecast by including returns volatility (ROA\_sd) and a loss indicator (Loss).

Third, we controlled for forecast characteristics such as forecast horizon (Horizon) and the number of analysts following (Ana\_num) that are known to be related to forecast outcomes. Forecasts that are issued closer to the earning announcement incorporate up-to-date information, while a firm’s information environment is likely to be richer when it is covered by more analysts (Healy & Palepu, 2001).

Fourth, we controlled for information disclosed on a number of important and publicly available information sources other than Twitter that analysts are likely to consider. To be specific, airline management, being corporate insiders, may voluntarily issue earnings guidance to the market $( M g m t \_ f c s t )$ and substantiate their views on an airline’s prospects. Journalists of the traditional mass media (Trad\_media) and web-based media (Web\_media) may publish articles that reveal significant information about an airline. The United States Department of Transportation regularly publishes updates on flight performance data including delays, diversions, and cancellations, which reveal negative information on customer satisfaction (Flight\_dissatisfaction). Likewise, the widely recognized Skytrax World

Airlines Awards (Awards) are given out to airlines providing outstanding services each year. These awards signal positive information on customer satisfaction.

## 3.3 Twitter Data

Twitter shares a free 1% sample of its tweets through streaming application program interfaces (APIs). An open-resource organization, Archive Team: The Twitter Stream Grab, downloads tweets with Twitter’s streaming APIs and shares historical Twitter raw data with the public. This website provides sets of Twitter data for the period January 2012 to November 2016. Because APIs do not return all tweets, one may question whether the data constitute a representative sample. Morstatter, Pfeffer, and Liu (2014) assessed the representativeness of tweets downloaded through Twitter’s streaming APIs. They found that “overall the tweets that come through the Sample API are a representative sample of the true activity on Twitter” (p. 556). Thus, we believe that our tweet sample is close to a sample randomly extracted from Twitter. We used the following procedural steps to extract and process relevant tweets about the targeted airlines.

## 3.4 Data Extraction and Cleaning

We identified all tweets related to the airlines of interest. The tweet line contained all information about the tweet, including the tweeter’s ID, time and date, and number of followers for that tweeter. The final data set contained 245,495 from 198,712 distinct Twitter users with regard to 10 airlines during the study period. Next, we extracted all the hashtags in the airline tweets. The number of hashtags was close to 35,000. These hashtags highlighted tweeters’ discussion topics, such as #delayed and #flyunited.

The data set we obtained from Archive Team has two limitations. First, it contains corrupted data. Twitter data retrieved from Archive Team: The Twitter Stream Grab do not contain the data for April 2012, January 2014, January 2015, and February 2015. As a result, the available data contain 55 months of Twitter activity. We used the tweets for the three months prior to the missing month to calculate an average and used this average to replace the missing values. Moreover, the data for some months are incomplete. For example, there are 31 days in December 2012, but the data contain only 27 days of tweets; similarly, June 2013 contains 28 days, July 2014 contains 27 days, and April 2015 contains 25 days. We thus standardized our variables of interest on tweet per day basis.

Second, we carefully handled tweets for airlines that were merged or acquired during the period of our study. AirTran Airways was bought by Southwest Airlines in 2010, US Airways merged with American Airlines in 2013, and Continental Airlines merged with United Airlines in 2012. These airlines kept the name of the latter airline in each pair. Considering that individuals might use the old names AirTran Airways and Continental Airlines in tweets and that the mergers of United Airlines and Southwest were completed before 2012, we included the tweets for AirTran Airways and Continental Airlines for Southwest Airlines and United Airlines, respectively. For American Airlines, because its merger with US Airways was completed in December 2013 and a new group was founded after that, we did not include any tweets about American Airlines and US Airways before December 2013 in the selected data and counted all tweets for US Airways after December 2013 as being for American Airlines.

## 3.4.1 Content Analysis and Aspect-based Sentiment Analysis

After the tweet extraction and cleaning, we went through two major computational steps, content analysis and aspect-based sentiment analysis, to code the tweets.

Content analysis is to systematically evaluate texts to interpret and code textual material. On Twitter, people connect and talk about many topics. Generally, people use the @ sign to call out usernames in tweets, such as @USAirways, to mention an entity in tweets and link tweets to their profiles. They use hashtags (#) to specify the discussion topics. When content analysis is applied to process tweets, it extracts relevant tweets from the @ sign. To extract tweets related to the focal airlines, our Python programs used the following list: @AmericanAir, @USAirways, @Delta, @United, @Continental, @SouthwestAir, @Airtran, @JetBlue, @AlaskaAir, @Allegiant, @HawaiianAir, @SpiritAirlines and @VirginAmerica. Further, because some posters do not use the @ signs to call out usernames in tweets, our program extracted these tweets by using the titles of our focal airlines.

We then inputted the extracted tweets for aspect-based sentiment analysis. Sentiment analysis is the process of identifying and categorizing opinions expressed in a piece of text to determine whether the person’s attitude toward a subject matter is positive, neutral, or negative. This classification relied on a set of keywords and a machine learning algorithm. In our study, we used a cloud NLP software package developed by Aylien to conduct aspect-based sentiment analyses.<sup>3</sup> We chose this software package because it was widely used (Dale, 2015) and one of the best opinion-mining packages (Batista et al., 2015). It can handle complex structures, such as comparative sentences, negation, transferred negation, and double negation. This software package assigns tweets into positive, neutral, and negative (for tweet valence), and into subjective and objective (for tweet subjectivity). For each assignment, the package provides a confidence score. Another strength of the chosen package is the capability to conduct aspect-based sentiment analysis. Aspect-based sentiment analysis assigns a tweet to service quality dimensions as specified by the ACSI. At the time of writing, the software package by Aylien only covers four industries: airlines, hotels, cars, and restaurants. Appendices D and E presents examples of tweets. Appendix F provides some technical details of Aylien’s aspect-based sentiment analysis.

Managements’ earnings guidance data were sourced from Zacks Investment Research database through WRDS.

## 4.3 Post Hoc Analysis

## 4.3.1 How Does Time Lag Between Tweet Posting and the Release of Analyst Forecasts Affect Our Findings?

In Section 4.2, our regressions use a time lag of one month. We consider a time lag of one month appropriate, given that Twitter data are available in real time and analysts update their forecasts on a monthly basis. To strengthen our results, we varied the length of time lag and repeated the regressions.

We used a time lag of three months to rerun the regressions. In this arrangement, the dependent variable is analyst forecast error in the m<sup>th</sup> month, and the explanatory variables are the values occurring in the $( \mathrm { m } \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 3 ) ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ month. In the first regression, which associates the number of distinct service dimensions covered by tweets with analyst forecast errors, we find that the number of distinct service dimensions covered three months prior to the analyst forecasts does not have a significant association with forecast error $( \beta = - 0 . 0 3 0 , t = - 0 . 3 5 0 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . The third regression compares the influence of positive and negative tweets on analyst forecast error. The results of the first regression show that the number of distinct service dimensions covered by tweets posted six months prior to the analyst forecasts does not have a significant association with forecast error $( \beta = - 0 . 0 9 3 , t = - 1 . 0 7 1 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . The number of posters six months prior to the forecast also becomes nonsignificant $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 6 , t = 0 . 1 9 2 , p >$ 0.1). In the third regression, comparing the effects of positive and negative tweets, the combined results show that both positive tweets posted six months prior to analyst forecasts $( \beta = 0 . 1 3 8 , t = 1 . 3 1 1 , p >$ 0.1) and negative tweets posted six months prior to analyst forecasts $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 1 , t = - 0 . 4 2 2 , p > 0 . 1 )$ do not have a significant association with analyst forecast error. In the last regression, which compares the influence of subjective and objective tweets, the results show that subjective tweets posted six months prior to analyst forecasts $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 4 , t = - 0 . 0 4 0 , p >$ 0.1) and objective tweets $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 9 , t = - 0 . 1 3 0 , p >$ 0.1) do not have a significant association with analyst forecast error.

In summary, with a longer time lag (i.e., a threemonth and a six-month lag) between the independent variables and the predictor (i.e., analyst forecast error), none of the independent variables of interest has a significant association with analyst forecast error. Interestingly, the coefficient signs are all in the same direction as those reported in Section 4.2, with the only exception the coefficient for the number of posters with a six-month lag, where it is practically equal to zero. For example, the number of distinct service dimensions covered by tweets exerts a negative effect on forecast error. Negative tweets exert a more negative effect on forecast error than do positive tweets. Subjective tweets exert a more negative effect on forecast error than do objective tweets. However, all these coefficients have small magnitudes and are nonsignificant. In other words, although the effect directions for a three-month time lag and a six-month time lag are the same as for a onemonth time lag, the associations are mostly nonsignificant.

To complement the above analysis using lagged tweets, we further explored if tweets can be used to improve longer-term forecast accuracy by shifting our focus from quarterly earnings forecast errors to annual earnings forecast errors. Different from the above analyses which compare the effects of recent (i.e., one month) and old tweets (i.e. three or six months) on quarterly earnings forecast accuracy, this post hoc analysis considers the aggregate effects of tweets in a longer timeframe (tweets during the 12- month rolling window before a certain earnings forecast is made) on the forecast accuracy of a longerterm earnings target (annual earnings forecast). Focusing on annual earnings forecasts, we reran all regressions for our five hypotheses. The results are presented in Appendix B. In short, the results are consistent with our earlier findings where quarterly earnings forecasts were used. The results from this analysis suggest that tweets may also serve to improve longer-term forecast accuracy.

In conclusion, when we used tweets to predict quarterly earnings forecast error, among the three options for time lag (one month, three months, and six months), we identified significant associations between tweet characteristics and forecast error only for the option of a one-month time lag. Recent tweets are more influential on analyst forecast accuracy than historical tweets. When we consider all tweets in a year to predict annual earnings forecast error, the effects of tweets on analyst forecast accuracy remain.

## 4.3.2 How Does the Accuracy of Sentiment Analysis by Aylien Affect Our Findings?

The sentiment in the tweets can be vague and not strongly expressed by the user. Thus, this may affect the regression results because of inaccurate sentiment classifications. Therefore, we conducted a series of robustness tests to examine how the results of hypotheses testing for H3 and H4 are affected when the accuracy of sentiment analysis varies. The sentiment analysis by Aylien outputs a polarity confidence, which is a number between 0 and 1 for each tweet that indicates the strength of the sentiment expressed within the tweet. Scores closer to 1 indicate higher confidence in the classified sentiment of the tweet. In the robustness tests, we ran the regression models using only positive and negative tweets with a polarity confidence of 0.5 to retest H3. Then, we repeated the same procedure using only subjective and objective tweets with a confidence score of 0.5 to retest H4. The results are reported in Table 5. In short, the results hold.

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Key Findings

Our study develops a broad understanding of how tweets can enhance analyst forecasting. We investigated a series of questions on three aspects of tweeting—coverage, sentiment, and dispersion—and their associations with analyst forecast accuracy. Table 6 summarizes our results of hypothesis testing. In the following, we discuss three major findings.

First, more people posting tweets that describe various service dimensions of a firm increases the information available to analysts, resulting in an increase in forecast accuracy. Our study suggests that tweets revealing people’s opinions about a firm’s service quality are useful to analysts for making an accurate forecast of firm earnings. We developed two hypotheses (H1 and H2) to explore this association. H1 predicts that a greater number of service dimensions covered by tweets related to airline service quality increases analyst forecast accuracy. The results confirm our prediction. Further, similar to the prediction of H2, the number of distinct posters sending tweets in relation to airline services is associated with analyst forecast accuracy. Combining the results of H1 and H2, tweets conveying how people think about an airline’s service are directly related to people’s future purchases and the airline’s future revenue, allowing analysts to accurately forecast its earnings.

Second, tweet sentiment suggests that the value of tweets for analysts depends on their content. In this respect, we examined two variables—tweet valence and tweet subjectivity—to discern the differential effects of valence and subjectivity on forecast accuracy. Similar to H3’s prediction, negative opinions about airline services posted on Twitter have a stronger association with accuracy of forecasts than do positive opinions. In contrast to H4, tweets expressing posters’ subjective evaluations about airline services do not have a stronger association with forecast accuracy than do tweets featuring only objective descriptions of service quality. Combining the results of H3 and H4, our results present recent adopters of tweet analytics with some pointers: negative tweets have more positive associations with forecast accuracy, but tweet subjectivity may not be a consideration factor for analysts in making their forecasts.

Third, on the basis of the significant result of H3, we investigated how dispersion of tweet valence influences analyst forecast accuracy. H5 shows that when the valence of people’s tweeting about a firm’s service quality is more diverse, analyst forecast accuracy is lower.

## 5.2 Theoretical Contributions

Our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we adopted a different rationale to consider the metrics of diffusion theory and predict how information characteristics affect individuals’ judgment. We examined three aspects of tweeting (i.e., coverage, sentiment, and dispersion). They neatly map to the metrics in diffusion theory (i.e., volume, valence, and dispersion). These metrics are often used in the literature on online reviews to examine how online reviews influence potential shoppers to buy. Although some of our findings (such as H1) are similar to the literature on online reviews, our theoretical arguments are different. A large volume of online reviews promotes product awareness and increases consumers’ purchase intentions (Dellarocas et al., 2007), but in our case, a large tweet coverage informs analysts about service quality to make an accurate forecast.

Some of our findings (such as H3 and H5) are different from those of the literature on online reviews. Previous studies suggest that positive reviews (Park et al., 2007) and diverse reviews (Weathers et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2012) exert a stronger effect on consumers’ purchase intentions, in contrast to our findings that negative reviews (H3) and low tweet dispersion (H5) have stronger associations with analyst forecast accuracy. A possible reason for this difference between the findings, even though all use diffusion theory, may be the different intentions of the respective study subjects.

For example, regarding previous literature on online reviews, the key purpose of online reviewers is to convey information to influence potential buyers; thus, both positive and negative reviews are informative. In contrast, for the purposes of our study, posters tweet to share their thoughts about airlines; as such, their purpose is not necessarily to influence other people at all. Hence, some tweets may not be informative to decision makers. Our findings suggest that, in our context, negative tweets are more informative to analysts—as reflected by a stronger association with analyst forecast accuracy—than are positive tweets.

Likewise, some reviewers may write reviews for the singular purpose of influencing other shoppers (i.e., because they are compensated in some way for doing so), making online review credibility a significant concern. Therefore, in the online review context, review dispersion helps improve perceptions of review credibility. In contrast, for our context, diverse tweets may confuse analysts in their attempts to make accurate forecasts.

Taken together, diffusion theory provides researchers with a framework to explore the metrics of information (volume, valence, and dispersion). Our study sheds light on diffusion theory by illustrating that high volume, high valence, and high dispersion of information may not necessarily help decision makers. Whether high volume, high valence, and high dispersion of information are helpful depends on the context in which information is created. Future research should identify contextual factors to enrich diffusion theory—for instance, which circumstances high (or low) volume, high (or low) valence, and high (or low) dispersion of information are beneficial to decision makers.

Further, adding to diffusion theory, our study considered tweet subjectivity (as a characteristic of “valence”). The result of H4 indicates no detected association between tweet subjectivity and analyst forecast accuracy. We probed into this result by questioning the accuracy of sentiment analysis. Further tests in Section 4.3.2 suggest that the result of H4 remains unchanged even when tweets with low confidence scores are filtered out. Our result is different from the literature on consumer research (such as Mittal & Kamakura, 2001), which suggests that subjective information better reflects repurchase intent. This is because repurchase intent embodies subjective personal feelings; however, in the prediction of corporate economic outcomes (such as earnings forecasts), both subjective and objective tweets are informative to analysts. Our findings imply that application domain is an important consideration factor when one determines the relative informativeness between subjective and objective information.

Second, our study extends the understanding of how information extracted from Twitter can benefit a specific group of stakeholders—analysts—who have been largely overlooked in prior research, by evaluating whether tweets represent increased information for enhancing analyst forecast accuracy. Information systems researchers have examined the role of tweeting in the context of creating complex informational dynamics that propel predictions in stock market prices (e.g., Bollen et al., 2011; Mittal & Kamakura, 2001). However, the rationale of how tweets add value to analyst forecasting is different from how they add value to stock trading. Day traders and technical analysts use technical indicators to measure and profit from short-term price changes often caused by market momentum associated with a stock (Hvidkjaer, 2006). They advocate speed and analyze tweets to detect market momentum, which is not based on fundamentals (Renault, 2017). Rather, they want a directional signal to figure out the “crowd psychology” of the market. In contrast, analyst forecasts are based on fundamentals and expectations of firm growth and profitability. Because earnings forecasts advocate prediction accuracy, analysts have to identify relevant tweets and scrutinize tweet content to understand factors that influence a firm’s growth and profitability. Because the service quality of firms in the consumer sector affects future earnings of these firms (Ittner & Larcker, 1998), we infer people’s perceptions of a firm’s service quality based on highly disaggregated tweets. Our theoretical rationale for how tweets bring value to analyst forecasting is different from that of prior studies on stock trading. In our case, tweeting informs analysts about a firm’s business operations and its long-term growth. Our analysis was enabled by a recent technology, aspect-based sentiment analysis, which can identify tweets about service quality. This opens up a new research avenue that relates tweets to business operations and long-term value activities.

Third, our study adds to the literature on analyst forecasting by incorporating Twitter data in its examination. In accounting and finance, researchers leverage the opportunities brought by big data for their disciplines (Alles, 2015; Suddaby, Saxton, & Gunz, 2015; Vasarhelyi, Kogan, & Tuttle, 2015; Warren, Moffitt, & Byrnes, 2015). Further, the potential of contemporary textual analysis has been highlighted in a recent study by Loughran and McDonald (2016). The authors remarked that “almost all quantitative data in this arena are contextualized by textual information, which we are just now beginning to explore for deeper insights” (Loughran & McDonald, 2016, p. 1225). We consider Twitter to be a source of big data, and aspectbased sentiment analysis a type of contemporary textual analysis. Prior studies (e.g., Blankespoor, Miller, & White, 2014; Bartov, Faurel, & Mohanram, 2018; Prokofieva, 2015) started to explore the role of Twitter in corporate disclosures and information dissemination; however, few of them used contemporary textual analysis. Our study responds to Loughran and McDonald’s (2016) call by exploring the associations between tweet characteristics and analyst forecasting accuracy with the use of aspectbased sentiment analysis.

Our study demonstrates that tweets are useful because they provide analysts with firm-specific “nanoeconomic” data and enable them to conduct a bottom-up analysis of the firm’s fundamentals. In the past, communication occurring between individuals was invisible to firms, but Twitter now makes it visible and firms can improve their knowledge of who knows and thinks what. For instance, analysts collect numerous perspectives from people, including opinions about product or service quality, management, and where a firm is situated in its respective industry, and integrate this piecemeal information with existing corporate disclosures into a more holistic view of the fundamentals of the firm. In our research context, Twitter makes communication among the public (i.e., air travelers, in our case) visible to analysts and this improves analysts’ knowledge of what air travelers know about the airlines and what they think about the quality of an airline’s service. Theoretically, this study extends the literature on analyst forecasting that takes a top-down perspective and adopts only corporate disclosure and news media by using macroeconomic factors to examine the industry in which a firm operates or the economy as a whole. This type of analysis seeks out industries and companies that represent promising opportunities in the context of the business cycle. However, these studies cannot easily account for the effect of individual perceptions of a firm and their behaviors on firm earnings. Our study takes a bottom-up approach to generate information from aggregated tweets. It sheds light on how Twitter alleviates informational friction and enhances the decision quality of information users (i.e., analysts, in our case) in financial markets.

## 5.3 Practical Contributions

Our study contributes to practice in three ways. First, it informs practitioners, especially analysts, about the value of Twitter data in finance domains. Twitter continues to expand at a precipitous pace; unfortunately, most people see only the hedonic benefits brought by Twitter to individual users. Some people even suggest that tweets do not convey much value-relevant information because Twitter has no mechanism to incentivize high-quality conversations. In contrast to such suggestions, our study shows that Twitter has utilitarian benefits for firms in the professional services sector—for example, tweets can help business professionals enhance their job performance. Specifically, our study demonstrates associations between tweet characteristics and forecast accuracy, which is good news for practitioners.

In forecasting firm earnings, analysts’ ability to access data sources is a key factor impacting their forecast quality. In the era of the data revolution, a senior executive, Takashi Suwabe of Goldman Sachs, points out that financial institutions are now enabled to process unstructured data to “identify strong businesses with attractive valuations, positive sentiment and a strong connection with positive themes that are trending in the markets” (GSAM, 2018, p. 1). However, which data sources financial institutions use and how, exactly, they use data sources for analysis is, to our knowledge, not documented in any public sources—possibly because these questions have a profound impact on competitive advantages. Our study sheds light on the first question by revealing the associations between tweets and analyst forecast accuracy. This aligns with anecdotal evidence suggesting that firms do consider tweets in making forecasts. Specifically, we find that tweet coverage, tweet sentiment, and the dispersion of tweets regarding people’s opinions about service quality are relevant to the prediction of a firm’s earnings. Our findings encourage institutions that have not yet adopted opinion mining to extract information from tweets to better predict of firm earnings. The next challenge hinges on the answer to the second question—tweets are useful for analyst forecasting, but how, exactly, should analysts process tweets for forecasting purposes? This is a very practical question. Analysts must understand the dynamics of the relevant firm’s industry and its competitive positioning within that industry, and should use general market trends as a benchmark, incorporating views derived from tweets about the firm’s prospects to guide the forecasting process. However, while this question is essentially beyond the scope of our paper, we would encourage firms to progressively create insightful, robust tweet analytics to monitor relevant activity on Twitter and extract insights from relevant tweets.

Second, after the publication by Bollen et al. (2011), hedge funds were established that analyzed tweets to determine where to invest. Many of these funds were successful; for instance, during the month in which the \$40 million Derwent Capital Markets fund was in operation, it reportedly returned 1.86%, beating the overall market as well as the average hedge fund. Interestingly, a few of these funds closed their doors after just a few months of operation because their analysis worked so well that the fund’s principal investors suggested creating a mass-market product to reach a larger market (Tweney, 2012). This provides evidence that practitioners have realized the potential of using Twitter data for predicting firm finances. Some studies (e.g., Luo, Zhang, & Duan, 2013)

scrutinize the predictive relationships between social media data and stock returns.

Our study broadens the application scope of Twitter data to the analyst forecasting domain by using airlines as its context. The rationale for our argument is that tweets about service quality reflect the potential purchases of these Twitter users, which is useful information for predicting future earnings. This rationale is likewise appropriate to other industries in the consumer sector of the economy. For instance, tweets about the service quality of hotels, resorts, and vacation properties owned by Hyatt Hotels Corporation could be used to help predict the future earnings of Hyatt Hotels Corporation. At the time of writing, Aylien’s aspect-based sentiment analysis was capable of identifying the service quality of hotels, regarding details such as room amenities, staff service, room view, and room comfort—qualities that are likely associated with customer satisfaction and future repurchasing. In the future, we encourage researchers to explore the scope of applicability of our findings in various industries.

Third, our study investigates the usefulness of nonfinancial metrics derived from Twitter data to predict firm earnings. Few studies have examined the association between firm value and nonfinancial disclosures (and have not done so in the context of Twitter). Behn and Riley (1999) used airline operating statistics to infer consumer satisfaction when predicting quarterly revenues, expenses, and operating incomes. Dhaliwal et al. (2012) showed that issuing stand-alone corporate social responsibility reports is associated with lower analyst forecast error. In times of rapid change, firm disclosures are unlikely to be timely and will have limited value for analysts. By offering an abundant source of timely information, using Twitter data can help to address this problem. For instance, on the day of the 2016 United States presidential election, 35 million tweets about the election were recorded. These tweets provided instantaneous insight into what people thought about the two candidates. Jackson and Sykora (2016) analyzed real-time tweets posted on that date to predict the election outcome, and their predictions actually outperformed those of pollsters. In relation to analyst forecasting, our study finds that Twitter data can be a source of timely nonfinancial information associated with firm earnings in the consumer sector and that this information can complement financial disclosures to enhance forecast accuracy. As reported in Section 4.3.1, the association between tweets and forecast accuracy is more salient for analyst forecasts that are issued very soon after relevant tweets are posted. This implies that opinion mining of Twitter data outperforms many other data sources in terms of timeliness.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Research

This paper has two primary limitations. First, only a single industry—the airline industry—is addressed. While this is partly because people frequently tweet about airline services, we also employed advanced aspect-based sentiment analysis to classify tweets into service dimensions and, at the time of writing, this advanced analysis was only applicable in four areas: airlines, hotels, cars, and restaurants. Given that, among these four areas, the aviation industry contributes the most to the world’s gross domestic product, we chose to focus on airlines. Unavoidably, this may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research should explore the use of opinion mining in forecasting the earnings of firms in other consumer industries. Second, we collected data from Twitter because it was the most widely used social media platform at the time this study was conducted. Another popular social media platform is Facebook, which focus on networks of acquaintances, and thus involves different discussion dynamics. Future research could be conducted to compare the effectiveness of extracting consumer satisfaction about a firm’s products on different social media platforms for purposes of forecast accuracy. As noted by Fortin and Uncles (2011), Twitter is a popular social medium, but it cannot credibly represent the entire Internet.

## 6 Conclusions

Our study develops a broad understanding of how Twitter data can enhance analyst forecasting. For firms in consumer sectors, consumer satisfaction is an intangible that can lead to more reliable firm valuation. Although offline information (such as service investigation reports and expert reviews) can approximate consumer satisfaction with a firm’s service, these data sources are at a high level of abstraction. Thus, analysts are constrained from operating at levels of finer detail and cannot revealing the fundamental phenomena underlying the coevolution of firms and consumer behavior. Given anecdotal evidence that analysts have adopted opinion mining to scrutinize tweets, we ask whether timely, nonfinancial information extracted from highly disaggregated tweets are strongly associated with analyst forecast accuracy and question whether this information might complement firm disclosures. Our results support the hypotheses that data are provided when anonymous people discuss a firm’s services casually on Twitter and that the mining of these data is positively associated with analyst forecast accuracy. These results are robust even when adjusted for simultaneity in terms of adherence to mass media and analyst forecast accuracy.

## What are Natural Language Processing and Deep Learning?

NLP is an area of computer science and artificial intelligence concerned with “teaching” computers to understand the complexity of, and interpret, human language. It has been applied to a wide array of computer applications, ranging from simple tasks, such as instructions to Siri on iPhone or iPad, to highly complex endeavors, such as the full comprehension of news articles. Aylien’s aspect-based sentiment analysis falls between the two extremes. Deep Learning is a class of machine learning algorithms that model hierarchical abstractions in input data with the help of multiple layers. It has been applied to NLP. Examples of Deep Learning algorithms include convolutional neural networks, deep neural networks, deep belief networks, and recurrent neural networks. If readers are interested in an overview of Deep Learning algorithms and their applications, we suggest that they refer to Deng and Yu (2014) and to LeCun, Bengio, and Hinton (2015). Further, Schmidhuber (2015) provides a review of Deep Learning in neural networks. In industry, apart from NLP, Deep Learning has been applied to computer vision, speech recognition, audio recognition, and social network filtering (Deng, Hinton, & Kingsbury, 2013; Liu et al., 2017).

## Aspect-based sentiment analysis by Aylien

The aspect-based sentiment analysis program by Aylien uses Deep Learning based on a combination of convolutional neural networks and long short-term memory networks (Ruder, Ghaffari, & Breslin, 2016a, 2016b). Deep neural networks are stacked neural networks that contain multiple hidden layers between the input and the output layers. They can model highly complex nonlinear relationships. Convolutional neural networks are shift-invariant or space-invariant artificial neural networks. They are empirically shown to achieve excellent results in various NLP applications, such as semantic parsing (Grefenstette, Blunsom, de Freitas, & Hermann, 2014), sentence modeling (Kalchbrenner, Grefenstette, & Blunsom, 2014), and sentence classification (Collobert & Weston, 2008; Kim, 2014). Using long shortterm memory architecture, the aspect-based sentiment analysis program by Aylien is specifically designed for sequence prediction problems. Readers should refer to Ruder (2017) for a review of deep neural networks in NLP, and to Zhang, Wang, and Liu (2018) for a review of Deep Learning in sentiment analysis.

A major step in aspect-based sentiment analysis is aspect extraction. Aspect extraction detects the specific aspects of a product the opinion holder is praising or criticizing (Poria, Cambria, & Gelbukh, 2016). For example, in the sentence, “The seat quality of this airline is amazing,” the aspect is “seat quality” of the entity represented by “this airline.” Aylien’s aspect-based sentiment analysis programs use a seven-layer deep neural network to tag each word in opinionated sentences as either aspect or nonaspect words, and calculate the frequency of aspect versus nonaspect words and their semantic similarity to eliminate irrelevant aspects that are not semantically related to the product (Ruder et al., 2016c). The research team at Aylien also developed a set of linguistic patterns for the same purpose. The resulting ensemble classifier, coupled with a word-embedding model for sentiment analysis, allows their approach to attain significantly better accuracy than state-of-the-art methods. Specifically, Ruder et al. (2016c) used the developed aspect-based sentiment analysis to process reviews about restaurants, hotels, phones, and laptops, and compared the performance with other methods (such as sentence-level convolutional neural networks), and empirically showed that their approach performs better than existing methods. The predictive accuracy for English reviews is 85.3%; for reviews in other languages (French, Spanish, Dutch, Turkish, Arabic, and Russian), it ranges from 75.4% to 84.8%, and the lowest accuracy is 73.3% for Chinese reviews. The commercial version of Aylien has a fixed list of “aspects” (what we referred to as “service dimensions” in the main paper) and enables users to assign a tweet to service quality dimensions matched to the ACSI. At the time of writing, the commercial version of Aylien does not dynamically generate the list of service dimensions.

In addition, Aylien adopts a continuous domain adaptation algorithm (Ruder et al., 2016d; Ruder & Plank, 2017), in contrast to discrete domain adaptation algorithms for most aspect-based sentiment analysis tools. People adapt their language style to a conversation topic. For instance, when people tweet about entertainment elites, they may use a different language style from tweeting about friends and acquaintances. In NLP research, “domains” are topics of conversation. When the data sets for training and for testing come from different domains, Deep Learning models encounter data they have never seen before and, as a result, the performance of models declines. To minimize thi decline, Deep Learning models need to be able to adapt to novel data and transfer “knowledge” learned from the original domain to a new domain. Most sentiment analysis algorithms compartmentalize the world in stationary domains, which have clear boundaries, but the real world is more ambiguous and domains permeate each other. In addition, people’s conversation domains are semantically related and likely to overlap. Aylien uses this notion to develop continuous domain adaptation algorithms with Bayesian optimization. For details of the modeling, refer to Ruder et al. (2016d). The authors evaluated the learned representations, the model, and the variation of language itself. Further, for details of the “transfer knowledge” algorithms, we suggest that readers refer to Ruder and Plank (2017). The code of their programs is also available on http://ruder.io/publications/. Ruder and Plank (2017) evaluated their algorithms on sentiment analysis across four products (books, DVD, electronics, and kitchen products). The accuracy of their algorithms ranges from 78.3% to 86.3%.

At the time of writing, Aylien is extending its aspect-based sentiment analysis algorithms by incorporating the analysis of emojis. Aylien collected 500,000 tweets in 15 languages and investigated the use of the emojis contained in each tweet to predict tweet sentiment. Wood and Ruder (2016) trained six support vector machine binary classifiers that captured six emotions (job, disgust, surprise, fear, sadness, and anger) and provided the evaluation results of their classifiers. In brief, the classifier for “joy” performed the best (with 0.80 precision, 0.97 recall, and 0.87 F1), and the performance of the remaining classifiers varied. Wood and Ruder (2016) indicate that this incorporation is in its relative infancy and that the development of emotion annotated corpora is complicated by the difficulty of the annotation task and resulting inconsistencies in human annotations

Last but not least, Aylien is continuously improving its aspect-based sentiment analysis tool. More research directions identified by Aylien can be found on the official website (see http://blog.aylien.com/author/sebastian/).
