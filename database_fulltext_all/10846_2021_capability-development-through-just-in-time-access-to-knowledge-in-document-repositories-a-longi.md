# CAPABILITY DEVELOPMENT THROUGH JUST-IN-TIME ACCESS TO KNOWLEDGE IN DOCUMENT REPOSITORIES: A LONGITUDINAL EXAMINATION OF TECHNICAL PROBLEM SOLVING<sup>1</sup>

Mani Subramani Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {subra010@umn.edu}

Mihir Wagle Software Developer, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, U.S.A., {wagle008@umn.edu}

Gautam Ray and Alok Gupta Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {gautamr@umn.edu} {alok@umn.edu}

With knowledge and expertise increasingly being recognized as important, firms have made significant investments in document repositories as part of their knowledge management initiatives. Document repositories are intended to enhance the ability to access codified knowledge and help improve task performance through knowledge reuse. However, it is not clear what effects they have on how knowledge workers perform their tasks. Using longitudinal data on repository accesses and calls to technical support by field technicians in an engineering firm, we examine how just-in-time access to codified knowledge affects patterns of help-seeking from technical support. We find evidence that greater accessing of codified knowledge reduces calls for help to technical support. The type of codified knowledge accessed from the repository affects field technicians’ calling behavior. Accessing general knowledge reduces calls to support for low-complexity problems, while accessing procedural knowledge reduces calls related to highcomplexity problems. Further, accessing procedural knowledge is significantly associated with promotion, suggesting that the use of document repositories can help individuals build firm-specific human capital. Building on the insights of cognitive load theory, this study suggests that making information available just in time through document repositories reduces the cognitive load involved for task performance and enables learning. This work contributes to a greater understanding of the value of knowledge management systems and suggests that, beyond the efficiencies gained from knowledge reuse, just-in-time access to knowledge repositories builds problem solving capabilities and contributes to human capital development.

Keywords: Codified knowledge, cognitive load theory (CLT), document repositories, just-in-time access, just-in-time information, help-seeking, human capital development, knowledge management systems, promotion, internalization, socialization, technical problem solving

## Introduction

The knowledge-based resources of a firm, comprising documented knowledge, human capital, and the dynamic capability to learn, are increasingly recognized as key sources of sustainable competitive advantage (Sambamurthy and Subramani 2005). Further, performance differences across firms are linked to variance in learning occurring during task performance (Conner and Prahalad 1996). Scholars have also suggested that some organizational advantages of firms arise from their ability to share existing knowledge and create new knowledge (Haas and Hansen 2007). It is thus not surprising that firms have made significant investments in IT-enabled tools to aggregate and share knowledge, commonly termed knowledge management systems (KMS). Document repositories that enable firms to capture and codify knowledge and make it accessible to employees just-in-time as needed for task performance are a key component of KMS (Davenport and Glaser 2002). Document repositories are intended to serve as central sources of validated knowledge (Durcikova and Gray 2009) and facilitate efficiency and effectiveness through the reuse of organizational knowledge (Ko and Dennis 2011; Durcikova et al. 2011). The implementation of document repositories is often accompanied by changes to help-seeking practices, with employees encouraged to look for inputs in document repositories before making potentially disruptive requests for help from knowledgeable colleagues (Haas and Hansen 2007).

Given the importance of knowledge work and the significant improvement in task performance made possible by facilitating access to organizational knowledge (Reagans et al. 2005; Sambamurthy and Subramani 2005), greater clarity on how KMS can influence the development of the skill and expertise of knowledge workers is an important issue for research as well as practice. This issue is particularly salient because research suggests that firms’ investments in IT tend to be complemented by more skilled labor (Bresnahan et al., 2002), particularly in the performance of nonroutine, analytical tasks requiring experience and judgment. With firms increasingly relying on more skilled knowledge workers, there is a need to expand the conception of the performance of knowledge-intensive tasks beyond problem solving to include learning and capability development (Boh et al. 2007). A finer-grained understanding of how patterns of just-in-time access to knowledge through KMS use during task performance can influence individuals’ skill and capability development is a step forward in this direction.

The SECI model of knowledge creation in the management and organizational literature (Nonaka 1994) provides a sociological perspective on knowledge (Nonaka and von Krogh 2009; Brown and Duguid 2001). This model highlights internalization (individuals drawing on codified knowledge to build their conceptual understanding) and socialization (through interpersonal interactions among individuals) as two modes of learning and capability development that enable knowledgeable action and task performance. These views of capability development correspond to mechanisms underlying the two approaches facilitated by information technologies for managing and sharing knowledge—codification and personalization (Alavi and Leidner 2001). Codification refers to the creation and transfer of knowledge through allowing individuals to obtain knowledge by accessing documents from knowledge repositories, while personalization refers to the creation and transfer of knowledge through systems facilitating interpersonal interactions between individuals.

Prior research on document repositories has focused on the motivations of individuals to contribute to repositories (Durcikova and Gray 2009; Kankanhalli et al. 2005) and on factors influencing the use of KMS (Durcikova and Gray 2009; Poston and Spier 2005). Despite the pioneering insights of the SECI model and the articulation of processes involved in managing knowledge through information technologies (Hahn and Subramani 2000; Poston and Speier 2005), the broader question of how the ability to access knowledge and information on demand from KMS contributes to individuallevel capabilities and affects how knowledge workers perform their tasks remains unexplored. Further, prior research treats all documents in repositories as homogeneous (e.g., Ko and Dennis 2011), without recognizing inherent differences in the nature of the content that could lead to varying patterns of access, reuse, and outcomes for users. While individual learning is acknowledged as the foundation of organizational capabilities, there is considerable uncertainty about the role of access to codified knowledge in influencing individual learning and task performance (Argote et al. 2003). Scholars have argued for a deeper understanding of how the use of tools embedded in the context of work influences individual learning and knowledge creation (Kim et al. 2012).

To this end, we draw on the literature on educational psychology that examines the cognitive elements determining learning and knowledge acquisition by individuals. Cognitive load theory (CLT), a key theoretical perspective in this literature, proposes that the level of individual learning is determined by the level of cognitive capacity available for processing novel inputs (van Merriënboer and Sweller 2008). CLT views the architecture of human cognitive processing as comprising a limited working memory supplemented by a relatively unlimited long-term memory that holds knowledge and information organized in schemas (van Merrienboer and Sweller 2005). Schemas are cognitive frameworks and knowledge structures used to interpret, structure, and organize categories of information and the relationships among them. Expertise develops as learners mindfully combine simple schemas into more complex ones. Expertise in troubleshooting, for example, involves transitioning from understanding the functioning and problems of individual subsystems in simple schemas to developing complex schemas that encode reasoning about problems and cause-effect relationships (Kester et. al 2006). Schemas organize and store knowledge in long-term memory and significantly reduce the cognitive load in task performance because the variety of interrelated elements of information organized in schemas when retrieved from longterm memory are processed as one element in working memory.

From the perspective of CLT, learning occurs through schema construction and schema elaboration: the ongoing development of new schemas and the creation of more complex schemas by reorganizing multiple lower-level schemas into higher-level schemas. Since schema construction is cognitively demanding, a central insight of CLT is that minimizing the cognitive load on individuals when they process novel information facilitates learning by freeing up cognitive capacity for new schema construction and the augmentation of existing schemas in longterm memory. This is currently a very active area of research in educational psychology (Paas et al. 2004). Studies confirm that the reduction of cognitive load through the thoughtful design of instructional material enhances learning (Van Merriënboer et al. 2003; Van Merriënboer and Sweller 2005). Inputs provided just in time in instructional contexts free up cognitive resources in working memory and significantly enhance learning and capability development (Kester et al. 2004; Kester et al. 2006). In particular, CLT highlights that the ability to recall relevant information with minimal effort significantly reduces the cognitive load involved in task performance. This implies that choices in the design of task environments (e.g., making information available just in time through document repositories) may reduce the cognitive load involved in task performance and enable learning and capability development. Drawing on this insight from CLT, we examine how just-intime access to knowledge from a document repository facilitates learning and capability development.

We examine this issue in the context of an engineering firm’s central document repository accessed by field technicians engaged in the servicing of industrial heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems in the United States. Field technicians are knowledge workers who are dispatched by the firm from service centers located across the USA to customer locations to fix problems in the functioning of HVAC equipment. Field technicians have online access to a central document repository that contains a large body of both general knowledge (such as wiring diagrams, factory test reports, and technical manuals) as well as specific problem solution knowledge (describing frequently experienced field problems and outlining the procedure to fix them). When field technicians responding to customer calls encounter equipment that they cannot restore to working order using their own knowledge and knowledge that they can look up in the repository, they can call the central help desk staffed by experienced technical support engineers (TSE) for help. Prior studies of equipment maintenance highlight that technicians take pride in being able to solve problems and use the option to involve centrally located technical support engineers only as a last resort (Orr 2006). Calls to technical support for help thus reflect instances when field technicians encounter problems with malfunctioning equipment that they cannot address using their own resources and the knowledge available in the repository. In this context, if field technicians can enhance their problem solving capabilities by accessing documents in the repository, they are more likely to be able to solve field problems on their own and will likely make fewer calls to the central help desk in subsequent periods. This feature of the context therefore provides a relevant setting to examine how just-in-time access to different kinds of codified knowledge helps individuals to build their own problem solving capabilities over time.

We analyzed the repository access as well as the calls to technical support of 1847 field technicians employed by Aircom over a three-year period to examine the central question of this research: How does just-in-time access to codified knowledge in online document repositories help build individual-level capabilities? This study makes several theoretical and empirical contributions. Prior research has studied knowledge repositories from an efficiency point of view, where the goal is to store and reuse organizational knowledge. In this research, we make the argument that knowledge repositories also serve to develop the human capital of knowledge workers who access the repository in ways that influence their work patterns in future periods. Empirically, we find that accessing more documents reduces calls to technical support, suggesting that access to codified knowledge by technicians is associated with capability development. Further, accessing procedural knowledge in problem solution documents reduces calls pertaining to problems of high complexity, whereas accessing conceptual declarative knowledge contained in general documents reduces calls pertaining to problems of low complexity. We also find evidence that accessing problem solution documents enhances the likelihood of promotion, reflecting the role of just-in-time access to procedural knowledge in building firmspecific human capital.

## Empirical Context

The setting for our research is the technical support unit of Aircom, a large U.S. engineering firm that manufactures, sells, and services HVAC systems for commercial clients. Warranty and ongoing maintenance services are provided by field technicians. Customers call Aircom’s local field support office to report instances of malfunctioning equipment, and the first available field technician is dispatched to address the problem based on the technician’s availability and proximity to client sites. Field technicians working on resolving equipment malfunctions at customer locations work under time pressure, and service call resolution times are an important metric contributing to their performance assessments within Aircom.

Field technicians are specialized tradespeople with nationally recognized certifications in HVAC maintenance who also receive training from Aircom to service their line of products. In addition to the standard printed documentation available in the field office, field technicians have online access to a central document repository containing documents related to equipment maintenance and troubleshooting. The repository contains two types of documents: (1) general documents created by engineering, quality control, and training groups, and (2) problem solution documents created by TSEs at the central call center to document solutions to frequently reported field problems. General documents comprise engineering documents and equipment repair manuals needed for problem solving and troubleshooting. General documents describe systems hierarchically—for example, commencing with the description of the overall system (e.g., air conditioning system), followed by the technical details of individual subsystems (e.g., condenser, motor, temperature controller) and the assemblies within subsystems (e.g., controller boards, fan housing, etc.). Using these documents requires familiarity with domainspecific technical vocabulary that all trained technicians possess. General documents provide descriptive details combined with conceptual knowledge of equipment that is homogeneous and independent of context—in other words, these documents provide general knowledge (Becker, 1962) that is not specific to the products of Aircom and is likely to be valuable to field technicians in general, regardless of which firm in the industry they work for.

In contrast to general documents, problem solution documents provide interpretive procedural knowledge, describing specific problems and how to solve them. Procedural knowledge in problem solution documents is organized as a sequential process (akin to a recipe) to be followed stepwise to solve a problem. Understanding the knowledge in problem solution documents requires domain knowledge related to the equipment, such as the operation of the systems and subsystems, as problem solution documents focus closely on describing the salient details of problems and their associated solutions but are not comprehensive in describing the operation of the equipment. Thus, problem solution documents are narrow in scope and provide specific know-how related to particular problems observed with specific equipment under particular site and operating conditions. In other words, problem solution documents provide specific knowledge (Becker, 1962) about how to solve specific problems with particular Aircom equipment.

Viewing documentation as comprising general documents and problem solution documents based on the knowledge they contain reflects the fundamental distinction between declarative and procedural knowledge (Cohen 1991). General documents contain descriptive facts and conceptual knowledge: knowledge of concepts or principles including relations or connections among bodies of knowledge. General documents enable individuals to build mental models of equipment, including their attributes, their purposes, and details of the functioning of systems and subsystems. In contrast, problem solution documents contain procedural how-to knowledge and provide linear or step-by-step illustrations of procedures for problem solving that convey details of initial and intermediate problem states observed in malfunctioning equipment, along with steps involved in solving the problem. Problem solution documents enable individuals to learn about specific problem solutions and to abstract knowledge of generalizable solutions from documented instances (van Merriënboer et al. 2003).

The knowledge repository is accessible to field technicians online in self-service mode. All field technicians carry company-issued ruggedized laptops with wireless cards that they can use to access the knowledge repository from client sites. Typically, field technicians access the knowledge repository in just-in-time mode at the client site to solve the problem at hand expeditiously, rather than accessing the repository in their office to improve their knowledge and expertise. All field technicians also carry company-issued cell phones that can be used to call the technical support center when they cannot solve a problem on their own. Field technicians are encouraged to look up solutions in the knowledge repository before calling the technical support center, but repository use is voluntary and not mandated.

The technical support center is staffed by experienced TSEs. TSEs are the source of help for field technicians confronting repair or maintenance problems that they are unable to diagnose or fix at customer locations. Calls from the field to the technical support center are queued and allocated to the first available TSE. Solving field problems often requires considerable analysis and exploration. The technical support center has analog and digital simulators in several locations around the room that TSEs can use to simulate specific situations being described by field technicians in order to aid problem diagnosis and solution development.

TSEs taking calls from field technicians often improvise solutions involving considerable knowledge, experience, and ingenuity to address problems encountered at client sites. The goal of the firm is to have such solutions documented and made available in the document repository so that other field technicians facing the same or similar problems can potentially reuse the solution. Document submissions to the repository are voluntary and not compensated. TSEs are encouraged to document solutions and the novel approaches they devise in solving field problems and submit them for inclusion in the knowledge repository. A team of senior engineers reviews submissions for accuracy and clarity and often returns documents to authors with suggestions to enhance the quality of the document, typical of knowledge validation processes in technical contexts (Durcikova and Gray 2009). Documents are published to the repository after the review process. The knowledge repository at Aircom is growing continuously, with new documents added to the repository every week.

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Accessing Knowledge Documents and Calling Behavior

How do knowledge workers develop their capabilities? A large number of studies across different industries suggest that with greater task experience, individuals increase their proficiency through learning by doing (Edmondson et al. 2007). In the IS literature, this argument has been demonstrated in the context of a computing call center (Kim, et al. 2012), software development (Boh et al. 2007), and the use of a physician referral system (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2011). Analogously, in our context, field technicians can develop their capabilities by accumulating knowledge over the course of their tenure (Kim et al. 2012). The learning literature also distinguishes between learning by oneself and learning from others (Reagans et al. 2005). For example, consultants learn by answering more calls as well as by sharing their knowledge with others through socialization (Kim et al. 2012), and the diversity of experience in a software development group improves the productivity of individual developers (Boh et al. 2007). In this study, we focus on learning through just-in-time access to knowledge codified and stored in a repository by others, a phenomenon also termed learning by investment (Ryu et al. 2005). KMS are characterized as sources of organizational capabilities (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Ko and Dennis 2011). However, there has been little prior research examining the mechanisms linking the use of these systems to the development of individual-level capabilities. In other words, the question is: How does individuals’ use of KMS help build individual-level capabilities and influence task execution and performance?

There is increasing recognition of opportunities to supplement formal training by learner-driven just-in-time delivery of information and knowledge. Individuals are likely to find inputs delivered in the context of task performance relevant and thus develop cognitive structures to retain the information by integrating it with what they already know (Kester et al. 2001). Theories of learning from cognitive science provide the microfoundation for how just-in-time access to new knowledge develops the problem solving capabilities of field technicians. CLT suggests that individual learning during task performance is facilitated when task-relevant information and knowledge is available to minimize the cognitive load involved in task performance (van Merrienboer et al. 2003). CLT views the architecture of human cognitive processing as comprising a limited working memory to process novel material supplemented by long-term memory containing knowledge and information organized into schemas (van Merrienboer and Sweller 2005). All inputs need to be processed in the working memory before they can be stored as schemas in long-term memory. When the demands of task performance occupy the limited capacity of working memory, there may be little capacity available for the cognitive processes of schema construction and schema elaboration needed for learning to occur. CLT highlights that taking the load off the learner’s mind during task execution by making task-relevant knowledge readily available (e.g., through just-in-time access to relevant information) minimizes the cognitive load involved in task performance and can free up cognitive capacities to facilitate learning (Kester et al. 2006, van Merriënboer et al. 2003).

We build on these insights of CLT to suggest that accessing knowledge relevant to problem solving in a just-in-time fashion from the searchable document repository reduces the cognitive load for field technicians and facilitates schema construction and task-related learning, which aids task performance in subsequent periods. Knowledge from documents can substitute for missing knowledge structures in cognitive schemas and can help individuals incorporate them into their current schemas. The availability of knowledge from the document repository is qualitatively different from conventional access to manuals and other paper-based documentation in terms of the extent to which the support is integrated with the task environment. For instance, paperbased manuals are available for reference by technicians at service support centers. However, in our field observations, we found no instance of technicians carrying manuals to job sites in their vans. In contrast, technicians carry the companyissued laptop at all times and the document repository is accessible in one or two clicks when they need to look up information on equipment or documentation pertaining to the current problem they face. The integration of repository access with elements of task performance reduces extraneous cognitive load from the split-attention effect and enables learning by allowing individuals to obtain inputs seamlessly in the context of task performance (Kester et al. 2006). The embedding of the knowledge repository in the context of task performance, accessible in a window on the laptop already being used during the task, is analogous to the learning assistance provided by training wheels on a child’s bike, which are viewed as ideal model scaffolding and task support (de Bruin and van Merriënboer 2017).

CLT is particularly appropriate in our context, where individual learning and capability development occur through the ability to access information just in time from a document repository, for three reasons. First, ready access to information significantly reduces the cognitive load during task performance. Technical problem solving to restore malfunctioning systems back to working order is a nonroutine task (Orr 2006; Das 2003). The difficulty of matching the symptoms exhibited by equipment malfunctions with problems that field technicians can recall and know how to solve is a source of significant stress in problem diagnosis. Orr highlights the level of frustration and uncertainty involved in problem solving in the case of copier repair as follows:

There is an inherent uncertainty in the situation of diagnosis. In difficult diagnoses the technicians are contending with the limits of their knowledge. The known weaknesses of their sources of information suggest that they may be presumed to have an incomplete set of facts about the machine; they lack the perspective of understanding which would integrate those facts into a coherent representation which might or might not indicate the need for additional facts. They do not know if they have overlooked something, whether there are more facts to gather, or whether this is a new problem that is completely beyond their experience and understanding. They do not know whether they lack a fact or an interpretation (Orr 1996, p. 127).

From the perspective of CLT, establishing a correspondence between elements of their prior experience and knowledge base and the symptoms exhibited by the equipment imposes an extraneous cognitive load on technicians. This extraneous cognitive load reduces their cognitive capacity and hampers the discerning of patterns of cause and effect involved in schema construction and learning. However, if a document repository is available, technicians can obtain information that they cannot recall or troubleshooting procedures that they have not learned, thus reducing stress and anxiety about diagnosis and problem solving at the job site. Therefore, the ability to search a document repository for general information about field problems and for validated solutions that can be reused or adapted can reduce the cognitive load in task performance and free up cognitive capacity, which creates the potential for learning.

Second, instructional theories highlight that complex learning occurs when individuals are exposed to information in the context of real-life problem solving tasks. This approach to learning from authentic problem solving is found in educational approaches such as the case method and goalbased learning (Schank et al. 1994) that are based on the insight that the learning and recall of complex information is enhanced when information is provided to individuals in the context requiring its application, versus when it is provided in a decontextualized fashion such as in training settings. Information provided during task performance (e.g., at the time of problem diagnosis or equipment repair) is integrated into memory, along with the experience of task performance, so that when an individual observes a similar problem in the future, it triggers memories of the earlier problem solving experience and facilitates the recall of the relevant information.<sup>2</sup> Schema construction and schema retrieval in such settings is enhanced by the presentation of mutually interrelated elements of information that individuals identify as salient for problem solving. The process of experience recall also leads to learning when individuals implicitly compare the current problem to prior instances and discern generalizable elements in the conjunction of experiences. The abstraction of generalizable patterns from specific instances is an important process that results in schema elaboration, which facilitates creative problem solving by allowing individuals to make informed judgments in novel situations based on principles that are grounded in prior experiences (Kester et al. 2001, Kester et al. 2006).

Third, the context of just-in-time access to knowledge from document repositories has characteristics similar to selfdirected learning environments. Studies of contexts where individuals play an active role in seeking inputs and have the freedom to choose what they learn and how they learn it highlight that self-directed individuals pay more attention to content, develop strategies for reasoning in the domain, and are better at applying knowledge for problem solving (Hmelo et al. 1997; Candy 1991). In the case of document repositories, the active role of individuals in searching, sorting through alternatives, and obtaining relevant inputs as needed for task performance is similarly likely to encourage learning and the development of varied and elaborate schemas associated with problem solving expertise. Technicians seek knowledge from the document repository in order to apply it to solve the problem at hand. When they locate a document with knowledge that is potentially relevant to the situation they face, they are likely to focus on the details in the document and apply them to evolve problem solving strategies. Accessing relevant knowledge when it is required for problem solving and engaging with the knowledge to develop problem solving strategies for the situation at hand can thus help individuals develop and enrich contextualized schemas for problem solving.

Field technicians are employed to fix malfunctioning HVAC systems. They draw on their personal experience, knowledge, and expertise to solve problems. However, if they are not able to fix a specific HVAC system on their own, they can call the technical support center for help. If field technicians develop and elaborate contextual schemas pertaining to conceptual models of equipment functioning, issue diagnosis, and problem solving through just-in-time access to documents stored in the knowledge repository, they are likely to be able to solve more problems on their own and are less likely to call the technical support center. Hence, we expect that as field technicians access a greater number of documents stored in the knowledge repository, they build reasoning and problem solving capabilities and will need to call the technical support center less frequently. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1: The cumulative number of documents accessed by a field technician is negatively associated with the likelihood of calling the technical support center for help.

## Accessing Different Types of Documents and Different Types of Calls

In developing support for Hypothesis 1, we argue that field technicians develop problem solving capabilities by accessing documents from the knowledge repository and will subsequently need to call the help desk less. The next question is: What types of calls do documents reduce? Equipment designers often cannot anticipate many of the ways in which equipment will be used in the field or the problems that arise from equipment operating under varying field conditions (Orr 2006). Therefore, field technicians often need to call technical support for information to clarify the applicability of details in technical service bulletins and specific issues beyond those included in prescribed maintenance procedures. In other instances, field technicians call TSEs for input to diagnose and solve unusual problems they encounter that may require the creation of novel solutions. In this regard, the capabilities created by accessing general documents and problem solution documents can play distinct roles in problem solving.

Prior literature on technical support (Pentland and Reuter 1994; Das 2003) views technical support as comprising four categories of problem solving tasks: information retrieval, plan synthesis, state abstraction, and abductive diagnosis. The four types of tasks involve increasing levels of problem complexity—information retrieval being the simplest, with plan synthesis, state abstraction, and abductive diagnosis increasing with the level of problem complexity. This categorization of problem solving tasks and corresponding problem solving actions is useful for clarifying the work of field technicians. For tasks characterized as requiring information retrieval and plan synthesis, field technicians need to locate relevant information and adapt this information to help formulate a plan to perform specific tasks and achieve particular goals. The dominant move to accomplish information retrieval and plan synthesis is locate—consulting the knowledge repository to find declarative information to address the problem at hand.

For state abstraction problems, the field technician needs to formulate logic and reasoning to assess the current state so that the problem at hand can be addressed. Solving state abstraction tasks involves a combination of locate and adapt moves—applying general information to the specifics of the context to address the problem at hand. For abductive diagnosis problems, the field technician needs to formulate a diagnostic explanation so that the problem at hand can be addressed. Abductive diagnosis involves a deeper understanding of cause-effect relationships. Solving problems of this kind largely involves generate moves, i.e., the creation of a new solution for the specific problem at hand.

Having access to a document repository can help field technicians address information retrieval and plan synthesis problems by searching the repository for inputs and reducing the need to call the technical support center for the information required. General documents provide declarative information pertaining to the task at hand and enable individuals to construct cognitive schemas through elaboration (Reigeluth, 1999). As individuals access more general documents, they integrate the novel declarative information they obtain with preexisting, relevant cognitive schemas stored in long-term memory. In the context of technical support, the ability to access general documents from the repository reduces the cognitive load involved in task performance and helps individuals develop and elaborate cognitive schemas encoding general domain knowledge. For instance, field technicians encountering a malfunctioning system may be aware of likely problems that exhibit these symptoms and potential strategies to effect an adequate solution. However, they may be uncertain about whether the solutions they can recall are still valid and whether there are simpler solutions available than the ones that they know about. The retrieval and application of declarative information from the repository reduces the cognitive load from such uncertainty and frees up cognitive capacity to allow field technicians to enrich their cognitive schemas with the particulars of the problem and the solution during task performance.

To provide a concrete example from the context of our study, when a technician is assigned to relocate an existing HVAC rooftop unit to another location, the technician will require information from the technical spec sheet for the HVAC on the weight of the unit and the exact dimensions to ensure that the unit can be installed in the new location. Anxiety about whether the information the technician can recall about these details to perform the task is accurate increases the cognitive load experienced in the process of task performance. In such instances, accessing technical information from general documents in the document repository reduces the cognitive load caused by uncertainty, and the technician’s cognitive resources can be fruitfully applied to reasoning about the task at hand, thus enhancing learning and the elaboration of cognitive schemas. Over time, as field technicians access a greater number of general documents in the course of task performance, the ongoing enhancement of cognitive schemas is likely to reduce their need to call technical support for input in the future. We therefore hypothesize that:

H2a: The cumulative number of general documents accessed by a field technician is negatively associated with the likelihood of calling the technical support center for lowcomplexity problems.

In general, field technicians are likely to seek input from others when facing more complex state abstraction and abductive diagnosis problems. Interacting with a knowledgeable individual is particularly helpful when field technicians face situations where multiple choices seem equally suitable, when they want to obtain another perspective on a complex issue, or when they seek confirmation that they are on the right track in addressing novel problems (Cross and Sproull 2004). In these instances—when individuals require more than just an answer to a question—the ability to consult a knowledgeable individual is particularly valuable.

Using a case study of two call centers, Schefe and Timbrell (2004) found that problems of uncertainty concern the lack of information and can be addressed using repository searches, whereas problems of ambiguity and equivocality concern the lack of knowledge and can be solved through rich interactive conversations with experts. In our context, where problem solution documents describe novel solutions to specific field problems, accessing problem solution documents can substitute for acquiring this knowledge through interactions with TSEs. Problem solution documents describe specific problems and provide a stepby-step guide to the solution similar to worked-out examples studied in the literature (Pass and van Merrienboer 1994). Problem solution documents are a source of specific knowledge illustrating problem solving strategies that field technicians can adapt to generate solutions for state abstraction and abductive analysis problems through learning from examples and through analogical transfer from individual instances (Logan 1988; Pirolli 1991; Novick 1988).

Worked-out examples reduce cognitive load during task performance by directing individuals’ attention to problem states and associated solution steps and enabling individuals to construct generalized schemas (van Merrienboer et al. 2003). Examining worked-out examples facilitates schema construction and transfer performance—the ability to generalize learning to address related problems (Novick 1988; van Gerven et al. 2002).

Overall, problem solution documents that describe specific problems and provide a step-by-step guide to the solution aid problem schema development by individuals by reducing cognitive load and facilitating schema construction in several ways. First, problem solution documents facilitate the interpretation and construction of problem schemas through learning from examples. This reduces cognitive load during task performance and facilitates schema elaboration. Problem solution documents can also serve as cues to support schema automation and the development of general strategies, allowing individuals to generalize problem solving schemas to related problem categories through analogical transfer (Logan 1988; Pirolli 1991). Accessing problem solution documents thus broadens the range of problems that field technicians can address based on their own knowledge. Thus, the greater the number of problem solution documents that field technicians access, the greater the level of problem solving capability they develop.

From this perspective, accessing problem solution documents facilitates the development of a more elaborate repertoire of problem schemas that field technicians can adapt to generate solutions. These problem schemas significantly reduce the cognitive load involved in task performance since they direct individuals’ attention to relevant problem states and associated solution steps and enable individuals to adapt procedures they can recall to address field problems (van Merrienboer et al. 2003). Therefore, the greater the number of problem solution documents that field technicians access, the greater their ability to solve complex field problems on their own, and the lower their likelihood of needing to call technical support. Thus, we state the following hypotheses:

H2b: The cumulative number of problem solution documents accessed by a field technician is negatively associated with the likelihood of calling the technical support center for high-complexity problems.

## Accessing Different Types of Documents and Promotion

Why would field technicians make the effort to search for codified knowledge when they could simply call a technical support engineer whenever they lacked the capability to address the problem at hand? We suggest that the motivation to learn by accessing documented knowledge can arise from differential organizational incentives to build general and specific individual capabilities. For example, Mithas and Krishnan (2008) suggest that firms place a greater value (i.e., reward with higher wages) on the accumulation of general IT experience (e.g., IT experience at other firms) than on specific IT experience (e.g., IT experience at the same firm). These findings may provide a rationale for why field technicians may wish to access general documents but do not clarify why field technicians choose to access problem solution documents. However, more generally, this literature does not examine whether IT—particularly KMS, which facilitates access to information and prior knowledge just in time as needed for task performance (Davenport and Glaser 2002)—contributes to human capital development.

Firm-specific human capital improves firm performance (Hatch and Dyer, 2004). The influential work of Becker (1962) highlights a key distinction between general knowledge and specific knowledge. Skills relying on general knowledge are those that are seen as deployable across multiple organizations, whereas skills that are based on specialized knowledge are more valuable to employees in the current organization and are less valuable to other firms in the labor market. Therefore, when an employee develops general knowledge that is valued by the industry, firms cannot appropriate performance benefits from such human capital, as employees can easily transfer this benefit to others in the industry in return for higher wages. However, when employees develop firm-specific human capital, firms can derive private benefits from such human capital, which contributes significantly to knowledge-based competitive advantage (Subramani and Venkatraman 2003). This implies that the incentives of individuals and of firms are asymmetric with respect to the acquisition of these two types of knowledge. In competitive labor markets, employees likely prefer to acquire general knowledge because it allows them to command higher wages from current and alternative employers based on their individual productivity derived from general knowledge (Katz and Murphy 1992). In contrast, firms likely prefer that employees possess specialized knowledge, since it provides unique advantages to firms compared to the other firms they compete with. Thus, firms should provide incentives to knowledge workers to develop productivity-enhancing, firm-specific human capital (Fairburn and Malcomson 1994).

Career advancement is one way that firms can encourage field technicians to develop firm-specific human capital (Prendergast, 1993). Prior research has observed that promotions represent an important source of wage growth (McCue 1996) and that promotions are used by firms to signal the acquisition of capabilities that are valued by the firm. From this perspective, promotions are tournaments and the wage increase associated with a promotion is a prize that serves as an incentive to individuals to acquire specialized knowledge (Waldman 2013). The prevalence of promotion practices that value firm-specific human capital (Ghosh and Waldman 2010), along with empirical evidence suggesting that the acquisition of firm-specific knowledge by individuals enhances the probability of their being promoted within the current firm, is consistent with these arguments (Melero 2010).

As discussed in the development of support for H1 and H2, accessing general and problem solution documents may affect the likelihood and nature of calls to the technical support center; that is, it could improve field technician performance because it enables technicians to solve problems in the field without needing to call the technical support center. This is the immediate impact of accessing the knowledge repository. In addition, field technicians who access more documents from knowledge repositories can develop more elaborate schemas that facilitate the efficient recall of complex troubleshooting knowledge. This can allow such field technicians to diagnose problems, particularly in instances where equipment exhibits ambiguous symptoms, because more elaborate schemas enhance individuals’ ability to engage in analogical transfer— the recall and adaptation of prior knowledge to solve novel problems based on the correspondence of deep-level structural features to relevant prior instances (Novick 1988). Further, more elaborate schemas developed by accessing a greater number of documents enables efficient principle-based reasoning and generalization (Lewis 1988), which aids troubleshooting. The reduction in cognitive load is particularly important in complex problem solving situations, as it allows individuals to reason about and discount co-occurring symptoms that may be irrelevant and misleading, discern key underlying problems, and devise solutions (Holyoak and Koh 1987). Overall, greater accessing of documented knowledge enhances individuals’ proficiency in problem solving in ways that can distinguish them from their peers. Over time, this can lead field technicians to be recognized for their capabilities and be rewarded with promotion. Thus, we expect that the field technicians who access and read a cumulatively greater number of documents are more likely to be promoted.

Our finer-grained conceptualization of document type enables the examination of the extent to which promotions are likely to be associated with accessing general and problem solution documents. Accessing general documents provides conceptual information and allows field technicians to build and elaborate their schemas regarding HVAC equipment and their modes of failure—domain knowledge that is valuable to all firms in the industry. In contrast, problem solution documents that describe how to solve specific problems associated with Aircom’s equipment aid the development and elaboration of problem solving schemas and deepen field technicians’ firm-specific knowledge. As discussed earlier, firms benefit more from field technicians building firm-specific knowledge than from technicians acquiring general knowledge and are thus more likely to incentivize the accumulation of firm-specific knowledge with promotion (Waldman 2013). Therefore, we expect greater access to problem solution documents to be associated with promotion. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis.

H3: The cumulative number of problem solution documents accessed by a field technician is positively associated with the likelihood of promotion for the field technician.

## Empirical Analysis

## Data and Variables

As discussed earlier, the setting for our research is the warranty and maintenance services provided to U.S. customers of Aircom by around 1800 field technicians.<sup>3</sup> These field technicians have online access to a knowledge repository that provides access to expertise in the form of documents related to equipment maintenance and troubleshooting. For this research, we are interested in examining the impact of field technicians’ problem solving capabilities developed through accessing the knowledge repository, as reflected in their calling behavior. We are also interested in studying whether the accumulation of knowledge developed through accessing the knowledge repository is related to field technician promotion. The analyses are based on technician-day (H1 and H2) and technician-year (H3) fixed-effect logit models.

## Dependent Variables

We had access to call logs at the technical support center that contained data on field technicians’ calls to the technical support center.<sup>4</sup> If field technicians accumulate sufficient knowledge and skills by accessing knowledge documents, we anticipate that they will be less likely to call for help in subsequent periods. Thus, our first dependent variable is whether a field technician calls the technical support center on a given day (MadeACall). This dependent variable was used to test Hypothesis 1.

Aircom has a ticket management system used by TSEs to record call details such as caller name, equipment involved, and the nature of the problem. The ticketing system provides a drop-down menu in the call-reason field for the TSE taking the call to indicate the nature of the problem. Aircom managers use this data to code the complexity of the call on a 5-point scale, and we used this measure to classify calls as either low-complexity calls or high-complexity calls. We classified calls of Complexity 1, 2, and 3 as low-complexity calls and those of Complexity 4 and 5 as high-complexity calls. Accordingly, we also examined the impact of accessing knowledge documents on the likelihood of making a lowcomplexity call on a given day (MadeALowCXCall), and the likelihood of making a high-complexity call on a given day (MadeAHighCXCall). These two dependent variables were used to test Hypothesis 2.

Field technicians employed by Aircom are promoted annually based on an internal HR process that evaluates individual employees on a variety of performance indicators. Based on internal HR data on field technicians during the study period, we coded three binary variables (Promotion\_2009, Promotion\_2010, and Promotion\_2011), set to 1 for each field technician who was promoted in a given year.

## Independent Variables

Our main independent variables are the measures of document access. Total\_Documents is the total number of documents accessed by a field technician up to a given day. We distinguished between two kinds of documents in the repository: (1) general documents authored by the marketing, quality control, and the training group, and (2) problem solution documents authored by TSE and accepted into the repository after technical review. Using detailed repository search logs, we treated the cumulative number of general documents accessed up to a given day (General Documents) as the day-level measure of general knowledge, and the cumulative number of problem solution documents accessed up to a given day (Problem Solution Documents) as the measure of specific knowledge.

## Control Variables

We controlled for several attributes of field technicians that may influence their level of knowledge and how they perform their tasks. The experience of a field technician may influence the ability of a field technician to solve problems in the field and their need to call the technical support center. Hence, we controlled for the learning and experience of the field technician on a given day by incorporating their tenure (in days), obtained from internal company data (Field Tech Tenure). For each field technician in our dataset, Field Tech Tenure on any given day is the number of days the technician had worked since being hired.

We also controlled for knowledge acquired by field technicians through socialization in calls to technical support. Using data on calls to technical support, we calculated the cumulative number of prior calls to technical support (Num\_Prior\_Calls) up to a given day. Incorporating the number of calls to TSE in prior periods controlled for the extent of knowledge that field technicians had gained in prior problem solving interactions with TSE. The greater the number of such personal interactions, the greater the level of informal and often undocumented specialized knowledge that field technicians are likely to have gained (Orr 2006).

A field technician’s ability to obtain the knowledge for problem solving is likely to be influenced by the level of codified knowledge in the repository (Gray and Durcikova 2005) and can reduce a field technician’s need to call the technical support center. Thus, drawing on document repository logs, we also included the number of repository documents on a given day (Num\_Repository\_Documents) as a control variable to capture the level of formalization of knowledge in the firm that is likely to influence the likelihood of calling for help (Kumar and Telang 2012). The number of problems occurring in the field, which influences the likelihood of calls to the technical support center, is related to the volume of equipment supported in the field. Therefore, we controlled for the total volume of equipment supported by including the monthly (million) dollar value of maintenance contracts (Maintenance Contracts). We also controlled for the monthly count of field office personnel, which includes field technicians and administrative support staff available to support the equipment in the field (Field Staff). The likelihood of field technicians calling the technical support center is also influenced by the ease of access to TSEs. Call wait times are generally around 30 minutes during busy periods, and wait times are reduced by increased staffing of help lines at the tech support center.<sup>5</sup> The call handling system is also configured to minimize hold times by handling calls in the order they are received. Since callers on hold are connected to the first available TSE, the allocation of calls to TSEs approximates random assignment.

Drawing on data on staffing at the technical support center, we controlled for TSE accessibility based on the total time (in hours) that TSEs were logged on and available to take calls on a given day (TSE Staffing). Calls for maintenance of HVAC systems by customers exhibit seasonal variations. To account for such temporal factors influencing calls, we controlled for the quarter of the year in our analyses. ## Analysis and Results

Our dataset comprises data on 1847 field technicians employed by Aircom during the three-year study period. Across all technician-days, the average tenure of field technicians was 1037 days. On average, field technicians accessed 35 general documents and 24 problem solution documents and made an average of 44 calls to the technical support center (see Table 1). The repository was growing during the period and had, on average, 28,677 documents. The average rate of making a call, making a low-complexity call, and making a high-complexity call is 0.035, 0.012, and 0.023, respectively. Thus, the likelihood that a given field technician made a call, a lowcomplexity call, or a high-complexity call on a given day is 3.5%, 1.2%, and 2.3%, respectively.

## Accessing the Knowledge Repository and Promotions

In the study period, 246 individuals—33 in 2009, 121 in 2010, and 92 in 2011—were promoted to the next level based on their performance in the prior year. Promotions in Aircom are based on assessments of technical capability and individual performance, and repository use is not a criterion that is considered. As a result, the relationship between accessing documented knowledge and promotion reflects the extent to which knowledge gained from repository access is important in building individual capabilities. The dependent variable in the analysis was the lagged binary variable promoted (in 2009, 2010, and 2011) as a function of the cumulative number of general (General Documents) and problem solution (Problem Solution Documents) documents accessed by the end of the prior year. Similarly, the size of the labor force (Field Staff) is negatively related to promotion, since an increased number of potential contenders reduces the likelihood of advancement in career tournaments (Sheridan et al. 1990). These findings increase confidence in the empirical model. Model 1 in Table 6 examines the relationship between the independent variables for all employees in the service organization and the likelihood of promotion. This analysis indicates that there is no significant relationship between the cumulative number of general documents accessed and being promoted. However, the cumulative number of problem solution documents accessed is related to promotions in the following year (p < 0.01). This result is consistent with Hypothesis 3. Model 1 suggests that accessing an additional problem solution document increases the likelihood of promotion by 0.43%. In Model 3, we only included employees who each accessed at least five general documents and five problem solution documents. This analysis also produced results consistent with Model 1, suggesting that accessing problem solution documents is positively related to promotion.

## Discussion and Conclusion

High-performing organizations are increasingly attempting to improve organizational process execution by enabling easy access to knowledge in real time and in the setting where problems and errors occur. Doing so allows problems to be solved in context, taking advantage of subtleties and information specific to the circumstance that may otherwise be lost when the problem is solved by escalating it and by moving the locus of problem solving to an expert at a different location. However, implementing mechanisms that allow a geographically dispersed workforce to access inputs as and when needed in the course of task performance has hitherto been problematic. To this end, KMS in general, and document repositories in particular, have emerged as important means to facilitate access to codified knowledge. However, while providing information to support task performance is often a key motivation for firm investments in document repositories, our study highlights that just-in-time access to knowledge enhances individual learning and capability development. Our work using longitudinal data on the use and the consequences of a document repository provides evidence of the broader role of document repositories in enhancing a key firm resource—its human capital—and facilitating the development of firm-specific human capital by employees. More generally, the results highlight that knowledge repositories, beyond just facilitating knowledge reuse to improve task efficiency, can enable on-the-job learning and the development of general and firm-specific knowledge. We thus contribute to a more nuanced view of the role of knowledge repositories as tools that can be influential in shaping knowledge worker capabilities. Hence, our research highlights that knowledge repositories should be viewed as important components of the infrastructure in knowledgeintensive firms that, beyond their role in supporting task performance, contribute to the development of human capital.

Prior research has examined learning by doing, where productivity increases with one’s cumulative output, as a key mechanism to develop individual skill and expertise; however, few studies highlight how learning is intertwined in the context of task performance. In this research, we highlight how IT-enabled mechanisms that enable individuals to access codified knowledge on demand lead to greater learning and the development of problem solving capabilities. Our study provides evidence of a significant reduction in calls for help by knowledge workers in subsequent periods attributable to the learning and the accumulation of knowledge derived from accessing codified knowledge from an online document repository as needed to enable task performance. We observed greater autonomous task performance (evidenced by a lower likelihood of calling technical specialists for help) related to individuals accessing both general conceptual knowledge and specific procedural knowledge. A finer-grained analysis suggests that accessing general knowledge reduces calls for help pertaining to low-complexity problems, while accessing specific procedural knowledge reduces calls pertaining to high-complexity problems. These differing patterns of learning highlight how ready access to codified knowledge through document repositories enhances the problem solving capabilities of individuals.

Regarding intellectually demanding work that challenges workers and requires close attention to details for task performance, CLT highlights that ready access to codified knowledge can free up cognitive capacity and trigger the controlled and effortful processing of stimuli that results in the development of new cognitive structures to spur learning and new knowledge creation (Sweller 1988). An implication of our results is that novel insights and the refined understanding of phenomena associated with just-in-time knowledge access can alter both the scope and effectiveness of future problem solving, creating the potential for individuals to become resourceful and innovative in addressing problems in related and adjacent areas of expertise. Brown and Duguid (1991) suggest that the routines inherent in work are conservative and resistant to change, learning and knowledge acquisition are viewed as distinct from working, and innovation is generally viewed as disruptive but necessary for working and learning. In this regard, our study, building on CLT, suggests that justin-time access to codified knowledge can free up cognitive capacity and facilitate learning while working.

CLT is a theoretical perspective developed in the educational psychology and cognitive psychology literatures to explain the acquisition of cognitive skills by learners in domains such as mathematics, physics, and computer programming. While CLT has been very influential in educational psychology, the application of CLT has largely been confined to the design of instructional materials and methodologies to enhance classroom training and skill acquisition (Renkl and Atkinson 2003). Recent studies have drawn on the theory to examine instructional designs in domains that involve imparting both conceptual knowledge and hands-on task skills such as the training of doctors (Hmelo et al. 1997) and nurses (Josephsen 2015). Our study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to apply CLT to examine learning and skill development during task performance in a field setting. Further, prior studies drawing on CLT have mainly employed experimental methodologies and surveys with self-reported data by subjects. Our study highlights the value of using archival data logs of document repositories to assess empirical support for propositions of psychological and cognitive theories such as CLT. This approach has the advantage of not being contaminated by biases inherent in self-reports and offers the potential of enabling evidence-based conclusions from reallife settings pertaining to links between instructional content access and learning outcomes for individuals.

Our work also extends knowledge on the differing roles of the codification of declarative and procedural knowledge in firms. Prior research treats documents containing codified knowledge as homogeneous, without recognizing inherent differences in the nature of the codified knowledge (Gray and Durcikova 2005). However, the educational psychology literature has long distinguished between different types of codified knowledge—those with expositions of general knowledge and context-independent explanations of principles, and those that provide illustrative problems and solved examples (Baroody et al. 1986; Lefevre and Dixon 1986). Each of these types of codified knowledge is recognized as serving different functions in learning and capability building. Documents containing general knowledge are viewed as helping individuals to learn fundamental principles and formulate theory-driven explanations of phenomena. In contrast, studying documents with illustrative problems and solved examples is viewed as helping individuals build problem solving capabilities by learning from example and by broadening problem solving abilities through analogical transfer from individual examples illustrated in the problem solution document (Holyoak and Koh 1987; Novick 1988). Our finding that different types of codified knowledge enhance task performance differently for simple and complex problems extends prior findings in IS research that repository use by individuals enhances task performance (Ko and Dennis 2011). Further, our work underlines the importance of moving beyond a focus on the extent of repository use and attending more closely to the type of codified knowledge accessed in assessing the influence of repository use on outcomes.

Prior literature examining whether KMS can be used for speed or for learning (Gray and Durcikova 2005) finds that KMS are largely used to enhance task efficiency and that the role of repositories for learning is limited. Our study highlights that just-in-time access to knowledge documents for problem solving builds capabilities that are reflected in reduced calls for help in future periods. In other words, speed and learning are not mutually exclusive: just-in-time access to documents for speed in problem solving leads to learning—i.e., it builds capabilities that help individuals solve field problems, therefore reducing calls in future periods. Information technologies for streamlined knowledge access embedded in the context of work can enable both task performance efficiency (e.g., speed) as well as cognitive skill acquisition (i.e., learning). With the ready availability of codified knowledge through document repositories, speed and learning in the course of knowledgeintensive tasks can thus be complementary.

This study offers potentially important areas for future research. Although there is a large body of research emphasizing knowledge as a social practice and highlighting the social process involved in knowledge creation and sharing (Nonaka 1994; Sambamurthy and Subramani 2005), there has been little attention directed to the psychological and cognitive components of learning and knowledgeable action. By identifying differing roles for declarative knowledge in general documents and procedural knowledge in problem solution documents based on cognitive load theory (CLT), this study helps lay a foundation for expanding the conversation surrounding knowledge in organizational contexts to include phenomena with cognitive psychological explanations. For instance, a variety of individuals’ adaptive responses to information environments in flux have cognitive explanations, such as their reliance on information sources, information filtering tactics regarding the potential value of knowledge documents from proximal cues, and decisionmaking about stopping rules in information search and knowledge access. A systematic inquiry into these domains can contribute to our understanding of the knowledgeable and resourceful practices of individuals that enable human capital development in organizational contexts.

Our work also provides useful directions for researchers in other domains where tasks are knowledge intensive, highly variable and performed under time pressure. Service providers such as nurses and physicians in emergency rooms need to diagnose and solve underlying problems revealed by symptoms that are often ambiguous and unreliable (Faraj and Xiao 2006). Examining how just-in-time repository access supplements interpersonal help-seeking and how it influences interpersonal interactions and collaborative task performance in such contexts can enhance the understanding of the cognitive and social factors involved in knowledge-intensive tasks. Our study using archival data on document accesses by individuals also highlights how CLT can be fruitfully applied to a broader class of learning phenomena, such as online learning and distance education. Future research could also benefit by integrating CLT and insights from research in creativity (Madjar et al. 2011), improvisation (Miner et al. 2001), and resourcefulness (Baker and Nelson 2005) into the study of work supported by document repositories to help provide deeper insights into the cognitive microprocesses underlying the development of firm-specific human capital and subsequent task performance.

For managers, our study highlights the broader benefits of investments in knowledge management technologies that facilitate knowledge access in the context of task performance and demonstrates the importance of developing a culture that encourages ongoing knowledge codification by knowledge workers. Our results suggest that the payoffs from these technologies and the organizational changes made to implement them occur over time and that the cumulative value in future periods can be considerable, in terms of reduced support costs and the enhanced contributions of more knowledgeable employees. We also provide evidence that, over time, document repositories can contribute to the development of the human capital of knowledge workers. Our study informs the development of business cases and justification for KM investments by highlighting the broader and richer payoffs from human capital development that are often overlooked in cost-benefit calculations. Further, our finding that documents with conceptual and procedural knowledge contribute differently to capability creation can guide managers in the development of incentives for repository creation and maintenance.

The findings of this study are subject to certain limitations that also highlight avenues for future research. This study is based on data from one company. Thus, the generalizability and external validity of the findings need to be examined in other industries and contexts. Further, we did not have access to data on the quality of work performed by field technicians at customer locations. Examining how the quality of work is influenced by individuals’ use of codified knowledge is an important question that should be pursued in future research. In this study, based on our fieldwork in Aircom, we focused on one key mechanism through which individuals become knowledgeable—accessing codified knowledge—and we controlled for knowledge gained through learning by doing and through interactions with TSEs over the phone. While the work of the mobile technicians we studied is largely solitary, studying other contexts where there is great opportunity for individuals to have informal interpersonal interactions in the course of their work could yield insights on alternative mechanisms through which individuals become more knowledgeable.<sup>8</sup> While the likelihood that the technicians we studied would call others for help is low, examining the contribution of interactions among mobile workers in other contexts where this may be likely could potentially yield further insights. As our workplaces grow richer in technology-enabled support for communication and information access, while also simultaneously becoming more impoverished in opportunities for interpersonal interaction and knowledge sharing due to reduced physical collocation, a greater understanding of the consequences of document repositories in task performance could yield important insights.

Scholars in the IS and strategy literatures have long recognized that knowledge is a critical resource (Conner and Prahalad 1996; Subramani 2004). In line with these perspectives and the far-sighted prediction by Drucker (1999), our study suggests that the establishment of infrastructure for just-in-time knowledge access may be an important mechanism for firms to develop distinctive resources by making knowledge workers not only more productive but also more knowledgeable, thus enhancing the quality of the human capital deployed by the firm. Research on the origins of competitive advantage suggests a deeper appreciation for idiosyncratic elements that contribute to capability creation (Ahuja and Katila 2004). Our study helps highlight document repositories in the context of technical problem solving as a key mechanism through which firms can enhance firmspecific human capital and craft their competitive advantage.

## About the Authors

Mani Subramani is an associate professor in the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. His research focuses on the strategic role of information technology in firms and in interfirm relationships. His current areas of research are knowledge management and the development of organizational capabilities through information technologies. His work has been published in the Academy of Management Journal, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Journal of International Business Studies, MIS Quarterly, Organizational Studies, and Sloan Management Review.

Mihir Wagle is a software developer for the M365 group at Microsoft. His research interests question how IT affects the nature of work, contributes to human capital development, and creates value. He received his master’s degree from Purdue University in 2010 and his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota in 2016.

Gautam Ray is a professor in the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. His research interests are in the area of the impact of IT on firm scope and structure, and question how IT creates value. His research has appeared in the Academy of Management Journal, Communications of the ACM, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Operations Management, Management Science, Marketing Science, MIS Quarterly, and Strategic Management Journal. He received his Ph.D. from Ohio State University in 2000.

Alok Gupta is the associate dean for Faculty and Research in the Carlson School of Management at the University of Minnesota. He is the Curtis L. Carlson Schoolwide Chair in Information Management and the former chair of the Information and Decision Sciences Department. He was awarded the prestigious NSF CAREER Award for his research on dynamic pricing mechanisms on the internet in 2001 and named an INFORMS Information Systems Society Distinguished Fellow in 2014 and AIS Fellow in 2016. He has served in editorial positions for most of the major IS journals including senior editor at Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and associate editor at Management Science. Since 2017, he has served as editor-inchief of Information Systems Research.
