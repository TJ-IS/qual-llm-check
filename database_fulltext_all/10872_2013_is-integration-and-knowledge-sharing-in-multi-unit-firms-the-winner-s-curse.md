# IS integration and knowledge sharing in multi-unit firms: the winner’s curse<sub>w</sub>

Nilesh Saraf<sup>1</sup>, Chris Schlueter Langdon<sup>2</sup> and Omar El Sawy<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Knowledge sharing across business units (BUs) is paramount for enterprises that aim to exploit latent cross-BU synergies. This paper examines how information systems (ISs) integration and two forms of cross-BU knowledge complementarities (workflow interdependence and BU relatedness) affect an important dimension of a firm’s absorptive capacity – cross-BU knowledge sharing. The analysis, performed on survey data from 75 enterprises, supports all of our hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that cross-BU workflow serves as a conduit for knowledge transfer as participants continually engage in spontaneous adaptations. Second, IS application integration and cross-BU relatedness should both directly contribute to knowledge sharing. Third, IS application integration should positively moderate the impacts of cross-BU relatedness. Finally, and most interestingly, we hypothesized a redistribution of the effects of IS integration by which it becomes a key contributor to knowledge sharing, but also causes a reduction in the impact of workflow interdependence. Thus, while the seamlessness of high IS application integration has obvious benefits, it paradoxically also reduces the occasions for cross-BU workflow adaptations, which is a key mechanism for knowledge transfer. That is the phenomenon we call the ‘winner’s curse’. European Journal of Information Systems (2013) 22, 592–603.

Keywords: IS integration; multi-unit firms; knowledge sharing; business unit interdependence; workflow interdependence; absorptive capacity

## Introduction

In their effort to derive synergies across their business units (BUs), multiunit enterprises can take a variety of structural initiatives that may remove barriers to more effective knowledge sharing. Apart from divestiture or related acquisitions (Davis et al, 1992; Eisenhardt & Brown, 1999) or consolidation of operations (St John & Harrison, 1999; Rozemeijer et al, 2003), such organizations also implement formal business processes across BUs (Goold & Campbell, 1998), implement matrix structures (Ghoshal et al, 1994), or induce personal networks (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998; Van den Bulte & Moenart, 1998).

While the increased use of information systems (ISs) in multi-unit firms has afforded an opportunity, it is also represents a challenge for us to better understand how integrated ISs can enhance knowledge sharing and knowledge-based capabilities. Even though cross-BU knowledge sharing in itself is not the eventual objective of multi-unit enterprises, it is perhaps the most important precondition for greater performance (Chandler, 1990; Grant, 1996). Further, while it is commonly understood that IS integration can directly augment knowledge sharing between subunits, it is not clear whether, if at all, IS integration also alters the efficacy of other concurrent mechanisms described above that also contribute to knowledge sharing.

In this study, we focus at the cross-BU level and consider business-to-business (B2B) sales activities of individual BUs. We also focus on two commonly used mechanisms that foster inter-unit knowledge sharing – business relatedness and workflow interdependence – and how IS integration moderates their impact on knowledge sharing. The impact of business relatedness on knowledge sharing is highlighted in the literature on joint product bundling by BUs, typically in large information technology (IT) and financial firms (Mendelson & Pillai, 1998). Similarly, workflow that spans multiple BUs linked as a value chain plays an important role in fostering knowledge sharing (Rayport & Sviokla, 1995). Overall, except for a few recent studies (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2010) little is known about the impact of IT on cross-BU knowledge sharing. Therefore, we ask: How does IS integration affect cross-BU knowledge sharing? How does this relationship alter in the presence of two other mechanisms that can foster cross-BU knowledge sharing – cross-BU relatedness and workflow interdependence? These research questions essentially pertain to the cognitive and learning impacts of IT on organizations (Scott, 2000; Tippins & Sohi, 2003). Therefore, we utilize two rich streams of organizational theory to develop our research model – absorptive capacity (Zahra & George, 2002a) and the pragmatic perspective of knowledge and boundaries (Carlile, 2004), both adapted and exercised in the IS literature in somewhat different contexts.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Theoretical development begins in section ‘Enabling knowledge sharing with IS integration’, where we describe the nature of cross-BU linkages and the role of IS in multi-unit firms and identify cross-BU knowledge sharing as our dependent variable of interest. Then we develop a series of hypotheses, including the ‘winner’s curse’ phenomenon. In section ‘Methods’, we present the empirical method, analysis, and results; and section ‘Discussion of results’ discusses the results. Section ‘Implications and conclusion’ develops the implications of the study for research and practice.

## Enabling knowledge sharing with IS integration

## Theoretical framework: absorptive capacity and IT

In order to develop our theoretical framework, we considered the nature of knowledge transfer processes within organizations and between subunits. Knowledge integration and reconfiguration by subunits results in new knowledge that benefits the organization as a whole (Tenkasi & Boland, 1996; Carlile, 2004). The research literature explains how subunits acquire knowledge from different sources and share it recursively and interactively. Essentially, such sense-making embedded in organizational activities results in conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge forms, and thus leads to knowledge transfer across organizational subunits. This perspective is also reflected in studies where knowledge sharing is characterized as a ‘process’ by which one subunit is affected by the experience of another (Argote & Ingram, 2000, p. 85).

The role of ISs in facilitating such knowledge conversion processes by enabling conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge forms is well understood (Stein & Zwass, 1995). In the context of a multi-unit firm, ISs facilitate a smoother conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge forms between BUs by merging information generated from varied contexts within the multi-unit firm, and by enabling multiple modes of knowledge conversion (Nonaka & Konno, 1998). However, ISs are not deployed in an organizational vacuum, but rather are deployed in order to augment the beneficial effect of other organizational mechanisms. Therefore, our framework draws in the concept of absorptive capacity because of its consideration of the broader organizational context.

Absorptive capacity (ACAP) is the set of routines and processes by which organizations acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit knowledge to produce dynamic capabilities (Zahra & George, 2002a). In particular, in their framework, Zahra & George (2002a) refer to a second ACAP dimension, (knowledge) ‘assimilation’, as the routines and processes that allow one subunit to analyse, interpret, and understand the information obtained from other subunits (Lenox and King 2004). In the intraorganizational context ACAP is a component of firm-level dynamic capabilities, an important antecedent of which are IT competencies (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006, 2010, 2011). Thus, the notion of knowledge assimilation overlaps with the construct of knowledge sharing in knowledge management literature, implying that cross-unit knowledge sharing is an important dimension of ACAP.

The research studies on ACAP point to another key mechanism that can facilitate knowledge sharing: cross-BU knowledge complementarity (Zahra & George, 2002a). Knowledge complementarity between business partners or between BUs can manifest itself as overlapping scientific know-how, common past experience with a customer segment and so forth (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Knowledge complementarities contribute to knowledge sharing between BUs because of overlapping mental schemas that facilitate recontextualization of knowledge inflows from the other BUs. Our research questions, identified previously, focus on two types of knowledge complementarities – workflow interdependence and business relatedness.

In summary, based on a theoretical integration of knowledge management and absorptive capacity literatures, our proposed framework lays the groundwork for understanding how IS integration alters the effect of knowledge complementarity on ACAP as shown in Figure 1.

## Research model: IS integration, knowledge complementarity, and cross-BU knowledge sharing

IS integration We define IS integration as the extent to which the IS applications portfolio of a focal BU works as a functional whole in conjunction with those of other BUs. Whereas other prior studies (Broadbent et al, 1999) measure the capability of the enterprise-wide infrastructure, we specifically restrict our definition to the application and data level and also to the cross-BU level consistent with prior work (Barua et al, 2004; Rai et al, 2006; Schlueter Langdon, 2006).

We propose that IS integration enhances knowledge sharing among BUs by providing a smoother conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge forms, merging information generated from multiple contexts throughout a multi-unit firm, and enabling multiple modes of knowledge conversion (Nonaka & Konno, 1998; Nonaka et al, 2000). Similarly, El Sawy et al (1999) show how organizations use digital platforms for shared knowledgecreation spaces around critical business processes. ISs are also referred to as the ‘boundary’ objects (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Carlile, 2002) because they can potentially help functional departments organize their interconnections and share knowledge (Pawlowski et al, 2000; Pan & Leidner, 2003). In the empirical literature, at the interfirm level, IS integration linkages between firms have been shown to enable knowledge exchange (Malhotra et al, 2005). D’Adderio (2003), in a qualitative study exploring the impact of integrated systems on crossfunctional knowledge flows, views the activity of integrating IT systems as promoting communication and coordination of viewpoints by imposing similar product and process representations through the organization (Tenkasi & Boland, 1996). Therefore, we propose:

## H1: Higher cross-BU IS integration leads to a higher degree of knowledge sharing between the BUs.

Business relatedness as knowledge complementarity Complementarity between the knowledge bases of organizational units is a significant predictor of their ability to share knowledge and assimilate it (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). When two BUs have expertise in different but related customer segments (business relatedness) or interlink through a common workflow (functional relatedness), it creates a common context for exchanging knowledge.

An important dimension of knowledge complementarity is the relatedness between BUs, which refers to the degree of commonality in production systems, markets, customers and so on. Specifically, BUs may have common production techniques or overlapping scientific expertise, and they may serve similar customer segments or offer similar products (Rumelt, 1974). BUs become related when firms diversify into other areas using either their existing technological base (Breschi et al, 2003) or when they merge with (and acquire) other firms with, presumably, related resources (Eisenhardt & Brown, 1999).

Higher relatedness indicates a higher level of common knowledge, which increases the receptivity of one BU towards new knowledge from another, just as it does for partnering firms (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Therefore, if one BU interprets external information about an evolving product technology in a particular way then it is easy for a highly related BU to absorb these insights and knowledge. From the ‘communities of practice’ perspective, we can also consider BUs as separate practitioner fields engaged in distinct but somewhat overlapping customer segments, products, and technologies (Brown & Duguid, 2001). This suggests that product and customer relatedness between BUs helps them to alleviate the stickiness of knowledge spread across their boundaries (Szulanski, 1996). Therefore, we expect the following:

## H2: Higher cross-BU business relatedness leads to higher cross-BU knowledge sharing.

At the same time, higher business relatedness per se does not guarantee that BUs will share knowledge to the full extent possible, as is evident from several instances in which mergers and acquisitions have failed to create synergies (Eisenhardt & Brown, 1999; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2010). Thus, the fact that two BUs are related in terms of their customer and product markets does not mean that they will, by default, successfully share knowledge. Instead, additional organizational initiatives may need to foster knowledge sharing (Dixon, 2000). ERP implementation can lead to a convergence of viewpoints and thus higher ability to sense dependencies between functional units (Baskerville et al, 2000). For example, IS integration of customer acquisition with customer complaint modules spanning related BUs enables them to accurately reinterpret the information captured by either BU. Therefore, we claim that IS integration facilitates a deeper synthesis of mental models of participants if they belong to related BUs. Thus, we offer the following hypothesis:

## H3: IS integration positively moderates the relationship between business relatedness and cross-BU knowledge sharing.

Workflow interdependence as knowledge complementarity Whether or not they are related in terms of their customer and product markets, BUs are most often linked through several other formalized workflows to exploit latent synergies (Goold & Campbell, 1998). We consider workflow interdependence as the second dimension of knowledge complementarity. For example, banks implement processes connecting different customer-facing divisions in order to bundle several services during one customer interaction. Periodic budgeting and planning processes link various BUs, which is when they exchange outlooks on the market environment and discuss opportunities. Thus, irrespective of whether this workflow takes place in the presence of business relatedness or in the absence of it, workflow interdependence is a significant dimension of cross-BU knowledge complementarity.

We define workflow interdependence as the extent to which a BU is interdependent on other BUs in terms of its work activities. Sequential and pooled workflow between subunits signifies low interdependence (Thompson, 1967). An example is an order-fulfillment activity in the automobile industry in which two or more BUs supply unrelated accessories for the final assembly. Reciprocal and team workflows signify high interdependence between the subunits. An example is two BUs in a bank that are cross-selling complex products such as investment services and foreign-exchange instruments. When BUs are linked in terms of their workflow, it means that the employees enact certain complementary ‘routines’. Routines refer to a sequence of formalized business tasks that constitute a workflow (Feldman, 2000; Basu & Kumar, 2002). Routines are a conduit for learning by those enacting and continuously altering them (Brown & Duguid, 2001). Baum & Singh (1994, p. 16) categorize routines as genealogical entities that are ‘concerned with the conservation and transfer of production and organizing skills and knowledge’. The more pre-specified are the routines, the less they provide an opportunity for sharing knowledge, which is what happens when workflow interdependence between BUs is low and well planned. Therefore, we propose:

## H4: Higher cross-BU workflow interdependence leads to higher cross-BU knowledge sharing.

Our next argument counters the intuition offered by the IS literature on the business-process-enabling role of IT linkages (Mukhopadhyay & Kekre, 2002). In most of the studies conducted at the inter-firm and cross-BU levels, IS integration is seen as an enabler of processes spanning organizational units, thus resulting in high efficiencies in information sharing (Barua et al, 1995; Rayport & Sviokla, 1995; Subramani, 2004). In contrast, the point of departure of our model is the focus on cross-BU knowledge sharing as our dependent variable (instead of efficiency). Considering ISs as boundary objects (Pawlowski et al, 2000; Levina & Vaast, 2005), we suggest that IS integration is likely to lower the effect of workflow interdependence on knowledge sharing. For example, consider a recent study in which the exchanges between the product-development and engineering departments are characterized by high interdependence (D’Adderio, 2003). It illustrates that a highly interdependent workflow is a conduit for sharing knowledge between departments. However, when a CAD/CAM system replaces some of this workflow, the routines are partially embedded in the software itself and are partially enacted by it (D’Adderio, 2003). As a result, the effectiveness of workflow routines as conduits for transferring knowledge is reduced as individuals no longer enact those parts of the work routines embedded in the software code (though the level of interdependence between BUs is not affected because the tasks remain unchanged). Such transforma tive effects were also found in a recent study of a projectmanagement application implementation in which the integrated IT system essentially displaced existing human channels of knowledge exchange between subunits (Levina & Vaast, 2006). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H5: IS integration negatively moderates the relationship between workflow interdependence and cross-BU knowledge sharing.

## Methods

A survey questionnaire was developed for a crosssectional study with the BU as the level of analysis. The study was conducted in the context of sales activity in multi-unit firms, which is suitable for testing our hypotheses because it is characterized by exchange of explicit and tacit knowledge between BUs. Sales executives have to continually make sense of emerging market developments such as customer needs, problems, competitors’ promotional schemes, and new technologies (Glazer, 1991; Slater & Narver, 2000; Zahay & Griffin, 2004). In this scenario, cross-BU knowledge sharing is a strategically important goal.

## Measures

The respondents were asked to keep in mind only those other BUs important to their BUs to help them focus their responses and avoid ‘averaging’ their responses across all BUs.

Knowledge sharing This refers to the extent to which BUs share insights and know-how with other BUs about their environments, including suppliers, customers, competitors, and channel partners (Argote & Ingram, 2000). In order to avoid semantic overlap with the scale items for IS integration, the items for the knowledge sharing were worded so as to capture the intelligence aspect of the exchange rather than the exchange of raw customer data.

IS integration We developed a 5-item reflective scale capturing the seamlessness with which data and applications inter-operate across BU boundaries. Rather than capture the technological solutions for integrating BUs (e.g., ERP, EAI, data warehousing), we used outcome-based measures. The first two items capture semantic integration, and the last three capture syntactic integration.

Workflow interdependence There is a lack of consensus about the best measure for this construct (Van de Ven et al, 1976), and so we adapted the most-discussed measure (Van de Ven & Ferry, 1980), which in turn draws from Thompson (1967). This is a Guttman-like scale adapted to the cross-BU level that is essentially a weighted sum of responses to four items capturing increasing levels of interdependence – independent, sequential, reciprocal, and team. Each scale item captures the extent and type of workflow interdependence of a BU with other BUs. In computing the index, independent/ pooled workflow is given no weight (0), sequential workflow is assigned a weight of 0.33, reciprocal workflow is 0.66, and team workflow is 1.

Business relatedness The literature emphasizes two types of relatedness between BUs: production and marketing (Teece, 1980; Porter, 1985; Davis et al, 1992; Hagel & Singer, 1999). We used a 2-item Likert scale that measures the extent to which (1) the products and (2) the customer profiles of other BUs differed from those of the focal BU.

We also control for the size of the multi-unit firm because of the adverse effects it has on the ability to share knowledge among BUs, for two reasons. First, compared with smaller firms. larger organizations face more formal and informal constraints regarding cross-BU knowledge sharing (Dixon, 2000). Second, due to strong BU-level identity and incentive structure, BUs of large organizations are more aggressive in defending their knowledgestocks from other BUs, which is why cross-BU knowledge sharing is most often problematic in conglomerates and multinationals (Eisenhardt & Galunic, 2000) (Table 1).

## Data collection

Data for this study were collected from the high-tech (computing) and the financial services-related sectors. These sectors are especially interesting because of their highly dynamic nature and the high levels of IT intensity of firms (Mendelson & Pillai, 1998). B2B sales executives were targeted for answering our survey because we framed our theory development in the sales channel context. Specifically, the respondents were middle-level managers because they are the ones who are most likely to be aware of the tactical and the operational details as indicated by the key-informant approach (Phillips & Bagozzi, 1986). A sample was drawn from three different databases: (1) a respondent database maintained by an academic research center, (2) member companies of the CTIA – The wireless association, and (3) clients of an offshore market research agency. The sample excluded companies with revenue figures that were very small (below US\$15 million). The data collection was done in three (almost) concurrent waves within a span of three and a half months, with each wave corresponding to each of the sources. The designations of the respondents varied, including director of marketing, vice president of marketing, sales executive, sales manager, and newproduct development manager. One of the co-authors interviewed them briefly to validate their suitability for the survey. Thus, a data set of 75 usable responses (75 BUs) was collected with a response rate of approximately 17, 23, and 35% from each of the three respondent pools, respectively (overall 23%). The median revenue of the multi-unit firms is \$500 million, and the first quartile is \$100 million (see Table 3 for more univariate statistics).

Approximately 95% of the responses to the surveys were collected in the first attempt, and the rest in the second callback. Further, our callbacks indicated that non-respondents did not participate largely because of the lack of time. As this is a reason unrelated to the survey variables, it reduces the possibility of non-response bias (Colombo, 2000). ANOVA and t-tests indicated absence of non-response bias (P ¼ 0.42 for sales revenue and 0.64 for employee size). These two variables were most easily accessible for every respondent organization but were not available for all non-responding organizations. We could acquire sales revenue for 86% of the firms in the sampling frame and employee size for 88% of them. T-tests for bias arising from heterogeneity in the three waves of data collection reveal no significant difference in revenue and survey measures.

To detect the presence of common-method variance, the Harmon’s one-factor test was performed (Harmon, 1967; Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). Accordingly, a principalcomponents analysis of all five constructs in the model yielded five factors that had eigenvalues 41.0 and accounted for 59% of the total variance, with no single factor accounting for more than 19%. This suggests that common-method bias is not a concern. Further, the presence of moderating effects (discussed in the next section) also points to a mitigation of common-method bias (Evans, 1985; Brockner et al, 1997). The rationale is that even if the respondents anticipate a correlation between the independent and dependent variables they cannot anticipate moderating relationships, both of which are significant in our results.

## Data validation and analysis

The research model was operationalized as a multipleindicator, structural-equation model. The hypotheses were examined using partial least squares (PLS) (Chin, 1994). The robustness of the survey data was confirmed in multiple ways. Table 2 reports the mean, range, and standard deviation for each construct by summing across its items. Each survey item was also subjected to preliminary checks such as the normal P–P plot of standardized residuals and Q–Q plots of variables, as well as tests of normality (Kolmogorov–Smirnov). These tests do not indicate any concern regarding deviation from a normal distribution. To evaluate the convergent and discriminant validity of our measures, path loadings from the PLS Graph were obtained, and cross-loadings calculated (Table 2). Convergent validity is supported if the indicators of a construct correlate highly among themselves. As in Table 2, the loadings of all construct items are more than 0.7 (except for INT1, which is 0.65) and are significant at the 5% level. Similarly, discriminant validity can be inferred if the indicators of a construct load higher on their own construct than on other constructs. The results in Table 2 again suggest discriminant validity of our measures, with every item loading most highly on its own construct.

Discriminant validity of the constructs is verified because the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) of each construct in Table 3 is larger than the inter-construct correlations (Chin, 1998a). Finally, for the three multi-item reflective constructs, the composite reliability estimates are above 0.8 for all constructs (Table 3) indicating good internal consistency (Werts et al, 1978; Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

## Model estimation and results

The sample size appears to satisfy the rule of thumb. In our full model, the number of independent variables is at most seven. For PLS (consistent with the heuristic for multiple regression), the sample size should be 10 times the maximum number of paths leading into any one construct in the structural-equation model (Tabachnick & Fidell, 1989). This suggests a minimum sample size of 70. Interaction variables were created by multiplying all of the standardized indicator variables of respective constructs, WFLOW, INT, and RELATED (Chin et al, 2003). Thus, INTWF and INTREL consisted of 4 and 10 indicators, respectively.

While sample sizes are normatively prescribed for covariance-based, structurally equal models (LISREL, AMOS) or regression-based (PLS) models, the underlying theory that guides sample size is the one on statistical power (Marcoulides & Saunders, 2006; Marcoulides et al, 2009). Our power computations (Cohen, 1988) reveal that the power for Model 1 in Table 4 is high at 0.9. and it is 0.70 for Model 4, which is still in the acceptable range of 0.8 indicated in Chin et al (2003). The other two models (2 and 3) have lower power, although the results in these two models are consistent with Model 4. It is partly because of the usefulness of PLS estimation of structural equations that prior studies (Ravichandran & Rai, 2000; Majchrzak et al, 2005; Saraf et al, 2007; Johnson et al, 2008; Li et al, 2011) have been able to derive robust conclusions using equivalent sample sizes.

To assess the significance of the path coefficients, PLS Graph was used (Chin, 1998a) to generate 500 samples using a bootstrapping technique, and the results were interpreted in accordance with norms (Wold, 1985; Chin, 1998b). Table 4 presents four models with the full model, including displaying a satisfactory level of $R ^ { 2 }$ at 51% (Model 4). All main effects and both moderating effects are significant in the full and partial models. The bottom row presents the F-tests for the significance of the increase in $R ^ { 2 }$ (Carte & Russell, 2003), which is expected to be consistent with the significance of the coefficients. Thus, the results support the hypothesized main and moderating effects. Figure 2 shows the structural model results for Model 4. The results were also consistent with multiple regression estimates obtained with the summed latent-variable scores as the regression variables.

## Discussion of results

The results support the claim made often in the IS literature about the effectiveness of IS in enabling knowledge sharing. Our study also shows that business relatedness can indeed contribute to enhancing the absorptive capacity (ACAP) of multi-unit organizations. Similarly, we also found that higher workflow interdependence indeed leads to a higher degree of knowledge sharing because of its capacity to act as a conduit for transfer of learning. Importantly, we found that while both antecedents can lead to more knowledge sharing, IS integration has an interesting moderating effect that supports our theory that greater IS integration weakens the effect of workflow interdependence on knowledge sharing. Although this seems counter-intuitive to the IS literature on business process enablement, it does not if we consider integrated ISs as boundary objects. We argue that integrated ISs replace the boundary-spanning role of workflow routines. The increased embeddedness of routines in software code reduces the efficacy of reciprocal interdependence in creating interaction occasions for shared knowledge among participants across BUs. We term this effect the IS integration winner’s curse, borrowing the term from auction theory (Kagel & Levin, 1986). In common-value auctions with incomplete information, the winner’s curse is an observed phenomenon in which the winner of the auction, although having a net gain, will be ‘cursed’ and will tend to pay above the value of the auctioned asset, thus being worse off in absolute terms. It is to be noted that the significance of the moderating effect of IS integration on link between WFLOW and knowledge sharing is conceptually independent of the significance WFLOW on knowledge sharing. In other words, the marginal significance of the direct effect of WFLOW on knowledge sharing does not, in any way, diminish the importance of the negative moderating effect. Whereas the main results in Figure 2 only point to the net effect of WFLOW on knowledge sharing, Figure 3 (A) shows how the effect of WFLOW on knowledge sharing is fairly high for low levels of IS integration but reduces drastically for high IS integration.

The negative moderating effect, however, does not suggest that IS integration will, overall, lower cross-BU knowledge sharing. In fact, we note a net positive contribution of IS integration towards cross-BU knowledge sharing. However, a ‘redistribution’ of these effects occurs by which IS integration becomes a key contributor to knowledge sharing (an increase in $R ^ { 2 } )$ and workflow interdependence begins to have a reduced (yet positive) impact on knowledge sharing. The integrated ISs, by virtue of being boundary objects, appear to ‘crowd out’ the knowledge-boundary-spanning role of cross-BU workflows. Surprisingly, keeping workflows a little broken will have positive effects on cross-BU knowledge sharing.

## Limitations and future research

Our study has some limitations that can hopefully be addressed in future research. For example, future studies could collect longitudinal data with matched pairs in order to establish strict causality. Further, in order to draw an appropriate boundary around our phenomena of interest, we focused on the sales context. More research needs to be done to study ACAP during other business process contexts such as new-product development as also operationalizing tacit and explicit knowledge forms in order to trace knowledge conversion patterns. The literature has also advanced to a stage at which studies categorize business processes in terms of their knowledge intensity (Eppler et al, 1999; Massey et al, 2002). Whereas in our study we have adhered to a single empirical context of B2B sales (restricted to high-clock-speed industries such as high-tech and finance), future studies could achieve greater generalizability by including knowledge intensity as a contingency variable and collecting data from a variety of business contexts. Methodologically, our 2-item scale for business relatedness is a drawback because scales with three or more items are recommended for latent constructs (Chin et al, 2003). Finally, detecting moderating effects is challenging with small sample sizes as the power is often not as high as 0.8 (Chin et al, 2003), which is a limitation of our study.

## Implications and conclusion

## Implications for theory

This study contributes by exploring the intra-organizational foundations of absorptive capacity and the role of IT in augmenting ACAP. Zahra & George (2002b) suggested that the close connection between ACAP and IT can be further understood in terms of the role that ISs play in executing the routines that characterize the knowledge-management processes within organizations. We argue that the locus of such absorptive capacity is in the integrated IS portfolio itself – that is, the absorptive capacity resides in these cross-BU IT linkages. This notion of ACAP residing in the cross-BU IT linkages is also consistent with the understanding of interface standards. When standards are established at a lower level, it enables the exchange of richer and more contextual information (Antonelli, 1994). From the pragmatic perspective of knowledge (Brown & Duguid, 2001; Carlile, 2002), integrated ISs act as boundary objects that facilitate translation of knowledge from one community of practice (i.e., a BU) to another. Thus, we have extended theoretically the notion gradually emerging in the literature of the subtler, learning-related impacts of integrating applications, and databases across organizational subunits.

As another important contribution, our study explains how, with greater workflow interdependence (such as team or reciprocal interdependence), the impact of IS integration on knowledge sharing can get subdued. We have termed this the IS integration winner’s curse, in which IS integration displaces and may reduce the role of organizational workflow in leading to cross-BU knowledge sharing. We believe that there may be other facets of the IS integration winner’s curse that are waiting to be discovered by future studies.

## Implications for practitioners

There is a compelling need in literature to flesh out the conditions under which various IT initiatives are likely to lead to positive impact on key strategic outcomes. Understanding when IT initiatives may have the desired impact, and more importantly when such impact may not be realized, offers more concrete guidance to IT and sales executives, rather than the overall positive benefits of IT so often portrayed in the media. Adopting the framework of absorptive capacity and the pragmatic perspective of knowledge provides guidance to managers in addressing this challenge. Thus, for example, greater BU-level relatedness represents only a potential to disseminate proprietary knowledge located in one BU to other BUs. However, intended knowledge sharing may not necessarily happen as is evidenced by many failed acquisitions despite ex-ante business relatedness between the acquirer and the acquired firms. Therefore, while the theoretical effect of business relatedness on knowledge sharing is obvious this finding has little practical implication unless research examines moderating mechanisms that help realize the potential of business relatedness. Hence, our results that higher IS integration can moderate the impact of business relatedness on knowledge sharing has useful implications for managers. It implies that their IT initiatives should be focused on integrating related BUs. However, the IS Integration winner’s curse finding that workflow interdependence per se may not lead to higher absorptive capacity is instructive for managers who expect solely positive impacts of IS integration.
