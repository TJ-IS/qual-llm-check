## a b s t r a c t

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne and identify bidding strategies in real-life small loan auctions (Prosper.com). In such auctions, lenders bid for borrowers' loan listings and the winners get to fund the loan at an interest rate determined by the auction. The exceptionally large empirical database provided by Prosper.com offers a unique opportunity to test and further develop the theory of online auctions. This study shows that bidding behavior is not homogeneous among bidders, as the traditional auction theory suggests. Instead, bidders use many different bidding strategies. Moreover, learning and bidders' consistency over time in different auctions is studied.

## 1. Introduction

Prosper.com is the <sup>fi</sup>rst people-to-people lending marketplace, based on an online reverse auction. In this marketplace, people make applications for loans, called listings, and then other people make bids on these listings. The winning bidders get to fund the loan and the interest rate is determined by the auction — the more competition, the lower the interest rate. In other words, the idea is to link the person in need of money with people willing to lend money without an intermediating bank. Typically a loan is funded by many bidders (lenders), because most lenders only fund \$50–\$200 per each loan. Lenders bid for these small amounts across many loans to help diversify their risk. Prosper.com was launched publicly in February 2006 and has brokered so far over \$150 million worth of loans [14,18,19].

In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne and identify bidding strategies in real-life small loan C2C auctions (Prosper.com). The research subject is particularly interesting since Prosper.com is a C2C reverse auction where the consumers' bidding strategies have not yet been studied. Furthermore, the recent emergence of people-to-people lending sites and their popularity makes research in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of C2C reverse auctions urgently needed. Referring to the fundamental way of bidding strategy identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in C2C online forward auctions, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nine bidding strategies for Prosper.com. Prosper's C2C reverse auctions are different from forward auctions. The bids must fully fund the listings, whereas in multiple unit forward auctions bidders may most times purchase partial quantities without purchasing the entire lot. We also studied the changes of bidding strategies for a single bidder. The results comprehensively demonstrated that bidding strategies are diverse across individuals and within individual(s) in an auction.

The large empirical database provided by Prosper.com offers a unique opportunity to test and further develop the theory of online auctions. This study shows that bidding behavior is not homogeneous among bidders, as the traditional auction theory suggests. Instead, there are many different bidding strategies that can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from real-life bidding data. In total, nine different strategies were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, but three of them were most commonly followed. They were the evaluator strategy, late bidding, and a multi-bid strategy where the last bid is made late. The motivation behind the different strategies is discussed in the light of existing literature on auction theory and bidding behavior. Moreover, we have investigated learning and show that bidding experience changes the strategies used. In addition, we studied whether the bidders appeared to adjust their bidding strategies according to the listing (high risk, low risk) in question.

The terms of the loans on Prosper.com are <sup>fi</sup>xed to a 3-year, fully amortized, unsecured loan. The borrower can choose the loan amount freely between \$1000 and \$25,000. The average requested loan amount is \$7500 (median \$5000, standard deviation \$6500). The starting rate of the auction can be set anywhere between 0% and 36%. The average starting rate is 19% (median 18%, standard deviation 8%). The starting rate naturally is sensitive to the credit grade of the borrower. The credit grade (scaled between AA and HR, where AA is best and HR worst) is calculated by Experian. It takes into account all the credit information variables and classi<sup>fi</sup>es the borrowers into seven categories.

This study is based on empirical data provided by Prosper.com. Much of the data is freely available on the Prosper.com website. It consists of all the bids and listings made since the beginning of the service. The data was exported from the website to Microsoft Access database where it was analyzed. The <sup>fi</sup>rst listings are from November 2005. The data was exported in the end of July 2008; hence there is more than two and a half years of bidding data available. In this study we excluded all incomplete listings where the loans did not get funded. In total there were 312,562 listings, of which only 26,251 were completed. We further omitted listings where the borrowers had chosen the auto-funding option (now known as the “no auction” format in which the event closes as soon as the amount of the loan is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled), leaving us with 19,014 listings to study, with 3.2 million bids in total.

Our research draws on past auction research, notably [4,5,22]. Our research, however, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different. We study multi-unit C2C reverse auctions in the context of Prosper.com, whereas their main focus is on B2C and C2C forward auctions. Moreover, we carefully look at the impact of learning on bidding strategies and the strategy-wise consistency of individual bidders in different auctions.

Our paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a literature review of past empirical studies on bidder strategies. Section 3 details a Visual Basic Algorithm Imitating Prosper.com Auction Mechanism, which was needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd out about the excess decrement. Broad patterns in bidding behavior on Prosper.com are described in Section 4. Bidding strategies are de<sup>fi</sup>ned and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Section 5. Sensitivity analysis is described in Section 6. Learning is studied in Section 7, and the conclusions are provided in Section 8.

## 2. Past research on auction strategies

Traditionally, empirical research in auctions meant laboratory experiments. In such laboratory experiments it was commonly noted that all people did not bid according to the game-theoretic equilibrium, but bidding behavior even in the simplest auctions seemed to be much more complicated. See, for example, [8,10–12] for empirical laboratory studies. A critical article about the reasons why traditional auction theory fails to explain real-life bidding behavior is provided by Smith ([23], cited in ref. [25], p. 262)

In an online environment there is a unique opportunity to study real-life auctions. The progress of an auction can be recorded and the bidding behavior of each bidder can be studied afterwards. In an online environment the data collection can be automated and therefore the total number of bidders analyzed can be considerable. Although online auctions are relatively new as a research subject, a number of studies have been conducted about the bidding behavior in online forward auctions. Most of such studies have focused on the auction site eBay.com, which is the market leader in facilitating online auctions.

Rafaeli and Noy [20] consider an auction as a social process. They have formalized the factors affecting bidding behavior in online auctions. According to them the determinants of bidding behavior consist of personal factors, environmental factors, and social factors. This is in line with several auction studies. The experience and learning aspects of bidding are highlighted in refs. [5,15,26]. Also the risk-pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the bidder affects the bidding behavior. Finally, it has been empirically observed that people seem to draw some utility merely out of participating in an auction [3] (also see Swoopo.com; an auction site considered to be “entertainment shopping”). This naturally has an effect on the bidding behavior and eventually the price the bidder is willing to pay for the item. We have seen in the traditional auctions that even small changes in the auction mechanism or information architecture (environmental factors) may have a large effect on the bidding behavior [13], Social factors include other bidders and the seller. The information about other bidders is extremely valuable if available. The bid history reveals the valuation of other bidders, which can be an essential part of the bidder's own valuation, especially if there is a strong common value factor in the auction. Another aspect is herding. This means that when an auction builds up a critical mass of bidders, others will join it too. One would think that bidders try to avoid competition, but herding has been empirically observed to happen [6].

One of the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between traditional and online auctions is their duration. In traditional auctions the duration is usually minutes. On the contrary, the duration of online auctions is usually several days. The extended duration of online auctions enables bidders to review different auctions without a hurry. The longer auction duration accumulates more bidders. The long auction duration has also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on the bidding behavior. In online auctions timing has become an important strategic instrument. Now the question is not only about how much to bid, but also when to place the bid. The idea in bidding late is that you can decrease the competition and eventually get the item at a lower price [21]. Late bidding also hides valuable information about the bidder's valuation from other bidders. This is particularly important in an auction with a large common value component. Indeed the strategy of bidding late is widely used in online auctions [1,2,9,17,22].

When the number of bidders is not constant, contrary to what is assumed by traditional auction theory, the incentive compatibility and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of even simple auction mechanisms are not valid anymore. In their study, Vakrat and Seidmann [24] modeled the number of bidders in an auction as an arrival process, where the number of arriving bidders is a function of minimum bid, duration, quantity, and average closing price. The complicated arrival process of the bidders further emphasizes the importance of timing one's bids.

One interesting device that addresses the late bidding problem is proxy bidding or the bidding agent. The idea is that the bidder outsources the bidding to an agent which will bid according to predetermined criteria. First these bidding agents were programmed by individual bidders so that, for example, in sniping they could deliver tens of bids within seconds. This provided the bidder with a bidding agent a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant competitive edge over other bidders. Later, many auction sites incorporated proxy bidding as part of their services for all the bidders. This is the case with Prosper.com as well.

Most of the empirical studies on online bidding behavior have looked at one single behavioral aspect at a time. These include, for example, timing [4,21], and “jump-bidding” [7]. Bapna et al. [3,5] took a wider perspective and discovered that the bidding strategies in online auctions were not homogeneous. People pursued different strategies in the same auctions. It seemed that there is not necessarily one dominant strategy. They identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four main strategies. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was the evaluator strategy. These bidders submitted only one bid during the auction. The bid was made early or in the middle of the auction. The second strategy was that of the opportunist's. Such people made their bids late, hunting for bargains. The auction had a soft ending rule, implying that sniping was impossible. The third strategy was the participatory strategy. These bidders entered the auction early and exited late. Throughout the auction they made new bids as they became outbid. Bapna et al. [5] introduced a new fourth strategy: the sip-and-dippers. Such bidders usually placed two bids: one early in the auction and the other later toward the end of the auction. Bapna et al. [5] discovered that the evaluators were by far the largest bidder class, consisting of over 50% of all bidders. The second largest class was the opportunists with roughly 25% of all bidders.

Another interesting study concerning bidding strategies is that of Shah et al. [22]. They studied bidding strategies on eBay. They identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four strategies: evaluator, skeptic, late bidder, and unmasking strategies. They introduced a new useful parameter, the excess increment. This was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the excess amount in a bid over the minimum required bid. According to Shah et al. [22] one of the most dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks in studying the excess increment is to recover the ask price at the moment each bid was made. For this purpose they replayed each auction and saved the results. This approach is used in this study as well. Shah et al. [22] were able to connect the evaluator strategy with high excess increment. In their study the evaluators bid both early and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher than the required minimum bid. The skeptic strategy is close to the participatory strategy de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Bapna et al. [5]. Here the strategy was not de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on time of entry and exit, but zero excess increment. The late bidding strategy is equivalent to the opportunists (Bapna et al. [5]). The fourth category, unmasking, was new. It involved a series of closely placed bids with variable excess increments. Shah et al. [22] found out that 66% of bidders bid only once per auction. The rest of the bidders used multibid strategies. The most widely used strategy was late bidding with up to 58% of all single bid engagements. The evaluator strategy came next with 13% of single bid engagements. Shah et al. [22] also realized that the boundaries between different strategies had to be arbitrarily chosen. They tried to overcome this problem by looking at the data with cluster analysis. The main problem was that 88% of all bids did not <sup>fi</sup>t any category. In the present study this problem is approached with sensitivity analysis instead of clustering.

All studies mentioned above deal with bidding strategies in B2C or C2C forward auctions. This study contributes to the literature by examining bidding strategies in C2C reverse auctions. Reverse auctions as such, particularly in the B2B context, have been widely studied, however to our knowledge, never from the bidding strategy point of view. Besides Prosper's reverse auction procedures are different from standard multi-unit forward auctions where also partial quantities may be bought and sold. With Prosper, the listings (auctions) are either fully funded or not.

## 3. Visual basic algorithm imitating Prosper.com auction mechanism

The database included the minimum rates of those bidders who had been outbid. Naturally, the minimum rates of the winners were not published since that is privileged information. One piece of information missing was the current rate of the auction at the time the bid was made. This was needed in order to determine the excess decrement, i.e., how much below the current rate the bid was made. For this purpose we developed a Visual Basic algorithm which imitated the auction mechanism on Prosper.com. Every listing was “re-auctioned” and each bid was entered into the auction one by one. This way the progress of the auction was documented and the current interest rate of the listing after each bid was calculated and saved into the database. By doing this, we were able to recover the current rate of the auction (the rate that the bidder at least has to bid to become active) for all bids.

In principle, the process was 100% accurate and we were able to extract correct current rates for all bids. There were some small calculation errors related to rounding, database errors and special circumstances, but their effect was minimal.<sup>1</sup> The algorithm was tested against several manually calculated real-life listings to ensure it operated properly. Finally, the <sup>fi</sup>nal rates calculated with the algorithm were compared to the <sup>fi</sup>nal rates reported in the database and this also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the algorithm was accurate.

## 4. Broad patterns in bidding behavior on Prosper.com

In this section the Prosper.com data is analyzed at a general level, giving us an overview on how bids are placed on Prosper.com. The results are later utilized when the different bidding strategies are de<sup>fi</sup>ned.

## 4.1. Competition among bidders

The average number of bids in auctions was 170 (median 124). The average fall in the <sup>fi</sup>nal rate was 3.9 percentage points (median 3.0 pp) and the average starting rate in these auctions was 20.1% (median 19.0%). From the total of 3.2 million “competitive” bids, 1.7 million (53%) have been outbid, con<sup>fi</sup>rming that the competition among bidders is real and lively.

The bid-ratio is another measure of competition in multi-unit auctions. The bid-ratio measures how many times the bid amounts cover the auctioned amount. Data from the funded auctions shows us that the average bid-ratio is 2.41 (median 2.20). This is quite similar to empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings on Treasury bond auctions. For example, in Sweden the average bid-ratio happens to be the same 2.41 [16].

## 4.2. Timing of bids

Over 42% of all bids were made during the last day. This is late especially when we consider that the average duration of the auction was 7.6 days (median 7).

When we look at bidding during the last 24 h more carefully, we can see similar results. The bidding activity again clearly increases towards the end of the auction and peaks at the last 30 min (Fig. 1).

## 4.3. Number of bids

Unlike in most other online auctions, most bidders on Prosper.com make only one bid per auction. This is a clear indication that the auction mechanism encouraging you to enter your true valuation works with many of the bidders. This phenomenon is also explained by the fact that the auction is a multi-unit auction where there are multiple bidders. Since the average loan amount is \$6657 (median \$5000) and the average bid amount \$89.29 (median \$50), there are dozens of winners per auction. Over 87% of bidders made only one bid per auction, 8% of bidders made two bids and 2% made 3 bids.

## 4.4. Bid amount

People can choose their bid amount freely between the minimum amount of \$50 (this amount has since changed to \$25) and the amount requested in the listing. The average bid amount was \$89.29. But if we look at the data more closely, we can see that the median bid amount equals the minimum amount of \$50. The reason behind the popularity of small bid amounts is clear. By diversifying their investments into multiple loans the effect of one bad loan on the return is minimal.

## 4.5. Proxy bidding

On average people seem to take some advantage of proxy bidding by setting the minimum rates below the maximum allowed rate. This could suggest that at least some bidders see the auction mechanism as partly incentive compatible. The minimum rates of the winners are not published and therefore the analysis cannot be done for the winning bidders. Therefore we have data for this analysis from 1.7 million outbid bids only. Adapting the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of excess increment [22] into this context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne excess decrement as the excess rate under the maximum allowed bid. This represents the bidder's use of the proxy system. In this study, the excess decrement takes into account the bid decrement of 0.05 percentage points, which is required for all new bids by the auction mechanism.

The results are interesting. In Fig. 2 we can see that the excess decrement is heavily skewed towards zero. More than half of the outbid bids were made with less than 1 percentage point excess decrement. When we remember that the average fall in the interest rate of the listing was 3.5 percentage points, already 1-2 percentage point excess decrement can be regarded as quite considerable.

## 4.6. Portfolio bidding

Bidding on Prosper.com is easy and requires only little effort from the bidder. However, searching for the right listing can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, especially if the bidder wants to make a lot of diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed small investments. To ease this task, Prosper.com offers a portfolio tool. With this tool the bidder can make standing orders, where the criteria for suitable listings are entered and then the tool scans all the existing listings for suitable ones. When the tool <sup>fi</sup>nds a listing that matches the criteria, it automatically makes a bid in that listing according to the boundaries set by the user.

Portfolio bidders are quite dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to spot from the data. One possibility for identifying a portfolio bid is to look at the timing of the bid. Some of these bids are made during the very <sup>fi</sup>rst seconds of the listing, In total. 11.191 bids were made within the first 10 s of the auction. This constitutes only 0.3% of all bids. But not all portfolio bids are made at the start. People might adjust their portfolio plans. In this case the portfolio bids enter the auction at some point of the auction and they are more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to spot. However, there is one additional way of identifying the portfolio bids. The chances of two bidders making a bid in the same auction at exactly the same second are small. Still, in the data there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of bids with exactly the same time stamp. These bids are most likely made through standing orders. The number of bids that are made in the same auction with the same time stamp is 153,702. This is roughly 5% of all bids.

## 4.7. Bidder turnover and experience

Prosper.com is still a new service. Every day new users <sup>fi</sup>nd the site and start bidding. In the dataset there were 49,958 different bidders in total. On average, people have stayed active on Prosper.com for 207 days (median 137 days). During this time the average bidder made 65 bids (median 23). Of the 49,958 bidders, 26,019 made bids in the last 3 months. These bidders are considered active. The rest of the bidders (23,939) have stopped bidding at least temporally. One third of the active bidders has been bidding for less than 3 months and can be categorized as novice. Over 65% have been active for less than 1 year. On the other hand, there is a considerable number of bidders that can be considered very experienced. These bidders enable us to study learning on Prosper.

## 5. Bidding strategies on Prosper.com

Next, individual bidders' strategies are studied. We assume that the bidder pursues the same strategy during the same auction. Between auctions, however, the bidder may change her strategy. The 3.3 million bids under review form 2.7 million auction engagements.

## 5.1. Strategy definitions

The bidder can choose two parameters when making the bid: the bid amount and the minimum interest rate. Furthermore, the bidder can choose when to place the bid and whether to make multiple bids. In this study we assume that the bid amount is unrelated to other decision factors. Thus, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three parameters to de<sup>fi</sup>ne bidding strategies: (1) the number of bids made in one auction, (2) timing of the bid (early or late), and (3) the excess decrement.

The “number of bids” parameter splits strategies into single and multi-bidding. Timing categorizes between late bidding (including sniping) and other strategies. The time limit for late bidding is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the last 12 h of the auction. Sniping is a special case which takes place during the last 30 min. Finally, the third level parameter is the excess decrement, which divides the strategies between evaluators and opportunists. The break-point for skeptic or opportunistic strategies is 0.3 percentage points and for the evaluator strategy it is 1.0 percentage point. Using these parameters we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nine different bidding strategies (see Table 1).

In Fig. 4, the connection between different bidding strategies and the three de<sup>fi</sup>ning parameters is illustrated as a tree-graph. First, the bidding strategies are de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on the number of bids. Secondly, the timing de<sup>fi</sup>nes whether the strategy is about late bidding or not. Finally, for the early bids we can calculate the excess decrement, which concludes the strategy de<sup>fi</sup>nition. In addition to these rules there are a couple of special strategies which are determined based on more complex factors (see Table 1). The reason there is no multi-bid strategy for early bids with a large excess decrement is that it very rarely happened.

## 5.1.1. Single-bid strategies

5.1.1.1. Late bidding and sniping. One of the most distinguishable strategies is late bidding. The idea of having an advantage by bidding late is very characteristic for online auctions. Making a single bid late is highly popular among Prosper.com bidders as well. Up to 25% of all engagements are single bid engagements made within the last 12 h. A special case of late bidding is sniping, where one waits until the very last minutes of the auction. This is particularly popular in single-item auctions (as on eBay), where there is only one winner. By bidding at the last second you win for sure, unless there are other snipers in the same auction as well. Most auction sites have solved this problem by introducing soft ending rules. On Prosper.com the developers have decided not to use this approach and the ending time is <sup>fi</sup>xed. The reasoning for this probably was that since there are multiple winners, sniping wouldn't become a problem. Indeed sniping is a marginal strategy (3%) but nevertheless it is used on Prosper.com.

By bidding late, the bidder can clearly increase her chances of winning the auction. Up to 72% of the late bidders are winning. When looking at all bids, the winning ratio is only 46%. A particularly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference can be seen between the very late and early bids. The winning ratio of the snipers is over 90%, as we can see from Table 2. The winning ratio of bids made during the <sup>fi</sup>rst 24 h is only 41%. All in all, the incentives to bid late are clearly present in Prosper. com as well.

5.1.1.2. Evaluators. The evaluator strategy is probably closest to the original purpose of the Prosper.com auction mechanism. Here the bidder takes full advantage of the ease of bidding by making one bid early in the auction with a minimum rate well below the current rate. The bid has to be made at least 24 h before the end of the auction and the excess decrement has to be more than 1 percentage point. In addition, winners that have bid at least 24 h before the auction closed were determined as evaluators, if the interest rate of the listing had decreased over 1 percentage point after submitting the bid. The difference between the winner and the outbid evaluators is that we cannot be sure if the winning evaluators would have rebid, had they been outbid during the auction. Therefore, by accepting the winning evaluators (and the winning late bidders) into the strategy de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, there is a certain risk of underestimating the proportion of the multi-bid strategy. However, bidding in this dataset was relatively competitive and therefore the winners were included in the strategy de<sup>fi</sup>nitions. The share of outbid and winning evaluators is quite balanced: 53% of the evaluators were outbid and 47% winning. As we can see, this strategy is actually very popular; up to 33% of all bidders used it.

There are many possible reasons for following the evaluator strategy. First, bidding is easy and requires low monitoring costs. For bidders, who see the auction mechanism as incentive compatible and are able to determine their private value, the evaluator strategy should actually be dominating other strategies. In addition, bidding early enables the bidder to signal other bidders of their intentions. For instance, if the listing is unlikely to get funded, the bidder can attract other bidders by bidding early in the auction to increase its chances of funding.

From the borrower's point of view, the evaluators are particularly good bidders. They commit into the auction early and start pushing the rate down immediately. With these bidders the auction stays very simple and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. However, the borrower is perhaps even in a better situation when there are evaluators and opportunists, skeptics or late bidders involved. The evaluators determine the “base rate” for the listing and then the other bidders drive the rate further down. If there is a strong common value component, the other bidders might drive the rate even below the “correct market price” and thus, activate the winner's curse. For the borrower this is naturally pro<sup>fi</sup>table.

5.1.1.3. Opportunists. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the opportunistic strategy as follows: the bidder makes just one bid with a small excess decrement. By bidding only marginally below the maximum allowed rate, the bidder maximizes her chances of being outbid. The opportunists clearly show signs of disbelief in the auction mechanism and they have serious trouble in determining proper minimum rate. These bidders are probably quite cautious and risk-averse. One possible explanation is that they can be exercising a multi-bid strategy, but decide to leave the auction for some reason right after the <sup>fi</sup>rst bid. When the bidder makes many opportunistic bids in the same auction, she is considered to be following the skeptic strategy. The opportunistic strategy can hardly be considered very optimal and it is indeed quite rare. Only 6% of all engagements were categorized as opportunists. The opportunistic strategy can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only from bidders who have been outbid, because of concealed minimum rates of the winners. Therefore, there are no winners in this strategy.

5.1.1.4. Portfolio bidders. Bidders adopting the portfolio strategy use the standing order functionality of the Prosper.com site. They let the system <sup>fi</sup>nd the listings and make the bids for them according to the desired criteria. These bids were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the data by taking the bids made within the same second and looking separately at the bids made during the <sup>fi</sup>rst seconds of the auction. As previously stated, there will be some bids left unaccounted for because the standing orders are handled just as any other bids by Prosper.com auction mechanism. However, the sample is so large that a few missing bids have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the <sup>fi</sup>nal results. All in all, 5% is the best approximation for the share of portfolio bidders.

The Portfolio strategy is by far the most cost effective way of making diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed bids on Prosper.com. The search and monitoring costs are minimal. However, this strategy has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant drawbacks as well. Determining the minimum rate for a group of listings based on simple criteria is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Even if the bidder is able to adjust the criteria optimally in such a way that, on average, the minimum rate matches her personal valuation, it means that half the time the rate is too low and half the time it is too high. In this case the winner's curse makes bidding dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. By following a portfolio strategy the bidder usually wins only the auctions where her minimum rate was too low.

## 5.1.2. Multi-bid strategies

The general idea in the multi-bid strategy is that, when being outbid, you rebid with lower minimum rate. Up to 92% of all multibids contain at least one outbid bid. Overall the multi-bid strategy is closely related to the late bidding strategy. Up to 54% of all multi-bids have at least one bid made during the last 12 h. Furthermore, on average the excess decrement in the multi-bids is only 1.2 percentage points (median 0.6 pp) while in the single bids it is 2.0 percentage points (median 1.2 pp). These features of the multi-bid strategy are quite natural since the bidder who makes multiple bids with low excess decrements, has to bid late to ever win the auction. In short, the multi-bid strategy is about rebidding when being outbid with low excess decrement and making at least the last bid late.

The multi-bid strategy could be characterized somewhat dubious, since the bidder does not fully trust the auction mechanism. She takes small cautious steps and can leave the auction easily after each step. This strategy requires extra effort from the bidder in terms of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant monitoring costs. Yet there are some logical reasons for this behavior. First, as previously mentioned, the money you bid on Prosper.com is tied up until the auction ends or you become outbid. This means that by making multiple small bids you can free up your money and leave the auction every now and then when outbid. The second reason for adopting the multi-bid strategy is uncertainty and the common value component. If the bidder cannot explicitly determine her minimum rate or it depends heavily on the minimum rates of other bidders, she might want to make one small bid at a time and then look at the situation again. The third reason is signaling. By making a small bid early in the auction the bidder can signal other bidders that she is interested in the auction but not yet reveals her true minimum rate. By rebidding in the last minutes one can take advantage of the information revealed by other bids and conceal the true bid from others. If nobody bids early, there is a risk for informational cascade where all participants conceal their interest in the listing and the listing will never be funded. Thus, making a signaling bid can make sense at least for more experienced bidders, whose actions are closely monitored by other bidders.

5.1.2.1. All late multi-bidders. Multi-bidders form quite a robust group where the strategy is dominated by making small bids and placing the last bid late. However, we can identify even <sup>fi</sup>ner strategies among them. One such category is formed by people who make all their bids late. In other words, they start bidding late with a very low decrement and rebid if being outbid. Basically, these people stay really active the full 12 last hours. The share of this strategy among the multi-bidders is about 16%. This strategy is a straight multi-bid follow-up for the late bidding strategy. The only difference is that they seem to use such small excess decrements that they keep on getting outbid even in the <sup>fi</sup>nal bidding phase. This strategy is risk-averse, since these persons want to reserve the opportunity to drop out almost any minute despite the additional monitoring costs this strategy requires.

5.1.2.2. Last bid late multi-bidders. In this strategy the last bid is made late. The last bid late strategy is relatively close to the sip-and-dip strategy of Bapna et al. [5]. The general idea is to make one or more bids quite early in the auction and then make the winning bid late. The all late strategy is a special case of this. As we saw earlier, there are some logical reasons for signaling your interest early with a preliminary bid but then enter late the <sup>fi</sup>nal bid and win. These people clearly do not see the auction mechanism as incentive compatible, but more like a game where they lure out the other bidders to <sup>fi</sup>nance the listing and then strike at the last moment to get the highest possible interest rate. It is also enlightening to look at the proportion of the all late and the last bid late strategies as combined. The combined proportion of these strategies is 51.1% of all multi-bid strategies, emphasizing that bidding late is an inseparable part of multi-bid strategies.

5.1.2.3. Skeptic multi-bidders. In the skeptic strategy bidders make all their bids with extremely low (0.3 pp) excess decrements. These bidders form 9% of all multi-bidders. Here a bidder, who has outbid bids with excess decrement less than 0.3 percentage points and has a winning bid (with unknown excess decrement), is counted as a skeptic bidder as well. The bidders using this strategy show certain lack of trust in the auction mechanism in general and want to keep the bidding process strictly under their own control. By being outbid constantly they have very often the option to leave the auction if they decide to change their mind. Therefore these bidders can be considered very risk-averse.

5.1.2.4. Stepped bidding. Stepped bidding is analogous to the evaluator strategy, but here the bidder makes many bids early in the auction with varying minimum rates. The idea is to deliver a demand schedule instead of just a single point on the demand curve. By making stepped bids the bidder can de<sup>fi</sup>ne how the bidding amount decreases as the interest rate decreases. As we saw earlier, many of the auction mechanisms similar to Prosper.com use full demand schedules instead of single bids. On Prosper.com this can be done only if the bidder makes simultaneously a number of bids with a small amount (usually \$50) and with varying minimum rates. However, the minimum amount of \$50 heavily constricts the building of the demand schedule. Adding just a few different minimum rates already increases the total bid amount signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to a higher level than the average (\$89). The stepped bidding strategy was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the data by looking at early bids, where the same bidder made multiple bids within 5 min. In addition, it was checked that the maximum excess decrement was larger than 0.3 percentage points. This ensures that the question is not about unmasking [22], where bidders make small bids in a short time period in order to determine other bidders' minimum rates or shilling where the seller, disguised as a bidder, tries to drive up the price incrementally.

The multi-bid strategies speci<sup>fi</sup>ed above are relatively unpopular at least when compared to the entire data. For example, the most popular multi-bid strategy, the last bid late strategy, makes up only 4.6% of all bidding strategies. In addition, there is still quite large a share of unknown bidders. These unknown bidders make multiple bids but not very late nor with extremely low decrements. The sensitivity analysis illustrates the matter further.

Making multiple bids cannot be considered a very ef<sup>fi</sup>cient strategy. In order to win, the bidder must make many bids and follow the auction constantly, which requires time and energy from the bidder. In terms of the winning ratio the strategy seems relatively good. The winning ratio (at least one winning bid) in this strategy is 51%, slightly higher than the average. However, if one takes into account all the bids made, the winning ratio is only 25%, implying that these bidders really have to work to win.

## 5.1.3. Bidding behavior in listings with different credit grades

Above we saw that only few bidders followed one speci<sup>fi</sup>c strategy at all times. People seemed to change their strategies over time. However, it is possible that bidders changed their strategies depending on what kind of listing they were bidding on. Listings with lower credit grades are naturally riskier than listings with high credit grades. In Fig. 6 the proportions of different strategies are charted with different credit grades. Indeed, the popularity of particular strategies seems to change gradually as the credit grade of the listing changes. Interestingly, the evaluator strategy is most popular with middle grade listings B–D. With high credit grade listings (AA and A) and with very low credit grade listings (HR) the proportion of evaluator bidders is lower. In the high credit grade listings, the opportunist and the portfolio strategies make up for the low number of evaluators. In the low credit grade listings, the late bidding strategy is clearly the substitute for the evaluator strategy. Apparently the middle credit grade listings are the easiest to value in advance. The competition seems to be too intense for the evaluator strategy in the highest credit grades. The low credit grades are unpredictable for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of the evaluator strategy.

Generally, the popularity of the late bidding strategy increases as the credit grade decreases, implying that bidders are more cautious with high-risk ratings and wait for information on other bidders' valuations until they make their own bids. The popularity of the sniping strategy increases too as the credit grade worsens. The popularity of the opportunistic and the portfolio strategies decreases as the credit grade worsens. The opportunistic strategy is risk-averse and bidders following this strategy seem to favor less risky listings as well. The risk involved probably explains the low popularity of the portfolio bids among low credit grade listings. Pro<sup>fi</sup>table investments in these listings require some case-speci<sup>fi</sup>c evaluation. The share of the multi-bid strategy increases slightly as the credit grade worsens.

## 6. Sensitivity analysis for bidding strategies

The strategy categorizations were determined based on the general bidding data presented above. There are no strict natural limits that could be applied and hence a proper sensitivity analysis is needed.

The results of the sensitivity analysis for the single bid strategies are presented in Table 3. The sniper category is clearly sensitive to the time limit. The timing of bids is heavily skewed towards the end of the auction. The current limit is now the last 30 min, which comprises 3.2% of all the engagements. The results are well in line with the general bidding behavior on Prosper.com.

The late bidder category is also sensitive to the time limit. Currently the limit is set at 12 h, which means a proportion of 24.7% (including the snipers) of all engagements. The results are again in line with the general bidding behavior and the fact that people tend to bid late. The 24 h is too wide in order to claim that the bid was made intentionally late.

The opportunist strategy looked at bidders who made their bids with minimal excess decrements. With a 0.3 pp excess decrement the proportion of opportunists is 5.7%. By decreasing the limit to 0.1 percentages points, their proportion drops to 3.6%. If the limit is increased to 0.5 percentage points, the proportion of bidders increases to 8.1%. The average decrease between starting rate and <sup>fi</sup>nal rate in listings was 3.9 percentage points. The 0.3 percentage point limit constitutes 7.7% of the average rate decrease. This limit seems suitable since it is not too strict, but still makes it a distinguishable strategy.

The evaluator strategy has two components. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the excess decrement (for outbid bids) or rate decrease (for winning bids), which has to be large. Later in this section the term excess decrement is used for both outbid and winning bids. The second component is the early timing of the bid. The current limits are 1 percentage point excess decrement and 24 h before the end of auction. The proportion of bidders with these limits is 32.1%. If the requirement for high excess decrement is increased from 1 percentage point to 1.5 percentage points, the proportion of evaluator bidders drops to 27.5%. If the limit is decreased to 0.5 percentage points, the proportion increases to 37.8%.

The other component of the evaluator strategy was timing. Currently the minimum was set to 1 day before the end of the auction, but if the requirement is tightened to 2 days, the proportion of this strategy falls to 24.7%. The idea is that bidders need to make their bids early or at least not late. A true evaluator would research the auction, determine the minimum rate and make the bid. This can happen at any moment during the auction but chances of somebody entering the auction in the very last minutes unintentionally are very small. In that sense 12 h seems somewhat too short, since late bidding was determined somewhere around that time as well. However, the average duration of the auction was only 7.9 days; hence requiring 2 days is quite drastic as well. Probably most people do not see bidding between 1 and 2 days before the end of the auction yet as late bidding. As we saw earlier, the bidding activity starts really to accumulate somewhere between 10 and 12 h before the closing of the auction. The 1 day limit was also used by Shah et al. [22] in their de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the evaluator strategy.

## 7. Bidding behavior over time on Prosper.com

One of the interesting research questions in online auctions is to study how bidding has changed over time. The more people bid, supposedly the better they learn to bid. Furthermore, over time bidding should converge towards the best bidding strategies. The method used here is to compare bids made within the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months and last 6 months of our Prosper.com database. The datasets are quite asymmetric in terms of size, because there were only 779 completed listings made during the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months of 2006. In these listings a total of 66,172 bids were made. In comparison, in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months of 2008 there were 6669 completed listings with 1,145,971 bids made.

## 7.1. Changes in bidding behavior over time

Let us start with the timing of the bids. In 2006 (<sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months), people made their bids on average 3.02 days (median 2.03) before the end of the auction. In 2008 (<sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months), this <sup>fi</sup>gure had decreased to 2.47 days (median 1.39). The negative difference in the means is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p-value 0.0000).

At the same time, the bid decrements have increased. The average excess decrement in the <sup>fi</sup>rst dataset was 0.85 percentage points (median 0.45 pp); in the second dataset it was already 2.10 percentage points (median 1.17 pp). This is an interesting result. People really seem to have built some trust in the auction mechanism and the average excess decrement has statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased (p-value 0.0000). Apparently bidders have seen how the bidding mechanism works and that there is little advantage in making two bids with small decrements compared to making one with bigger decrement.

People also make fewer bids in the same auction than before. In 2006 the proportion of multi-bidders was 23%; in 2008 only 8.6%. People have learned to trust the auction mechanism and see it more incentive compatible than before. Increased bid decrements also mean that bidders are not that easily outbid and there is less need for placing another bid.

One interesting background factor affecting bidding behavior might be competition among bidders. The winning ratio in the overall data is 46.7%. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst 6 months of 2006 the winning ratio was 49.0%. Interestingly the winning ratio in <sup>fi</sup>rst half of 2008 was even higher: 50.9%. If we look at the situation just before the credit crisis, which started in late spring 2007, the competition was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly harder. In January to March 2007 the winning ratio was down to only 40.1%. Apparently the credit crisis had increased the demand and/or decreased the supply of loans on Prosper.com.

## 7.2. Changes in the proportions of different strategies over time

The most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nding is that the evaluator strategy has become signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more popular as time has passed. As we can see in Fig. 7, in 1–6/2006 only 20% of all bidders used this strategy, while in 1–6/2008 the <sup>fi</sup>gure was as high as 35%. This is a clear shift toward incentive compatible bidding. It seems that people have, on the one hand, moved towards bidding late and, on the other hand, towards bidding early with large excess decrements.

The remaining <sup>fi</sup>ve bidders seemed to follow the same mix of strategies throughout their bidding activity. A common starting strategy (7/20) was the multi-bid strategy. They started with the multi-bid strategy, then changed to portfolio bidding and ended up using the evaluator or the late bidding strategies. Here we must remember that only 20 bidders were studied and therefore none of the results are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. However, this kind of strategy path would be very well in line with our empirical and theoretical <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Another interesting issue is the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in winning percentages. The lowest winning percentage is only 17%, whereas the highest winning percentage is 85%. On average the winning percentage in this data sample is 61%. In terms of winning, some bidders seem apparently to be much more successful than others. However, winning the auction does not guarantee pro<sup>fi</sup>t for the bidder because of possible winner's curse.

## 8. Conclusions

The predictions about bidding behavior provided by the auction theory do not seem to hold in real-life online auctions. Clearly bidding on Prosper.com does not follow any theoretically dominant strategy. There are many heterogeneous bidding strategies that are widely used. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is in line with [4,5,22]. Three main strategies seem to rise above the others. The most widely used was the evaluator strategy. Here the bidder makes one bid early in the auction with a minimum rate well below the current rate. The second widely used strategy was late bidding. The bidders using this strategy probably saw a heavy common value component in the Prosper.com auctions. They did not give out any information on their valuations for other bidders until the auction nearly closed. The third major strategy was multi-bidding. This is a kind of blend of the other strategies in the sense that it allows people to make early bids for signaling and other reasons, but they still bid late.

People seemed to adjust their bidding according to their learning experience. We saw that the late bidding and evaluator strategies had become more popular over time. Also the bidder level data seemed to support this view. From the bidder's perspective our conclusion is that it seems to make sense to withhold information from other bidders as long as possible and then make a late bid in the end. This approach also increases the chances of winning signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Furthermore, the bidders appeared to adjust their bidding strategies according to the listing in question. With high credit grade listings, the less frequently used strategies such as the opportunist and the portfolio strategies were emphasized. With middle grade listings the evaluator strategy was the most popular strategy. The popularity of the late bidding and sniping strategies was highest with the low credit grade listings.

Online auctions offer great potential for studying many interesting research questions. As we saw, traditional auction theory does not explain the complicated bidding behavior seen in online auctions. Thus, there is room for new theories and frameworks that could be used to analyze online auction mechanisms. From the empirical point of view, online auctions provide an excellent source of data, from which the academic community can bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

## 8.1. Practical implications for the auction design on Prosper.com

We suggest that a bidder should be given the opportunity to submit a demand schedule instead of individual single bids. In principle, by making stepped bids at certain intervals the bidder can already deliver a demand schedule, but this is restricted because of the minimum bid amount of \$50. Therefore, the bidders should have an option to decrease the amount gradually as the interest rate decreases. Auction theory suggests that the true valuation of the bidder is not just a single interest rate with some amount, but the amount is a function of the interest rate. By allowing bidders to enter demand schedules instead of just individual bids, the auction mechanism could be one step closer to incentive compatibility and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Another improvement suggestion is to consider applying the soft ending rule. Up to 5% of all bids were submitted during the last 30 min.

Learning could be taken into account in the auction design. Bidding on Prosper.com is easy. This is very important for new users. However, for experienced users Prosper.com could provide more advanced bidding tools such as the demand schedule. Prosper.com could also try to accelerate the learning effect by including strategic considerations into the introductory materials aimed at new bidders.

## 8.2. Future research

Asking the bidder to recognize and justify their bidding strategy among the ones presented in our study could provide us with interesting results. This could explain why people choose to use different strategies.

In addition, the bidding strategies should be studied in terms of their eventual success, i.e., <sup>fi</sup>nal rate of return. It is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to study whether the strategy has won the auction or not. Because of winner's curse, the bidding strategy could be traced back to the <sup>fi</sup>nal interest rate of the consequent loan. In addition, this interest rate could be adjusted for risk by considering whether the loan has defaulted later. Only after that could we truly compare the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability between different strategies.

A more technical research issue would be to develop a simulation platform for Prosper.com. Now that we have information on how people make bids on Prosper.com, it could be possible to simulate bidding. Giving suitable parameters for different strategies and blending them in the right proportions, the bidding behavior could be modeled mathematically. The simulation platform could be used in <sup>fi</sup>ne-tuning different auction parameters, such as duration, bid decrement, and minimum bid (see ref. [4]).
