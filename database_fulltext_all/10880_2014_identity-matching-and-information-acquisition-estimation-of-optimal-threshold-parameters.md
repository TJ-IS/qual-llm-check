## a b s t r a c t

With the growing volume of collected and stored data from customer interactions that have recently shifted towards online channels, an important challenge faced by today's businesses is appropriately dealing with data quality problems. A key step in the data cleaning process is the matching and merging of customer records to assess the identity of individuals. The practical importance of this research is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by a large client <sup>fi</sup>rm that deals with private label credit cards. They needed to know whether there existed histories of new customers within the company, in order to decide on the appropriate parameters of possible card offerings. The company incurs substantial costs if they incorrectly “match” an incoming application with an existing customer (Type I error), and also if they falsely assume that there is no match (Type II error). While there is a good deal of generic identity matching software available, that will provide a “strength” score for each potential match, the question of how to use the scores for new applications is of great interest and is addressed in this work. The academic significance lies in the analysis of the score thresholds that are typically used in decision making. That is, upper and lower thresholds are set, where matches are accepted above the former, rejected below the latter, and more information is gathered between the two. We show, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, that the optimal thresholds can be considered to be parameters of a matching distribution, and a number of estimators of these parameters are developed and analyzed. Then extensive computations show the effects of various factors on the convergence rates of the estimates.

## 1. Introduction

With the growing volume of collected and stored data from customer interactions that have recently shifted towards online channels, an important challenge faced by today's businesses is appropriately dealing with data quality problems. A key step in the data cleaning process is the matching and merging of customer records to assess the identity of individuals. In a variety of situations, organizations use high-speed searching and matching capabilities integrated into their processes to identify existing relationships and historical customer information, to aid in critical decision making applications. For example, our client <sup>fi</sup>rm in dealing with private label credit cards needed to know whether there existed histories of new customers within the company, in order to decide on the appropriate parameters of possible card offerings.

If the records to be matched pertain to people, the social security number (SSN) would be a highly desirable identi<sup>fi</sup>er. However, faced with the recent growing problem of identity theft, a number of states are actively enacting legislation that prevents businesses from seeking SSN information from applicants. More importantly, federal law restricts most businesses to collect SSNs only when a transaction requires the Internal Revenue Service to be noti<sup>fi</sup>ed, or when it is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial transaction that is subject to the federal Customer Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Program rules (www.privacyrights.org). Consequently, despite their importance in aiding ‘identity matching’, many businesses are forced to operate without such unique identi<sup>fi</sup>ers. This makes identity matching a challenge in situations such as cross-checking customer information with other data sources.

A viable approach to lower the risks associated with wrong decisions is to implement a matching algorithm. There is a good deal of commercially available software (e.g. www.datalever.com/matching.html, www.dataqualityapps.com) that provides record comparison capabilities to assess whether two records pertain to the same individual. In this work we do not focus on the design of such software, but focus on the best way to implement it, for their particular client database, as requested by our client company.

In the typical matching framework each new record (usually called an enquiry) is fed to the algorithm. For each incoming record, it <sup>fi</sup>nds the most closely matching record resident in the database and assigns it a score (Y). Without loss of generality, let the possible scores be denoted by $\pmb { \mathsf { S } } = ( S _ { 1 } , . . . . , S _ { K } )$ where $S _ { 1 } < S _ { 2 } < \cdots < S _ { K } ,$ , and higher scores indicate more likely matches. That is, scores can be considered to be proxies for the probabilities of true matches, although the actual relationship between scores and probabilities can only be determined based on the actual data in the company's database. That is, they will differ among different databases for a generic software. In many cases, for instance the experience of our client, Y is virtually universally unique in that it applies to exactly one resident record. (Later in this work we indicate how to deal with the case of multiple “best” matches.) These scores are then compared to context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c lower and upper thresholds $( S _ { \hat { L } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ , set by the organization, to determine how to proceed.

The resulting rules are of the following form: accept the match $\operatorname { i f } Y \geq$ $S _ { \hat { H } } ;$ reject the match if $Y { < } S _ { \hat { L } } { ; }$ and gather additional information if $S _ { \hat { L } } { \le } Y$ ${ < } S _ { \hat { H } } .$ . Notice that the rule is structured so that in the special case of a single threshold, e.g. where the cost of acquiring additional information is prohibitive, the solution is well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned when $Y = S _ { \hat { I } } = S _ { \hat { H } }$ , namely to accept the match. Also observe that the ‘hats’ over L and H represent the observation that organizations calibrate the appropriate thresholds for particular applications, through sampling from historical data. That is, they attempt to estimate the optimal thresholds $( S _ { L } , S _ { H } )$ , which can be considered to be parameters of the population probability distribution of correct matches given scores.

In this setting, the decision maker (organization) is faced with three fundamental expected costs, two of which are associated with errors. These are, the expected cost of incorrectly matching a new customer with an existing customer, which can be considered to be a Type I error, where the null hypothesis is that the records are not a match, and that of not being able to <sup>fi</sup>nd a match when the true matching record does exist in its database (Type II). The third expected cost is of gaining enough additional information to help determine the correct result. In general, that information could be stochastic (increases the reliability of the score) or deterministic (actually determines with probability one whether the two records match). In this work, we only consider the latter option and leave the former for future investigation. Thus the problem is how to balance these three costs, all of which were considered to be substantial by our client company. A number of factors create signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges in addressing these issues.

While a higher score generally indicates higher ‘match probability’, the relationship between score and match probability is context dependent (due to factors such as technology used for data collection, customer base, application setting, and business environment) and can be nonlinear. That is, an increase in strength may not imply a proportional increase in match probability. This is illustrated in Fig. 1, which shows two different possible mappings between score and match probability based on the application settings. For example, consider two possible scenarios, one where the customer base is predominantly young and mobile career professionals and the other where the customer base is older and more settled. In the latter case, deviances in address information are more indicative of a lack of a match than in the former. Assuming that ‘address’ is one of the <sup>fi</sup>elds used by the selected matching algorithm, the result of the two scenarios could reasonably be as depicted in Fig. 1.

Given such context speci<sup>fi</sup>city, organizations need to resort to calibration of the thresholds through a sampling strategy. In many business settings, such sampling can be a costly and time consuming manual endeavor, since it entails signi<sup>fi</sup>cant human effort to ascertain whether a pair of records that are matched by the software actually refers to the same individual. In our client organization, procuring sample data on about 3000 individuals was a six-month process that involved signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant investigative work, ensuring compliance with regulatory guidelines, and procuring legal consent from customers.

Consequently, a major challenge that arises in this context is the development of statistically robust estimators for $( S _ { L } , S _ { H } )$ , and is a central issue addressed in this work. In the remainder of the paper, we compare possible estimators and <sup>fi</sup>nd one to be dominant. We also investigate the statistical viability of the sampling strategy needed to effectively implement the estimation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a brief review of the literature on record matching. Section 3 presents and analyzes some possible threshold estimators. Section 4 presents concluding remarks and directions for future research, and supplementary computational results are presented in the two appendices.

## 2. Related literature

Here we review the literature on record matching algorithms and on acquiring additional information to supplement the interpretation of the resulting scores. However it should be emphasized that, to the best of our knowledge, there is no extant literature comparing possible threshold estimators through sampling and analyzing their statistical characteristics.

Record matching has been extensively studied [3,8,17,21]. Many solution models have been developed ranging from probabilistic record matching models [19] to distance-based techniques [4,11]. For example, a typical matching algorithm such as a distance based technique [6,16] expresses the similarity between two records as a function of the weighted sum of the distances between their attribute values. In the database community, the same problem has been termed ‘merge/purge’ which is centered on the removal of duplicate data when merging multiple databases. For example, in [9], a rule-based knowledge base is used to implement a solution. In machine learning, this problem is attacked by applying supervised learning techniques to record linkage when labeled data is available [18,20].

Much attention has been devoted to the problem of how to assign scores to the matched pairs. However, less has been paid to the problem of deciding ‘when’ to classify records as matches, given the scores [2]. In the FS-model [8] framework, two thresholds are provided to classify a record pair as a match, a non-match or a possible match. The region between two thresholds is referred to as the clerical review region.

These thresholds are determined by a priori error bounds on false matches and false non-matches. The false match rate is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of falsely matched pairs divided by the number of declared match pairs. Intuitively, the false match rate is very sensitive to the setting of the threshold levels. Belin and Rubin [2] introduced methods for false match rate estimation. They use training data sets reviewed by human experts, and provide a predicted probability of a match as a function of the weight. Armstrong and Mayda [1] also suggest an estimation technique for acquiring these error rates and provide modelbased estimators for record linkage error rates. The general problem of estimating these error rates is still an open problem, known as the regression problem [22].

Most recent research on data integration focuses on the problems caused by entity heterogeneity i.e. different representations of the same entity exist in different databases. Dey et al. [5] model the uncertainty inherent in the matching process due to errors in data collection, entry, and representation. A probabilistic model in attribute level is used to match entities from two different databases. They explicitly use the Type I and Type II error costs in their model. More recently, Jiang et al. [10] proposes a framework for integrating multiple data sources when a single ‘best’ value has to be chosen for every attribute of an entity.

The problem of optimally acquiring additional information to aid decision making has been widely studied [12–15]. Zheng and Padmanabhan [23] address the problem of determining how to selectively acquire additional customer data to build better models. Motivated by approaches in active learning, they address the problem of acquiring additional data to build better models than those which use only local data. Dey [7] approached the entity heterogeneity problem with a third possibility of referring a pair of records to a human expert for manual matching. Since human effort is costly, a heuristic cost minimization solution is provided. When the option of referring a record pair to an expert is exercised, it is assumed that the correct decision will be made. That is, the information is “perfect”. Our work complements and contributes to the literature by analyzing the statistical challenges that arise in calibrating the matching algorithms based on sample data.

## 3. Threshold estimation challenges and strategies

Again, we consider a 1–1 matching where a new record can only be matched with at most one record in the database, and that record pair has the highest score among the candidate records in the database. For the cases where the highest score is not unique, a simple option would be the following: If that score is low, simply consider all such records to be non-matches. Otherwise, the combined costs of gathering additional perfect information on all of these records would be balanced against the Type I and Type II error costs described below.

Let R denote the population probability of true match given $Y = S _ { i }$ and let $R = \{ R _ { 1 } , . . . , R _ { K } \}$ . Note that it is reasonable to assume $R _ { 1 } < R _ { 2 } < _ { \cdots } < R _ { K } .$ Let $C _ { 1 }$ and $C _ { 2 }$ be the costs of Type I and Type II errors respectively, as determined by the company. The expected <sup>fi</sup>xed price of acquiring additional perfect information is P. Then it is easy to establish $( S _ { L } ,$ $S _ { H } )$ as follows. For $S _ { L }$ , we balance the expected cost of a false non match against that of a false match and against P for all scores, i.e. $C _ { 2 } R _ { 1 }$ against P and against $C _ { 1 } \left( 1 - R _ { i } \right)$ . Then if R were known it follows that

$$
S _ {L} = \max _ {i = 1, \dots , K} \left\{S _ {i} | R _ {i} \leq \min \left\{\frac {P}{C _ {2}}; \frac {c _ {1}}{c _ {1} + c _ {2}} \right\} \right\}.\tag{1}
$$

Similarly, for $S _ { H }$ we compare $C _ { 1 } \left( 1 - R _ { i } \right)$ to P and to $C _ { 2 } R _ { i } ,$ so that

$$
S _ {H} = \min _ {i = 1, \dots , K} \left\{S _ {i} | R _ {i} \leq \max \left\{1 - \frac {P}{C _ {1}}; \frac {c _ {1}}{c _ {1} + c _ {2}} \right\} \right\}\tag{2}
$$

Example 1. For an example suppose $K = 1 0 , P = 3 , C _ { 1 } = 5 , C _ { 2 } = 7 ,$ and $( R _ { 1 } , . . . , R _ { 1 0 } ) = ( . 0 6 , . 1 1 , . 2 1 , . 3 7 , . 4 1 . . 4 2 , . 5 4 , . 6 1 , . 8 5 , . 9 3 )$ . Then $\begin{array} { r } { 1 - \frac { P } { C _ { 1 } } = 0 . 4 ; \frac { P } { C _ { 2 } } = 0 . 4 2 9 ; \frac { c _ { 1 } } { c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } } = 0 . 4 1 7 } \end{array}$ . Then it follows from Eqs. (1) and (2) that $S _ { L } = 5$ and $S _ { H } = 6 .$ . Note that it is possible that one or both of $S _ { L }$ and $S _ { H }$ may not exist, perhaps because P is very small compared to the error costs. Then it would follow that, without additional information, either no matches would be accepted or none rejected or both occur, i.e. the thresholds are irrelevant. Of course in the latter situation there would basically be very little reason for purchasing the software in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place. Our experience with practical scenarios is that both thresholds do exist, and we will implicitly assume that to be the case in the rest of the paper.

Let $F _ { i }$ denote the fraction of records in the population that have score $S _ { i } .$ The $F _ { i }$ can be determined with relative ease, since matching algorithms are implemented to run in real-time and can process large numbers of records in short periods of time. While, technically speaking, the $F _ { i }$ thus obtained are still ‘estimates’, the large datasets that are available to estimate these values make it reasonable to treat the obtained values as population parameters. Therefore, in our analysis we treat $C _ { 1 } , C _ { 2 } , P ,$ {F<sub>i</sub>} as known. Consider a sample of size N where the numbers of records with score $S _ { i }$ in the sample is given by $n _ { i }$ (generated in proportion to $F _ { i } )$ and let $r _ { i }$ denote the sample match probability given $S _ { i } .$ Clearly r represents an estimate of $R _ { i }$ and the $( S _ { \hat { L } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ derived from Eqs. (1) and (2) with $r _ { i }$ replacing $R _ { i } ,$ are estimates of $( S _ { L } , S _ { H } )$

Since $( S _ { \hat { L } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ are functions of the independent random variables $r = \{ r _ { 1 } , . . . , r _ { K } \}$ , a key challenge in developing ef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimators arises from the possible non-monotonicity of the $r _ { i }$ . While the $R _ { i }$ are naturally monotonic, i.e. $R _ { i + 1 } \geq R _ { i } , i \in \{ 1 , . . . , K - 1 \}$ , due to sampling errors this property does not necessarily hold for the r (see e.g. Fig. 2 for a possible example). This could create signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenges in sample design and threshold estimation. For instance, for Eqs. (1) and (2) to make sense we must have $S _ { L } \leq S _ { H } .$ . This can naturally be assumed to be true because of the monotonicity of the $R _ { i } ,$ but it may very well not be true if the $R _ { i }$ are replaced by $r _ { i }$ in Eqs. (1) and (2).

## 3.1. Threshold estimators

Here we consider four distinct estimators: Top–Down; Bottom– Up; Cost-Based (CB); and Maximum Likelihood-Based (MLB). Loosely speaking, the <sup>fi</sup>rst two consist simply of scanning down or up until, based on the sample probabilities $r _ { i } ,$ the optimal decision changes from match to purchase information or from purchase information to nonmatch or vice-versa. They are described more precisely below and, as also noted below, they are very susceptible to poor performance in the presence of nonmonotonicity. On the other hand, when monotonicity holds they produce the same thresholds as the other two more interesting estimators. Thus they will not be considered after this section.

## 3.1.1. Bottom–Up and Top–Down

The Bottom–Up estimator works as follows. The scores are scanned upwards, beginning at $s _ { 1 } ,$ , until the following occurs for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time, an index t is encountered such that $\scriptstyle r _ { t } \leq \operatorname* { m i n } \left\{ { \frac { P } { c _ { 2 } } } ; { \frac { c _ { 1 } } { c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } } } \right\}$ and $r _ { t + 1 }$ Nmin $\left\{ { \frac { p } { c _ { 2 } } } ; { \frac { c _ { 1 } } { c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } } } \right\}$ . If such a t exists then $S _ { \hat { L } } = S _ { t } . \operatorname { I f } S _ { \hat { L } }$ is found, the scan continues upward until the <sup>fi</sup>rst time an index w is found such that $r _ { w - 1 }$ bmax $\textstyle \left\{ 1 - { \frac { P } { c _ { 1 } } } ; { \frac { c _ { 1 } } { c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } } } \right\}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { r _ { w } { \ge } \operatorname* { m a x } \biggl \{ 1 - \frac { P } { c _ { 1 } } ; \frac { c _ { 1 } } { c _ { 1 } + c _ { 2 } } \biggr \} } \end{array}$ . As before, if such a w exists then $S _ { \hat { H } } = S _ { w } .$ The Top–Down estimator has the same criteria but begins at the top and works down. We include them here only to indicate that they are extremely vulnerable to nonmonotonicity and therefore of little practical value. For instance, in the monotone Example 1, if $r _ { i } = R _ { i } , i = 1 , . . . ,$ 10 then these two estimators would both <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal thresholds. But suppose there were incidents of extreme non monotonicity, such as $r = [ . 0 2 , . 4 3 , . 2 5 , . 3 7 , . 6 5 , . 6 9$ .72, .38, .85, .93]. Then both would <sup>fi</sup>nd $S _ { \hat { L } } = 2$ and $S _ { \hat { H } } = 9$ and most of the probabilities between the two thresholds would be ignored, leading to estimates that are unlikely to be accurate. (In this case the optimal thresholds are $\left( 5 , 6 \right) )$ . The computational experience to be presented later in the paper veri<sup>fi</sup>es that these estimators should not be seriously considered.

## 3.1.2. Cost-Based

The Cost-Based estimator (CB) simply enumerates all $K ( K - 1 )$ possible threshold pairs, calculates the expected total cost of each, and selects the pair that yields the lowest total expected cost. That is, it chooses $( S _ { \hat { L } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ to be the values of $\textstyle { \mathrm { ~ \mathcal { S } } } _ { a }$ and $S _ { b }$ respectively that minimize the function (3) below.

$$
C _ {2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {a - 1} n _ {i} r _ {i} + P \sum_ {i = a} ^ {b - 1} n _ {i} r _ {i} + C _ {1} \sum_ {i = b} ^ {k} n _ {i} (1 - r _ {i})\tag{3}
$$

Given that K is typically a relatively small number, the calculations involved are not onerous. Also note that this estimator ignores the question of whether or not nonmonotonicity exists. This leads us to consider the following estimator.

## 3.1.3. Maximum Likelihood-Based

Here we consider a rather unique departure in that we attempt to free the estimators from the limitations of relying blindly on the vector ${ \bf r } = ( r _ { 1 } , . . . . , r _ { K } )$ of sample probabilities. We <sup>fi</sup>rst note that, given the $\mathbf R =$ $( R _ { 1 } , . . . , R _ { K } ) , S _ { L }$ and $S _ { H }$ are uniquely determined. In the other three estimators we used r to estimate R, and then determine $S _ { \hat { I } }$ and $S _ { \hat { H } }$ based on r. The Cost-Based estimator still relies entirely on r but would seem to be less sensitive to its potential nonmonotonicity. On the other hand, if we assume that monotonicity is present in the population, and we have nonmonotonicity in the sample, it may be better to replace r with estimates $\mathbf { w } = ( w _ { 1 } , . . . , w _ { K } )$ that enforce monotonicity. That is the basis of the Maximum Likelihood-Based estimator (MLB).

Now, MLB is only maximum likelihood in that it estimates a vector R of parameters, subject to the monotonicity condition. We use MLB to <sup>fi</sup>rst calculate w and then $( S _ { \hat { L } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ are determined from w in place of r. Let x be the number of correctly matched records with score $S _ { i \cdot }$ Since x is Binomially distributed with parameters n and $R _ { i } ,$ it follows that for given values of R the likelihood of the observed $x _ { i } , i = 1 , . . . , K ,$ is given by

$$
L (R) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {K} \binom {n _ {i}} {x _ {i}} (R _ {i}) ^ {x _ {i}} (1 - R _ {i}) ^ {n _ {i} - x _ {i}}.\tag{4}
$$

Then we substitute w for R in Eq. (4) and add the monotonicity condition w $\geq w _ { i - 1 } , i = 1 , . . . , K .$ . In addition, due to the form of L(R), we maximize the natural logarithm, Ln(L(R)), rather than L(R) itself. It follows that the resulting optimization problem is

$$
\max \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {K} x _ {i} \ln (w _ {i}) + (n _ {i} - x _ {i}) \ln (1 - w _ {i})\tag{5}
$$

s.t.

$$
w _ {i} \geq w _ {i - 1}, i = 1, \dots , K\tag{6}
$$

$$
0 \leq w _ {i} \leq 1, i = 1,..., K.\tag{7}
$$

The problem (Eqs. (5)–(7)) is a rather simple nonlinear program with only bounding constraints on the variables. It is easily solved by any standard optimization software. Then, as usual, it is straightforward to obtain the threshold estimates from the costs $C _ { 1 } , C _ { 2 } ,$ , and P.

## Observation 1. MLB and CB always yield the same thresholds.

This observation is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by our computational experience. The proof follows from the following proposition, which basically says that nonmonotonicity does not damage the performance of CB since “nonmonotone points” will not be chosen as thresholds. De<sup>fi</sup>ne a nonmonotone (increasing) subset of sample probabilities to be of the form:

$$
\mathbf {r} ^ {\text { nonm }} = \left(r _ {q + 1},..., r _ {q + t}\right) \text {   where   } r _ {q} <   r _ {q + 1} <  ... <   r _ {q + t}
$$

Proposition. CB will not choose any member of a nonmonotone subset to be either $S _ { \hat { L } } \ : 0 \Gamma S _ { \hat { H } }$

Proof. Suppose any element $S _ { * } \in { \mathbf { r } } ^ { n o n m }$ is chosen as $S _ { \hat { H } }$ . Then consider moving $S _ { \hat { H } }$ to either $S _ { * - 1 }$ or to $S _ { * + 1 } .$ If the move is to $S _ { * } - 1 ,$ , the only score affected is that o $\therefore S _ { * } - 1$ itself. That is, the move would cause records with that score to be accepted, rather than calling for additional information. Thus the move to $S _ { * - 1 }$ would be pro<sup>fi</sup>table if $P > C _ { 1 } ( 1 - r _ { * - 1 } ) .$ If, however, $P \leq C _ { 1 } ( 1 - r _ { * - 1 } )$ it is pro<sup>fi</sup>table either to move $S _ { \hat { H } } { \mathrm { t } } 0 S _ { * } - 1 0 1$ not to have an ${ \cal S } _ { \hat { H } } \dot { 1 } \mathrm { f } \ast = { \cal K } .$ This follows because $r _ { * } < r _ { * } - 1$ so that $P < C _ { 1 } ( 1 - r _ { * } )$ . ## 3.2. Performance analysis of the Cost-Based estimator

Here we computationally assess the estimates $( S _ { \hat { H } } , S _ { \hat { H } } )$ produced by CB, and focus on the following questions, the answers to which will help a company determine the most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way to implement the matching software:

1. Does nonmonotonicity of the sample distribution of match probability vs. scores cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cant degradation of the estimator, in terms of variance, bias, and the probability of <sup>fi</sup>nding the true population thresholds?

2. Does the estimator perform best when that distribution is linear, as opposed to monotone but nonlinear?

3. How does changing the price P, of perfect information, impact performance?

4. What is the rate of decrease of estimator variance as a function of sample size?

In the main simulations, we set $K = 1 0 , F _ { i } = 0 . 1 , i = 1 , . . . , 1 0 ,$ and $\pmb { S } =$ $\{ 0 , 1 , . . . , 9 \} .$ . For the sake of generality we chose $F = 0 . 1$ as it represents a uniform distribution across the score. ## 3.2.2. Non-monotone samples

Here we investigate whether the performance of the estimators is adversely affected when the sample probabilities are not monotone. Tables 5–7 provide the summary results.

The clear take-away from Tables 5–7 is that estimator performance is not degraded by nonmonotonicity, but even in some cases may be positively affected by it. Note that for samples sizes of 5000 all cases based on R\_Linear were monotone. However, in the convex and concave cases, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the samples drawn continue to exhibit non-monotonicity even for large samples. In these situations, despite the persistent occurrence of non-monotonicity there is no discernible performance degradation. This indicates that organizations do not need to resort to any additional measures to ‘correct’ for these perceived anomalies.

## 4. Conclusions and future research

The practical importance of this research is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by a large client <sup>fi</sup>rm client <sup>fi</sup>rm that deals with private label credit cards. They needed to know whether there existed histories of new customers within the company, in order to decide on the appropriate parameters of possible card offerings. The company incurs substantial costs if they incorrectly “match” an incoming application with an existing customer (Type I error), and also if they falsely assume that there is no match (Type II error). While there is a good deal of generic identity matching software available, that will provide a “strength” score for each potential match, the question of how to use the scores for new applications is of great interest and is addressed in this work. That is, upper and lower thresholds must be set, where matches are accepted above the former, rejected below the latter, and more information is gathered between the two. We develop a method for determining ef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates of the optimal (minimum expected cost) thresholds. The latter can easily be incorporated into the statistical database of any company that needs to make matching decisions.

A number of issues deserve further investigation. A Bayesian approach to the problem can be developed to learn, in each instance, whether additional ‘imperfect’ information is acquired to dynamically calibrate the thresholds. Such a framework can integrate initial sampling decisions with information acquisition while the system is in use, to develop a holistic model that minimizes the overall costs of calibrating and using the matching software. While the current work has focused exclusively on identity matching, similar approaches can be developed for the more general classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problems. We are currently addressing some of these issues.
