# Group value and intention to use — A study of multi-agency disaster management information systems for public safety<sup>☆</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

This paper examines and extends the theory of information systems success in the context of large-scale disaster management (DM) for public safety. In the recent past, various evaluation reports on DM efforts have concluded that information quality and system quality are major hurdles for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective multiagency DM and are critical antecedents for information systems (IS) success. In contrast to the wealth of literature on IS success in pro<sup>fi</sup>t-oriented business environments, research regarding drivers of public sector IS success is scarce. This research develops and empirically tests a model that explains IS usage intention as a re<sup>fl</sup>ective measure of IS success in the public sector DM domain. In this paper, the effects of the expected value of IS for the entire group of collaborating DM agencies, task support, user satisfaction, and three speci<sup>fi</sup>c information/service quality dimensions on usage intention are examined. Data was collected from emergency responders using a questionnaire survey method during multi-agency, cross-national DM exercises at the Dutch–German border. The results of the data analysis revealed that expected group value is a key determinant of intention to use in the public sector DM domain. The data analysis also showed that perceived task support only has an indirect effect, through user satisfaction, on the usage intention. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that previously suggested IS success models for business environments are likely to fall short in their explanatory power and applicability for highly volatile complex disaster environments that require immediate coordinated responses from a large number of organizations. Possible directions for future research are also discussed along with other <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications.

## 1. Introduction

The use and performance of communication and decision support systems in disaster management (DM) have attracted the attention of researchers for over two decades [52]. However, it is only recently that researchers have started to emphasize decision support and information sharing across different DM agencies as well as the quality of the shared information. The main reason for the current emerging interest may lie in the fact that evaluation reports on some recent disasters, such as 9/11 [20], the SARS outbreak in Asia [45] and the <sup>fi</sup>re in the Schiphol Retention Complex [62], have revealed that poor information quality hampered the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of interagency disaster response activities. For example, during the response to the 9/11 attacks <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters were not able to receive the information about the WTC towers that police had [20]. Information about the structure of the WTC towers only became available to DM organizations several days after the disaster [20]. A common problem is the fact that although most of the data objects are available somewhere in the complex network, the processing of the data objects into relevant information accessible to the right person at the right time is missing.

Response to disasters, whether natural (e.g., <sup>fl</sup>oods, earthquakes) or human induced (e.g., terrorist attacks), is a complex process [11] that involves severe time pressure [51], high uncertainty [4] and many stakeholders [15], which results in unpredictable information needs [38]. In other words, DM operations are information-intensive activities [17,21]. However, most disaster responders have to cope with incomplete, unavailable and/or outdated information when a disaster strikes. Access to quality information is essential for DM agencies to decide and act under demanding conditions [31] since poor information quality can be lethal for both emergency responders and victims [27,58]. Accordingly, decision-making pertaining to the hazards of a disaster relies heavily on effective information sharing and coordination between a large number of autonomous actors including various DM agencies, private organizations, and civilians [9]. The challenges for individual or group decision support systems in disaster response situations are both diverse and massive. The people who must work together have no history of doing so, they have not developed a trust or understanding of one another's abilities, and the totality of resources they each bring to bear have never before been exercised [61].

There is a wealth of literature on information quality and information systems (IS) success in the pro<sup>fi</sup>t-oriented business environment. However, research on the drivers of IS success in the public sector where attaining the public good is often an important goal is relatively scarce. One exception is the work done by Fisher and Kingma [27], which underlines the criticality of IQ for DM. Public goods, such as public safety that this study focuses on, are delivered for the welfare of people (instead of for pro<sup>fi</sup>t), ideally in a non-rivaled and non-exclusive manner for all societal groups. In disaster response situations characterized by highly volatile and chaotic environments, decision makers are forced to make swift decisions based on limited and/or incomplete information. This can put them under an extreme amount of pressure due to the immediate and potentially catastrophic consequences for making the wrong decision. Therefore, previously developed models of IS usage and success in a business environment are likely to fall short in their explanatory power and applicability in public domains in general and DM in particular. Another gap in the IS literature is that testable hypotheses and empirical (especially quantitative) studies are relatively scarce in the DM domain. One practical reason for this may be the fact that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to collect empirical data during a real disaster since the situation is unforeseeable, dangerous, and may prohibit researchers from approaching the disaster site Furthermore multiple contexts events scope control and time related problems make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to collect data in disaster <sup>fi</sup>eld studies [36]. In order to close the gap in the understanding of IS success this research develops and empirically tests a model of IS success for the DM domain in the public sector. Following a recent study in the IS success area [65], we adopt emergency responders' IS usage intention as a proxy measure of IS success. We examine the effects of expected value of the IS for the whole group of collaborating DM agencies, task support, user satisfaction, and three speci<sup>fi</sup>c information/systems quality dimensions on the usage intention. For the empirical testing of our model, we analyze questionnaire survey data collected from professional emergency responders during a series of multi-agency, international DM exercises at the Dutch– German border.

This research is geared toward improvement of IS design for public sector applications. In the process of achieving this objective the study also enhanced our understanding of IS success in the multi-agency DM context. The contribution of this paper is twofold. First, the study extends previous IS success models by theorizing and empirically validating the effects of expected group value (as opposed to the value to an individual organization). With expected group value, the extended model can explain why some IS' don't work in public sector applications while comparably designed IS' work <sup>fi</sup>ne in private sector applications. The second contribution of the study comes from the examination of relevant information quality (IQ) and systems quality (SQ) attributes expected to play important roles in the DM domain. This domainspeci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge provides the researchers in the DM domain with an important empirical baseline for future research in addition to being used as a design guideline by practitioners (e.g., IS designer/developer) in the domain. The analytical framework and <sup>fi</sup>ndings can also offer useful insights into other domains with similar characteristics such as high pressure, multiple stakeholders, uncertainty, unpredictable information needs, and orientation toward public services.

In the following section the theoretical foundation of our model of disaster management information systems (DMIS) success is presented. The research design and method of the study are explained in a subsequent section followed by the results of the data analysis. The paper concludes with a discussion about the implications of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

This study builds on the previous literature in the IS success area that suggests various IS success measures and their antecedents. DeLone and McLean's model of IS success [22] has provided a foundational structure of IS success related variables. In DeLone and McLean's model IS success is measured at both individual and organizational levels and is determined by systems use and user satisfaction. In their model, systems use and user satisfaction fully mediate the effects of IQ and SQ on individual and organizational impacts. A multitude of IS related models have been derived from the DeLone and McLean model including various models of systems success, user satisfaction, technology acceptance and use, and IQ/SQ [48,67]. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is one of the extensions designed to predict acceptance and usage of new technology in an organization [18] and then is generalized in various contexts [63] based on socio-psychological theories of human behavior such as Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [26] and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [2,54]. DeLone and McLean [23] have also updated their model by replacing individual and organizational performances with net bene<sup>fi</sup>t. The updated model also distinguishes usage intention and service quality from systems use and systems quality, respectively. In this section we scrutinize and integrate the aforementioned theories to identify key variables that can re<sup>fl</sup>ect IS success in the public sector in general and DM in particular. In addition, we develop a model that hypothesizes the relationships among the key IS success variables and their determinants.

## 2.1. IS success in multi-agency disaster management

A recurring challenge in organizational IS success research is the ability to understand the linkage between IS and its impact on organizational performance. Au et al. de<sup>fi</sup>ne IS performance as “perceived outcome from IS use” ([5] p. 46) and argue that there are strong relationships between user satisfaction and intention to use. The majority of IS success studies in the past were conducted in the private sector business environment where decisions are made under less time–pressure and uncertainty in comparison to disaster situations [37]. Consequently, the focus of those studies was on realizing the potential of the IS in question to improve individual users' performance. An assumption in those studies is that the performance of an organization co-varies with the sum of the performance of individuals within the organization. For example, in the original DeLone and McLean model [22] individual impact was considered as the dominant determinant of organizational impact, while systems usage was suggested as a surrogate measure for IS success. Their updated model [24] does not distinguish between individual and organizational level impacts. It uses a single-construct performance measure (i.e., net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) that embraces various concepts such as cost and time savings, market expansion, increased sales, and reduced search costs. DeLone and McLean have also pointed out the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of isolating the contribution of IS from other contributors to organizational performance. This prevented attempts to measure the impact of IS on overall organizational performance. It is likely that this problem also exists in the public sector interorganizational IS context. The problem is even more serious in multiagency disaster response situations where IS is used to support not only individuals' primary responsibilities within his/her own agency but also the coordination of multi-agency DM operations that are focused on enhancing public safety.

DM environments are broadly conceptualized in this paper to include large-scale emergency response and crisis management for public safety<sup>1</sup> [42,46]. They have a couple of distinctive characteristics that set them apart from the typical IS success study contexts in the private sector. First, the management of disasters usually requires several autonomous public agencies to collaboratively mitigate, prepare, respond, and recover from heterogeneous and dynamic sets of hazards to society [35]. This paper primarily focuses on the response phase in the DM cycle. Second, almost every disaster involves a high level of novelty to deal with the unexpected under uncertainty and time pressure [33,49]. The IS use and the effects of the IS use on performance can greatly vary from one response situation to another. Multi-agency disaster response operations can be viewed as a combined cognitive, behavioral, and social activity of a group of participating emergency responders, which requires some degree of improvisation [40]. Accordingly, emergency responders must allocate their time and limited cognitive attention to two types of related yet not exactly aligned activities: 1) activities to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their primary responsibilities usually assigned by their own agency and 2) activities to support or coordinate with other agencies. One important problem is that individual responders often do not know, do not need to know, and/or are not able to know what all of the other DM agencies are doing. This fact in combination with severe time pressure and limited cognitive attention makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to understand how to improve overall group-level response effectiveness with their supporting activities. Some IS that are designed to support information sharing across organizational boundaries may increase the chance for a group of DM agencies to achieve a higher level of performance as a whole. However, it may not improve the individual level performance of every IS user for the aforementioned reasons. Indeed, individual level productivity may decrease due to the time spent on providing information to or sifting through irrelevant information from other agencies. Thus, the assumption that individual use of IS would improve group performance as well as individual performance cannot be taken for granted in the domain of this study.

## 2.2. Intention to use information systems

A sensible approach to measure IS success in a perplexing DM situation is to measure emergency responders' intention to use IS as an indicator of IS success (but not necessarily as a proxy measure of actual use) [65]. Emergency responders of various DM agencies are encouraged and sometimes are required to use IS provided by a higher authority [7]. However, the acceptance of new technologies may con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the self-interest of some of the <sup>fi</sup>rst responders. They may prefer to continue to use systems that they already know well [59] and/or have been designed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for their own tasks unless they can see a clear advantage of using the new technologies. The resistance of emergency responders can completely dismiss any potential contribution of a new IS [66]. Such resistance can occur at any level (e.g., individual, agency, group) and is often justi<sup>fi</sup>able unless the use of the IS has been <sup>fi</sup>rmly institutionalized. Therefore, it is important to study individual emergency responder's intention to use IS in order to approximate the success of IS for DM. In this study, we propose to consider intention to use DMIS as a criterion variable that represents an important aspect of IS Success for DM agencies and to examine several factors that can determine the intention. Fig. 1 illustrates our conceptual framework that adopts intention to use DMIS as the indicators for IS Success. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne actual constructs that represent the conceptual components and hypothesize their relationships in the sub-sections that follow.

## 2.3. User satisfaction

User satisfaction is an important component to measure IS success. It can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which users believe that the IS available to them meets their information requirements [6]. User satisfaction can also be conceptualized as an attitude about IS [67]. Nevertheless, user satisfaction as an attitude should be distinguished from the more widely known attitude concept which is the attitude toward a behavior in TRA/TPB-based models. Attitude toward a behavior is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual's positive or negative feelings about performing a target behavior [26] such as use of an IS, whereas general attitude refers to attitude toward a more general object (e.g., IS) associated with the target behavior. Wixom and Todd [67] termed these different types of attitude as behavioral attitude and objectbased attitude, respectively. One criticism of user satisfaction, when conceptualized as an object-based attitude, is the fact that it has a limited ability to predict system usage or intention to use in comparison to attitude toward systems use because of lack of speci<sup>fi</sup>cation on target behavior. According to TRA/TPB, attitude and behavioral belief measures should be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in a way that corresponds to the time, target, and context of the behavior of interest, in order to be a good predictor of the behavior or behavioral intention [3].

In our study user satisfaction is treated as an attitude toward an object (DMIS) but operationalized in a simulated context of target behavior. That is, emergency responders evaluate their use of IS in a multi-agency DM exercise designed to simulate a real emergency and then report their satisfaction with the IS in that particular context. Therefore, user satisfaction in this study represents both the evaluation of the IS and the evaluation of the usage experience with the IS. This is similar to the operationalization of user satisfaction in post-adoption usage behavior studies where user satisfaction shows a strong and direct positive effect on the continuous use of the systems [10,57]. In addition, behavioral attitude in many IT acceptance models has been considered as a core construct that can fully mediate the effects of various behavioral beliefs on behavioral intentions [53]. Based on the abovementioned discussion we hypothesize that:

H1. User satisfaction has a positive effect on intention to use.

## 2.4. Perceived task support

The primary objective of providing IS in organizations has been to provide support to their employees, so they can perform their tasks better. Task support, in this study context, is the extent to which IS helps or hinders the users' ability to perform the task requirement [6]. Task support has been one of the most important concerns in IS success and technology acceptance studies although there have been various terms to name the concept. Davis (1989) used the term “perceived usefulness” in his TAM and de<sup>fi</sup>ned it as the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance [19]. Thompson and Higgins (1991) used “perceived job-<sup>fi</sup>t” to measure the extent to which an individual believes that using a technology can enhance the performance of his or her job [56]. Goodhue (1995) used “task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t” to describe the degree to which a technology assists an individual in performing his or her portfolio. Similarly, Compeau and Higgins [16] used the construct “outcome performance” to measure task support, which is divided into performance expectation and personal expectation. Performance expectation refers to the performance related consequences of the behavior and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally deals with job related outcomes, whereas personal expectation refers to the personal consequences of the behavior and deals speci<sup>fi</sup>cally with the individual esteem and sense of accomplishment [16].

Fig. 1. Conceptual framework to understand IS success for disaster management.

Regardless of the term used the concept of task support has been found to be one of the strongest determinants of intention to use an IS or actual usage behaviors [63]. However, the role of task support in IS success models is unclear. A large number of models derived from the DeLone and McLean models do not incorporate the concept of task support. Goodhue (1995) found that some studies even presumed that better IS would lead to higher user evaluations regardless of the task [28]. This may be an incorrect assumption especially in the DM context where performance levels can easily be deteriorated by information overload or system use consuming scarce time. Goodhue and Thompson (1995) show that high task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t increases not only the likelihood of utilization, but also the performance impact of the system [29]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## H2a. Perceived task support has a positive effect on intention to use.

We also predict an additional effect of perceived task support on user satisfaction. User satisfaction is conceptualized as an attitude toward the IS and IS usage behavior in this study. A large number of previous studies have shown strong positive effects of task support or similar concepts (e.g., perceived usefulness) on user satisfaction [6] or attitude toward systems use [54]. Thus we hypothesize that:

H2b. Perceived task support has positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user satisfaction.

## 2.5. Expected group value

Various factors such as types of IS, management practices, organizational structure, operational/institutional environments, and the level of IS value can differentiate the impact of the IS on different levels of an organization [39]. In the IS success literature individual impact refers to “an indication that an IS has given the user a better understanding of the decision context, has improved his or her decision-making productivity, has produced a change in user activity, or has changed the decision maker's perception of the importance or usefulness of the IS” ([22] p. 69). Organizational performance, on the other hand, can be broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the overall performance of a <sup>fi</sup>rm including productivity, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, market value, competitive advantage, etc. [39]. While task support may account for a major portion of these individual and organizational level performances, there is another important dimension of IS impact that should be taken into account in the public sector multiagency DM context: IS impact on group performance for public safety. As discussed earlier, to a certain extent IS for individual emergency responders can also support coordination of multi-agency operations, which may not coincide with the interest of the individual's agency. Therefore, we propose to bring in the concept of expected group value to represent the belief about the positive impact or value of IS on the performance of the group of DM agencies collaborating as a whole in a particular disaster response situation.

A group task is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the behavioral requirements for accomplishing a stated goal of a group [69]. If a task for a group goal con<sup>fl</sup>icts with some private issues, such as the performance evaluation criteria within a member organization, the group member may try to rede<sup>fi</sup>ne the task to accommodate the con<sup>fl</sup>icting criteria. Likewise, if the group task cannot be properly facilitated by the provided IS, the task may be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the IS to <sup>fi</sup>t the task processes or functions to the existing technological structure, rather than the real needs in the group [69]. Furthermore, IS can induce changes in inter-organizational relationships, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence the nature of the IS itself [13,30,68]. A related concept is transformational bene<sup>fi</sup>t that refers to the positive results of changes in the structure and capacity of a <sup>fi</sup>rm [12]. Transformational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be an important component of the overall business value realized from IS use. When the scope of the transformational bene<sup>fi</sup>t is expanded beyond the organizational boundary (e.g., e-government, Supply Chain Management) IS can change the structure and processes of inter-organizational operations and improve the performance of the group of organizations as a whole. This is a typical situation observed in public sector multiagency DM operations [68].

We assume altruistic IS users in the public sector whose subjective utility derived from a public good (e.g., saving others' lives, helping other agencies, achieving group goals) is as high as, if not higher than the utility derived from an equivalent private good (e.g., ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the primary responsibilities for their own agency, achieving a higher performance level than others). Since expected group value is a belief about group-level consequences on a particular IS usage behavior we can anticipate a positive effect of the belief on user satisfaction. Nevertheless, the effect may not be fully mediated by satisfaction due to the characteristics of the object-based attitude [67] in our satisfaction measure. We also specify a positive effect from perceived task support to expected group value because the evaluation of IS support on individual tasks may be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the performance of the individual in two roles: one role for his/her own agency and the other role for the collaborating group of agencies. Listed below are the three hypotheses developed in the previous discussion.

H2c. Perceived task support has a positive effect on expected group value.

H3a. Expected group value has a positive effect on intention to use.

H3b. Expected group value has a positive effect on user satisfaction.

2.6. Information and systems quality

The concept of ‘quality’ is often de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “<sup>fi</sup>tness for use” [34] and is widely used in both business and IS related domains as a strategic and technological concept. However, in IS literature, quality itself is relatively “ill-de<sup>fi</sup>ned” [44]. The concept of quality is usually bound to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c object that it quali<sup>fi</sup>es such as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product, process, service, information, or IS. IQ is not a new concept in IS research as it has been widely accepted as a key antecedent of IS success [22]. IQ is a multidimensional concept that describes properties of the informational output of IS. It captures a wide range of attributes such as accuracy, timeliness, completeness, consistency, relevance and <sup>fi</sup>tness for use [64]. Miller [41] adds format, compatibility, security and appropriate amount of data as important variables for measuring the IQ. SQ has received less treatment than IQ in IS literature, and the components of SQ are often intermingled with dimensions that are closely related to service quality and ease of use [44]. These interrelationships make it more important to ensure conceptual clarity in the speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and distinction of constructs. SQ is used to evaluate multiple dimensions of the information processing system required to produce the output. The focus of SQ is therefore on the characteristics of the IS itself rather than on the characteristics of its resulting product [22]. The performance measures that are commonly used include system <sup>fl</sup>exibility, integration, response time, and reliability [44].

![](/api/attachments/CTV3AQQD/fulltext/images/5b50884cf82cd5360d75ea1d8d4dbdf43b135a92a6f1e324ef54f3edce007a8b.jpg)  
Fig. 2. A model of IS success for multi-agency disaster management.

Emergency managers have learned that accurate and timely information is as crucial as rapid, coherent coordination among the responding agencies [60]. Moreover, since DM operations are information-intensive processes [21], their effectiveness largely depends on the availability of necessary information [17]. A recent study on information attributes for DM revealed that only three dimensions of IQ and SQ were distinctively emphasized in recent large-scale DM situations: information accessibility, timeliness, and security [50]. We have focused on the <sup>fi</sup>rst two dimensions in the current study because our study context does not involve a measurable threat of information security, in which case the importance of information security is expected to decrease [50]. Both accessibility and timeliness have been suggested as important dimensions in emergency response [14,20,32,47], although empirical support is almost nonexistent. Accessibility refers to the ease with which information can be accessed or extracted from the system, whereas timeliness refers to the degree to which the system offers timely responses to requests for information or action [67]. With improved accessibility an IS user can see what others do. This will assist the users to coordinate their actions within the big picture and increase the chance to <sup>fi</sup>nd an answer to their own question from someone else's knowledge. Thus, accessibility will have a particularly strong positive impact on both group and individual level performance when a large number of organizations are involved in a collaborative operation. IS are often designed to meet certain criteria for response time for their particular purposes. No other IS needs stricter quality control for the timeliness dimension than DMIS. Regardless of the other dimensions of IQ and SQ, information not delivered in time does no good. It may lead to an inappropriate response in a volatile situation, which can seriously deteriorate the situation. Therefore, we test the following impact of the two quality dimensions on expected group value and perceived task support:

H4a. Accessibility has a positive effect on expected group value.

H4b. Accessibility has a positive effect on perceived task support.

H5a. Timeliness has a positive effect on expected group value.

H5b. Timeliness has a positive effect on perceived task support.

In addition to the accessibility and timeliness dimensions suggested as important dimensions for a wide range of disasters, we also include the relevance dimension in IQ [1] as a determinant of perceived task support. Relevant information is information that is “applicable and helpful for the task at hand” ([50] p. 288). A recent study [9] argues that emergency responders are greatly concerned about information overload and are afraid of being distracted by irrelevant information during emergency response operations. While information overload can occur, regardless of relevancy, when the amount of received information exceeds the processing capacity of the receiver, irrelevant information can easily cause information overload when the number of potential information sources (i.e., emergency responders) is large. Therefore, when a DMIS fails to <sup>fi</sup>lter out irrelevant information, the users' perception of its task support can be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreased.

H6. Relevance has a positive effect on perceived task support.

## 3. Research design and methodology

## 3.1. Multi-agency DM exercises and survey study design

This study adopts a survey method to collect data from professional emergency responders participating in multi-agency DM exercises. The studied exercises (labeled ROAR) took place in Spring 2008 as a part of Program VIKING,<sup>2</sup> an international <sup>fl</sup>ood/high-water management project between the Netherlands and Germany. The exercises were designed and supervised by the Province of Gelderland for the Dutch region and the Province of Nordrhein-Westfalen for the German region. The risk of major <sup>fl</sup>ooding is particularly high in these two provinces where the Rhine River <sup>fl</sup>ows through Germany into the Netherlands. The explicit goals of the exercises, as formulated by the exercise leader, were to improve multi-agency collaboration between

DM agencies on different echelons of response (i.e., strategic, tactical, and operational), information sharing between the Dutch and German DM agencies, information provisioning to the operational units during <sup>fl</sup>oods, and skills in using IS for <sup>fl</sup>ood control.

Three exercises were conducted over a three-day period involving various DM agencies, NGOs, and private organizations. Each exercise was focused on a different level, i.e. operational, tactical, and strategic echelon. The exercise participants were required to respond to a disaster situation as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in a scenario. The scenario was written in script format and was only known to the exercise organizers. Participants were stimulated to act via personalized (role speci<sup>fi</sup>c) instructions and short messages during the exercise. The scenario simulated a GRIP 4 level disaster, which is the highest level disaster according to the Dutch classi<sup>fi</sup>cation system [9]. The scenario contained multiple incidents including dike breaks alongside the Rhine River and a shipwreck spilling unknown toxic chemicals into the river. Uncertainty was built into the scenario with the inclusion of an unknown number of civilians and livestock present in the area as well as the time it would take before the entire region would be <sup>fl</sup>ooded. All three exercises used the same scenario and were organized in a relay structure. The <sup>fi</sup>rst exercise was for <sup>fi</sup>eld units primarily focusing on operational echelon response activities. The next exercise was the tactical echelon, which picked up where the <sup>fi</sup>eld units (operational echelon) left off. Finally, the strategic level exercise picked up where the tactical level left off. Hence, the information output of each exercise was used as input for the next higher echelon in the exercise. In this way each exercise was dependent on the information provided by the preceding one. During each exercise, the exercise organizers provided counter play to the echelon being trained. For example when the tactical echelon was being trained, the counter play decisions of the strategic echelon and actions/information needs of the operational level <sup>fi</sup>eld units were provided by the exercise organizers based on a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned script in addition to the results of the previous exercise. The exercise participants were not familiar with the exercise plot. They took the exercise seriously and were committed to achieve the exercise goals even though they knew a real disaster situation may develop in a completely different way.

The researchers administered a paper-based structured questionnaire to the participants of the tactical echelon exercise. This exercise involved the largest group of emergency responders from various DM agencies. All of the participants of this exercise made use of DMIS, which was not the case in the other exercises. It is not uncommon that emergency responders interact with multiple ICTs, directly or indirectly through an intermediary (e.g., dedicated operator) [8]. Such ICTs are often developed by different DMIS initiatives, and some of the ICTs may be integrated, redeveloped, and/or repackaged later by another initiative, making it very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to draw a clear boundary of IS in a DM situation. However, from an insider's perspective any ICT supported in a DM situation will be perceived as a part of DMIS. Therefore, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne a DMIS as an implicit mix of systems that consist of all of<sup>fi</sup>cially supported ICTs in the particular DM situation. In the case of our study this includes <sup>fl</sup>ood simulators, messaging, document sharing applications, geographical maps, etc. In order to provide a common reference frame, we listed the names of the <sup>fi</sup>ve major ICTs supported in the exercise (i.e., FLIWAS, Evacuation Calculator, HIS Viewer, CCS, and Flood Atlas) at the beginning of the questionnaire. Survey respondents were asked to indicate each of the ICTs they used and/or name the ICT if it was not one of the major ICTs listed. The exercise situations and participants were neither controlled nor manipulated by the researchers. The total number of the exercise participants was 51 (exclusive of 3 observers and 4 facilitators), which formed our survey sample. We ensured a high response rate (46 usable responses: 90% response rate) by administrating the survey immediately after the exercise organizer announced that it was time to end the exercise. The participants were allowed to leave the room for lunch and return later for a group evaluation. We made the decision to administer the surveys before the group evaluation in order to avoid any bias from collective re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the group. Completed questionnaires were collected at the exit doors to take advantage of the unique situation that these participants were taking time out of their daily work schedule to participate in the exercises. Since some of the participants were on call they had to rush out of the room before completing the survey. Although they promised to return the survey to the exercise organizers later we did not receive any surveys from them. This was in accordance with our expectations since most of the participants have high workloads and operate in a volatile and complex environment. Nevertheless, we did not notice any common characteristic that could cause a non-responder bias.

## 3.2. Measurement instrument and analysis technique

Measurement items were <sup>fi</sup>rst developed in English and then translated into Dutch. Originally we developed multi-item measurement instruments for the aforementioned constructs. However, we used a shortened version of the questionnaire that includes singleitem measures, primarily due to the tight exercise schedule. This allowed the participants to <sup>fi</sup>ll out the questionnaire in a short time frame, which contributed to a high response rate. In the original multi-item measures, most of the measurement items were adapted from the information/system quality (construct 1–3 in Table 2), IS adoption (construct 4), and IS success (construct 6 and 7) literature. The measurement items for expected group value (construct 5) were developed by reinterpreting multiple concepts in these areas and widening the scope to re<sup>fl</sup>ect public value. The original measures were tested and re<sup>fi</sup>ned using a separate, but similar, sample under similar conditions<sup>3</sup> in a previous study [9]. The single-item measures used in the current study were selected from the original multi-item measures based on how clearly they represented the underlying concepts in the studied exercise. The single-item measures are presented in Table 2. All items were measured in a 7-point interval scale with three anchors: 1=Strongly Disagree, 4=Neutral, and 7=Strongly Agree. It should be noted that the wording of the measurement items must be carefully selected and/or translated within the context of the studied DM activity. For example, the measurement item for expected group value used the term “participating regions” to represent the whole group of DM agencies participating in the regional exercise. The regional exercise included several safety regions in the <sup>fl</sup>ood-prone area lying across the Dutch– German border. This operationalization took advantage of the fact that the survey participants were well aware of the scope of the studied exercise (i.e., who they were working with as a group) by the time the survey was administered. If the survey is used in a different context the wording may need to be changed according to the conventional terminology used in the studied DM community as well as the scope of the DM operation.

The collected data was analyzed using a path analysis technique utilizing PLS Graph software. Path analysis is a regression-based technique that examines causal relationships between multiple, interrelated constructs [43], each of which is represented by a single-item measure or an aggregated measure [25,55]. Each path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates the portion of the standard deviation of the dependent variable for which the independent variable is accountable [43]. While the data analysis tool that we are using (PLS Graph 3.0) is capable of more advanced analyses (e.g., SEM), we have chosen path analysis as our analysis technique for three reasons. First, the subjects participating in our study were extremely busy and had very limited time to respond to our survey. Therefore we could not use the original multiitem measures required for a SEM technique. Second, path analysis can still examine multiple direct/indirect paths simultaneously [25]. Thus, a path model can easily be extended to a SEM model in the future. In addition, path analysis can be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and analyzed in PLS Graph enabling us to take advantage of its advanced functionality (e.g., bootstrapping) and optimization algorithms. Finally, path analysis techniques are subject to the same sample size requirement as multiple regression and partial least square-based (PLS) SEM techniques. Since the largest number of paths to one variable is three in our model, the sample size (n = 46) meets the minimum requirement (n=30) for our model analysis. Therefore, path analysis was the logical alternative for this research.

## 4. Data analysis and results

We <sup>fi</sup>rst ran an ANOVA test to see if the four endogenous variables in our model were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the different types of DM agencies participating in the exercise. Due to the heterogeneous composition of the sample group, only two (police and <sup>fi</sup>re) out of the eight agency types surveyed had 10 or more cases. In order to make the group sizes comparable, we combined regional- and national-level water management agencies together as the Water Mgmt group. We also created an “Others” group, which consisted of medical services, municipality, province, army, and other/no answer. Table 3 shows the group mean values and F values for the four endogenous variables. The results indicate that there was no group that had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different mean value for any of the variables.

As mentioned in the previous section the data collected was analyzed by a path analysis technique using PLS Graph. Table 4 shows the correlations between the variables included in the model. All AVE values are 1 because every variable was measured by a single-item measure. The reliability and construct validity of the measurement model could not be examined for the same reason.

The results of the path analysis are graphically presented in Fig. 3.

The results of model testing indicate that most of the relationship hypothesized in the proposed model are observable in the collected data. The model exhibits very strong explanatory power, explaining over 55% of the variance in intention to use. However, there are also some unexpected results. First of all, perceived task support did not show signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct impact on intention to use (H2a: 0.094), while expected group value had a very strong direct impact (H3a: 0.529, pb .001). User satisfaction also showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect (H1: 0.287, pb.05) on intention to use. Both perceived task support (H2b: 0.342, pb.01) and expected group value (H3b: 0.347, pb.05) seem to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indirect effects, through user satisfaction, on intention to use. The two variables explained over 30% of the variance in user satisfaction. The hypothesized in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived task support on expected group value (H2c: 0.103) was also not observed.

In terms of the role of IQ and SQ dimensions, relevance to task (H6: 0.594, pb.001) and response timeliness (H5b: 0.238, pb.05) showed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effects on perceived task support, explaining nearly 50% of its variance. The positive impact of response timeliness (H5a: 0.255) and information accessibility (H5a: 0.217) on expected group value look substantive, but the effects were statistically non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in our dataset. Consequently, only 21% of the variance in expected group value is explained by the three hypothesized determinants in this study. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient from information accessibility to perceived task support (H4b: −0.035), which is negligible and in the opposite direction, suggests that information accessibility by itself does not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on perceived task support.

## 4.1. Reflection on the findings based on the supplementary qualitative data

In parallel to the quantitative data collection, the research team also collected qualitative data before, during, and after the surveyed crossborder exercises. Although the scope of the qualitative data collection was more general than that of the quantitative survey study, we could observe many instances and responses that support our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the quantitative data. Our qualitative approach employed three data collection instruments for this purpose: (1) ex-ante and ex-post document analysis, (2) semi-structured, opportunity-based interviews with exercise participants and the DMIS architects and (3) participatory observations. The documents analyzed included the exercise storyboard, script, project team meeting reports, DMIS speci<sup>fi</sup>cation documents, DMIS log <sup>fi</sup>les, and the of<sup>fi</sup>cial exercise assessment reports (two in Dutch, one in German) written by external auditors (consulting companies). Formal interviews were conducted with two of the three exercise leaders and the chief information technology architect prior to the studied exercises. The exercise participants (DM personnel) were interviewed in an ad-hoc manner whenever the opportunity arose during or after the exercises. The researchers observed all three exercises (i.e., operational, tactical and strategic echelon exercises) and listened to the debrie<sup>fi</sup>ng sessions after each exercise. In some cases where an IQ or SQ issue emerged we requested the participants to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the value of the DMIS both for the support of their own tasks and the tasks of the group as a whole. Regarding the motivation to use DMIS, the effects of task support and group value were clearly evident in interview responses gathered from the participants. However, the strong mediating effect of the attitude concept (i.e., user satisfaction) was not obvious in the qualitative data. For example, we observed that some of the exercise participants who were expected to share information using FLIWAS did not do so. When asked why, they often responded “I have a lot of other stuff to worry about and do not see the direct advantage of using FLIWAS for my own tasks.” The ‘other stuff’ here refers to agency and role speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks unrelated to cross-agency information sharing and collaborative planning. On the other hand, some frequent users of FLIWAS indicated that they used the ICT because “the system might contain valuable information for our [the participant'] organization and our colleagues on the other side of the border might need the information we have.” As one of the tactical level participants explained, “in the <sup>fi</sup>rst hour we rush to send out resources [e.g., <sup>fi</sup>rst responders, water pumping equipment, sandbags] to the disaster area. Once the <sup>fi</sup>rst responders start working, we get little feedback on what is happening in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Since we are remote from the disaster area, any information we can collect, from every possible channel, may help us develop a higher level of situational awareness. Knowing the fact that some agencies would use their proprietary systems rather than FLIWAS, I think a tool that can present information from all the different sources would save us a lot of time.”

The interviews with the exercise leaders and information system architects were very helpful in understanding the design principles and roles of DMIS in the cross-border DM initiative. As mentioned earlier, Program VIKING had an objective to improve emergency responses to large-scale <sup>fl</sup>oods that often span across the Dutch–German border. Consequently, most ROAR exercise participants shared the idea that helping others in the larger community of the same faith is also helping themselves. In addition, the geographic properties of the region demand that agencies from both sides of the border work together in <sup>fl</sup>ood management. An IS architect emphasized this as “Cross-border crisis management requires collaboration of several relief agencies from both countries. We have come a long way since we started our collaboration efforts. … It has been two years since we have developed a DMIS (e.g., FLIWAS) that can support DM agencies collaborating on both sides of the borders.” Nevertheless, the need for and capability of an ICT does not guarantee acceptance. This is especially true in a crisis situation which can be time limited and life threatening. As the IS architect noted “Each of these [participating] DM agencies has its own preference for DMIS [for its own responsibilities], and it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to enforce a single standardized DMIS.” Historically, every agency on both sides of the border developed their own set of ICT tools for dealing with a <sup>fl</sup>ood. Even though FLIWAS was the <sup>fi</sup>rst system to be used on both sides of the border, some of the agencies were not about to abandon their ICTs in which they had invested a lot of time and money. An exercise leader also added that “despite the expected value of FLIWAS for the participating agencies, we still need to gain the support of policy makers and individual emergency responders in utilizing FLIWAS for cross-border information sharing. … These cross-border exercises are meant to demonstrate the value of having cross-border ICT to the disaster management community [around the <sup>fl</sup>ood-prone region].” Some other interviewees also pointed out that it was far more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to encourage the tactical and operation level emergency responders to use FLIWAS compared to the strategic level responders. This is because they are often forced to deal with immediate threatening situations and do not have the opportunity to appreciate the role that cross-agency ICTs plays in minimizing the <sup>fl</sup>ood impact on the entire community (both Dutch and German DM agencies). One respondent complained that “they [tactical & operational level emergency responders] only focus on intraorganization information sharing instead of inter-organizational or cross-border,” even though their commanders on both sides acknowledged the value of using FLIWAS. As stated in the VIKING project brochure, the whole idea behind the VIKING consortium and FUIWAS is to demonstrate the value of cross-border information sharing and build on the results for more effective <sup>fl</sup>ood management.

## 5. Discussions

This study exempli<sup>fi</sup>es the distinctive climate in the public sector DM domain and bears important implications for IS success research. The results of the data analysis revealed that expected group value is a key determinant of intention to use in the public sector DM domain. On the other hand, perceived task support only has an indirect effect, through user satisfaction, on the usage intention. This means that user satisfaction fully mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of perceived task support. Since expected group value also has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on user satisfaction, perceived task support may not play a decisive role by itself in IS success in the DM domain. This <sup>fi</sup>nding calls for a wider scope that goes beyond the personal or organizational boundaries dominating most private sector IS research. It also calls for recognition of IS users' concerns for group value, which requires a different design and incentive mechanism for public sector IS to support multi-agency collaboration. Previously suggested models of IS success, including the revised DeLone and McLean model [24], fall short in explaining IS success in some public sector domains and should be extended to take into account the group-level value (public good) of interconnected IS. A postmortem analysis of the 9/11 response also emphasized that “public service clearly emerged as a community value, not just a government function” ([20], p. 60) and illustrated instances where some organizational processes (e.g., procurement procedures) were ignored in an effort to serve the public quickly and compassionately.

The four endogenous variables on the right side of the model (i.e., perceived task support, expected group value, user satisfaction, and intention to use) can all be considered as indicators of IS success. However, the analysis results strengthen our argument that a system that supports an individual user's tasks very well may not necessarily support multi-agency collaboration for group goals (e.g., the nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant link from perceived task support to expected group value). Furthermore, the relationships around user satisfaction suggest that individual users' satisfaction, which may be determined by both perceived task support and expected group value, does not fully mediate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of expected group value on intention to use. Thus, in the public sector, an expectation of public good may exert a stronger direct effect on the usage intention than users' satisfaction. It is also recommended that public sector IS researchers and practitioners either include multiple IS success measures or consciously select a measure that best suits the purpose of the research.

The IQ and SQ dimensions explain perceived task support relatively well compared to expected group value. It is understandable because they have long been developed and tested together in the private sector. The strong effect of the relevance dimension reiterates the serious concerns about information overload and distractions during the volatile disaster response phase. Regarding the determinants of expected group value, the two IQ/SQ dimensions explained more than 20% of the variance in expected group value, even when the effect of perceived task support was excluded.<sup>5</sup> However, their effects were statistically non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, which might have been caused by the small sample size. Therefore, there exists an urgent need to retest these hypotheses (H4a and H5a) using a larger sample. In addition there needs to be an effort to identify other determinants of expected group value, given the strong positive effects of the expectation on intention to use. Non-technical factors, such as understanding of other agencies' roles, cross-organizational business/operation processes, and political con-<sup>fl</sup>icts, may be good issues to explore. Dawes et al. [20] points out that most service organizations (e.g., police, <sup>fi</sup>re) have specialized structures, policies, processes, and incentives designed exclusively for each agency. This has contributed to the long-standing organizational and policy barriers to cross-agency information sharing and coordination. Therefore, technology itself cannot be a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition to solve the underlying organizational and data quality problems [20].

Practitioners in the DM area should also pay close attention to ways to leverage IS to improve their group-level DM performance. Based on the domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we argue that multi-agency DM systems should include roles and capabilities that can validate, <sup>fi</sup>lter, enrich and selectively forward information that is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for the right person at the right time in order to better support the various tasks that emergency responders carry out. “Getting the right kind of information into the hands of <sup>fi</sup>rst responders in a form that they trust and could use” ([20], p. 63) has been a major challenge. Unfortunately, policy makers and DM leaders often lack a sophisticated understanding of ICT capabilities and limitations, which may lead to the wrong assumption that new ICTs will solve all their problems [20]. IS designers of multiagency DMIS need to be able to recognize these challenges and limitations. In this way they will be able to implement information gateways and human-computer interfaces (HCI) that can assure relevancy and timely delivery of information for various DM personnel. This will result in enhanced interoperability and accessibility to information as well as ensure that the information does not end up as mere information overload. While many information gateways and interface roles may involve a human actor (e.g., information manager, orchestrator) [8], some capabilities may be implemented in automated elements (e.g., DSS, expert systems) positioned at major data collection points in the structure of a DM system. With regard to the importance of information gateway and interface functions, we observed visible differences in the cross-agency information <sup>fl</sup>ows when the level of training and authority of the information managers (i.e., human information gateway/interface) changed. The concept of well-versed “reserve corps” ([20], p. 64) who carry on their regular (small-scale) emergency responsibilities in normal situations but then are reassigned during a multi-agency DM operation to roles such as: information analyst, gateway, interface, etc. suggests a good solution to alleviating problems regarding training and authority.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research directions

As mentioned in the research design section this study includes several limitations. Some of the limitations were inevitable because the studied subjects were real DM personnel on duty, which limited the time we had to survey and interview them after the exercise. Indeed, the restrictions on our data collection were imposed not only for privacy and sensitivity, but also to prevent any vacuum in the emergency response capacity in the surrounding regions. Nevertheless, we managed to collect 46 usable survey responses, which are extremely valuable especially considering the lack of such data in the DM domain. The limited access to emergency responders also caused a weakness in our measurement instrument and analytical technique. In order to <sup>fi</sup>t the questionnaire in the given data collection time frame, we had to use a single-item measure for every construct in the model. Consequently, we could not use SEM techniques for data analysis, which leaves questions about the reliability and validity of the measurement model. While some of the practical restrictions and limitations could not be overcome, we tried to supplement the survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings by using qualitative data. Thanks to extensive collaboration with members of the VIKING consortium we were allowed to observe all of the exercises, interview information architects and participants, and were given unrestricted access to project documents. The researchers observed all three exercises, interviewed various DM personnels involved in the exercises, and examined the documentation of the exercises and Program Viking itself. This has led us to the conclusion that the path analysis results are in accordance with the rich qualitative data and <sup>fi</sup>eld experience.

The current study conceptualizes DMIS as a package of multiple ICTs, which includes voice communication systems, message exchange systems, geographic information systems (GIS), and <sup>fl</sup>ood simulation systems. The situation of having many individual systems is partly due to the lack of resources and authority to implement an integrated package that satis<sup>fi</sup>es all DM needs for all types of DM agencies. While the issue of integration is outside of the scope of this paper, we observed that none of the available ICTs was universally used by all participants in the exercises. Moreover, no agency had access to every ICT, which means that no agency had access to all of the information available. Therefore, the aggregation of multiple systems was necessary and appropriate in this study. However, such aggregation may cancel out some individual technology speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects. Future research may <sup>fi</sup>nd additional, possibly more speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects, by examining an individual or fully integrated IS. In doing so, different or even incompatible functionalities and information requirements for various stakeholders may be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Future research may extend this study in several directions. First, de<sup>fi</sup>ning IS success in multi-agency DM is a challenging, yet critical, prerequisite to evaluate and improve IS in this area. This study suggested intention to use as a re<sup>fl</sup>ective measure of IS success, but it is just one preferred measure rather than the best measure for all situations. The large number of stakeholders usually involved in multi-agency DM environments makes it extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to reach a consensus on the measure, as shown in our study results. In particular, the concept of expected group value may be de<sup>fi</sup>ned at multiple levels and in multiple dimensions, each of which will have a different impact on other DMIS success measures. Therefore, we need more studies that examine various alternative success measures and relationships among those measures. This will enable practitioners to make an informed decision when they need to measure their IS success or initiate a new IS development/adoption project. A search for other major determinants of expected group values is also an important and recommended direction for future research. Although the ANOVA result suggests that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference, in terms of the group mean values of the endogenous variables between the four types of DM agencies (see Table 3), it is still possible that the type of DM task can moderate the relationships between the variables. For example, information managers whose primary task is to support inter-agency information sharing and coordination may show a stronger relationship between perceived task support and expected group value (H2c) than some of the other DM workers. In a similar vein, an individuals' job satisfaction may differ depending on their roles in a DM operation (e.g., saving lives in the <sup>fi</sup>eld vs. relaying messages between different agencies without understanding their meaning). This may in<sup>fl</sup>uence their perception of the value of DMIS and thus acceptance of it. A follow-up study that validates these possibilities will provide a clearer understanding of the relationships between these variables. Replication studies with a larger dataset and measurement studies that test and re<sup>fi</sup>ne multi-item measures are also needed for rigorous future research in this area.
