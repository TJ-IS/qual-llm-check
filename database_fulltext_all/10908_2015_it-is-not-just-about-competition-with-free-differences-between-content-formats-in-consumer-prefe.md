# It Is Not Just About Competition with “Free”: Differences Between Content Formats in Consumer Preferences and Willingness to Pay

ABSTRACT: While consumption of content in offline formats continues to decline, many providers are still struggling to monetize their content online, because consumers’ willingness to pay (WTP) for content in online formats is low. The availability of free content on the Internet is often considered the primary reason for this issue. However, we hold that the lower WTP is also related to a lower appraisal of online formats per se. Using a conjoint analysis and the example of newspaper subscriptions, we explore differences in consumer preferences and WTP between offline and online formats. Our results show that after price, format is the secondmost important attribute of a newspaper subscription. While consumers still prefer the printed newspaper to any online format, WTP differs across online formats and is strongly associated with device ownership. Our study provides a novel understanding of the previously neglected factor content format and its importance for content providers.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: conjoint analysis, consumer preferences, content formats, digital device ownership, digital media, media industries, willingness to pay.

The Internet and the digitization of communication have affected content consumption patterns substantially. Consumers can access text, images, audio, and video through multiple devices whenever and wherever they want to do so. As a result, the consumption of content in online formats, such as websites, e-books, and apps, has seen major growth rates in recent years, while offline content formats, such as CDs or magazines, have become less popular [62]. This transition holds substantial challenges for the content industry as many content providers (i.e., organizations that create or acquire content to make it available to recipients) struggle to build sustainable revenue models around content in online formats [18, 60, 72].

On the one hand, advertising revenues, which have traditionally been a major source of income for many content providers, are lower online than offline [62]. Mobile devices’ smaller screen sizes have put further pressure on online advertising revenues. Although costs for online content formats are usually lower, these cost savings are often not sufficient to balance out the decreasing advertising revenues and thus increase the need for content providers to focus on other potential revenue sources. On the other hand, per unit sales of content have dropped, a development that was partly ascribed to illegal file sharing on the Internet [7, 21]. As a result, content providers have sought to devise new distribution strategies in order to cope with this issue [6, 67]. After a number of failed approaches in the past, many have recently tried to use freemium or metered model approaches to establish subscription-based services [8, 25, 35]. These are advantageous for content providers, since subscription revenues are more projectable and less volatile during economic downturns. However, subscriptions, particularly to online formats of newspapers and magazines, appear to suffer from an overall lack of willingness to pay (WTP), that is, the highest price a buyer is willing to accept for a service or a certain quantity of a good [75], for content on the Internet [14, 38].

Little is known about the determinants of consumers’ WTP for subscriptions to content in online formats. Previous research has cited the widespread availability of legal and illegal free alternatives as a potential reason for a declining WTP for online content [26, 67]. The availability of free content has been fostered by digitization, for instance owing to user-generated content, lower production costs, and more piracy [58]. Consumers may not be willing to pay for content if other alternatives are free, especially if content quality is difficult to judge or does not differ significantly. However, in our view, previous discussions have mostly neglected a second digitization-inherent factor: content format. Digital technologies have enabled content providers to offer their content in various online formats, as a substitute for or along with existing offline formats. Research has shown that consumers differentiate between tangible and intangible goods and that they often establish a strong preference for tangibles, which indicates a potentially lower WTP for online formats [68, 73]. Therefore, our study questions insufficient online revenues not from the perspective of competition with free options. Instead, we see format as a central driver for differences in consumer preferences and WTP for content.

Owing to the lack of rigorous previous research on content format, our study has an explorative character. Hence, the scope and the goal of this explorative study is not to claim or test any causal relationships but to gather rich insights into consumers’ appreciation of different content formats. In particular, we examine how consumers’ perceived value of content and their WTP change along with the content format. Digitization affects various content industries in different ways, and we use the example of newspaper subscriptions, because the newspaper industry is one of the content industries that is struggling the most with this transformation [19, 44]. Subscriptions are a core revenue source for newspaper publishers, and many of them offer their content in a variety of online formats. We use a choice-based conjoint (CBC) analysis to compare print newspaper subscriptions with subscriptions to several online newspaper formats and assess the importance of content format compared with other relevant decision criteria. By introducing a scenario in which respondents can choose between the different subscriptions that a single newspaper publisher offers, CBC makes it possible to measure customer preferences and WTP for different content formats, while it allows to exclude other influencing factors, such as competition with free alternatives. Since certain online newspaper formats can only be accessed from specific devices (e.g., smartphones and tablets), we also draw comparisons between consumers who own these devices and those who do not.

Our work sheds light on consumers’ evaluations of content in different formats and shows that WTP is strongly associated with the format in which content is delivered. A representative consumer sample increases generalizability and helps to provide results that have important managerial implications for content providers concerning the targeting and pricing of subscriptions to content in online formats, as well as alignment with offline formats. Appropriate content pricing across formats and distribution channels is not only seen as an essential basis for overcoming content providers’ structural problems and generating sufficient revenues online but also as a means to offer better-fitting products to consumers, that is, products with characteristics that strongly correspond to the consumers’ preferences [22].

## Conceptual Foundations

## Channel and Format Choice for Content Providers

Targeting the right marketing and sales channels to reach current and potential customers is a question that concerns all firms. This question has gained momentum, because the Internet has opened up new possibilities to approach consumers and interact with them. Which channels consumers prefer for gathering information and executing transactions depends on the relative advantages and disadvantages of each channel [13, 29]. For instance, online shopping enables consumers to save time and costs [45], but the ways to experience physical products in e-commerce are limited, and purchasing and consumption processes are interrupted by the product delivery [42, 78].

The introduction of the Internet as a distribution channel has affected the content industry even more. It is not only the way an interaction or a transaction is initiated, but also the product or service itself that can be fully digitized and thus reproduced at virtually no cost as well as instantly delivered [5]. Although the content industry has been subject to many studies in information systems research, marketing, media studies, and communication science, there is no common understanding of the concept of content as yet [53]. For the purpose of our study we define content as the representation of meanings or—speaking in the terms that McLuhan uses [52]— messages that are the subject of public communication. The basic forms of representation are text, audio, image, and video. Consuming content therefore means to read, listen to, look at, or watch content. Content providers, which are commonly referred to as media companies [39], are organizations that create or acquire content to make it available to recipients. This requires them to decide on the distribution channels, that is, the means to deliver the content to the recipient. Content providers also need to make a format choice decision by selecting and packaging the content in formats that meet the requirements of these channels. The content format specifies the appearance of the content to the recipient. For each format, content providers can employ different revenue models, such as advertising, subscriptions, or single-item sales, to monetize their content. Depending on the format, the recipient may need a certain (set of) end-user device(s) that enables the consumption of the content. There is a close interdependency between content format, the distribution channel, and the end-user device. The format has to adhere to the technological requirements of the channel and the device. While certain content formats (e.g., websites) can be accessed from several devices, others (e.g., apps) are technically or structurally bound to a few devices or even one device. To ensure compatibility with and between channels and devices, many formats have become have become standards. This has led to standard wars between similar formats [28]. When new technologies for content consumption appear, content providers need to define new formats [76]. We can classify formats according to the type of technology that is used to store or transmit content (Table 1). Within these technological boundaries, content providers can determine the appearance of the content.

The identification of promising content formats has become a crucial part of content provider strategies for two reasons: On the one hand, multiformat distribution often demands changes to the organization of content providers. Compared with offline publishing or broadcasting, online content formats exhibit different characteristics regarding the amount and type of content that can be distributed [71]. This may require additional competencies and the reengineering of internal workflows [27]. Likewise, channel choice—and thus also format choice—has a major impact on revenue generation. Each additional format that a content provider decides to offer incurs further costs. To maximize profits, content providers need to analyze each format’s revenue potential carefully [30]. To do so, content providers must assess consumers’ appreciation of the different formats.

## Content Consumption in Online Formats

Besides the content provider perspective, previous studies have explored how individuals perceive and consume content in online formats. Three current literature streams are related to this research field: differences in user experience between offline and online formats, use of digital devices for content consumption, and WTP for content in online formats.

Previous studies confirm that consumers show less involvement with content in online than in offline formats [68, 73]. However, it is still not fully clear how the perceptions of the same content delivered in offline or online formats differ. Tewksbury and Althaus [1, 69] conducted a multiday experiment that revealed different agenda-setting effects (i.e., readers ascribed higher importance to different topics) for the desktop website and the print edition of the New York Times. Conversely, a replication study by d’Haenens et al. [20] found no evidence for these effects, and Mitchelstein and Boczkowski [56] concluded that news consumption on screens does not differ considerably from news consumption on paper. These mixed findings indicate the need to further explore how consumers process and evaluate content in different formats and on different devices.

Content consumption belongs to the most frequent activities on the Internet and on mobile devices, such as tablets or smartphones [70, 81]. In their early stages, the use of such devices for content consumption was rather limited, with uncertainty about costs and low usability being among the obstacles to adoption [2, 77]. Mobile devices were used mostly to access news during commute [24]. By now, the increasing bandwidth of Internet connections not only allows ubiquitous access to any type of content but also facilitates content consumption in additional usage situations [57, 61]. However, there are very few research insights into content consumption on tablets and smartphones, compared with offline content formats. Chyi and Chadha [16] found that although smartphones and tablets were very likely to be used for news access at least once a week, desktop computers remained the most popular device for daily news consumption. The news consumption enjoyment level was still highest when reading the printed newspaper.

Concerning the monetization of content in online formats, consumers’ WTP remains low, although content consumption is one of the main activities online [80]. Sinha and

Mandel [66] as well as Chiang and Assane [12] measured a WTP of less than \$1 for downloading a song online. In a telephone survey by Chyi [15] among Hong Kong residents, only 22 percent of the respondents said they were likely to pay for news in online formats in the future. Among U.S. Internet users, 60 percent stated they were very unlikely to do so [17]. Lopes and Galletta [47] observed an average WTP of \$28.63 for an annual subscription to a fictitious sports-related website. In another survey, U.S. Internet users on average indicated a WTP of \$3.10 per month for news content in online formats [14].

These results give rise to the question of the potential reasons for the overall low WTP for content in online formats. Among the predictors that have been used to explain WTP are demographics, usage purpose and intensity, interest in the content, free mentality, convenience, expected benefits, and reputation [12, 26, 31, 47, 74]. While it is critical to understand these factors’ effects on consumers’ WTP, what these factors have in common is that they are difficult to change in the short term or are even out of content providers’ areas of influence. By contrast, the role of the format—although an integral part of the product in determining the value of content—has been neglected.

To the best of our knowledge, the only insights concerning the WTP for content in various online formats stem from Chyi [14], who found that paying intent and WTP for a printed newspaper were higher than for a desktop website or an app. Our literature analysis has revealed that no study has systematically investigated the format’s role in consumers’ evaluation of content and the device ownership’s impact on format preferences and WTP. Our research seeks to provide the first empirical evidence by integrating format as an attribute in a CBC analysis for newspaper subscriptions. This allows us to analyze the differences in WTP for printed and online newspaper formats.

## Research Methodology

## Choice-based Conjoint Analysis

The theoretical foundation of conjoint analysis (CA) comprises several assumptions [34]. The fundamental supposition is that a product consists of a set of attributes that can adopt various levels. Each of these levels can be assigned numerical values that express the amount of value this level provides to a specific respondent. In their entirety, these part-worths describe the preference structure of that particular person. The difference between the part-worths of the most preferred and the least preferred level is referred to as an attribute’s importance weight. The importance weight reveals an attribute’s impact in the overall preference structure. It is assumed that the respondent determines the total utility of a product configuration by adding the part-worths of its attribute levels [32]. This implies that the part-worths of different product attributes can compensate each other. CA reverses the composition process and deducts the part-worths of the attribute levels from the evaluation of the product as a whole.

Since its first applications in marketing research, CA has seen numerous advancements. In the early years of conjoint applications, the most common procedure required the respondent to rank or rate an artificially constructed set of product configurations, each of which specified every attribute under consideration [33]. However, these tasks require high mental effort, and it is difficult for interviewees to express their preferences’ strengths accurately. An alternative way to estimate consumer preferences, which more closely mimics actual buying decisions, is the analysis of discrete choices between product profiles. Discrete choice analysis has been put forward among others by Luce [49] and McFadden [51] and integrated into CA by Louviere and Woodworth [48]. The CBC gained popularity in the 1990s and became the most widely applied CA method [23, 63].

CBC is a suitable approach for our research objectives because it allows to evaluate the format’s role in the preference structure for newspaper subscriptions and to compare these preference structures between consumer groups, such as users and nonusers of smartphones [11]. At the same time, competition with free alternatives can be ruled out by not offering a free option in the choice tasks. Additionally, CBC can be used to measure differences in WTP for varying product configurations. In fact, pricing studies are a major field of application of CBC [63].

## Identification of Attributes

Our research focuses on the relation between content format, consumer preferences, and WTP. However, in order to provide a comprehensive picture, we needed to identify other potential attributes involved in consumers’ choice processes between different newspaper subscriptions [34]. To identify these attributes, we compared the product portfolios of all domestic daily newspapers that were already selling subscriptions in online formats. Since our study took place in Germany, we analyzed the publications as listed by the Federation of German Newspaper Publishers [9]. Currently, 108 out of 351 daily German newspapers charge for access to content on their website. Considering the formal requirements for formulating conjoint attributes, as noted by Hair et al. [34], we extracted four fundamental attributes of newspaper subscriptions, in addition to format, from our product portfolio comparison (Table 2).

Most important for our research, subscriptions to newspapers differ in the way the content is presented to consumers. Besides the print edition, we identified its digital facsimile (the so-called e-paper), desktop websites, mobile websites, smartphone apps and tablet apps as relevant formats for newspaper publishers. Websites and e-papers can be accessed from several devices, but apps can only be installed on a smartphone or a tablet. While e-readers were considered as mobile devices for news consumption, we omitted them in the interest of brevity, since first research results indicate that e-readers are less suitable for reading newspapers [40]. We integrated price as an attrbute that denotes the monthly fee for access to the content. Although measuring the utilities for different price points does not have much value on its own —because consumers will usually prefer lower to higher prices—it is necessary in order to calculate differences in WTP for different formats. The prices we observed in our product portfolio comparison ranged from €3.50 per month for access to a desktop website to €48.90 for a subscription to a printed newspaper for one month. Thus, we chose a price range between €2.00 and €64.00 for our CBC. Our observations further revealed that some newspaper publishers gave discounts to consumers who agree to longer minimum subscription durations. Consequently, we added an attribute named “subscription duration.” Furthermore, our analysis revealed that the German daily newspapers can be categorized according to the geographical area they covered and according to the journalistic style that they adopted [59]. The market comprised 343 local or regional newspapers and 8 national newspapers. Out of the 351 daily newspapers, 343 were broadsheets, and 8 were tabloids. Although the latter group is small in numbers, it accounted for 18.4 percent of the market in terms of circulation. To account for the role of content in the customers’ decision between different newspaper subscriptions, we incorporated the attributes geographical focus and journalistic style into our CBC.

## Implementation and Data Analysis

The survey was built using CBC Web from Sawtooth Software and consisted of three parts. At the beginning, a set of questions was posed about demographic data, ownership, and usage time of smartphones and tablets, as well as Internet and newspaper use. Subsequently, the respondents were introduced into a scenario in which they were told that a local newspaper publisher from their area was considering the launch of new products and thus needed information about their preferences for newspaper subscriptions. The attributes and their levels were explained as the different options that the publisher considered. The last part consisted of the CBC itself. The questionnaire’s CBC section comprised eighteen choice tasks, fifteen of which were randomly designed by the software according to the balanced overlap generation method and varied from one participant to the next [64]. Three fixed choice tasks were added manually to measure reliability and validity. In each task, the respondents had to choose from three full profile stimuli to ensure that they considered all attributes simultaneously and to increase the validity of the results [43]. A translated version of such a choice task is depicted in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Example of a Choice Task in the CBC Questionnaire

For conceptual and methodological reasons, we decided against providing the option to choose none of the stimuli. First, our study seeks to investigate the format’s effect on consumer preferences while ruling out competition from free alternatives. Providing a “none” option could have allowed participants to speculate about the availability of other kinds of product offers in the market and to consider these in their decisions. Owing to the intended heterogeneity of our participant sample (e.g. in terms of age and geographical region), it would have been impossible to control for these speculations and isolate the differences in preferences and willingness to pay between newspaper formats. Second, prior research has found that the “none” option is also used by respondents to avoid cumbersome decisions, especially if many choice tasks and complex stimuli are used [10]. This could have imposed potential biases and less reliable results owing to missing decisions between certain alternatives. We thus refrained from implementing a “none” option.

To estimate the individual part-worths, we applied the hierarchical Bayes (HB) method, which has proved to be more precise in estimating utilities than ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation [3]. The HB method further offers the possibility to compute part-worths from CBC data on the individual level [65], which was essential to compare consumer groups. Our HB estimation comprised 100,000 burn-in iterations and another 10,000 iterations to generate the parameter estimates. We analyzed the differences in preference structures depending on device ownership by splitting our sample into owners and non-owners of smartphones and tablets. After calculating the part-worths averages for each group, we used t-tests for independent samples to check whether the sample means differed significantly. To ensure that each respondent had the same impact when part-worth averages were computed, we rescaled the raw utilities from HB estimation using the zerocentered diffs method, which comprised two steps. First, from each part-worth, we subtracted the mean utility of the attribute for that respondent. As a result, utility levels for each attribute were centered on the zero point. Subsequently, the partworths were rescaled such that, for each respondent, the cumulated utility differences between the best and the worst levels across all attributes equaled the number of attributes times 100.

## Deriving Differences in WTP

Because the part-worths estimated in CBC are difficult to interpret, we translated differences in the utilities between the various formats into differences in WTP. To this end, we used a procedure proposed by Kohli and Mahajan [46], who suggest comparing the total utility of a certain product configuration with the total utility of a reference product, which serves as a threshold. The maximum price at which the focal product’s total utility is still above the threshold denotes the respondent’s WTP for this product. If only a single attribute of the reference product is changed, the difference in WTP between the reference product and the altered product configuration is the WTP for the change of this attribute.

This approach draws on three assumptions. Let $u _ { i t }$ be the total utility of product configuration t to respondent $i ,$ and $u _ { i R e f }$ the utility of the reference product to this respondent. The first assumption is that $u _ { i t }$ may be decomposed into the joint utility of all attribute specifications but price $\left. u _ { i t \right| - p }$ and the utility of a price level $u _ { i } ( \boldsymbol { p } )$ which is in line with the basic assumption of CA:

$$
u _ {i t} = u _ {i t | - p} + u _ {i} (p _ {t}).
$$

Furthermore, it is assumed that a consumer will switch to the product configuration that offers the highest utility. If ε represents an arbitrarily small utility value, consumer i would buy product configuration t if price $p$ is chosen such that the following condition holds:

$$
u _ {i t | - p} + u _ {i} (p) = u _ {i R e f} + \varepsilon .
$$

To check whether this condition holds, we needed to calculate the utility of various price points. Our CBC delivered utility estimates for six price points between €2.00 and €64.00. To derive the utilities for additional price points between these six points, we used linear interpolation. Let $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ denote two of the price points for which the utility is known. For any arbitrary price $p \in ( p _ { 1 } , p _ { 2 } )$ , the utility is given by:

$$
u _ {i} (p) = u _ {i} \left(p _ {1}\right) + \frac {\left(p - p _ {1}\right) \left(u _ {i} \left(p _ {2}\right) - u _ {i} \left(p _ {1}\right)\right)}{\left(p _ {2} - p _ {1}\right)}.
$$

If utilities of prices below €2.00 or above €64.00 were required, we also used this formula for linear extrapolation. In the first case, €2.00 and €4.00, and in the second case, €32.00 and €64.00 were used as $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ , respectively.

For the calculation of the differences in WTP, the selection of the reference product is very important. Kohli and Mahajan defined the reference product as the respondent’s “most preferred product among all product offerings in his or her evoked set” [46, p. 348]. In their case—students choosing between different apartments—the respondents’ reference products were the apartments they were occupying at the time. Our study used a reference product that is widely available and established in the market: a subscription to the print edition of a local newspaper in broadsheet journalism style, and the possibility to terminate the subscription within one month. To find an acceptable price for this reference product for calculating the

WTP differences, we inserted a thorough description of the reference product as well as an open contingent valuation into the questionnaire [36]. The average of the stated WTP in each consumer group was used as the price of the reference product in the WTP calculations.

## Results

## Data Collection and Sample Description

Before rolling out our survey, we provided the questionnaire to researchers from the fields of information systems and marketing, as well as practitioners, to conduct a pretest. The respondents were asked to complete the entire survey and provide qualitative feedback on whether the questionnaire was comprehensible and free of flaws. We received fifteen answers and consequently revised the wording slightly and adapted the design of the questionnaire. The final survey was conducted in March 2015 in cooperation with a market research company. Since our goal was to investigate preferences for newspaper subscriptions in different offline and online formats, and the use of the latter requires Internet access, the basic population encompassed the domestic Internet user population. Because content consumption on the Internet enjoys great popularity across all age groups, we were very interested in obtaining results of high generalizability and thus used a sample that was representative of the German Internet users concerning age and gender. In total, we received 508 complete answers, 2 of which were excluded owing to technical reasons. The demographics of the remaining 506 respondents are shown in Table 3. Of the participants, 51.38 percent were male and 48.62 percent female. The respondents were between eighteen and sixty-nine years old. Of these, 74.31 percent owned a smartphone, 46.84 percent a tablet, and 46.64 percent regularly read a printed newspaper.

## Measures for Reliability and Validity

To determine the reliability of respondents’ answers, two of the three fixed choice tasks in the conjoint questions were identical. Only the first of these tasks entered the part-worths estimation; the second was used as a “holdout” choice task to calculate the test-retest reliability [32]. In our sample, 87.0 percent of the respondents answered consistently to the identical choice tasks, which we consider fairly high and comparable to the values obtained in other studies [37, 54].

Additionally, we tested the predictive validity of the estimated part-worths by measuring the individual hit rate. To do so, the third of the three fixed choice tasks was inserted at the end of the questionnaire’s conjoint section as an additional holdout choice task. The hit rate denotes the percentage of times the answer to this last choice can be predicted correctly by the respondents’ part-worth estimates. In our study, 90.7 percent of the decisions could be successfully predicted, which is considered to be a high percentage [41].

## Discussion

We investigated how consumer preferences and WTP change along with content format, and how preferences and WTP are related to the ownership of certain devices. We observed that after price, the format of a newspaper subscription has the second-highest importance of all product characteristics. Content format is thus more important than the much-acclaimed journalistic style. It is also noteworthy that consumers ascribe low importance to the minimum subscription duration. Apparently, if consumers are interested in specific content and convinced of its appeal, they do not mind engaging in longer contractual periods. This is good news for content providers, since it paves the way for stable revenues, provided they convince consumers of their content.

Concerning the different content formats, our intergroup comparisons reveal that despite a number of online formats, some of which have already been established for years, the print newspaper remains the format with the highest utility for most consumers. This observation is in line with previous research, which showed that consumers enjoy the print version of a newspaper more than certain other online versions [14]. However, there are big differences in preferences for the print edition, as indicated by the highest standard deviation among all formats. Furthermore, this preference’s strength decreases with ownership of mobile devices. This could indicate that owners of mobile devices are generally more tech-savvy and already more familiar with the usage of newspaper formats that differ substantially from the traditional print format. After the print format, desktop websites have the second-highest (albeit significantly lower) part-worths. The preference for desktop websites is comparably stable, as reflected by the lowest standard deviation of all formats. The utilities of mobile websites and e-papers follow; despite their dissimilar appearance, both formats exhibit similar part-worths. While e-papers are digital facsimiles of the print version, the mobile website is often a graphically limited version of the desktop website. Tablets have large screen sizes and are well suited to display e-papers, but the e-paper format has fairly low utilities among tablet users. The average part-worths for smartphone and tablet apps are significantly higher for owners of the respective devices. Nevertheless, even within these groups, the part-worths of both app types are lowest among all formats. Apparently, in the current form, apps do not fulfill the requirements that consumers have for reading news on their mobile devices.

These insights are directly reflected in the assessment of consumers’ WTP. We found that the WTP for online newspaper subscriptions of any format is substantially lower than for the print edition. Our results indicate that the perceived value of content printed on paper and delivered to consumers’ doorstep is higher than the perceived value of the same content provided electronically. Lower involvement with online- than with offline-content formats could be one explanation for the lower WTP [73]. Across all consumer groups, the desktop website is the online format for which consumers are willing to pay the most, and all other formats follow accordingly, as presented in the discussion of the part-worths. The ranking of formats according to their part-worths does not change with device ownership, except for the e-paper format. Owners of mobile devices assign much smaller differences in preferences and WTP between the different formats. This is particularly due to the fairly low utilities that non-owners assign to formats that are suited to devices they do not own, and it indicates consumers’ lack of WTP for content formats they have not yet experienced. Hence, non-owners first need to experience these formats in order to develop a WTP for them. Content providers could foster this by equipping promotion booths with digital devices and content in specific online formats.

## Conclusion and Further Implications

Even many years after the first attempts to deliver content in online formats, a large number of providers are still struggling with the transformation of their revenue models into the online world. Lower advertising revenues online accompany a generally lower WTP for content online and constrict content providers’ options. While the availability of free alternatives has thus far been held responsible for the lack of WTP, our study took the content format as a possible cause and presented valuable explorative insights regarding the differences in consumers’ perceptions of online and offline formats. Specifically, we compared different product configurations for newspaper subscriptions and isolated the factor format, such that differences in the perceived value of these products can be ascribed to the way in which the content is presented to consumers.

We found that the perceived value of newspaper subscriptions is mostly not associated with content-related attributes (such as journalistic style or content scope) but mainly with price and content format. While the influence of price has been analyzed in previous research, content format as a factor has been neglected. We discovered that content format has the second-highest importance and therefore should be central to content providers’ strategies. Furthermore, the acceptance of newspaper subscriptions varies not only between offline and online formats but also across different online formats. We provided indications on the rank order of different content formats and found substantial differences between owners and nonowners of mobile devices with regard to their preferences and their WTP. Our results have important implications for content providers.

First, although content can easily be converted from one online format into another, content providers should not apply uniform pricing for all their online formats, but they should adapt pricing to the content format in question. The conjoint results for the WTP differences between subscriptions to the printed newspaper and to desktop websites were lower than the price differences we observed in the market. From a product value perspective, if consumers’ WTP for two content formats is similar, the pricing of these formats should also be similar, and thus existing price differences between these formats may need to be reduced. For formats with a low WTP, the additional costs of offering content in such formats must be considered. While charging lower prices for certain formats may open the floodgates to substitution effects, bundling formats of lower WTP with formats of higher WTP can be a means to achieve additional revenues while preventing substitution effects. Such bundling strategies can already be observed in practice fairly frequently, and it has been shown that bundling can facilitate charging consumers higher prices than they are actually willing to pay [4].

Second, for content providers who seek to establish a paid content revenue model on the Internet, it is encouraging that the desktop website proved to be the secondmost popular format for content consumption in almost every consumer segment. On the one hand, most content providers have already gained experience in the provision of this format, and thus, ongoing costs are presumably fairly low. On the other hand, content providers may target a broad audience using this format, since it is accessible from multiple devices. However, this also implies that if content providers offer content on their desktop websites for free (and seek to monetize website content through other sources, such as advertising), consumers will not be willing to sign up for paid content in other, lower-rated online formats either. Our results provide a first basis for a joint assessment of different content formats and their alignment within a multichannel strategy.

Third, to establish a sustainable revenue model, content providers need to understand their customers and evaluate in which contexts their products are used. Consequently, they need to choose the formats that best meet consumers’ needs and offer adequately priced products. While many consumers today expect timely and location-independent content delivery on several devices and in various formats, content providers need to know that not all formats are appreciated equally. Although many content providers used to have high hopes for content distribution to mobile devices [55], our results indicate that it is currently not possible to charge high prices for subscription-based news apps or e-papers. Nevertheless, many newspaper publishers still cling to the idea of providing a facsimile of the print edition for digital devices. Examining the usage habits of their existing and potential customers could help content providers to avoid investments in less promising formats and instead concentrate their resources on revenue-maximizing product offers.

## Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Despite taking the utmost care in the preparation and the execution of our analysis, this study’s results are subject to some limitations. First, we obtained a sample that was representative of the adult population of Internet users in Germany in regard to age and gender. Although the German market is to some extent representative of other European or non-European markets, there could be country-specific or cultural factors that have influenced our results. We therefore suggest that future researchers replicate our study in different countries.

Second, we conducted our analysis using newspaper subscriptions, because the newspaper industry is one of the content industries most affected by digitization. However, content formats depend on the content type in question, and thus consumers’ appreciation of different formats should be assessed for each content type individually. We leave this undertaking to future researchers and expect a close connection between content type, format, and device ownership to hold. Future research may also benefit from a closer examination of the fit between different content types, formats, and devices concerning consumers’ demands around design and functionality as underlying drivers for potential differences.

Third, for reasons of complexity, we examined the different newspaper subscription formats individually. However, many newspaper publishers offer their content across channels in bundles. For instance, subscribers to the print edition are often also granted access to paid content on desktop or mobile websites. To bundle their products successfully, however, newspaper publishers need to determine the profit-maximizing prices for their product packages as well as the stand-alone versions. This makes subscriptionbased content an interesting field for research on bundling strategies, which directly relate to questions of substitution and complementary intermedia effects [50, 79].

Fourth, our analysis is restricted to the comparison of part-worths and WTP means. Examining the actual distributions of the part-worths within certain consumer groups would yield a richer picture of consumer preferences and provide additional information for content providers. We invite future researchers to discern different consumer groups based on their format preferences and the underlying reasons for their choices. Such an analysis would open a link to other research on technology adoption, contingent on factors such as self-efficacy, habits and cultural effects.

Finally, our study focused on the analysis of how content format relates to consumer preferences. However, a content provider’s decision on content pricing should take into account more than just format considerations. Other factors, such as the competition in specific markets or for specific products, need to be considered so as to provide a competitive offer. In this respect, it is also possible that consumers still have binding obligations to another content provider or at least are influenced by their previous content choices. We therefore suggest combining our exploratory results with insights from other research and integrating different factors into a joint research model.
