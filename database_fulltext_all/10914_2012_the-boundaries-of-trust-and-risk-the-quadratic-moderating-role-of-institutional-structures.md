# The Boundaries of Trust and Risk: The Quadratic Moderating Role of Institutional Structures

David Gefen

LeBow School of Management, Drexel University, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19104, gefend@drexel.edu

Paul A. Pavlou

Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19122, pavlou@temple.edu

prevalent assumption in the literature is that trust and risk are always relevant in online marketplaces, and that there is always a need to build trust and reduce risk irrespective of context. Challenging this assumption, this study seeks to identify the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity in the context of institutional structures in online marketplaces. The perceived effectiveness of institutional structures (PEIS), defined as the extent buyers believe that appropriate conditions are in place to facilitate transactions with sellers, sets the boundaries of trust and risk by moderating their effects on transaction activity in a quadratic (inverted-U) fashion. Specifically, at the lower boundary condition of PEIS (among buyers who believe institutional structures are ineffective), the high situational uncertainty they perceive should make these buyers unwilling to become vulnerable to sellers, thus rendering trust and risk immaterial to their decision making. Trust and risk should also be immaterial at the higher boundary condition of PEIS (among buyers who believe institutional structures are very effective), because the insufficient situational uncertainty makes trust and risk irrelevant to these buyers’ decision making because of a lack of vulnerability. Only between these two boundary conditions (among buyers who perceive moderate levels of PEIS), and thus a moderate degree of situational uncertainty and vulnerability in the marketplace, should trust and risk have a significant effect on transaction activity. Data from 398 buyers on eBay’s and Amazon’s online marketplaces support the quadratic moderating role of PEIS on the effect of risk on transaction activity, but not on the effect of trust. Theoretical and practical implications on specifying the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk and understanding the direct and moderating role of institutional structures are discussed.

Key words: online marketplaces; trust; risk; institutional structures; quadratic moderating effects; polynomial regression; response surface methodology

## 1. Introduction

Online marketplaces are multibillion-dollar enterprises. There is a broad consensus by both academicians and practitioners that trust is always necessary and beneficial in online markets, whereas risk is harmful and necessary to reduce, presumably because trust and risk are always relevant in the buyer’s decision-making process (Kim et al. 2008). There is strong empirical support of the direct effects of trust and risk on transaction activity in online markets, including commercial sites (e.g., McKnight et al. 2002) and online auction marketplaces (e.g., Pavlou and Gefen 2005); see the electronic companion.<sup>1</sup> Accordingly, commercial websites and online auction marketplaces have heavily invested in trust-building tools to foster buyers’ trust and reduce their risk to enhance transaction activity and market liquidity. For example, online marketplaces, such as eBay and Amazon, have invested in a comprehensive set of institutional structures to facilitate buyer–seller transactions by striving to build trust and reduce risk from their marketplaces (Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Nevertheless, by ignoring the trade-off in the design of trust-building tools to optimize the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity, commercial sites and online marketplaces may have overinvested in institutional structures to build buyers’ trust and reduce their risk from sellers. Institutional structures are not only costly to build and maintain, but they also may not even maximize transaction activity by fully accounting for the effects of trust and risk.

This study challenges the academic literature and practitioner thinking, which have ignored the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity by assuming that they are unconditional and linear and not bounded by the institutional context. Specifically, this study posits that the institutional context moderates the effects of trust and risk in a nonlinear (inverted-U) fashion by making trust and risk irrelevant to the buyer’s transaction decision making and rendering their effects on transaction activity insignificant in some situations. The study focuses on the institutional context because people rely on institutional structures to create appropriate conditions for transaction activity in uncertain environments (e.g., Fukuyama 1995, Luhmann 1979), including online markets (e.g., Gefen 2000). Institutional structures are akin to “factors outside the relationship that make the decision significant and uncertain” (Mayer et al. 1995, p. 726). Hence, institutional structures shape the degree of situational uncertainty and vulnerability in the online marketplace; in doing so, they are proposed to determine whether trust and risk enter a buyer’s transaction decision making. This study thus seeks to specify the boundaries of trust and risk as they are determined by the institutional context.

There may be several reasons why the literature may have ignored the role of the institutional context in moderating the effects of trust and risk. First, the literature has focused on institutional contexts where trust and risk are likely to be influential and may have ignored contexts in which the effects of trust and risk are insignificant. This is perhaps because insignificant effects are often less interesting and such findings tend to remain underreported. Second, empirical studies may have focused on institutional contexts that were constant for all buyers, thus possibly not having enough variance in the institutional context to play a significant role in moderating how trust and risk influence transaction decisions. Thus the institutional environment may have been assumed to be uniformly perceived by all buyers to be equally effective, and thus not variable in its ability to moderate the effects of trust and risk. However, the effectiveness of institutional structures is likely to vary across buyers (Pavlou and Gefen 2004), thereby making the institutional context variable across buyers. Finally, complex moderating effects, such as those proposed in this study, are generally more difficult to empirically detect than direct linear effects, and the literature may have ignored higher-order, nonlinear effects, such as the quadratic moderating effects of institutional structures, that are proposed in this study.

Aiming to introduce the institutional context as an important moderating variable in determining the boundaries of trust and risk, we instead argue that buyers differentially assess the ability of institutional structures to facilitate transactions in online marketplaces, and buyers’ perceived effectiveness of institutional structures (PEIS)<sup>2</sup> is proposed to moderate the effects of trust and of risk on transaction activity. Our research question therefore examines the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk on transactions as these are moderated by the institutional context: How does PEIS moderate the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity in online marketplaces?

We argue that PEIS, the extent that buyers believe that appropriate conditions are in place to facilitate transactions with the community of sellers in the marketplace, besides its direct role in building trust in the community of sellers (Pavlou and Gefen 2004), should also moderate the effects of trust and risk on buyers’ transaction activity.<sup>3</sup> This is because PEIS reflects the individual assessment of situational uncertainty in the marketplace, i.e., the vulnerability each buyer perceives when transacting with sellers, which shapes whether trust and risk enter the buyer’s decision making process. At one extreme, among buyers who believe PEIS to be very low and thus feel high vulnerability, they should a priori be unwilling to transact, making trust and risk immaterial to these buyers’ decision making. At the other extreme, among buyers who perceive PEIS to be very high and who thus feel little or no vulnerability, there should be no need to rely on trust or risk, also rendering the effects of trust and risk immaterial. Only among buyers who are between these two conditions, and who perceive moderate levels of situational uncertainty and vulnerability in the marketplace, should trust and risk enter these buyer’s transaction decision making. These three conditions are herein proposed to form the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity.

By specifying the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity in terms of PEIS in online markets, this study contributes to the literature in setting the effects of trust and risk in a broader institutional context. In doing so, it challenges the assumption that trust is always necessary and there is always a need to reduce risk irrespective of context, thereby stressing the need to take into account the institutional context to fully understand the complex effects of trust and risk on transaction activity. The study aims to sensitize managers about the trade-offs in the design of institutional structures in online markets to develop institutional structures that help achieve the optimal effects of trust and risk to maximize transaction activity and market liquidity. Notably, by showing that buyers, on average, perceive institutional structures to be more effective than the peak point that minimizes the negative effect of risk and maximizes transaction activity, it warns managers that they may have overinvested in institutional structures, perhaps because of their eagerness to prevent uncertainty from their marketplaces. Accordingly, it introduces an important trade-off in the design of institutional structures in online marketplaces that might help achieve the optimal effects of trust and risk to maximize transaction activity and market liquidity. Second, by specifying the dual (direct and quadratic moderating) role of institutional structures in online marketplaces, the study extends the literature on understanding the complex role of institutional structures in online markets. Also, by explaining a higher degree of variance in transaction activity, albeit by a small margin (largely because of higher-order nonlinear effects), the study has implications for better predicting actual transaction activity and market liquidity in online marketplaces. Finally, by ex post showing that institutional structures not only focus on economic but also on social intangible aspects, the study implies that there may be an unrealized potential in the design of institutional structures in online markets that could be harnessed to take advantage of the effects of trust.

## 2. Literature Review

Trust is the willingness to be vulnerable to a trustee under conditions of situational uncertainty because of the belief that the trustee will act according to the trustor’s confident expectations (Mayer et al. 1995). The trustee may be an individual, organization, or members of a community, such as sellers in online marketplaces (Pavlou and Gefen 2004). In the context of online marketplaces, Pavlou and Gefen theorized that it makes more sense to assess trust in the community of sellers than in an individual seller because buyers seldom deal with the same seller more than once, and also because buyers generalize their trust and risk beliefs to the entire community of sellers in a marketplace. Therefore, trust is herein defined as the buyer’s willingness to be vulnerable to the community of sellers in the marketplace based on expectations that the sellers will adhere to appropriate rules of transaction conduct. Likewise, risk is defined as the belief in a potential of suffering a tangible loss, while transacting with the community of sellers in the marketplace.

Trust has a direct effect on transaction activity, which is justified by the intangible social aspects of the interaction (Blau 1964, Kelley 1979). Trust is important to the social aspects of human interaction because people expect others to treat them with responsiveness and respect (Gefen 2002). These intangible aspects, however, are often unenforceable, adding social vulnerability. However, a person can assume away this social vulnerability by trusting the other person to behave properly (Blau 1964). Risk also has a direct effect on transaction activity, but it targets the negative tangible economic vulnerability aspects of the transaction (e.g., Jarvenpaa et al. 2000, Pavlou 2003). The dual effects of trust and risk on transaction activity parallel the well-established assumption in the literature that buyer–seller relationships have both a tangible economic and an intangible social component (e.g., Clark et al. 1986, Tauber 1972). Both components have been studied in online markets (e.g., Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Indeed, the “nonpecuniary benefits of trading” were shown to double the trading time in online portfolio trading markets (Dorn and Sengmueller 2009, p. 591). Although the tangible economic characteristics of the transaction (i.e., purchasing products) are often viewed as the main component in online marketplaces because of their tangible economic focus, e-commerce studies (e.g., Pavlou 2002) support the need to also study the intangible social aspects of the transaction. In fact, the intangible social characteristics also determine the buyers’ transaction activity in online markets.<sup>4</sup>

## 3. Conceptual Development

Because PEIS reflects how vulnerable each buyer feels in regard to the situational uncertainty in the marketplace, our basic proposition is that PEIS should determine the effects of trust and risk on the decision to transact with the community of sellers in the marketplace. This logic builds on the seminal theoretical work of Mayer et al. (1995), who posited that to understand the effects of trust and risk, it is necessary to bear in mind the situational uncertainty and vulnerability of the institutional context. Although the institutional context has been identified in the literature to shape the level of vulnerability (e.g., Coleman 1990), to our knowledge, the role of the institutional structures and the associated vulnerability have not been studied in terms of moderating the effects of trust and risk.

To examine the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity within the realm of institutional structures, we view transactions as the decision outcome of a buyer’s process that is primarily influenced by trust and risk (Kim et al. 2008). The role of trust and risk in decision making dates back to the early days of trust research. Kelley and Thibaut (1978) and Thibaut and Kelley (1959) viewed decision making in social contexts as a cost/benefit analysis by a person who would only make a decision if the expected benefits outweigh the costs. In addition to reducing the costs of vulnerability, trust brings benefits on its own right because trust-based relationships add social value (Blau 1964) and reduce risk, which is an additional cost (Gefen 2004). Although many factors guide the decision to transact (Pavlou and Fygenson 2006), trust and risk are often shown to be the most influential variables on transaction decisions in uncertain environments, and the literature views trust and risk as the two primary determinants of the buyer’s decision to engage in online transactions (e.g., Gefen et al. 2003, Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky 1999, Pavlou 2003). This study draws upon the decision making under uncertainty literature to examine the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity in the context of the marketplace’s institutional structures. Trust and risk are proposed to vary in the degree of their effects on each buyer’s decision to transact with the community of sellers in the marketplace based on the level of situational uncertainty and vulnerability each individual buyer perceives in each online marketplace.

By reflecting the extent that each buyer believes that appropriate conditions are in place to enable transactions with the community of sellers in the marketplace, PEIS captures each buyer’s perceived vulnerability in the online marketplace, and it is proposed to shape whether and to what degree trust and risk enter her transaction decision making. Specifically, the role of PEIS as a proxy for vulnerability in the marketplace can be simplified into three extreme points (see Figure 1).

Buyers with very low PEIS levels (point 1) feel vulnerable as they perceive that the institutional structures lack the ability to reduce situational uncertainty. People in arguably equivalent situations (e.g., Luhmann 1979, Mayer et al. 1995) decide a priori not to transact because of their perception of high vulnerability. As Fukuyama (1995) explains, when the governing power is perceived to be ineffective in enacting appropriate market rules, people decide to refrain from any economic activity with strangers. Trust and risk are unlikely to enter the decision-making process of these buyers who perceive very high situational uncertainty. This is captured by point 1 (buyers with very low levels of PEIS), which is the lower boundary where trust and risk are not expected to have a significant effect on transaction activity.

At the other extreme, at very high levels of PEIS (point 3) are buyers who perceive very low situational uncertainty because of what they believe to be very effective institutional structures. These buyers should perceive little or no vulnerability because they believe their transactions are guaranteed by the marketplace’s institutional structures. With little or no vulnerability in mind, trust should be outside these buyers’ transaction decision-making process because trust is the “willingness to be vulnerable” (Mayer et al. 1995, p. 726). In economic cost-benefit analysis (Kelley and Thibaut 1978), people use trust to counterbalance risk. With little perceived vulnerability in mind, risk should also not be a factor in these buyers’ transaction decision-making process given the lack of situational uncertainty. Point 3 is the upper boundary where trust and risk are not proposed to have a significant effect on transaction activity.

This proposition for boundary points 1 and 3 extends the literature that has assumed both trust and risk to be influential for all buyers. Only between these two boundaries—that is, for buyers who perceive moderate levels of PEIS (point 2) and thus have modest levels of situational uncertainty and perceived vulnerability—trust and risk are proposed to enter these buyers’ decision-making process. This is perhaps roughly the range of situational uncertainty where the literature showed trust and risk to affect transaction activity (see Online Appendix 1). Point 2 is conceptually interesting and practically useful because it is the peak point that the effects of trust and risk are maximized, and the range of point 2 denotes where the literature has shown the effects of trust and risk to be significant.<sup>5</sup>

## 3.1. Moderating Role of PEIS on the Trust → Transaction Activity Relationship

PEIS, reflecting each buyer’s perceived vulnerability in the marketplace, is proposed to moderate the direct effect of trust on the buyer’s decision to transact. Trust comes into play as a solution to alleviate vulnerability (Mayer et al. 1995), and it is thus expected to enter a buyer’s decision making only when there is perceived vulnerability in the marketplace. Figure 2 shows a three-dimensional plot of the proposed effect of trust on transaction activity being moderated by PEIS with the superimposed arrows reflecting the nonsignificant (flat) effect of trust on transaction activity around points 1 and 3 (for buyers with either very low or very high PEIS), while the upward arrow in the middle reflecting the maximum positive angle of the effect of trust at moderate levels of PEIS around point 2 (for buyers with moderate PEIS). The area around point 2 is perhaps the area that the trust literature has shown trust to have its expected strongest effects on transactions. Figure 2 illustrates our proposition that trust does not always have a positive effect on transaction activity. The positive shape of the hypothetical graph in Figure 2 suggests that transaction activity is expected to be higher for higher levels of PEIS.<sup>6</sup>

As reviewed earlier, trust has a direct effect on transaction activity by alleviating the buyers’ social vulnerability. Social vulnerability goes beyond the economic aspects of the transaction. When institutional structures are perceived to be ineffective in ensuring an acceptable transaction conduct by sellers (Figure 2, point 1), such as buyers being treated respectfully, responsively, and courteously, buyers could be expected to avoid transacting with sellers altogether, as in other transaction settings (Zeithaml et al. 1996). This is consistent with the theoretical logic of Mayer et al. (1995). With buyers at point 1 having decided a priori to avoid the marketplace because of very high social vulnerability, their trust in the community of sellers should not be part of their decision-making process. This sets the lower boundary of the weak effect of trust on transaction activity for buyers with very low levels of PEIS. Furthermore, trust should be immaterial in the decision making for those buyers with very high PEIS levels who have strong confi dence in institutional structures to ensure the appropriate social conduct (point 3 in Figure 2). Because these buyers are certain that sellers will be forced to act in a socially appropriate manner because of effective institutional structures in the marketplace, these buyers should not perceive any vulnerability. Trust should not be a consideration in the buyer’s decision-making process because trust is immaterial when there is no perceived vulnerability (Mayer et al. 1995) or uncertainty (Gambetta 1988). Point 3 thus sets the upper boundary of the nonsignificant direct effect of trust on transaction activity. Only between these two boundaries (around point 2) among buyers who have moderate levels of PEIS to ensure but not guarantee acceptable social conduct by the community of sellers should the effect of trust on transaction activity be significant. This follows Luhmann (1988), who explained that for those people who have decided to trust in uncertain environments, trust comes into play as a way to overcome their perceived vulnerability. In sum, trust should not have a significant effect on transaction activity for buyers at either very low (point 1) or very high (point 3) levels of PEIS (because trust should not enter the transaction decision-making process of these buyers); however, trust should have a significant effect on transaction activity for buyers with moderate levels of PEIS (point 2), where trust is used to reduce their vulnerability. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> The direct effect of trust on transaction activity is weak for buyers with very low levels of PEIS (point 1), it is substantial for buyers with moderate levels of PEIS (point 2), and it is also weak for buyers with very high levels of PEIS (point 3).

## Risk → Transaction Activity Relationship

We also propose that PEIS should moderate the effect of risk on transaction activity. Similar to H1, the level of PEIS is proposed to determine whether risk will enter each buyer’s decision-making process to shape his or her transaction activity. Although risk was long shown to determine online transaction decisions (e.g., Kim et al. 2008), we propose that its effect will be significant only for buyers at moderate levels of perceived vulnerability (point 2). Figure 3 shows the hypothetical three-dimensional plot of the direct effect of risk on transaction activity. The superimposed arrows show the weak (flat) effects around points 1 and 3 and the substantial negative slope at moderate levels of PEIS (point 2).

At point 1, among those buyers who perceive the institutional structures to be ineffective in protecting them from tangible losses, such as having inadequate payment systems, these buyers should be unwilling to transact because they perceive inadequate protection from the economic vulnerability posed by the community of sellers in the marketplace. Whereas the literature has viewed risk as always being an important predictor of behavior intentions (Bauer 1960), a very high degree of situational uncertainty in the marketplace is proposed to make risk from sellers immaterial in buyers’ decision-making process. Viewing the decision to transact as a cost/benefit analysis (Kelley and Thibaut 1978), if the buyers at point 1 perceive very high situational uncertainty in the marketplace, such as fear of credit card fraud or unreliable escrow services, these buyers will not transact in the marketplace, irrespective of how risky they perceive the community of sellers to be. Indeed, extreme levels of economic vulnerability should render risk outside of a person’s decision-making process (Bauer 1960, Bettman 1973). This is consistent with Dowling and Staelin’s (1994) model, where risk ceases to be influential in decision making once the ability to suffer a potential loss is high enough to inhibit a transaction. Also among buyers who perceive very high levels of PEIS (point 3), risk should fall outside their decisionmaking process because they believe the institutional structures to effectively protect them from economic losses. Only between these two boundaries among buyers who believe the institutional structures to be moderately (but not extremely) effective, to protect them from the marketplace’s economic vulnerability (point 2) should risk enter these buyers’ decisionmaking process, and therefore, to have its peak effect on transaction activity. This is consistent with Dowling and Staelin (1994), who propose that risk must be most influential when the level of uncertainty is perceived to be modest. Summarizing the arguments for the three levels of PEIS (see Figure 3), we propose the following.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> The negative effect of risk on transaction activity is weak for buyers with very low levels of PEIS (point 1), it is substantial for buyers with moderate levels of PEIS (point 2), and it is also weak for buyers with very high levels of PEIS (point 3).

Integrating H1 and H2, whereas H1 deals with the quadratic moderating role of PEIS on the direct effect of trust on transaction activity—dealing with the intangible social aspects of the transaction and social vulnerability, H2 deals with the quadratic moderating role of PEIS on the effect of risk on transaction activity that deals with the tangible aspects and economic vulnerability. Nonetheless, both hypotheses are based on the logic of decision making under uncertainty across different buyers who differ on their level of PEIS (see Figure 1).

3.3. Direct Effect of PEIS on Transaction Activity In addition to its moderating role, PEIS itself is proposed to have a direct effect on transaction activity. The rationale for H3 is also based on the logic of transaction decision making under uncertainty. In uncertain environments, people rely on institutional structures to create appropriate conditions for secure transactions (e.g., Fukuyama 1995, Luhmann 1979). For buyers with very low levels of PEIS (point 1), the necessary conditions for reducing situational uncertainty and vulnerability in the marketplace to facilitate transactions are perceived to be too low. As in other uncertain settings (Fukuyama 1995), these buyers too should be reluctant to transact with sellers when they think the marketplace is not governed properly, thus not relying on PEIS to guide their transaction decision making. Hence, PEIS is unlikely to affect these buyers’ transaction decision making, because the minimal threshold of institutional guarantees is not reached. However, for buyers at point 2 who perceive moderate levels of PEIS and who believe that appropriate market conditions exist to facilitate transactions in the marketplace, PEIS should have a positive effect on transaction activity by helping buyers overcome their vulnerability. Hence, buyers at point 2 should be more inclined to transact with sellers than buyers at point 1 with low levels of PEIS. Although PEIS is expected to continue having an increasingly positive effect on transaction activity for buyers at point 3 (those with high levels of PEIS), this is not proposed to be the case. Rather, we propose diminishing returns to the positive effect of PEIS, because there is no added value from an increase in PEIS for buyers who perceive the institutional structures to be already effective. Therefore, buyers at point 3 should not have stronger intentions to transact with sellers than buyers at point 2 because all these buyers get a similar value from PEIS.

## 4. Research Methodology

## 4.1. Study Setting

Data were collected from eBay’s and Amazon’s online marketplaces.<sup>7</sup> These two marketplaces were chosen because they have similar institutional structures (see Online Appendix 2), despite being managed by different firms. Moreover, combined, these two marketplaces span more than 90% of the online auction share. Selecting reputable online marketplaces was a deliberate design choice, as we needed to have marketplaces with solid institutional structures so an adequate number of buyers would view them to be effective. Despite focusing on successful online marketplaces, buyers’ perceptions about their institutional structures span the full range of PEIS across the original 7-point scale with adequate variance (STD = 102) and coefficient of variation $( \mathrm { C O V } = 0 . 3 )$ making it possible to test our hypotheses without the need for data from less reputable marketplaces. Having data from less reputable marketplaces might have introduced an unavoidable exogenous variance with potential threats to internal validity because of the possibility of alternative explanations (Shadish et al. 2002). For example, variance in PEIS level across effective and ineffective online marketplaces may be because of different institutional structures, differences across buyers, marketplace size, differences in products, among other things. In contrast, eBay and Amazon have the same types of institutional structures (credit card guarantees, escrow services, feedback mechanism, market rules) (see Online Appendix 2), cater to similar buyers, have a similar size, and offer similar products. In fact, the level of PEIS on eBay $( \mu = \bar { 5 } . 4 5 )$ and Amazon $( \mu = 5 . 3 0 )$ is similar $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . In addition, other online marketplaces are either small or specialized, raising concern of internal validity because eBay and Amazon are large and broad based. Finally, online marketplaces with weak institutional structures are unlikely to foster a high volume of transaction activity, thus making it difficult to identify active buyers who are likely to continue transacting in the future (our ultimate dependent variable). Buyer demographics (i.e., age, gender, annual income, Internet experience) were indeed very similar across the two marketplaces.

## 4.3. Data Collection

Our study followed Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) data collection method by randomly extracting e-mail addresses from 800 eBay buyers and 1,600 Amazon buyers. A survey was e-mailed to these addresses; the e-mail explained the purpose of the study and assured the anonymity of the respondents. The surveys differed only in terms of name (eBay or Amazon) on the measurement items (see the appendix). All items were measured on standard Likert-type scales anchored at 1 = “strongly disagree,” 7 = “strongly agree,” and 4 = “neither agree nor disagree.” Actual transactions were measured as the total number of actual completed auctions (won bids) during the following year, as objectively obtained from each online marketplace.

To encourage participation, respondents were offered a monetary award of \$250 to be raffled among the participants and summary of the results. N = 122 complete responses were obtained (15% response rate) from eBay and N = 274 from Amazon (17%) for a total of N = 396 responses. Nonresponse bias was assessed by verifying that (1) the respondents’ demographics are similar to those of current online consumers and (2) early and late respondents did not significantly differ in their responses. Early respondents were those who responded within the first two weeks. The two groups for both marketplaces were compared based on their demographics (i.e., age, gender, annual income, Internet experience). All t-test comparisons between the means of the two groups for both samples showed nonsignificant differences.

The correlation matrix and average variance extracted (AVEs) of the study’s principal constructs are shown in Table 2. All measurement scales showed high reliability and evidence of convergent and discriminant validity. A confirmatory factor analysis showed that all measurement items loaded highly on their own factors and had much lower cross loadings (see Online Appendix 3).

## 5.2. Polynomial Regression Modeling

All hypotheses were tested with a polynomial regression model with transaction activity as the dependent variable using hierarchical analysis (Equation (1)) in a confirmatory fashion by relaxing the constraints for hypotheses falsification (Edwards 2001). The direct effect of PEIS was captured with a quadratic term across the three focal points in Figure 1:

$$
\begin{array}{l} Z _ {\text {TRANSACTIONS}} \\ = a _ {0} + b _ {1} X _ {\text {Trust}} + b _ {2} X _ {\text {Risk}} + b _ {3} Y _ {\text {PEIS}} + b _ {4} \left(X _ {\text {Trust}}\right) ^ {2} \\ \quad + b _ {5} \left(X _ {\text {Risk}}\right) ^ {2} + b _ {6} \left(X _ {\text {Trust}}\right) * \left(X _ {\text {Risk}}\right) + b _ {7} X _ {\text {Trust}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} \\ \quad + b _ {8} X _ {\text {Risk}} * Y + b _ {9} Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2} + b _ {1 0} X _ {\text {Trust}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2} \\ \quad + b _ {1 1} X _ {\text {Risk}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2} + b _ {i} C i + \varepsilon_ {0}, \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where $Z _ { \mathrm { T R A N S A C T I O N S } } =$ transaction intentions, $X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } =$ risk, $X _ { \mathrm { T r u s t } } = \mathrm { t r u s t } _ { \mathrm { . } }$ $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } =$ institutional structures (PEIS), and Ci = controls.

All measurement items were first standardized to reduce collinearity, and single-item vectors were created for each construct by averaging each construct’s items. Because PEIS is modeled as a formative construct, we created a single composite vector score using the weights of the formative model of PEIS based on Pavlou and Gefen (2004). Specifically,

$Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } }$ was modeled as a single vector created by the weighted measurement items of the PEIS indicators:

$$

$$

where $Y _ { \mathrm { f e e d b a c k } } = \mathrm { f e e d b a c k }$ technologies, $Y _ { \mathrm { e s c r o w s } } =$ escrow services, $Y _ { \mathrm { c r e d i t c a r d s } } = { \mathrm { c r e d i t } }$ card guarantees, and $Y _ { \mathrm { i n t e r m e d i a r y } } = \mathrm { t r u s t }$ in intermediary.

Because higher-order terms are susceptible to outliers, prior to hypothesis testing, we ran diagnostic procedures for detecting influential and high-leverage outliers—partial regression plots, studentized residuals, DFITS, DFBETAS for each variable, covariance ratios, Cook’s distances, and the diagonal elements of the hat matrix—following Belsley et al. (1980) and Bollen and Jackman (1990). Five cases (about 1%) that exceeded any of the minimum cutoff criteria using a very conservative approach were excluded. However, dropping these five cases did not dramatically alter the results. Indeed, “with very large samples, such as those typical of survey research, it is relatively unlikely that a few cases are responsible for the observed patterns” (Bollen and Jackman 1990, p. 511). The Durbin–Watson statistic of all the regressions were within accepted thresholds, indicating no autocorrelation problems. Moreover, there were no significant correlations among the measurement errors. Finally, multicollinearity was not a serious concern because the eigenvalues (around 1), condition indices (below 10), variance inflation factors (<3), and tolerance values $( > 0 . 6 0 )$ of all effects were all within acceptable limits.

Following Cohen et al. (2003) and Neter et al. (1990), the variables in Equation (1) were entered in the following order: first, the control variables (Block 1); second, the main independent variables (Block 2); third, the interaction effects of PEIS with trust and risk (Block $3 ) ; ^ { 8 }$ fourth, the quadratic PEIS term (Block 4); and finally, the cubic effects of PEIS (Block $5 ) . ^ { 9 }$ For comparison purposes, Table 3 reports the standardized regression coefficients.

## 5.3. Response Surface Methodology Analysis

To get a better understanding of the complex interrelationships among trust, risk, transaction activity, and PEIS, response surface methodology (RSM) (Box and Draper 1959, Edwards 1994, Myers 1976) was then used to analyze the three-dimensional surfaces that correspond to the proposed polynomial

Because only three variables can be shown in a three-dimensional graph simultaneously, the moderated effects of trust and risk on transaction activity by PEIS are shown on distinct three-dimensional graphs (Figure 4 for trust in §5.3.1, and Figure 5 for risk in §5.3.2). The response surfaces were created in $\mathbf { M a t l a b } ^ { \mathbb { B } }$ by modeling two polynomial regression equations (Equations (3) and (4)) with the highest $R ^ { 2 }$ variance explained, using the unstandardized coefficients of the polynomial regression model (Edwards 1994), which were shown in standardized form in Table 3 (Neter et al. 1990):

$$
\begin{array}{r l} Z _ {\text {TRANSACTIONS}} & = - 2. 9 6 2 + 0. 1 2 2 * X _ {\text {Trust}} + 0. 0 5 1 * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} \\ & \quad + 0. 0 3 0 * X _ {\text {Trust}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} - 0. 1 4 8 * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2} \\ & \quad - 0. 0 4 1 * X _ {\text {Trust}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2}, \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} Z _ {\text {TRANSACTIONS}} & = - 2. 9 6 2 - 0. 2 0 2 * X _ {\text {Risk}} + 0. 0 5 1 * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} \\ & + 0. 0 6 0 * X _ {\text {Risk}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} - 0. 1 4 8 * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2} \\ & + 0. 1 6 9 * X _ {\text {Risk}} * Y _ {\text {PEIS}} ^ {2}. \end{array} \tag {4}\tag{4}
$$

To interpret the response surfaces, three basic features are important (Edwards 2001): stationary points, principal axes, and slopes. (1) A stationary point is defined as the point at which the slope of the response surface is zero in all directions, corresponding to a maximum, minimum, or saddle point. The stationary point may theoretically be a peak point in which a desirable outcome (e.g., trust or transaction activity) is maximized or an undesirable outcome (e.g., risk) is minimized. The estimation of optimum conditions aims at identifying how to move toward or avoid such stationary points. (2) Principal axes are defined as two functions that intersect at the stationary point and run perpendicular to each other, with one having the least and the other the greatest rate of decrease. (3) Slopes along lines of interest on the $X - Y$ plot may offer useful information, such as low/moderate/high levels of PEIS. Lines of interest are common along the confirmation $( X = Y )$ and disconfirmation $( X { \bar { = } }$ −Y ) axes (e.g., Edwards and Parry 1993, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Nonetheless, the ${ \dot { X } } = Y$ and $X = - Y$ axes are of little interest to this study as PEIS does not necessarily match trust or risk and the difference between trust and risk is peripheral. Following Edwards and Parry (1993) and Edwards (2007), all variables were scale-centered by subtracting the scale midpoints (ranging from −3 to +3, corresponding to 1 and 7, respectively) to reduce multicollinearity and enable easier interpretation of the results in a standardized three-dimensional space.<sup>11</sup>

5.3.1. Response Surface for Trust → Transaction Activity Moderated Relationship by PEIS. Given that the stationary points and principal axes are not readily interpretable for third-order polynomial equations (Brown et al. 2011), we mostly rely on the slopes along lines of interest to examine the significance and valence of slopes across different levels of $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } }$ . Testing the significance level of the angle  of various curvatures along the response surfaces followed a bootstrapping approach (see Online Appendix 4).<sup>12</sup> The response surfaces shown in Figure 4 (Equation (3)) and Figure 5 (Equation (4)) only focus on the plots within the range of our data (Edwards 2001). The interpretation of the higherorder terms follows Edwards (2002) by plotting all hypothesized terms together (shown in the respective plots) while observing how the exclusion of each term changes the overall response surface.

Figure 4 shows a positively sloped concave surface for the interaction of trust and PEIS on transaction activity. The superimposed plane at transaction activity equals 0 reflects the region where transactions are likely to take place $( Z \ge 0 )$ . As noted earlier, only buyers with transaction intentions ≥4 on the original scale transacted during the next year. Because the stationary points are outside our data range, the local extreme points in our data range lie roughly along the $\dot { Y } _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0$ axis at $X _ { \mathrm { T r u s t } } = - 3$ (minimum) and $\bar { X } _ { \mathrm { T r u s t } } = 0$ (maximum). The local minimum (lower dot) is at $( X _ { \mathrm { M I N } } = - 3 , Y _ { \mathrm { M I N } } = - 3 )$ and the local maximum (upper dot) at $( X _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = + 3 , Y _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = 0 . 3 )$ . The first principal axis (superimposed on the XY plane) runs almost in parallel to the $X _ { \mathrm { { T r u s t } } }$ axis at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0 .$ , following the $\boldsymbol { Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } } = - 0 . 0 0 3 - 0 . 0 6 \cdot \boldsymbol { X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } }$ function. The steepest ascent along the principal axis is at $\alpha _ { Y = 0 } = 1 6 ^ { \circ } ( p <$ 00055 of the XY plane, which reflects the highest effect of trust on transaction activity.

The other lines of interest are at low $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = - 3 )$ and high $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = + 3 )$ levels of PEIS, also reflecting positively angled slopes $( \alpha _ { Y = - 3 } = 1 2 ^ { \circ }$ and $\alpha _ { Y = + 3 } = 1 5 ^ { \circ }$ of the XY plane $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , respectively, as shown by the superimposed arrows on both sides). The bootstrapping results for the difference in curvature among these three angles are not statistically different $( p > 0 . { \bar { 1 0 } } )$ , implying that transaction activity increases roughly linearly as trust increases, irrespective of the level of PEIS. The similar curvature across the entire PEIS spectrum implies that transaction activity is influenced by trust similarly across the entire range of PEIS. Thus the response surface in Figure 4 validates the polynomial results (see Table 4) that H1 is not supported. This indicates that the direct role of trust on transaction activity is not moderated by PEIS (neither linearly nor quadratically) and that there are no boundaries in terms of the role of PEIS on the direct effect of trust on transaction activity. This also implies that the direct effect of trust stays at nominal levels, and the effect of trust is not reinforced by PEIS to reach a presumably higher (or maximal) effect on transaction activity.

In terms of the direct effect of PEIS on transaction activity (H3), the response surface in Figure 4 is concave with moderate levels of PEIS (across the first principal axis at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0 . 3 )$ having the highest effect on transaction activity, while low $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } =$ $^ { - 3 ) }$ and high $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } \ : = \ : + 3 )$ levels of PEIS having an insignificant effect (thus only partially supporting H3). In addition to failing to fully support H3 for the area between points 2 and 3 (downward slope), the response surface also reveals an asymmetry in the quadratic effect of PEIS when comparing the upward slope from point 1 to 2 and the downward slope from point 2 to 3. The significant upward $( \alpha > 0 , p <$ 0001) slope $( \alpha = + 2 2 ^ { \circ } )$ between points 1 and 2 when PEIS increases from $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } - 3 \xrightarrow { - } 0 . 3 3$ is significantly $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ steeper than the significant downward $( \alpha <$ $0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ slope $( \alpha = - 1 2 ^ { \circ } )$ between points 2 and 3 when $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } }$ increases from $0 . 3 3  + 3$ (the maximum effect of trust on transaction activity occurs at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } =$ 0033). This asymmetric pattern implies that transaction activity increases more rapidly when PEIS increases from low (point 1) to moderate levels (point 2), but they reduce more gradually when PEIS increases from moderate to high levels (point 3).

5.3.2. Response Surface for Risk → Transaction Activity Moderated Relationship by PEIS. Figure 5 presents the function that shows the relationship between risk, PEIS, and transaction activity, which is a saddle surface. In saddle surfaces, the stationary points reflect the intersection of the lines along the steepest curvature, which are inflection points (slope initially increases and then decreases). Therefore the surface has two stationary (inflection) points along the first principal axis, one being a local maximum $( X _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = \hat { - } 1 0 . \hat { 1 9 } , Y _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = 0 . 3 3 )$ and the other a local minimum $( X _ { \mathrm { M I N } } = + 1 2 . 4 5 , Y _ { \mathrm { M I N } } = - 0 . 5 7 )$ (outside our data range). Yet, because both stationary points are outside our data range, the within-range maximum (top dot) $( X _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = - \mathrm { \bar { 3 } } , Y _ { \mathrm { M A X } } = 0 . 1 1 )$ and minimum (bottom dot) $( X _ { \mathrm { M N } } = + 3 , Y _ { \mathrm { M N } } = - 0 . 0 3 )$ levels of transaction activity correspond to the minimum $( X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } = - 3 )$ and maximum $\left( X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } ^ { - } = + 3 \right)$ levels of risk. The point that the minimum level of risk $( X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } = - 3 )$ maximizes transaction activity is at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0 . 1 1$ . This corresponds to 4.11 on the original 7-point scale of PEIS, which is lower than the mean value of PEIS at 5.32. This implies that the peak PEIS level that maximizes transaction activity is much lower than the actual mean value of PEIS.

The first principal axis follows the function $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } =$ $- 0 . 0 9 5 - 0 . { \dot { 0 } } 6 8 \cdot { \dot { X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } } }$ that runs roughly in parallel to the $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0$ line (represented by the solid line almost in parallel to the $\bar { Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } } = 0 ~ \mathrm { l i n e } )$ . The downward slope of the effect of risk has the greatest descent along the first principal axis (angle $\alpha = - 2 2 ^ { \circ } )$ , as shown by the superimposed middle arrow. However, in terms of the effect of risk on transaction activity along the two lines of interest (at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = - 3$ and $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } =$ $+ 3 )$ , the angle is not significantly different from zero $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ at either low $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = \dot { - } 3 ,$ , angle $\alpha = 4 ^ { \circ } )$ or high $( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = + 3 ,$ , angle $\alpha = 2 ^ { \circ } )$ levels of PEIS. These results imply that the direct negative effect of risk that minimizes transaction activity is significant only at moderate levels of PEIS $( - 2 < \dot { Y } _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } < 1 )$ (between 2 and 5 on the original 7-point scale), while it is no different from zero at very low or very high levels of PEIS $( - 2 > Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } > 1 )$ (either $< 2 \mathrm { o r } > 5$ on the original 7-point scale). These results not only graphically support H2, but they also specify the boundaries of risk (in terms of PEIS). Given that it is possible to specify when the slope of a response surface becomes significant (see Online Appendix 4), risk starts having a significant effect on transaction activity at rather low levels of PEIS (below $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 2$ on a 7-point scale) and stops having a significant slope at relatively moderate levels of PEIS (about $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 5$ on a 7-point scale). Interestingly, the mean value of $\mathrm { P E I S } = \mathbf { \bar { 5 } } . 3 2$ on the original 7-point scale of PEIS (shown as $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 1 . 3 2$ in Figure 5) is higher than $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 5 ,$ , where the effect of risk on transaction activity becomes insignificant.

Similar to Figure 4, the response surface is asymmetric around the first principal axis with a steeper decline from the ideal minimum point that maximizes transaction activity (point 2), as PEIS increases from very low levels (point 1) than when it decreases from the peak level (the contours on the XY plane reflect this asymmetry). The significant downward $( \alpha > 0 , p < 0 . 0 \dot { 1 } )$ slope $\dot { ( \alpha = - 4 1 ^ { \circ } ) }$ when PEIS increases from $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } - 3  0 . 1 1$ is significantly steeper $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ in absolute terms than the significant upward $( \alpha < 0 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ slope $( \alpha = + 2 7 ^ { \circ } )$ when PEIS increases from point 2 to $\hat { 3 } ~ ( Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } \colon 0 . 1 1  + 3 )$ . The asymmetry is consistent with the lopsided nature of the boundaries of risk (toward lower levels of PEIS from −2 < $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } < 1 )$ , which is consistent with the asymmetry in the quadratic effect of PEIS on transaction activity. Notably, the asymmetry in the quadratic role of PEIS could not be captured by polynomial regression analysis.

The response surface also reveals the levels of transaction intentions at which actual transactions are likely to occur (see Figure 5). As noted earlier, only buyers with transaction intentions $\geq 4$ on the original scale were likely to have winning bids the next year, which is denoted by the $Z = 0$ superimposed plane. Note that the minimum level of risk that transactions are likely to place at $Z = 0$ is at $X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } = - 1 . 0 3$ (around 3 on the original risk scale). Moreover, the ideal point that transaction activity is highest is at the local maximum at $Y _ { \mathrm { P E I S } } = 0 . 1 1$ , which corresponds to 4.11 on the original 7-point PEIS scale. This implies that it is not the higher the better for PEIS, further challenging the diminishing returns logic between points 2 and 3 (H3). Interestingly, the mean value of $\mathrm { P E I S } = 5 . 3 2 $ on the original 7-point scale is higher than the peak point (PEIS = 4011) that maximizes the effects of risk on transaction activity.

In terms of the type of effect PEIS has on transaction activity (H3), the surface in Figure 5 that runs almost in parallel to the principal axis is concave at low levels of risk, but it turns convex at high levels of risk. Adding to the logic of H3, the response surface shows an opposite effect at low versus high levels of risk. While these two opposite quadratic effects partially cancel each other out, there is still a significant quadratic effect because of the stronger quadratic function at the low levels of risk. As noted above, this is the region of interest because transactions are likely to take place at low risk $( X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } < - 1 )$ when transactions activity is high $( Z \ge 0 )$ . When splitting the data into low versus high levels of risk (cutoff point at $X _ { \mathrm { R i s k } } = 0 )$ , the effect of PEIS is quadratic on both subsamples, yet in opposite directions. These findings underscore the importance of the interaction between risk and PEIS to fully understand the complex interrelationship between risk and transaction activity.

In summary, Figure 5 shows that a quadratic moderating function of PEIS captures the effect of risk on transaction activity (supporting H2). However, Figure 4 does not reveal a nonlinear function, implying that a linear function best models the direct effect of trust on transaction activity (not supporting H1). The quadratic function partially supports the proposed effect of PEIS on transaction activity, at least between points 1 and 2, partially validating H3. Taken together, the response surfaces are generally consistent with the polynomial regression results (see Table 3).

## 5.4. What Determines PEIS?

PEIS has its origins in both the objective effectiveness (availability, convenience, and cost) of the marketplace’s institutional structures, each buyer’s own experiences with the institutional structures, and individual buyer characteristics (Pavlou and Gefen 2005). This is why PEIS was proposed to be the most relevant measure to capture the buyer’s own vulnerability perceptions from the community of sellers in the marketplace. To determine what predicts PEIS, we ex post ran a model using the study’s objective and subjective control variables (see Table 1). Table 5 shows the two regression models with (1) only the objective control variables (column 1) and (2) both the objective and subjective control variables (column 2). Table 5 confirms that PEIS is significantly determined by many objective and subjective factors, such as the comparison among the marketplace’s institutional structures $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , the buyer’s past transactions $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and particularly the buyer’s problematic past transactions $( \beta = - 0 . 2 3 , ~ p < 0 . { \dot { 0 } } 0 1 ) ;$ the buyer’s demographics (age: $\beta = - 0 . 1 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 5 ) ;$ the buyer’s assessment of his or her own experiences $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) ;$ and the buyer’s trust propensity $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Notably, the objective control variables explain 36.8% of the variance in PEIS, while the subjective variables increase the variance to 54.5%. This implies that both objective and subjective variables are needed to predict PEIS. The significant effect of the eBay versus Amazon dummy supports our assumption that PEIS varies across the two marketplaces despite both marketplaces having similar institutional structures in place (see Online Appendix 2).

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Summary and Key Findings

Extending the literature that viewed the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity to be significant irrespective of the institutional environment, this study identifies the boundaries of trust and risk in terms of PEIS in online marketplaces. This is theoretically useful by setting the role of trust and risk in a broader context that may limit their direct effects on the decision-making process. This study also shows that PEIS, besides its main role in building trust (Pavlou and Gefen 2004), also moderates the risk → transaction activity relationship (but not the trust → transaction activity relationship). The quadratic moderating effects of PEIS explains about 1.5 times more variance on transaction activity than the linear effects (after accounting for the control variables). PEIS itself also has a direct quadratic effect on transaction activity. The use of polynomial regression with RSM allows us to visualize the boundaries of trust and risk by graphically illustrating the moderating role of PEIS. The RSM analysis reveals that the pattern of the effects of trust and risk is asymmetric and negatively skewed (as highlighted in Figure 6), and the function is steeper for buyers with very low (point 1) than buyers with moderate (point 2) levels of PEIS and for buyers with moderate than buyers with very high levels of PEIS (point 3). The peak effects of trust and risk occur for buyers with PEIS levels that are lower than the average value of the distribution of the original scale of PEIS.

## 6.2. Implications for Theory and Research

The study makes two key contributions: First, it identifies the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk. Second, it extends the literature on the complex role of institutional structures in online marketplaces, as discussed below.

6.2.1. Implications for Identifying the Boundaries of Trust and Risk. The implied consensus in the literature is that the effects of trust and risk on transaction activity are unconditional and not moderated by the institutional context (see Online Appendix 1). Prior empirical work did not show support for the Mayer et al. (1995) theorized linear moderating role of situational uncertainty on the effects of trust. This may be because their proposed linear moderating effect does not capture the complex moderating role of the institutional context. This study extends the literature by theoretically identifying and empirically specifying the boundaries of the effects of trust and risk. The resulting boundaries are noteworthy. The results show that the effect of risk is bounded by a specific window, and it is only significant at moderate levels of PEIS, whereas the effect of trust on transaction activity extends throughout the spectrum of PEIS. These findings may be explained based on whether institutional structures focus on tangible (economic) or intangible (social) aspects of the buyers’ decisionmaking process. The lack of support for H1 may reflect that institutional structures in today’s online marketplaces focus on tangible assurances aimed at reducing the buyers’ economic vulnerability and not on the intangible aspects of the transaction that aim at reducing their social vulnerability. Although this may imply that online marketplaces have correctly recognized that buyers focus on economic losses in their transaction decision-making process, perhaps they have done so at the expense of ignoring relevant social aspects $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Clark et al. 1986, Tauber 1972). This may be why PEIS does not reinforce or attenuate the effect of trust on transaction activity beyond the rather modest level of $\beta \sim 0 . 1 0$ 0 In contrast, the effect of risk is moderated in a quadratic fashion—rendering its effect insignificant for buyers with both very low and also with very high levels of PEIS, but substantial for buyers with moderate levels of PEIS $( \beta \sim 0 . 2 0 )$ . The effect of risk for buyers at moderate levels of PEIS stresses the importance of tangible assurances in reducing the buyers economic vulnerability, implying the need for the literature to examine the effects of risk within the broader institutional environment.

This study also extends Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) model of predicting transaction activity by going beyond their linear relationships in adding nonlinearities and moderating effects to their original model. These effects not only extend theory, but they also help explain a higher degree of variance in transaction activity, largely because of the higher-order effects (see Table 3). The increased explained variance implies that the moderating role of PEIS not only helps identify the boundaries of trust and risk, but in doing so, it also better explains the study’s dependent variable. Although the added variance explained may not be extremely high, it must be assessed relative to the control variables that are difficult to manipulate (e.g., buyer’s past experience) and relative to the fact that enhancing transaction activity even by a small margin can have tremendous implications for increasing actual transaction volume and market liquidity in a marketplace.

6.2.2. Implications for the Role of Institutional Structures in Online Marketplaces. The proposed moderating role of PEIS was perhaps not intended by the online marketplaces involved in building institutional structures as a means to enhance transactions, but the hypothesized moderating effects are shown to be more influential than even the direct linear effects of trust and risk. This suggests the need to rethink the role of institutional structures in online marketplaces and integrate their moderating role. Regression and RSM analyses reveal some interesting results about the moderating role of PEIS that have potentially important theoretical implications.

First, as Figure 5 shows, the slopes of the effects of risk are steeper as PEIS moves from buyers with very low (point 1) to buyers with moderate (point 2) levels, but they reduce at a slower rate as PEIS moves from buyers with moderate to buyers with very high levels (point 3). This implies that there may be a threshold value of PEIS that is needed to overcome, on average, the buyers’ vulnerability to jump start transactions (accounting for the steep incline). This asymmetry suggests that the boundaries of risk are lopsided and negatively skewed (see Figure 6), implying a steep threshold that must be met by the marketplace’s institutional structures for risk to first enter a buyer’s decision-making process, whereas there is a slower process for the institutional context to be perceived extremely effective to force risk to exit the buyer’s decision-making process. Second, the peak negative effect of risk on transaction activity occurs at a lower level than the peak of the actual distribution of PEIS across all buyers (see Figure 6). This may imply that buyers, on average, perceive institutional structures to be more effective than the peak point that minimizes the negative effect of risk and maximizes transaction activity (see Figure 5). Third, while one might expect PEIS to continue having an increasing (yet with diminishing returns) positive effect on transaction activity, its effect subsides at very high levels of PEIS to result in a quadratic (drooping) effect. This counterintuitive finding supports Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) findings that PEIS does not have a significant linear effect on transaction activity. This finding also explains that the nonlinearity is because the effect of PEIS turns insignificant for buyers at higher PEIS levels, thus cancelling out the initial positive effect of PEIS. This counterintuitive finding could be because some buyers may view extremely effective institutional structures as too restrictive (too much of a good thing). As in other markets with strict regulations that stifle economic activity (Epstein 2006), overly stringent institutional structures may discourage buyers from transacting in the marketplace perhaps because these buyers believe that extremely effective institutional structures prevent good deals. Irrespective of whether this is a correct perception, this might imply that it is not “the higher the better” for institutional structures (thus PEIS = 7 on the original 7-point scale may not be the ideal level), but there is a peak level that maximizes both the direct effect of PEIS and also its moderating role on the direct negative effect of risk. These findings could not be readily identified with traditional research methods that do not capture higher-order effects. This level is empirically lower than the average value of PEIS, implying that online marketplaces may have gone too far in building an extremely safe institutional context (besides the cost of building effective institutional structures with solid guarantees).

## 6.3. Implications for Practice

For online marketplaces, such as eBay and Amazon, the results are telling. The actual distribution of PEIS is skewed to the right, indicating that most buyers are inclined to think highly of their institutional structures. Also, as hypothesized, the more effective PEIS are perceived to be, the less important risk is in the buyers’ decision to transact. Thus the results suggest that the attempts of online marketplaces to control risk have been overly effective. Although high or low levels of PEIS do take risk out of the buyer’s decisionmaking process, PEIS does not at any stage reduce the role of trust. This highlights the dual nature of online marketplaces, such as eBay and Amazon. Transacting is primarily an economic exchange, and this is where PEIS moderate the importance of risk; still, transacting does have a social component, and trust also comes into play in the decision-making process at all PEIS levels. In terms of the nonsupported moderating role of PEIS on the effects of trust, this suggests that institutional structures do not create the conditions that would render trust irrelevant in the decision-making process. Redesigning institutional structures to create the conditions that would make trust irrelevant to a buyer’s decision making may be challenging because instituting structures that address the social exchange and enforce appropriate social conduct (Blau 1964) are bounded by the difficulty in quantifying social interactions, such as feedback comments on the transaction’s social aspects (Pavlou and Dimoka 2006).

The moderating role of PEIS has other practical implications in designing institutional structures. Although one might expect that higher levels of PEIS would be better for transactions across the entire spectrum of PEIS (implying that maximum PEIS is ideal), our results suggest that this is not the case. The maximum peak level of transaction activity takes place at lower levels of PEIS (about 4 on the 7-point scale) than the average PEIS level $( \mu = 5 . 3 2 )$ across all buyers. This implies that online marketplaces may have overinvested in institutional structures to facilitate transaction liquidity, perhaps because of the eagerness of online marketplaces to exceed the minimum threshold of perceived vulnerability. Although this may have ensured that most buyers exceed the minimum required threshold level needed to transact in the marketplace, it may have unintentionally made other buyers exit because its institutional structures may have been viewed as too restrictive for “good deals” to take place. Perhaps online marketplaces should first try to ensure that most buyers have a modest level of PEIS that exceeds the minimum threshold, but simultaneously try to convince buyers that their institutional structures are not too restrictive to prevent lucrative market opportunities. These results may help sensitize managers about the importance of the trade-offs in the design of online marketplaces to avoid overinvesting in institutional structures but identifying the “sweet spot” (about 4 on the 7-point PEIS scale) that maximizes transactions.

The starkly different moderating role of PEIS on the relative effects of trust and risk highlights the sharp distinction between social and economic vulnerability. Although institutional structures have arguably focused on promoting transactions by reducing economic vulnerability with tangible economic guarantees, they may have ignored the social aspects of the transaction process. Thus, there may be still unrealized potential for encouraging transactions by reducing the buyers’ social vulnerability by catering to the transaction’s intangible aspects, thereby reinforcing the direct effect of trust on transaction activity. In doing so, online marketplaces can include both economic and social aspects in the design of their institutional structures to maximize transactions. Taken together, understanding the moderating role of PEIS could help the design of institutional structures to span additional important aspects of the transaction process.

## 6.4. Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

First, caution should be taken when drawing conclusions from a single study. Examining the moderating role of institutional structures in a broader institutional, societal, and cultural environment could enrich our study’s findings. Although institutional structures do determine the boundaries of trust and risk, other institutional factors that shape the level of situational uncertainty and perceived vulnerability in the environment could have a similar moderating role. Future research could examine the generalizability of our findings to other institutional structures and other contexts.

Second, even if our data are based on buyers in the two leading online marketplaces—namely, eBay and Amazon, which account for a large share of the online marketspace—examining smaller and unknown online marketplaces could add new perspectives to the proposed model. At any rate, this study needs to be replicated in other marketplaces because our empirical results may not necessarily generalize to less successful marketplaces. For example, institutional structures in successful online marketplaces, such as eBay and Amazon, are generally perceived to be effective. Future research, especially in online marketplaces where the institutional structures may be less effective, is needed, although it is doubtful whether such marketplaces would facilitate transactions in the long run. Such research could theorize the moderating role of other institutional structures in less effective marketplaces and empirically test whether, how, and why they would have a moderating (quadratic or linear) role on the relationships among trust, risk, and transaction activity.

Third, to maintain consistency with the literature, the measurement items in this study were chosen with the explicit intent to exactly replicate Pavlou and Gefen’s (2004) measurement items. This design choice aimed to avoid the introduction of an alternative explanation, and thereby create threats to internal validity that the quadratic moderating role of PEIS is because of the choice of measurement items rather than our proposed reasoning. Thus the measurement limitations of Pavlou and Gefen (2004) in this journal still apply in this study, namely, that risk may be because of other sources besides the community of sellers.<sup>13</sup> Although the scale did not explicitly specify the community of sellers as the target of risk, the external validity of the risk scale was verified by Pavlou and Gefen (2004) based on interviews with buyers who did confirm that risk arises primarily from the community of sellers and not by the intermediary. Moreover, the nature of the particular institutional structures may have changed over time, for example, escrow services are continuously evolving. Future research could revisit and attempt to refine the measurement scales.

Finally, PEIS is a subjectively assessed variable captured directly by buyers with self-reports. However, our results (see Table 5) imply that none of the 10 variables can single-handedly serve as a direct proxy for PEIS, and multiple objective and subjective variables are needed to adequately predict PEIS. Although we included multiple individual characteristics (e.g., past transactions, past problematic transactions, years in marketplace, age, gender, income) when predicting PEIS, additional individual characteristics may be needed to explain PEIS, and may even act as confounds in shaping both PEIS and the study’s dependent variable (transaction activity). Despite explaining 55% of the variance in PEIS, future research is needed to better understand what determines PEIS. It seems warranted, given the direct and moderating role of PEIS, for future research to more extensively examine both the antecedents of PEIS and the multidimensional role of PEIS as an important determinant of the success of online marketplaces.

## 7. Concluding Remarks

This study stresses the need to take into account the institutional context to fully understand the complex effects of trust and risk on transaction activity, thus offering a new perspective on the effects of trust and risk on the buyers’ decision making. Our results suggest that online marketplaces may have overinvested in institutional structures, perhaps because of their eagerness to eliminate uncertainty and vulnerability. This study aims to sensitize academics and managers of the trade-offs in the design of institutional structures in online marketplaces to develop institutional structures that help achieve the optimal effects of trust and risk to maximize online transaction activity and market liquidity.
