ABSTRACT: During an online session, a user may visit a number of websites, often following hyperlinks from one website to the next or using a search results page to find and visit pages of interest. In doing so, the user can lose track of which sites were visited and which were helpful in meeting the objective. Thus, sites that may have provided value to the user may not receive expected positive effects such as loyalty and intention to return. It is thus crucial to understand if and how an individual website is perceived by users within the context of multi-website online sessions—particularly in predicting desired outcomes, such as user loyalty, trust, brand image, and revisit intentions. To address this problem, we conceptualize the Web as a geography traversed by users who cross digital borders as they move from one website location to another. We introduce the concept of border strength, or the degree to which digital media artifacts mark a transition to a website, and propose a positive effect of border strength on users’ recognition of their locations. We then consider users’ attribution of credit for assistance in successfully completing an online task to those websites and their owners that supported the task. This attribution is a function of border strength and location recognition. We test these hypotheses using experimental data, which show that, indeed, some websites go unrecognized and that stronger borders increase users’ recognition of having visited a website and users’ credit attribution for their experience to the site. Our findings demonstrate the usefulness of the geography metaphor, suggest the need to further study dynamics regarding individual sites within the context of multi-site sessions and show the usefulness of erecting stronger borders to mark the entry into digital locations.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: digital borders, digital geography, online experience, web experience attribution, space and place theory, memory, website design, web traversal.

## Introduction

Since the World Wide Web became prominent in the 1990s, the way people use the Internet has evolved dramatically. In the early days of the Web, users were more likely to seek specific websites, whether based on recommendations from friends, advertisements that provided the addresses of companies’ websites, or other means. With the global expansion and development of the Web—including the advancement of search engines, social media, and link aggregators—the phenomenon of “destination websites” has begun to wane. Website users have become less likely to seek out specific websites, and instead visit sites based on search engine results or alternative entry points such as social media or e-commerce sites [55]. An internal memo at the New York Times, for instance, states that the company’s homepage witnessed a 50 percent reduction in traffic between 2011 and 2013 [54].

Furthermore, as online business models have matured, content and functions, around which a company may have built an entire website earlier in the Internet’s history, have now been rendered subservient to a larger website: consider e-commerce companies that sell their products through Amazon Marketplace, entertainment content available from and customer outreach occurring through Facebook, or news content found primarily through Google News. Consequently, users are more likely to “jump around” the Internet, follow links wherever they may be found, and conduct online sessions in which they visit dozens or even hundreds of websites and other content owner properties. Thus, users may now be less aware of which website they are visiting at any given time during such sessions.

From a user’s standpoint, this usually is not a problem: if desired information is found or a task is accomplished, then users are likely satisfied. However, for the owners of the websites visited, this potential lack of location awareness can be problematic. Research has shown that recognized websites can influence their users to the benefit of the site owner. For instance, feelings of immersion within a given digital content environment have been related to improved user attitude toward a website [45], increased probability of website adoption [36], and increased website loyalty [30]. Feelings of trust and security toward a given website have been related to intent to revisit [15], perceptions of service quality [19], improved brand image [37], and willingness to purchase from the site [e.g., 13, 14, 21, 37], and even willingness to disclose confidential information [37]. Research has also demonstrated the importance of website design quality, the sense of control experienced on a site, and a number of other site- and user-level constructs [e.g., 12, 23, 24, 43, 62].

Although such studies have provided important findings regarding website interaction, they have done so within the context of a particular site, recognized and perceived by the user as a discrete entity. However, if users are unaware of which sites they are visiting during a more typical multi-site online session, it is not clear the extent to which these findings would still hold. Moreover, research has also indicated that the traffic coming primarily from third-party links as opposed to arriving directly via uniform resource locator (URLs) can have a net detrimental effect on websites’ welfare [11]. Given these real-world possibilities, it is imperative to understand the effects of user interactions with a given site within the context of a multi-website online session. To do so, researchers must identify the factors that contribute to a given website’s identity being recognized and to that site’s receiving due credit for helping a user meet whatever requirements prompted the visit in the first place.

The importance of proposing pre-theoretical structures such as analogies, metaphors, and constructs to help foster original theoretical contributions to IS research has recently been emphasized as a way of getting away from research that seeks primarily only to reinforce established reference-discipline theories [17]. Along these lines, and given the lack of theory available in the IS canon to address the associated concerns, we leverage the literature on space and place [31, 48, 59] from the field of experiential geography to provide a theoretical foundation through the use of apt geographical metaphors. This literature, which has already proven useful to IS researchers investigating virtual communities such as Second Life [41, 49], describes how physical locations acquire meaning for an individual and thereby become recognized places. One important tactic in designating the existence and formal boundaries of a place is the erection of clear borders [58].

We propose that users are more likely to recognize a digital content location (e.g., a website) when that location more forcefully expresses its identity through a construct we term border strength. Then, we use perspectives on memory and attribution [e.g., 29, 50] to propose that border strength and location recognition significantly predict the degree to which a user will attribute the success of an online experience to a particular website. These hypotheses were tested using data collected in two parallel experimental data collections in which participants were asked to interact with a purpose-built set of websites to complete two different types of information-gathering tasks—one more goal-directed, the other more experiential—regarding Norwegian parliamentary elections. We also controlled for a number of variables that have previously been shown to affect online experiences to better isolate the effect of our new construct, border strength.

Our study’s cognitive focus allows for an immediate test of these concepts and a theoretical basis from which future researchers can investigate subsequent impacts on perceptions and future user behavior (e.g., intent to re-visit, intent to purchase). Results of our study have pressing implications for the design of websites and other digital content repositories. Our analysis demonstrates the importance of border strength in enabling users to recognize online locations during multi-site sessions and increasing attributions of credit to these locations. This supports the usefulness of the geography metaphor for understanding the dynamics of multi-location browsing behavior.

## Theory and Hypotheses

Many key Web concepts have long been referenced through geographical metaphors. A user “visits” (implying physical movement in a geography) a website, websites themselves have “home” pages and can be reached via “addresses”—also referred to as uniform resource “locators” (URLs)—and historically popular Web software packages have borne the names Internet “Explorer” and Netscape “Navigator.” Thus, geography is a natural, intuitive fit as a lens through which to understand multi-website online sessions. Such metaphors are powerful, though in the IS field perhaps underused, instruments of theory development [17, 27, 56].

In the 1970s, experiential geography researchers began formalizing the concepts and theory of space and place [e.g., 2, 7, 16, 32, 48, 57, 59, 60]. According to this body of work, place is defined as a location to which meaning is attached, whereas space refers to locations without specific meaning [32, 48, 60]. Ill-defined space becomes a meaningful place over time for an individual as the result of both sensory and social factors [16, 31, 48]. We thus propose applying these ideas to the Web to help better understand antecedents and consequences of user recognition of online location and attribution of credit for their experience.

Place can exist in both a formal and perceptual sense [48]. In the physical world, the formal sense of place refers to a defined location with clear boundaries for which there is general agreement or a generally-accepted definition. For example, consider the Pacific Rim, India, the English Channel, and the Flatiron District. Each of these names references a location with a generally accepted definition making each of the locations a formal place. Conversely, a person’s perception of a place— perceptual place—is idiosyncratic in that it depends on the individual’s experiences with locations and the specific meanings they attach to those locations based on their own experiences. For instance, formally, the city of Los Angeles is defined by its city limits. An individual, however, could consider all suburbs and municipalities in Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside, and Ventura counties as “Los Angeles”— or perhaps, depending on the person, Los Angeles is perceived in line with the actual city boundaries.

We propose that users’ online experiences exhibit a similar dichotomy. That is, online locations can be understood as both formal and perceptual places. Formal places coincide with domain names, rights holders, and general understandings. For instance, mentioning Amazon.com generally results in the common recognition of the e-commerce website hosted on that domain. Conversely, each individual interacts with online locations idiosyncratically and thereby develops a particular perception of such locations. These distinct perceptions may or may not coalesce into recognizing the locations visited (i.e., the websites may or may not become perceived places to the user). Furthermore, users’ idiosyncratic perceptions of locations and, in particular, of where one location stops and another starts, may or may not coincide with the formal places defined by website owners.

To better illustrate our borders metaphor, we present two cases. First, consider a user who, during a Facebook session, encounters a link to a story about California wildfires. The link appears to be coming from a known associate, is encountered while browsing Facebook, and if clicked, leads the user to a different website altogether (e.g., CNN). From the user’s perspective, if the borders are not perceived completely in line with the formal borders demarking territory, this story could be perceived as belonging to CNN, to Facebook, or to the user’s friend. Further confusion could occur when, after clicking through to CNN, the user encounters additional “click-bait” story headlines that, when clicked, take the user to another location altogether.

Or consider a consumer shopping for an elliptical exercise machine for home use. The user may start with a Google search for “best elliptical machines,” which results in a dizzying array of links. Some of these links lead to actual vendors (e.g., Bowflex), some are from consumer product aggregators that write for-profit articles that purport to rank machines, and other links are auto-created by Google as part of a question-and-answer format. When the user then clicks through one of the links, the user may not recognize (or, perhaps, may not care) that a transition to a new location has occurred. Additional confusion may arise within the shopping context after a user has clicked through to a shopping site, after which advertisements for specific products viewed seem to follow the user regardless of which site the user visits thereafter.

Furthermore, within the shopping context, many websites advertising through Google and on other websites act as search aggregators (e.g., Kayak for airfare). These search aggregators then further link to other websites where goods and services can be purchased, thus presenting additional potential points of border confusion. When choosing and paying for a trip, does the user perceive having just purchased the airfare from the airline? From Kayak? From Google?

In summary, these realistic cases of consumers transacting in the platform-based world, show that it is increasingly challenging to keep track of where one is on the Web. The resulting confusion and blending of providers can undermine accurate border perceptions.

## Border Strength and Digital Locations

Given this foundation, we propose the concept of the digital border as the point at which a user transitions from one digital content repository to another. A border specifies the boundary around a quantity of information in a digital environment (e.g., a website) and can be either formal (i.e., defined by the content owner) or perceptual (i.e., idiosyncratic to the user). We refer to the content defined by a border as a digital location.

Uncertainty and idiosyncrasy exist in the way users perceive the borders of a given website. Indeed, similar ideas of idiosyncratic or ambiguous borders around digital information can be found in Kent’s [26] discussion of arbitrary categorization of data and in the more recent idea by Kallinikos et al. [25] of an evolving website’s “ambivalent ontology.” In the physical world, experiential geographers maintain that different individuals form different sorts of attachments to physical locations based on a number of factors, such as personal experience with a location, beliefs about others who are from a given location, the visual appearance of a location, and the social connections that transpire within a given geography [16, 31, 48]. Similarly, users’ experiences viewing photos, page layouts, and colors form their perceptions of a digital location. In both the physical and digital worlds, different arrangements of the elements provide cues that the location has changed.

Experiential geographers also suggest that there can be consequential overlap between the formal and perceptual senses of place [48, 59]. Tuan [58], in discussing political borders, states that “politics creates a place by making it visible” (p. 163). For example, erecting signs, raising flags, and establishing other symbols and artifacts help establish an identity, enabling the “owner” of a geography (e.g., a city or national government) to better align the formal borders that it creates with the borders that are perceived by individuals. Transposing this concept to digital locations, owners of digital content (e.g., websites) could better ensure that their digital locations are recognized by similarly erecting artifacts. Many websites have made concerted attempts to increase recognition by requiring users to transition to their websites via an interstitial welcome page (e.g., Forbes, InformationWeek), displaying the number of articles the user has accessed in a given time period (e.g., New York Times), or using striking visual designs, interactive features, or other distinctive multimedia elements.

Border strength can be adjusted by manipulating the degree to which online media artifacts (e.g., visual cues, input requirements, and sound) mark the transition to a new formal location. In the context of physical geography, a border between two city neighborhoods is often relatively weak, often with no clear sign that a transition has taken place. The border between two countries, however, is generally much stronger, often entailing a physical barrier and the requirement to show a passport and answer questions from border control agents. Similarly, the borders of digital content locations can be strong or weak. We expect that websites with stronger borders will be better recognized by users. Thus, we predict:

Hypothesis 1. The greater the strength of the border of a digital location, the more likely a user performing tasks in a multi-website online session will be to recognize the digital location.

## Credit Attribution and Memory

Attribution theory suggests that credit or blame for an experience is given to internal or external factors depending on how a user perceives events related to the experience [61]. For instance, within the website usage context, those who have had difficulty completing a website task have been found to attribute their issues to the website where they were attempting to complete the task, and attitudes toward knowledge sharing websites have been determined by the quality of past experiences there [42, 63]. Thus, when a website successfully contributes to a user completing a task successfully and the user perceives the website as having done so, the user can be expected to attribute this positive experience to that website and its sponsor. Attribution theory is the foundation for the trust-transference phenomenon that has been repeatedly demonstrated in website research [33, 37]. However, given the possibility of users not recognizing a given website within a multi-site online session, this attribution of credit to a helpful website may not occur. Thus, in an environment in which deep linking is common and fewer users enter by the “front door” of a website’s homepage [55], acquiring credit attribution becomes challenging for website owners.

For example, consider a user who is in the market to buy a car that is economical and fun to drive. The user enters “economical car fun to drive” into a search engine, then clicks on each of the first four resulting links, reading the content found on each resulting page. The user thereby determines which car best fits the selected parameters of “economical” and “fun.” To what degree and to which website(s) does the user attribute the success of the completed task? One possibility is that the user remembers the experiences exactly as they happened and without bias and, as a result, assigns credit to each website based on its actual contribution. In this case, if each of the four websites contributed the same actual amount of information, then, assuming the user does not credit the search engine itself for a contribution, each website would receive 25 percent of the total credit.

However, if those experiences are inaccurately remembered or are biased, incorrect attribution might occur. Attribution is a function of memory, which is subject to distortion and inaccuracy [5, 22, 50, 51]. Reasons for this include absent-mindedness (i.e., forgetting details), inattention (i.e., not noticing details), and misattribution (i.e., attributing an experience to an incorrect source, such as a different visited website, the self, or an otherwise uninvolved party) [50].

A stronger border should counter the effects of absent-mindedness, inattention, and misattribution by reinforcing the identity of the website being visited and thereby drawing some of the user’s cognitive resources for perceiving events that aid attribution through various tactics such as delay (e.g., a website loads only after viewing an intermediate, site-identifying welcome page for 10 seconds), refocusing the user’s attention (e.g., through design cues), or requiring additional action by the user (e.g., requiring a click). We thus hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. The greater the border strength of a digital location, the more likely a user performing tasks in a multi-website online session will be to attribute credit for the quality of the experience to that digital location.

We also propose that there will be a significant relationship between the recognition of a location and the credit attribution for an experience. Our prediction is based on two concepts: contextual cuing and subjective hierarchies. Contextual cuing [8] explains that in a familiar setting, information can be processed more quickly, as users do not have to process both the context and the task itself. The effect essentially enables users to engage the location information in a higher-level “chunk” rather than requiring them to process all the details on each encounter. The central executive function of the brain can therefore focus on new details.

Subjective hierarchies are formed between contiguous objects [40]. Although the objects themselves are often very similar (e.g., trees, buildings), their arrangements often differ in key ways, with their clustering into those arrangements forming unique and recognizable hierarchies. This contiguity strengthens the tie between the task itself and the location. People form strong mental maps when recalling familiar territories. However, they often make errors in forming those hierarchies [40]. Therefore, we propose that familiar locations will be associated with greater processing devoted to the task and that stronger bonds will form between those locations. However, people will often make incorrect connections as they engage their previously held concepts of those locations. Thus, credit for an experience will more likely be attributed to websites that are recognized, regardless of whether the location deserves credit, which results in our final hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3. Recognition of a digital location by a user performing tasks during a multi-website online session will significantly increase that user’s credit attribution for the quality of the experience in the session to that location.

## Control Variables

In addition, we used several control variables to help isolate the strength of the relationships among our variables of interest. We have organized these control variables into three categories: user-level, task-level, and website-level. First, userlevel control variables include susceptibility to interpersonal influence (SII) [4], Web news use, and subject matter knowledge.

Second, to understand the applicability of border strength in the presence of different types of websites visited by users with different types of goals, we control for the effect of the nature of the task, whether goal-directed or experiential. A goal-directed task is one that is more extrinsically motivated, instrumental, more highly structured, and utilitarian [44]. Examples would therefore include finding a specific point of information, purchasing a specific product, or downloading a driver for a given peripheral device. Conversely, an experiential task is more intrinsically motivated, less instrumental, less structured, and more hedonic [44]. Information-gathering activities such as determining product preferences, learning about a new hobby, or exploring possible vacation destinations online are more experiential in nature. Other related user task-level control variables include task duration, extent of website interactions, and the level of cognitive absorption while performing the task [1].

Finally, website-level controls include average display rank, familiarity with the site, and frequency of site use, each of which was collected for each website from each participant. Each of these variables is briefly described in Table 1 and in more detail in Appendix A1.

## Methodology

## Experimental Design and Procedure

We collected data through two experiments. The two data collections followed the same tasks and procedures, except they were conducted at different times with different samples, in order to better test our theory and generalize the findings. The first experiment involved sampling from Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and the second experiment used a sample of undergraduate students. In both experiments, participants completed tasks on websites built specifically for the experiment. Participants were assigned to complete one of two news-related online tasks (goal-directed vs. experiential) regarding Norwegian elections using controlled websites designed for this experiment. Data were collected in three consecutive stages: pretask, experimental task, and post-task. The mean completion times for the two data collections were about 33 minutes (SD = 7.93) and 45 minutes (SD = 5.46) for the first and second data collection respectively, indicating that the MTurk users took less time than the undergraduate students to complete the experiment tasks. In the pretask stage, the participants were asked to complete survey instruments via Qualtrics™ related to the Web news use and subject matter knowledge constructs.

We needed an experimental task that required participants to engage in a multisite session to test our hypotheses. We thus identified Internet news research as a viable task since news research tasks often involve search engines and visits to multiple sites and therefore can make transitions between locations difficult to process. We also needed to determine a subject matter that would be appropriate for the task and that would not result in confounded findings. We thus conducted a pilot study wherein participants indicated on a 7-point Likert-type scale their level of interest regarding 18 different news stories from several categories (e.g., world events, entertainment, technology). From these candidate stories, Norwegian parliamentary elections was identified as an appropriate subject matter due to the low variance in interest (mean = 2.24; SD = 1.37), allowing us to provide a control for user behavior differences due to interest level. See Appendix B and Table B1 for details regarding this pilot study and the candidate subject matters.

In the experimental task stage, participants were randomly assigned to either the goal-directed or experiential task condition. They were then asked to complete their assigned tasks using a set of Web pages, starting from a Google-branded search results page created by the authors (see Appendix Figure B5). This search results page included links to nine unique articles regarding the subject matter (see Appendix Table B2) on nine branded pages. The order of these links was randomly assigned and recorded for each participant, and the average of each site’s order position (i.e., where it was found on the results page) was used to form the average display rank control variable.

Each of the nine articles was hosted on a page designed to look like a different news organization’s website. These organizations, which we identified based on the top-appearing sites from a Google search for the term “Norway elections,” included BBC.com, DW (Deutsche Welle), The Economist, The Independent, Inter Press Service, LATimes.com, NBC News, The Nordic Page, and Reuters. The pages included the actual sites’ mastheads as well as a branded footer (i.e., the bottom of the Web page included additional branding information) but were identical to each other in all other ways (e.g., fonts, font sizes, link colors, background color; see Appendix Figure B6). To minimize navigational volatility [9], each page included a navigational section titled “Stories from Other News Sites,” which included links leading to the related articles appearing on the other branded pages included in the experiment. The order of these links matched the order on the search results page.

For each participant, each branded page was randomly assigned one of nine news articles related to the 2013 Norwegian general election. Six articles were substantially adapted from existing articles and three were entirely original. Articles ranged in length from 506 to 767 words, and the reading difficulty level ranged from 11.0 to 12.5 on the Fleisch-Kincade scale [28] (see Appendix Table B2). For each participant, a given branded page always contained the same article content. For example, for User A, the article titled “Center-Right Alliance Victorious in Norway Elections” may have been assigned to the BBC, and therefore was shown as a BBC article throughout User A’s experience. Conversely, User B might have seen the article “Norway Risks Economic Overheating as Opposition Scores Victory” assigned to the BBC (persistently throughout the task), while the “Center-Right Alliance Victorious in Norway Elections” article would have been assigned persistently to another publication. Apparent links to pages excluded from the experiment (e.g., links to the news organization’s homepage) were inactive, and participants were informed a priori that this would be the case. Participants were instructed to restrict their content finding to the provided search results page and the articles linked from those results.

Assignment of participants to the two task conditions enabled us to gauge the salience of the border strength construct across different use cases. Those assigned to the goal-directed task condition were asked to answer 10 highly specific questions about the elections (see Appendix Table B3 for question text). To encourage navigation among the sites, “common” answers to five of the 10 questions could be found in any article (C1–C5), while “uncommon” answers to the other five questions (UC1–UC5) were distributed among five different articles and could be found only in that article (i.e., only one article contained the answer to UC1, a different article was the only one containing the answer to UC2, and so forth). The mean number of correct answers for all questions for those in the goal-directed task condition in the two experiments was 7.98 and 7.37 (out of 10). The goal-directed task in the two experiments required on average about 16 minutes and 30 minutes, respectively, to be completed.

Those assigned to the experiential task condition were asked to use the same search results page and linked news story pages to read about the Norwegian elections for 10 minutes, after which they would be expected to answer five broad, open-ended, short essay-type questions (e.g., “If you were a Norwegian considering whom to vote for in the 2013 election, what would have been the most important political issues involved in your decision?”; see Appendix Table B4 for question text). Each of these had multiple possible correct answers and required the participants to develop a framework for answering, thus making them more experiential and less goal-directed [6]. Participants assigned to the experiential task condition in the two data collections required about 15 minutes (SD = 8.36) and 28 minutes (SD = 5.48) on average, respectively, to complete their tasks (note that these times are different from those required to complete the entire experiment). These participants in the two data collections used about 132 and 143 words on average, respectively, in their essay answers.

In the high border strength condition, when participants clicked links on the search results or article pages, they were shown an interstitial web page in the same browser window prior to displaying the article content (see Figures 2a and 2b). Similar to actual practice on some websites (e.g., Forbes, InformationWeek), when the participant clicked on a link, that participant was taken to the interstitial page and instructed either to click another link to continue to the content page or to wait ten seconds for the content page to load. Although other operationalizations of border strength were considered (e.g., an overlay with a count of articles read, variation in terms of color scheme), the use of interstitial pages was selected for three reasons: (1) its obvious presence to the user (e.g., the user could more easily not notice an overlay on part of the page or an altered color scheme); (2) the extent to which it enabled for us to isolate the effect of the manipulation and avoid design-related confounds; and (3) its prevalent use in actual practice.

The interstitial page reinforced the identity of the website that was loading. If the participant clicked on the BBC News link, the interstitial page included a BBC News logo and the “Continue” link reinforced the name of the site. These interstitial pages were rendered as static graphical images on HTML-coded pages and thus would not be blocked by ad-blocking browser extensions.

To capture participant activity during the task, we used the Problem Steps Recorder (PSR) software found in the Windows operating system. To demonstrate the robustness and generalizability of our findings, we conducted this experiment across two different samples resulting in our two data collections: one from Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and another consisting of undergraduate students. Table 2 compares the two samples.

## Analysis and Results

In both data collections, the data were collected on two levels: websites and users. The dependent variables of interest were website location recognition and the credit attribution score awarded to each site. To test our hypotheses, we analyzed the experimental data using mixed-effects regressions by specifying site-specific fixed effects and user-specific random components. This approach helped us estimate the overall fixed effect of high border strength and derive the potential variations of the effect of high border strength across users by considering a user encountering multiple websites in an online session as a group-level variable. When the user-specific random effects portions of the mixedeffects specifications were statistically not significant, we derived fixed-effects estimates and utilized robust standard errors, derived by clustering the data pertaining to individuals, to test our hypotheses.

Note that location recognition is a binary response variable and credit attribution is a continuous score that varies from 0 to 100. The location recognition and credit attribution score of website i by user j were specified as shown in equations (1) and (2), respectively:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Log - odds (location recognition)} _ {\mathrm{ij}} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} (\text {high border strength}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} \\ \quad + \beta_ {2} (\text {site interactions}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + \beta_ {3} (\text {task duration}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} \\ \quad + \beta_ {4} (\text {cognitive absorption}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + \beta_ {5} (\text {SII}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} \\ \quad + \beta_ {6} (\text {Web news use}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + \beta_ {7} (\text {subject matter knowledge}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} \\ \quad + \beta_ {8} (\text {display rank}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + \beta_ {9} (\text {familiarity}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + \beta_ {1 0} (\text {frequency of use}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} \\ \quad + \beta_ {1 1} (\text {task type}) _ {\mathrm{ij}} + u _ {0 j} + u _ {1 j} (\text {high border strength}) + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{ij}} \end{array}\tag{Eq.1}
$$

where $\beta _ { 0 }$ to $\beta _ { 1 1 }$ represent the fixed-effects portion of the model, the random effect $u _ { 0 \mathrm { j } }$ is the user-specific intercept that represents each user’s shift from the overall mean $\left( \beta _ { 0 } \right)$ , and $u _ { \mathrm { l j } }$ is the random slope of high border strength that represents each user’s deviation in recognizing a digital location from the overall mean (β<sub>1</sub>).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {(credit attribution)} _ {i j} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} (\text {high border strength}) _ {i j} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {2} (\text {location recognition}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {3} (\text {site interactions}) _ {i j} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {4} (\text {task duration}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {5} (\text {cognitive absorption}) _ {i j} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {6} (\text {SII}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {7} (\text {Web news use}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {8} (\text {subject matter knowledge}) _ {i j} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {9} (\text {display rank}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {1 0} (\text {familiarity}) _ {i j} + \alpha_ {1 1} (\text {frequency of use}) _ {i j} \\ \quad + \alpha_ {1 2} (\text {task type}) _ {i j} + + z _ {0 j} + z _ {1 j} (\text {high border strength}) + \zeta_ {i j} \end{array}\tag{Eq.2}
$$

where ${ \mathfrak { a } } _ { 0 }$ is the population mean intercept representing the credit attributed to a website, the random effect $z _ { \mathrm { 0 j } }$ is the user-specific intercept that represents each user’s shift from the overall mean $\left( \mathbf { { a } } _ { 0 } \right)$ , and $z _ { \mathrm { 1 j } }$ is the random slope of high border strength that represents each user’s deviation in attributing credit to website from the overall mean $\left( \mathfrak { a } _ { 1 } \right)$

Next, Tables 6 and 7 present the regression results for the location recognition model for the data collections. In this model, we predicted whether a user recognized having used a site, and thus our dependent variable was binary and required a mixed-effects logistic regression approach for estimation. Only dyads in which the user interacted with the website were used in the regression, resulting in our n values of 1,019 and 661. In the first data collection, for the more goal-directed task, 85 users interacted with a mean of 8.0 websites per user $( \mathrm { S D } = 2 . 6 9 )$ . For the

experiential task, 77 users interacted with a mean of 4.4 websites per user (SD = 1.88). Similarly, in the second data collection, for the more goal-directed task, 60 users interacted with a mean of 8.0 websites $\left( \mathrm { S D } = 1 . 8 5 \right)$ per user, and for the experiential task, all the 59 users interacted with 8 websites at least once during the task.

The results from both experiments indicate that greater border strength predicts a higher probability of users recognizing locations and the borders between them (H1 supported). Referring to $\beta _ { 1 }$ shown in Table 6, the log odds of recognizing a website by a user performing a task in an online session increases by 0.972 units on average for the high border strength condition when compared to the low border strength condition. Similarly, as reported in Table 7, results using the data from the second experiment show that the log odds of recognizing a website by a user in the high border strength condition, on average, are higher by 1.136 units on average when compared to the low border strength condition. Furthermore, our results suggest that the effect of high border strength varies across users. In both samples, the user-level intercept for high border strength was significant. We estimated standard deviations of 1.392 and 0.596 for the user-specific intercept $( u _ { O \mathrm { j } } )$ across users in the two samples. In both the samples, the user-specific random slope coefficient $( u _ { \mathrm { 1 j } } )$ was not statistically significant. When we only specified the random intercept in the model (i.e., set $u _ { \mathrm { l j } } = 0 )$ , the results for the fixed effects were similar and the intraclass correlation (ICC) metrics, or the correlation between websites recognition for the same user, were estimated to be 0.370 and 0.097, respectively for the first and second sample.

We then estimated our mixed-effects regression model for user credit attribution. Similar to the first regression, this regression included only those user-website dyads in which credit attribution was given. For both the samples, the likelihood ratio (LR) test of the mixed-effects linear regression indicated that there were no significant random effects at the user level for credit attribution. Therefore, the fixed effects derived from the two-level, mixed-effects linear regression estimates were similar to those of an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression specification. In using OLS, we derived robust standard errors to test our hypotheses by accounting for the heteroscedasticity of credit attribution by users. Diagnostic tests did not reveal any problems due to multicollinearity or outliers.

## Discussion

Our research was driven by a persistent problem faced by website owners wherein users engaged in multi-site online sessions may not recognize or attribute credit to websites that help them accomplish their tasks. Consequently, Internet users engaging in multi-website online sessions presents a challenge to the applicability of the findings of established website-related research to actual practice given that so many of those findings are based on less realistic, single-site interactions that do not reflect how users actually search and surf the Web. To enable researchers to formulate constructs and variables that represent this location awareness and understand its causes, we leveraged metaphors from experiential geography’s space and place literature. Our analogizing the Internet to a physical geography enabled us to conceptualize websites and users’ perceptions of content as locations. Furthermore, this enabled us to propose an empirically testable model of hypotheses that test the key concept of border strength. Based on our experimentation and subsequent data analysis, we found strong empirical evidence of our theoretical claims. This evidence challenges the underlying assumption of traditional, single-site studies— namely, that users are aware of their online locations—does not always reflect reality.

Moreover, we found that, as predicted, users do not recognize all the online locations they visit. Crucially, we further discovered that our border strength construct significantly related to this location recognition, similar to the way in which symbols of physical borders increase recognition of borders and locations in the physical world [58]. Border strength was likewise significantly related to a user’s attributing credit to a website for helping complete a task. These findings remained significant for users completing both goal-directed and experiential information-seeking tasks as well as for users from populations with different demographics (MTurk workers, undergraduate students).

The successful test of these concepts suggests the potential for further value to be drawn from this Internet-geography metaphor, as applied to research and practice. One can imagine, for instance, potentially useful analogies between search engines and freeways, a user’s following of many links and a great journey, difficult-to-find sites and the ocean depths, and government-enforced content blockades and the Great Wall of China. Such analogies could help develop additional concepts and constructs that enrich both theory and understanding of user behavior and content owner outcomes within the multi-faceted world of the Web. Pragmatically, such powerful metaphors can inform design practice, when vetted with theory and empirical evidence, as is the case with our border strength concept.

## Key Findings and Other Implications

Our study has other notable implications for research and practice as well. Based on our finding of the salience of border strength, website researchers and designers should investigate using design elements to increase the strength and clarity of the borders demarcating a user’s point of entry. This is particularly crucial for sites in which few users enter by the “front door” of the traditional homepage after typing an intended URL in the address bar. Few users type in URLs for most searches, but instead they are more likely to arrive at a deep-linked page after a Google search or clicking on a social media link. Erecting these clearer borders could be achieved through interstitial pages, as was done in our study, or through other design-based tools such as animations, layouts, contrasting color palettes, welcome chimes, or other means.

We note another interesting result implicit in our findings: some respondents did not recognize the locations they visited and the borders that form them. In this study, we used a measure of recognition to indicate user memory. We measured this recognition by presenting participants with a website and asking whether they remembered having used it. This represents a lower threshold of memory than “recall,” in which participants would have been required to recall the identity of sites from memory and without prompting (e.g., Bagozzi and Silk [3]). Had recall been used instead, users’ lack of memory of location likely would have been even greater. This unawareness of online location is a major research finding that challenges the status quo of traditional website research. Much of the websiterelated research conducted to date has implicitly assumed that users recognize the sites they use, thus leading to rational and predictable outcomes (e.g., intent to revisit, intent to place order, and loyalty). However, our results indicate that some of these earlier assumptions and findings may not apply to sites that go unrecognized during a multi-site online session.

Regarding the effects of user task type (i.e., goal-directed vs. experiential), we found that border strength remains salient regardless of the task being conducted by the user. However, participants entering a site to complete the goal-directed task had higher levels of site recognition than those with the experiential task. If this holds, then websites that cater specifically to users completing goal-directed tasks may more easily assert their identities to users and thereby be recognized as discrete locations. In terms of attribution, users attempting to perform goal-directed tasks visiting a site were less likely to attribute credit to that site than were those with an experiential task. This finding may indicate that expectations play an important role in credit attribution: a user expecting a site to deliver an answer to a high-structure problem and who does not receive the full answer as expected may, as a result, be unwilling to attribute even deserved credit to that site.

Furthermore, our findings related to location recognition and credit attribution in a multi-site session have implications for examining the influence of site policies, for example, regarding protections against privacy violations. Without knowing under which site’s policy they fall, users in a multi-site online session might be confused about whom to hold responsible for privacy breaches or might not understand exactly which safeguards are in place. The potential for such confusion has proven to be a troublesome issue for Google, against whom European regulators have threatened legal action in response to its combining privacy policies across its owned sites (e.g., the Google search engine, YouTube, Google+). These regulators feel that Google’s perception of itself as a single entity may not match users’ perceptions of location and ownership, thus potentially raising privacy issues for users [10]. We believe continued research on location recognition and credit attribution in multi-site online sessions have the potential to shed light on such privacy issues.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study naturally has several limitations that provide interesting opportunities for additional research. First, because of our reliance on the PSR software available only on the Windows operating system, our sample was limited by operating system—only MTurk workers who had access to Windows could participate in the first data collection and, in the second data collection, students were required to complete the task in a lab using Windows-based computers. As such, our sample likely omitted (first data collection) or affected (second data collection participants who are not regular Windows users) users of Apple’s OS X, Linux distributions, and other non-PC devices (e.g., tablets, smartphones). Also, two respondents in the first data collection used touch-screen devices to complete the task, in which case the PSR was ineffective in capturing on-site behavior. These two cases were thus omitted from the data set. It therefore could be useful to replicate this study in a way that would allow for the inclusion of such cases.

These findings also suggest the need for additional research on border strength effects in other digital content contexts. A central feature of the Internet—and, indeed, a central theme of this study—is the movement of users from one site to another through online links. In a sense, a similar form of movement happens within other digital platforms. Those using smartphones or other mobile platforms move from one Internet-enabled application to the next, sometimes consuming content from different third parties within the same app. In the context of videostreaming devices, users can move from Netflix content to Amazon Prime content to YouTube content or can search for and find content using a third party (e.g., Roku) and access it directly without going through the “front door” of the streaming service. Future studies might consider how users perceive borders in these contexts and the implications of that perception for platform sponsors, application developers, and content owners.

We collected data from two distinct populations to provide additional robustness to our findings. Although it is possible that there may have been some cross-over (i.e., participants included in both pools), these two participant groups represent different populations and have dissimilar properties. The MTurk sample was considerably older with greater age diversity. Unlike the student sample, all of whom were undergraduate students, 59.3 percent of the MTurk sample had either never attended college or had already graduated. As with any experimental study, however, and despite our replicating across two different participant groups, there may have been sample bias. Whereas the research outcomes were similar for both groups were similar, we found some minor differences regarding the performance of the two different participant pools. On average, student subjects took a little longer to complete the task and visited slightly fewer sites during completion. Furthermore, we recognize that previous research has identified substantial cultural differences in online systems use [34, 47, 52]. Thus, to better generalize findings across cultures, it would be compelling to conduct additional studies on website borders across various cultural contexts.

Moreover, as with most experiments, the task assigned to participants in this study was designed specifically for this study and thus may not have reflected fully natural user behavior. For instance, users may not typically have as many as 10 specific questions to which they need answers when investigating news stories, or they may not have the need to interpret events, as was requested of users in the experiential condition. Nevertheless, the manipulations of this experiment indicated the presence of two phenomena: (1) in certain scenarios, users’ recognition of website borders and locations is imperfect, and (2) attribution may vary based on border recognition as well as on border strength and the goal-directedness of the task to be completed. Further studies in other settings would help confirm or add useful nuance to these findings.

Attribution is another potential concept for additional research. Our study did not investigate whether attribution was necessarily given to the site that provided useful information, but only asked participants to which sites they attributed credit for completing the task. Specifically, we considered attribution rather than correct attribution. Looking in particular at the goal-directed task group, in which a participant would have had to look at a minimum of six sites to answer all 10 questions correctly, it is interesting that most participants credited fewer than six sites. This indicates that credit attribution was withheld from some sites that were helpful in completing the task. Focusing specifically on the question of when users attribute credit to a site that actually helped solve a problem and when they do not would be an interesting extension of this study. Moreover, further discussion of the concepts of correct and incorrect attribution would be a potentially worthwhile endeavor for future researchers.

Finally, we caution that there may be downsides associated with erecting stronger borders. Based on our findings, increasing the strength of a site’s digital border can improve the site’s recognizability as an online location and its ability to receive credit attribution from users. However, other studies have shown that intrusions into a user’s online session can be a cause of annoyance [39]. As stronger borders could be considered intrusive, we suggest that additional research be conducted to determine which border-related design elements are the most effective. In addition to the interstitial pages used in our experiment, it is possible that other design elements—such as contrasting colors, sound, motion, or video—may help to reinforce the identity of a location and may increase recognition of a website while causing different amounts of annoyance. Additional research in this area would be helpful to determine the most effective form of border and the potential negative effects of increased border strength measures (e.g., annoyance, distrust).

## Conclusion

This study is the first in the IS field to conceptualize the Internet as a form of geography to which the concepts of space and place can be applied [7, 48, 59]. We find that, indeed, users in some cases do not know where they are online and do not necessarily recognize the websites they visit. This suggests that conclusions about how a website experience influences users’ attitudes and intentions toward a website may not generalize to the context of a multi-website online experience. As a result, website owners need to consider not only the degree to which certain constructs relate to a user’s intent to return to a website or intent to purchase, but also whether their site is discretely recognized in the first place. We introduce the concept of border strength and show that varying the strength of borders can have a significant effect on perception of online locations.

Using the concept of credit attribution, we again found that both border strength and goal-directedness are important in determining how users attribute success to sites visited. Interestingly, we could significantly predict user attribution of credit without also considering where users actually found the sought-after information included in the task. Our findings suggest that researchers can improve their understanding of user-website and user-digital content interaction by considering how a specific user-website dyad fits within the context of a broader, multi-website online session, particularly in an era of increasing navigation to websites from search engines rather than via direct entry of homepage URLs.
