# KNOW YOUR FIRM: MANAGING SOCIAL MEDIA ENGAGEMENT TO IMPROVE FIRM SALES PERFORMANCE<sup>1</sup>

Fei Ren Guanghua School of Management, Peking University, Beijing, CHINA {fren@gsm.pku.edu.cn}

Yong Tan Michael G. Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle, WA, U.S.A. {ytan@uw.edu}

Fei Wan School of Business and Management, Shanghai International Studies University, Shanghai, CHINA {wanfei0304@shisu.edu.cn}

We examine the impact of firm social media engagement on sales performance, answering “whether,” “what,” and “how” questions. The study uses a quasi-experimental design in a social e-commerce setting, for which propensity score matching and difference-in-differences methods quantify a mean 20.67% sales increase after firm social media adoption. We also find that firms that sell low-involvement products benefit more from social media adoption, compared to those that sell high-involvement products. Further, in terms of how to manage social media engagement, we find that informative content, in general, is effective for sales of high-involvement products, whereas promotional content, a new type of content discovered in this study, is more beneficial for sales of low-involvement products. Meanwhile, more social media followers generate better firm sales performance. We used instrumental variables and the control function method to address endogeneity issues and conducted robustness checks to support our conclusions. This study sheds light on the value of firm social media, particularly regarding industry differences and firm know-how.

Keywords: Firm social media, sales performance, product involvement, content analysis, quasi-experiment, propensity score matching, difference-in-differences, instrumental variable, control function

## Introduction

Social media has become an important online channel for firms to proactively promote brands and products and interactively communicate with consumers (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Firms often create fan pages on social media platforms and generate content to influence the consumer decision-making process.

We propose three research questions related to firms’ adoption and use of social media. First, to what extent does the adoption of social media generate business value, particularly regarding firm sales performance? Second, to what extent, if any, are there differences in the sales performance of social media among different types of firms, and what causes these differences? Third, based on their nature, how should firms effectively use social media to achieve better sales performance? Answering these questions is important for both researchers and practitioners.

First, although the use of social media involves connecting and engaging consumers, the ultimate goal of firms is to convert consumer interest into firm sales—in other words, social media ROI is very important for firms. Nevertheless, a survey of 427 marketers from U.S. companies found that 80% of companies were unable to quantify the value of their social media efforts (John et al., 2017). At the same time, research on the sales performance of the firm use of social media (i.e., firm-generated content, or FGC) is limited in comparison with that on consumer behavior. A few extant studies (e.g., Goh et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Kumar et al., 2016) have generated implications for the performance impact of FGC. Nevertheless, we have a limited understanding of industry (or firm) heterogeneity in terms of its influence on the value of FGC, as most of the studies have focused on single firms or industries. While two studies (Chung et al., 2020; Colicev et al., 2016) are based on multiple industries, their analyses are overall examinations without specific investigation into the industry difference.

Second, based on the nature of the industry (or firm), its effect on the consumer decision-making process differs, as does the effectiveness of firm social media content. Acknowledging industry differences in terms of social media impact is important for firms to be able to manage social media efforts. Likewise, social media is particularly suitable for some industries as a means of creating consumer trust<sup>2</sup> because, when facing different products, the consumer decision-making process can differ at different stages, and having to deal with uncertainty could lengthen the process (Engel et al., 1995). Therefore, the effectiveness of firm social media content depends on the extent to which it addresses consumers’ uncertainty and creates consumer trust. Research has not examined industry differences in sales performance as related to a firm’s social media content strategy. In this study, we use product involvement as an angle to investigate the performance difference. Since different levels of product involvement involve different consumer behaviors in the decision-making process (Zaichkowsky, 1985), we believe that this would cause different outcomes for firm social media content.

Third, real-world cases have indicated the importance of knowhow—that is, knowing how to effectively use social media to achieve better performance. Firms, even in the same industry, could have very different outcomes from their social media efforts. Starbucks is often mentioned as an example of excelling in social media. From campaign content to visual expression, Starbucks masters content that is effective in engaging consumers and therefore generates extremely high social media ROI. <sup>3</sup> In contrast, JD Wetherspoon, also in the food chain industry, has not been able to determine an effective way to generate social media content despite their best efforts, resulting in the closure of all of its social media accounts. <sup>4</sup> In the literature, studies have addressed the “how” question mainly from three aspects, i.e., volume of FGC, communication mode, and FGC content analysis. Given the rich firm social media content available, we believe content analysis could provide nuanced implications; however, such research is very limited in the literature, let alone research investigating content strategy with respect to industry (or firm) differences.<sup>5</sup> In this study, we focus on content analysis to investigate how to effectively manage firm social media efforts to improve sales performance according to the firm’s nature.

We aim to answer the three research questions proposed above. The launch of Weitao (an integrated microblogging function) on Taobao (China’s leading e-commerce platform) in November 2013 provides the means for a quasi-experimental design to quantify the sales effect of firm (Taobao store) social media adoption and engagement. Weitao is the sole social media marketing function embedded in the Taobao platform; when a firm chooses to use Weitao, the firm opens its own microblogging account to promote its store and products. This social e-commerce setting allows us to observe both firm social media engagement and joint firm sales.

Thus, in this study, we examine the firm-level sales performance of firm social media engagement. We examine multiple firms in different industries. We take product involvement as the theoretical perspective to understand the industry difference and to examine how the sales effect of a firm’s social media engagement may be different for firms with different levels of product involvement. We also explore how firms manage their social media efforts, especially the content of blog postings, to achieve better firm sales performance and examine how firms manage such efforts based on the nature of their industry. To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has adopted this focus.

We assembled a novel data set from taobao.com and its integrated social media function, Weitao, to examine the sales effect of the firm (Taobao stores) use of social media (Weitao). To address the endogeneity issue caused by firm self-selection (adopting Weitao), we employed a propensity score matching (PSM) method, and used a difference-in-differences (DID) method to quantify the sales effect. Overall, we found that the use of Weitao significantly boosted firm sales. In general, the firms in our study had a mean of 20.67% increase in sales as a result of Weitao adoption.

With respect to industry differences, our results show that firms in low-involvement product industries (stationery goods in this study) benefit more than those in the high-involvement product industries (cosmetics products in this study) from the adoption of Weitao. Specifically, we found that Weitao adoption yielded a mean sales increase of 27.79% for stores that sold stationery products, whereas stores that sold cosmetics products realized an average sales increase of 21.29%.

Regarding how social media efforts should be managed, we found that when stores made an effort to attract more Weitao followers, they benefitted more in terms of firm sales. However, effort, in terms of more Weitao blog postings, did not generate more sales. We further explored Weitao blog content and found that, overall, informative content was more significant in helping firms (especially those selling high-involvement products) address consumer purchase uncertainty and therefore promoted firm sales. Meanwhile, content analysis suggests a new type of content, i.e., promotional content, which we found to be effective for firms selling low-involvement products.

We used a control function model to address the hidden bias issue in the PSM, as well as instrumental variables to address endogeneity and a first differencing model to tease out firm heterogeneity. Our results are robust to various tests.

This study contributes to the value of social media FGC literature in the following ways. First, it contributes to the limited research on the firm performance of FGC by explicitly examining industry differences. The unique research setting allowed for a more accurate quantification of the sales effect of firm social media efforts, which thereby answered the “whether” question. Second, this study provides an angle, i.e., product involvement, to understand how industry differences in sales performance relate to firm social media, thereby answering the “what” question. Product involvement, in terms the consumer decision-making process, which can be influenced by social media content, can shed light on what causes the different effects of social media content. Third, the study enriches the understanding of the effectiveness of firm social media efforts, answering the “how” question. By analyzing different aspects of firm efforts, particularly through content analysis, we improve the understanding of how to manage FGC according to the nature of the firm and industry.

## Literature Review

## The Value of Social Media FGC

Social media provides two-way communication for firms to proactively market products and connect with potential customers (Kim & Ko, 2012). This study concerns the value of social media FGC, which has two substreams in the literature, i.e., the impact of social media FGC on consumer behavior and the impact of social media FGC on firm performance. We provide a summary of the two substreams with the key information of each study in Appendix A.

## Impact on Consumer Behavior

The first substream of research concerns the impact of social media FGC on consumer behavior. The extant research in this stream focuses on the consumer behavior side by investigating the behavioral outcomes of FGC or on the FGC side by investigating the content. For the former substream of research, studies overall find a positive impact of FGC on consumer behaviors such as brand loyalty (e.g., Erdoğmuş & Cicek, 2012; Rishika et al., 2013), brand trust (e.g., Laroche et al., 2013), customer relationships (e.g., Clark & Melancon, 2013; Gu & Ye, 2014; Kim & Ko, 2012), and consumer word of mouth (e.g., Homburg et al., 2015; Meire et al., 2019; Song et al., 2019). In these studies, FGC is often treated as an indicator of whether a firm opens a fan page or whether consumers participate in a firm social media community, and surveys are the dominant method used to measure consumer behavior. To fully understand the impact of FGC, however, we also need to examine whether the consumer behavior elicited by FGC translates into better firm performance in terms of sales. However, very few studies have examined this relationship. Two exceptions are John et al. (2017) and Song et al. (2019). While John et al. (2017) investigated the causal link between consumer behavior (“liking” a brand page) and consumer purchasing, their study focuses on the later stage of the link (i.e., from consumer behavior to firm performance) but not on the first stage (i.e., from FGC to consumer behavior), and firm performance is measured mainly by consumers’ propensity to purchase, based on lab experiment data. Song et al. (2019) found that movie FGC can predict box office revenue indirectly via user-generated content (UGC).

Another focus of the first substream of research on the FGC side has investigated content (or the frequency of content) and its impact on consumer behavior. In such research, consumer behavior, such as following, liking, or commenting, has been treated as a token act (e.g., Lee et al., 2018; Meire et al., 2019; Miller & Tucker, 2013; Colicev et al., 2018). These studies have found that active social media engagement and informative content can stimulate positive consumer behavior. However, as Chung et al. (2020) note, although the measurement of content in these studies is appropriate for individual-level analysis (for consumer behavior), it may not be applicable to an examination of the impact of FGC content strategy at the firm level. In the social e-commerce context of our study, the objective of FGC may be directed more toward sales performance than toward eliciting consumer behavior in terms of “liking” or commenting. Further, how firms should manage content according to the nature of their firm to generate better firm performance is also poorly understood. Thus, we investigated the impact of firm social media efforts on sales performance and conducted content analysis to examine the sales effect of FGC content types for different types of firms.

## Impact on Firm Performance

The second, much smaller substream of research concerns the impact of social media FGC on firm performance. Nevertheless, the studies in this substream greatly contribute to the understanding of the firm-level value of FGC and to the objective measurement of firm performance. The overall conclusion of these studies is that social media FGC has a positive influence on firm performance in terms of sales (e.g., Goh et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Kumar et al., 2016), stock market performance (e.g., Chung et al., 2020), and brand value (Colicev et al., 2016).

Since this substream of research closely relates to our study, we discuss it in depth, focusing on three areas: single-industry (or single-firm) studies, multiple-industry studies, and the management of FGC. Most studies in this substream are based on data from a single industry or firm, e.g., an apparel firm in Goh et al. (2013), a wine-selling specialty firm in Kumar et al. (2016), the music industry in Chen et al. (2015), and the financial services industry in Hewett et al. (2016) (see the complete list of studies in Appendix A).

There are two studies in this substream of research that examine multiple firms across different industries. Chung et al. (2020) studied 63 firms across 13 industries (e.g., financial services, information and communication, durable manufacturing) and compared their financial returns (measured by abnormal returns and Tobin’s q) to firm communication actions on social media. Based on a data set of 87 brands across 17 industries (e.g., financial services, technology, automotive, luxury), Colicev et al. (2016) examined the influence of brand actions on brand value. The results of these studies are more generalized than those of single-industry studies. However, neither investigates industry heterogeneity.

The third focus of this substream of research is how to manage FGC to influence firm performance. Extant studies have addressed this issue mainly according to three aspects—the volume of FGC, communication mode, and FGC content analysis. For the volume of FGC, studies have consistently found that the volume of FGC has a positive effect on firm performance (e.g., Song et al., 2019; Chung et al., 2020; Hewett et al., 2016; Colicev et al., 2016. See Appendix A for details of each study). Regarding the communication mode, studies have identified effective ways for firms to communicate with customers on social media. Goh et al. (2013) found that the direct communication mode is more effective. Chen et al. (2015) showed that for music sales, broadcasting personal messages on social media is much more effective than automated messages. In the context of a media company’s microblogging, Gong et al. (2017) determined that the firm’s direct tweets and influential retweets both had a positive effect on the viewing of the company’s shows, but in different ways. Chung et al. (2020) found that the timely response to negative customer messages is associated with increased firm market performance. Regarding FGC content analysis, only Goh et al. (2013) addressed this issue, finding that the effect of FGC is related to persuasive content, measured by valence. We believe that content analysis could provide many insights, given the availability of rich firm social media content. Therefore, in this study, we conducted content analysis to answer the “how” question and investigated firms’ effective content strategy with respect to the nature of the industry (or firm).

Overall, the limited studies in this substream of research have specifically examined neither the impact of FGC on firm performance in terms of industry differences nor how to manage FGC according to the nature of the industry. Due to the nature of the industry (or firm), social media content for different types of products may be perceived differently by consumers and may therefore influence consumers differently. In this study, we examine the sales impact of FGC and the industry difference, taking product involvement as a means to differentiate industries and gain insights into industry nature. We also investigate how FGC can be effectively managed with a focus on content analysis.

## Product Involvement

Product involvement is commonly defined as the consumer’s enduring perceptions of the importance of the product category, based on the consumer’s inherent needs, values, and interests (Zaichkowsky, 1985). Product involvement is a causal or motivating variable with a number of consequences for consumers’ purchase behavior and communication patterns. Taking consumers as a group, products differ in their tendency to motivate involvement. Low-involvement products refer to products that most consumers perceive as having little relevance to their core values and the product class and are associated with low brand commitment, whereas high-involvement products refer to products of high value to consumers or to products that represent a high level of purchase risk for consumers (Lastovicka, 1979). Typically, low-involvement products are associated with a low risk of purchase failure or have a low price tag, whereas highinvolvement products have a high price tag (Assael, 1984).

Studies have shown that product involvement influences the consumer decision-making process in terms of consumers’ willingness to search for information about the product, their perception of alternatives in the same product category, and brand loyalty (Belk, 1982; Quester & Lin, 2003; Vann, 1984; Zaichkowsky, 1985). Consumers typically go through different decision-making processes based on product involvement (Assael, 1984; Engel et al., 1995). For instance, the purchase of high-involvement products involves more information searching due to the high value or high-risk nature of the product (Zaichkowsky, 1985). Based on the literature, we believe that product involvement is a useful classification for understanding the effect of firm social media engagement, as social media content may influence certain stages of the consumer decision-making process pertinent to product involvement.

Researchers have developed product involvement measurements (Bloch, 1981; Zaichkowsky, 1985) such as the Personal Involvement Inventory (PII; Zaichkowsky, 1985). Based on the PII, cosmetics products are high involvement (Brisoux & Cheron, 1990) because they directly relate to consumer self-image and have many attributes that make it difficult to decide which item to purchase (Park & Moon, 2003; Suh & Youjae, 2006), whereas stationery goods (such as pens) are low involvement (Mittal, 1989) because they are consumable and considerable involvement is not needed for their purchase (Martin, 1998). In this study, we follow these identifications.

Prior literature (e.g., Gu et al., 2012) has examined the influence of product involvement on the effect of word of mouth (WOM). Gu et al. (2012) found that for firms selling low-involvement products, internal WOM has a greater influence on sales than external WOM, whereas for those selling high-involvement products, internal WOM has limited influence and external WOM has a significant impact on sales. However, the role of product involvement in the context of social media has not been examined.

In this study, by interactively providing product and brand information, we take the product involvement perspective to discern the extent to which firm social media engagement influences the consumer purchase decision for products of different levels of involvement. Following the literature, we chose cosmetics products as the high-involvement product and stationery goods as the low-involvement product.

## Hypothesis Development

We develop hypotheses for the relationship between firm social media engagement and sales performance based on consumer decision process theory (Engel et al., 1995) and social presence theory (Short et al., 1976). The consumer decision-making process refers to consumers’ process when purchasing a product and can be divided into five stages: need recognition, information searching, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision, and postpurchase behavior (Engel et al., 1995). By actively sharing product information and interacting with consumers, firm social media engagement can create social presence, which may affect one or more stages of the consumer decision-making process (Short et al., 1976) and may motivate consumers to make purchases and thus improve sales performance (O’Boyle, 2014).

## Firm Social Media Adoption and Sales Performance

In the consumer decision-making process, firm social media may act as an important source of information, which is likely more influential in the stages of information searching, the evaluation of alternatives, and postpurchase behavior. The information richness and interactivity of social media can create social presence, while the nature of the social network reinforces the influence. Overall, firm social media may increase the likelihood that firms’ products will be included in consumers’ consideration set, thereby impacting consumers’ purchase decisions.

Information searching is a critical stage in the decisionmaking process of most consumers (e.g., Assael, 1984; Punj & Staelin, 1983). Once they have identified a need, consumers start to search for the right product. Content generated by firms is an important information source for consumer searching, as it serves as the single authoritative source of product and brand information (Cole et al., 2011). Firm social media provides a significant amount of content about the brand and its products and may include product functions and qualities, new releases, and promotions. Firm social media also shares trend and product use tips that attract and educate consumers. In social media, such information can reach a larger audience and spread faster due to the network effect (Kwak et al., 2010). Furthermore, social media has the capability to create social presence (based on the sociability, interactivity, and personalization it facilitates), which can transmit rich information (Fulk et al., 1987). Therefore, at the stage of information searching, social media FGC can attract more consumers by providing rich information, eliciting their interest, and stimulating demand.

The evaluation of alternatives stage is when consumers decide which product attribute is important (Engel et al., 1995). In the evaluation process, consumers are looking for tangible and reliable information that will allow them to make a choice. Firm social media content, especially concrete content about product features, adds value to the evaluation process and can differentiate the focal firm from its competitors. In addition, the interactive feature of social media can effectively address consumers’ purchase concerns. Together, these features can enhance consumers’ product recognition and reduce uncertainties in the consumer decision-making process (Punj & Staelin, 1983).

When consumers evaluate alternatives, they also consider products from brands with which they have intimate relationships. Social media FGC can foster such intimate relationships by creating social presence (Kim et al. 2020). Specifically, firm social media may act as a social actor by posting “socially rich” content on the brand community page and communicating with consumers. Interactions between the firm and consumers can engender intimacy and immediacy, thereby forming social presence (Han et al., 2015). High levels of social presence can cause consumers to perceive a feeling of human contact, sociability, warmth, and sensitivity (Short et al., 1976), which contributes to positive consumer attitudes and influences aspects such as brand attitude, store image, and purchase intentions (Cyr et al., 2007; Moon et al., 2013).

In the postpurchase stage, firm social media can influence consumer behavior by facilitating the sharing of shopping and product use experiences, positive or negative, which can impact other consumers’ purchase intentions. Also, the social presence elicited by FGC can generate brand recall and keep the brand at the top of consumers’ minds, which has been shown to improve brand loyalty and lead to repurchase (Kumar & Benbasat, 2002; Lu et al., 2016).

Based on the above discussion, firm social media can improve sales performance by influencing the consumer decisionmaking process. Accordingly, we propose the following:

H1: Firm social media adoption has a positive impact on sales performance.

## Industry Differences in the Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Adoption: The Role of Product Involvement

Social media has become a significant channel for firms to influence consumer purchase decisions. This effect, however, depends on the nature of the industry. Product involvement is a critical element that shapes the way consumers search for, process, and save information (e.g., Bloch et al., 1986; Zaichkowsky, 1985) and may result in different firm social media engagement outcomes. Below, we discuss how product involvement causes different types of consumer information search and information processing in the decision-making process and how firm social media can influence the process.

First, consumer information search can be classified into two categories, i.e., ongoing and prepurchase (Bloch et al., 1986). For both types of information search, consumer behavior is subject to the type of product, specifically product involvement (Bloch et al., 1986), and firm social media serves as an important source of information. Ongoing search is defined as information-seeking activities that are independent of specific purchase needs or decisions (Bloch et al., 1986). Researchers have found that for an ongoing search, product involvement is an important prerequisite and may have a significant influence on consumer purchase behavior (Bloch et al., 1986). When looking for high-involvement products, consumers need more information to eliminate uncertainties and make decisions (Zaichkowsky, 1985). Therefore, they have a high motivation to participate in ongoing search activities, including following firm social media. If managed well, firm social media content can help create product awareness, facilitate interactions with consumers, and stimulate demand. Because ongoing search has no specific target, consumers may browse social media content broadly to obtain a good understanding of products and the brand, leading to a higher likelihood of purchase. For lowinvolvement products, consumers need less information and therefore have a low motivation to follow firm social media content. The ongoing search is easier and faster to complete, meaning that firm social media likely has less impact on the sales of low-involvement products.

Different from ongoing search that has no specific target, prepurchase search refers to search activities that facilitate specific purchase decision-making (Bloch et al., 1986). Such searches are intermittent but require focused information. Firm social media content can provide detailed product information, and the social media feature can address consumers’ specific queries, enhancing consumer purchase intentions. Prepurchase search is also influenced by product involvement (Bloch et al., 1986). Involvement theory suggests that product involvement can influence the degree of information searching and processing for the product (Belk, 1982; Quester & Lin, 2003; Vann, 1984; Zaichkowsky, 1985). Typically, consumers request more information when purchasing high-involvement products.

Second, for information processing, the effect of firm social media is also subject to the level of product involvement. According to the elaboration likelihood model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), the effectiveness of persuasion depends on information processing, for which there are two major routes: central and peripheral. Under the central route, when consumers have a high level of motivation and ability, they are willing to engage in considerable cognitive processing effort, called high elaboration likelihood, to evaluate productrelated information. If the product and firm-related information are convincing, consumers will develop positive attitudes and purchase intentions toward the product and brand. Under the peripheral route, however, consumers are either unwilling or unable to exert effort to process information and instead focus on peripheral cues, such as brands or visuals.

In a high-involvement situation, consumers are highly motivated to process social media content information and are thus likely to take the central route. Consumers tend to obtain more information from multiple sources to compare different products for their purchase decisions (Gu et al., 2012). In a high-involvement situation, they are more likely to request more information through social media to see detailed content and will make their decision after scrutinizing the received content. Firm social media thus affects consumer behavior through cognition, thereby influencing the purchase decision.

In contrast, in a low-involvement situation, consumers have little motivation to process firm social media content. Therefore, consumers tend to process information under the peripheral route. They are less likely to request more information and more likely to focus on peripheral information, such as product appearance or the brand itself, to make purchase decisions. Therefore, in this case, firm social media has less influence on consumers’ purchase decisions. Based on the above, we propose:

H2: Social media adoption has a greater impact on sales performance for firms that sell high-involvement products.

## Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Efforts

On social media platforms, firms make efforts to communicate with consumers. At its core, FGC is a process of computer-mediated communications in which interactions between the sender and receiver can change the receiver’s behavior or attitudes (Merton & Merton, 1968). Thus, we develop hypotheses for the relationships between firm social media efforts and sales performance based on communication theory (Krone et al., 1987; Mohr & Nevin, 1990). According to communication theory, the sender (firm), receiver (consumer), content, and effect are the main components of communication. Aspects of communication, including extensiveness, intensiveness, and content, all influence communication effectiveness (Mohr & Nevin, 1990). Therefore, firm social media efforts associated with these aspects may impact sales.

Extensive and intensive social media FGC help firms improve consumer awareness (Lou & Yuan, 2019; Hoyer & Brown, 1990), which is an initial step in the consumer decisionmaking process and a prerequisite for reaching subsequent stages (Lavidge & Steiner, 1961). In the context of social media, the extensiveness and intensiveness of communications are measured by the number of followers and the number of blog postings, respectively (Starbird & Palen, 2012; Mohr & Nevin, 1990).

Firms create content on social media platforms to attract consumers to follow their accounts. In social media, firms act as an information-source type of user that operates as the hub of a social network and usually has a large number of followers (Java et al., 2007). According to information diffusion, hubs are central to information dissemination in any network (Balcan et al., 2009), and information distributed from hubs can spread to a broader population (Starbird & Palen, 2012). Followers can then spread FGC to their friends, creating a ripple effect. The higher the degree of the hub (i.e., the number of followers connected to the hub), the greater the consumer awareness generated by firm social media. With greater consumer awareness, the chance that consumers will buy from the firm is higher. In addition, firm social media not only disseminates information but also interacts with consumers, e.g., by answering their questions, thus increasing the likelihood of having more loyal consumers. This leads to a stronger sales impact when considering loyal consumers’ contributions and their recommendations to other consumers on social media (Gremler & Brown, 1999). We therefore propose:

H3: Firm social media efforts with more followers are associated with better sales performance.

When consumers follow a firm’s social media page, they are continuously exposed to FGC. The volume of content is very important in such a channel because it transmits information, fosters decision-making, and encourages commitment and loyalty (Mohr & Nevin, 1990). Frequent firm communication with consumers can increase consumer awareness and potentially lead to improvements in firm performance. Furthermore, under continuous exposure to FGC, consumers’ attitudes toward a brand can be positively changed (Grimes & Kitchen, 2007; Rahman & Soesilo, 2018; Janiszewski, 1993), leading to the mere exposure effect (Zajonc, 1968). The mere exposure effect suggests that the mere repeated exposure to a stimulus object (even if unfamiliar) can enhance an individual’s attitude toward the object (Zajonc, 1968). On social media, the number of blog postings captures the intensity of communications between the firm and consumers and has a positive relationship with consumer awareness and product sales (Rui et al., 2013). An effective social media effort may involve more blog postings to maintain regular consumer interaction, generate the mere exposure effect, and strengthen the bond between the brand and consumers. Overall, consumers are more likely to make purchase decisions when they have a closer relationship with the firm (Rishika et al., 2013). Therefore, we propose:

H4: Firm social media efforts with more blog postings are associated with better sales performance.

While firm efforts in terms of the number of followers and blog postings affect consumer awareness, firm social media content serves as an important source of information for consumers and can influence consumer need recognition and product evaluation (Vakratsas & Ambler, 1999). Prior studies have noted that different content categories have different impacts on consumer behavior (e.g., Kumar et al., 2016; Rishika et al., 2013). In this study, we analyze the impact of FGC content types on sales performance.

Specifically, content can be classified as primarily informative or persuasive (e.g., Abernethy & Franke, 1996; Bertrand et al., 2010; Liaukonyte et al., 2015). Informative content refers to factual data on the nature and function of a product or service and can reduce uncertainty (Abernethy & Franke, 1996), whereas persuasive content assumes that consumers already have an understanding of the nature and function of the product but need to be convinced of the benefits that distinguish it from alternatives in the market (Armstrong, 2010; Berger & Milkman, 2012). In this study, we analyze which type of content is more effective for sales on social media platforms.

In their decision-making process, consumers are looking for tangible and reliable information. However, markets are characterized by imperfect information, which may deter consumers from learning about a product’s existence, quality, and characteristics. Informative content on social media platforms alleviates information deficiency, as consumers can receive detailed and additional information (e.g., origins, performance) at no cost. Informative content can influence multiple stages of the consumer decision-making process by enhancing consumers’ product recognition, facilitating information searching and the evaluation of alternatives, and thus increasing purchase intentions. For instance, prior studies have found that providing informative content leads to more immediate online brand searches, while persuasive content has no effect (Chandrasekaran et al., 2018; Du et al., 2019). In addition, informative content helps to reduce uncertainty (Abernethy & Franke, 1996), as it provides facts or cues about specific aspects of a product and is objective rather than subjective (Resnik & Stern, 1977). Furthermore, brand informative content on social media could also have a positive effect on consumer brand attitude, especially in the highinvolvement scenario (Lou et al., 2019).

In contrast, persuasive content seeks to induce needs and create product differentiation, influencing the consumer evaluation of alternatives in the decision-making process. The processing of persuasive content is a subjective consumer-pertinent process, whereas that of informative content is an objective productpertinent process. It is relatively difficult to alter consumers’ tastes and persuade them to buy. Furthermore, compared to consumer-initiated persuasive content, firm-initiated persuasive content is less convincing, especially in the e-commerce context we study. Therefore, we propose:

H5: Firm informative social media content is associated with better sales performance than persuasive content.

## Data and Model Specifications

Data in this study are obtained from Taobao (www.taobao.com). Taobao has the most extensive collection of products and services and the highest gross merchandise volume of any platform, with about 10 million sellers. Sellers on Taobao open and operate their own stores and market their products on social media platforms to improve performance. However, since one of the most popular third-party social media platforms blocked Taobao’s external link, creating fierce competition (Erisman, 2017), Taobao launched Weitao, its own seller-oriented microblogging social media function, on its ecommerce platform in November 2013. Taobao stores can choose to open a Weitao account to market their stores and products. In this case, Taobao stores are attached to a Weitao account that can be used to post microblogs to connect with consumers, which thereby creates their own brand community. Consumers that follow Weitao accounts receive corresponding store updates. However, Weitao is merely the store’s social media channel—all transactions are still conducted through the Taobao store.

The launch of Weitao enabled a quasi-experiment to examine the effect of Weitao adoption on store sales performance. We therefore employed a DID method to quantify this effect by examining sales differences from pre- to post-Weitao adoption and between stores with and without Weitao. To deal with the self-selection issue, we used a propensity score matching (PSM) method to match the treatment group (stores with Weitao) with a control group (stores without Weitao) (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). To ensure the validity of the DID approach, we examined differences between these two groups in pretreatment variables and inspected the pretreatment trends of store sales. We further constructed instrument variables to model the store’s decision to open a Weitao account and used a control function model to deal with any hidden bias issue due to unobservables in the PSM.

In addition to the overall impact of store social media adoption on sales performance, we examined industry differences by comparing high- and low-involvement industries. We also investigated how to manage social media efforts, with respect to industry nature, to achieve better firm sales performance. The analyses include content analysis for firm-level content types, a first differencing model for the sales effect of social media efforts (controlling for firm heterogeneity), instrumental variables, and the control function method for endogeneity.

## Data

The first step of our data collection procedure was to obtain all stores that opened a Weitao account. We collected stores in two industries, cosmetics and stationery, with cosmetics representing a high-involvement industry and stationery representing a low-involvement industry, following the literature on product involvement discussed above. We searched the keywords “cosmetics” and “stationery,” respectively, on the Weitao mobile search page and obtained a list of all Weitao accounts. <sup>6</sup> The search results gave us approximately 100 pages, representing all relevant Weitao accounts at that time. Note that Weitao was launched in November 2013. In the beginning, only a few stores chose to open a Weitao account. It took several months until a reasonable number of stores opened Weitao accounts. By observing this trend, we collected a sample of stores that opened Weitao accounts between April 2014 and June 2014. We dropped 934 cosmetics and 213 stationery Weitao accounts from Tmall (B2C) stores and kept only Weitao accounts from Taobao stores to avoid systematic bias between the two platforms. <sup>7</sup> For the final Weitao account list, we retrieved information from each Weitao account, including the Weitao account opening date, the store webpage attached to it, all blog postings, and the number of Weitao followers.

Based on the store webpage information retrieved from the Weitao account, we obtained the store profile, including the store name, store opening date, store ratings (including store description, service, and logistics ratings), accumulated store credits, the number of consumers who bookmarked the store, the total number of comments that the store received from the store’s opening to six months prior to when the opening of their Weitao account, and the number of comments that the store received in the previous month and in the previous week. Our final sample consisted of 422 cosmetics stores and 125 stationery stores, i.e., our treatment group, and no store sold both cosmetics products and stationery goods.

To obtain a pool for the control group to use with PSM, we searched the same keywords, “cosmetics” and “stationery,” on the Taobao PC store search page, refined search criteria to Taobao stores only, and recorded all search results. We dropped all stores that had opened a Weitao account from our sample, leaving 7,489 cosmetics stores and 4,134 stationery stores. These stores comprised the pool for the control group. We then retrieved the store profile from the store webpage, as we did for the treatment group.

Finally, we needed data for the outcome variable, i.e., store sales. Taobao does not provide real-time sales information on the store’s webpage, it only shows sales for the previous six months (in terms of the number of transactions), the previous month, and the previous week. Because we searched Weitao for newly opened accounts, data collection at that time point gave us store sales data for the six months (also one month and one week) prior to the opening of the Weitao account. We waited one week, one month, and six months to collect data again to obtain the respective store sales after they opened their Weitao accounts. Using this approach, we retrieved sales data for both the treatment group and the pool for control group. We also waited six months to collect the store profile and the information on the number of blog postings, the content of blog postings, and the number of followers from the Weitao account.

## Content Coding Process

We followed a content classification procedure to classify all of the collected Weitao blog postings, yielding a total of 4,989 blog postings (1,325 from stationery stores and 3,664 from cosmetics stores) in the six months after the Weitao account opened (note that the platform had a restriction on the maximum number of blog postings a store could post). The procedure included five steps: the orientation period, development of the training set, text mining on the training set, content classification, and factor analysis. We provide a detailed description of the coding procedure in Appendix B.

From the content-coding process, we obtained three factors that we labeled as informative, persuasive, and promotional. Promotional content, which includes promotions and deals, is a new factor (the literature primarily discusses informative and persuasive content). Based on our observation, promotional content is frequently used in Chinese social media marketing, especially in the e-commerce context. We conducted validity checks for the proposed three factors. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was 0.809, indicating a high level of validity. The common variances for all attributes were higher than 0.4—suggesting that the information of all attributes was effectively extracted. We also checked the factor loading coefficients between each attribute and its corresponding factor. All coefficients were higher than 0.4, confirming the validity (Chan & Idris, 2017). The corresponding relationship between content types and attributes is shown in Table B1.

Meanwhile, the two industry subsamples, cosmetics and stationery, showed different patterns of content posting. Specifically, the cosmetics stores posted more informative content—such as information about product origins, quality, performance, and user experience—than stationery stores. Stationery stores posted more promotional content than cosmetics stores and had significantly higher scores for two promotional attributes, namely price and delivery. For persuasive content, we found no significant difference between the two subsamples for either of the two persuasive attributes.

For each blog posting, we calculated the mean value of attributes for each type of content. Then, we calculated the mean value of blog postings at the store level and used them as the values of the store FGC content factors in the sales effect analysis. ## Empirical Specifications

## Propensity Score Matching

The objective of our PSM was to match the treatment group with a control group with similar covariate distributions before the store opened a Weitao account. The process involved two steps. The first step modeled the store’s decision to open a Weitao account as a function of store pretreatment covariates. We used a generalized boosted regression model (GBM) to estimate propensity scores (McCaffrey et al., 2004) because this algorithm can adaptively choose variables to include in the propensity score prediction model, regardless of function specification.<sup>8</sup> The second step performed matching based on the estimated propensity scores. We used 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching such that each store i in the treatment group was matched with a store in the pool of the control group with the smallest difference in the estimated propensity score from store i. This is the most effective method for settings in which the aim is to select samples for studying the follow-up period (Stuart, 2010), which fits our research setting. Note that the purpose and advantage of using propensity score matching are to ensure that the conditional distribution of the covariates is independent of the treatment assignment and to avoid the “curse of dimensionality” (Caliendo & Kopeinig, 2008). In this sense, the treatment group and the matched control group should have the closest propensity scores, although it is possible that a covariate could experience reduced balance after matching. Standardized bias and other balance checks are presented in the later empirical results section. We also used various other matching algorithms as robustness checks.

Our GBM specification is in Equation (1). The estimate $\hat { g } ^ { * } ( x )$ adds all predicting variables’ $g ^ { * } ( x )$ together. $g ^ { * } ( x )$ represents the sum of all regression trees that split from a predicting variable x. For example, $g _ { 1 } ^ { * } ( \ln s h o \bar { p } o p e n t i m e )$ refers to all regression trees that split from lnshopopentime and its following interactions with other predicting variables. $\hat { g } ( X )$ is then used to model the log-odds of treatment assignment $g ( X ) { = } { \log } \{ p ( X ) / [ 1 - p ( X ) ] \}$ to obtain the propensity score p(X).

$$
\begin{array}{c} \hat {g} (X) = g _ {0} + g _ {1} ^ {*} (\ln \text { shopopentime }) + g _ {2} ^ {*} (\ln \text { credit }) + g _ {3} ^ {*} (\text { score }) + g _ {4} ^ {*} (\ln \text { shopcollection }) \\ + g _ {5} ^ {*} (\ln \text { bhalfcomment }) + g _ {6} ^ {*} (\ln \text { monthcomment }) + g _ {7} ^ {*} (\ln \text { weekcomment }). \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

A set of predicting variables for the store’s decision to open a Weitao account is included in the model of Equation $( 1 ) . ^ { 9 }$ To determine these independent variables, we scrutinized Taobao store information. We believe that the following observed variables are related to both the store’s opening of a Weitao account and the store sales (Rubin & Thomas, 1996). These variables represent different aspects of a store, including age (lnshopopentime, measured by the logarithm of the store’s opening date), which reflects a store’s attitude toward new functions; store experience (lncredit, measured by the logarithm of accumulated store credit since the store opening); store quality (score, which is the first component score of principle component analysis on the three highly correlated store ratings—store description rating, service rating, and logistics rating); store consumer base (lnshopcollection, measured by the logarithm of the number of consumers who have bookmarked the store), which could potentially follow the store’s Weitao account; store activity level, measured by the number of reviews (take log) that a store received from its buyers about their transactions during three time periods—i.e., from the store’s opening to six months before the opening of their Weitao account (lnbhalfcomment), the month prior to the opening of their Weitao account (lnmonthcomment), and the week prior to the opening of their Weitao account (lnweekcomment). We found that active stores were more likely to open a Weitao account.

## Difference-in-Differences Analysis for the Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Adoption

After PSM, we conducted a DID analysis to examine the sales effect of Weitao adoption. The analysis is at the firm level, (store) with the model specification shown below, and is estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \text { halfyearsales} _ {i j t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { Ifweitao } _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} \text { Aweitao } _ {i j t} \\ + \beta_ {3} \text { Ifweitao } _ {i j} \times \text { Aweitao } _ {i j t} + \Theta C V _ {i j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In Equation (2), i denotes a matched pair of stores, j denotes a store from the treatment group or control group, and t denotes the time period (we use six months before and six months after the store opened a Weitao account). The dependent variable lnhalfyearsales<sub>ijt</sub> denotes the sales that the store received during the six months before or after the store opened a Weitao account. We think that six months is suitable for examining the firm-level Weitao effect. Later, we conducted a dynamic effect analysis from one week to one month to six months after the opening of a Weitao account. Key independent variables included Ifweitao<sub>ij</sub>, a dummy variable that equals 1 if store i opened a Weitao account and 0 otherwise; $A w e i t a o _ { i j t } ,$ a dummy variable that equals 1 for the period after a store opened a Weitao account and 0 for the period before; and the interaction term $I f w e i t a o _ { i j } \times A w e i t a o _ { i j t }$ , which gives the impact of social media adoption on store sales performance, and its coefficient ꞵ<sub>3</sub> is our focus. We included a variety of control variables, $C V _ { i j t } ,$ which influenced store sales: lnshopopentime, lncredit, score, lnshopcollection, lnbhalfcomment, lnmonthcomment, and lnweekcomment (as described above for Equation 1). We conducted DID analysis on both the full sample and subsamples of high- and low-involvement industries.

## First Differencing Model for Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Efforts

To investigate how to manage social media efforts to achieve better firm sales performance (i.e., H3-H5), we constructed models for the examination of the number of Weitao followers, number of blog postings, and blog posting content types. These analyses were conducted on the treatment group that adopted Weitao. We also analyzed industry differences in the sales effect of social media efforts between high- and lowinvolvement industries. To control for firm heterogeneity, we used a first differencing model.

Specifically, based on the main model (Equation 2), there can be equations for two time periods, i.e., the six months before Weitao account opening (Aweitao = 0) and the six months after Weitao account opening (Aweitao = 1). Taking the difference between the two equations, for store i in the treatment group, gives us the first differencing model:

$$
\Delta \ln \text { halfyearsales } _ {i} = \beta_ {2} + \beta_ {3} + \Theta \Delta C V _ {i} + \Delta \varepsilon_ {i}.\tag{3}
$$

In Equation (3), the dependent variable lnhalfyearsales<sub>i</sub> is the sales difference between the six months after a store opened a Weitao account and the six months before. Control variables (CVs) are the first difference of the same control variables as in Equation (1), including lncredit<sub>i</sub>, score<sub>i</sub>, ∆lnshopcollectioni, ∆lnbhalfcommenti, ∆lnmonthcommenti and lnweekcomment<sub>i</sub>, except that lnshopopentime<sub>i</sub> was eliminated by first differencing.

Based on Equation (3), we added terms of social media efforts, i.e., the number of Weitao followers (take log) lnfollower<sub>i</sub>, the number of blog postings weitaonum<sub>i</sub>, and Weitao content types (informative<sub>i</sub>, persuasive<sub>i</sub>, and promotional<sub>i</sub>), to capture their sales effect. Model specifications are shown below:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta \ln \text { halfyearsales } _ {i} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \ln \text { follower } _ {i} + \alpha_ {2} \Delta \ln \text { credit } _ {i} + \alpha_ {3} \Delta \ln \text { bhalfcomment } _ {i} \\ & + \alpha_ {4} \Delta \ln \text { monthcomment } _ {i} + \alpha_ {5} \Delta \ln \text { weekcomment } _ {i} + \alpha_ {6} \Delta \text { score } _ {i} + \eta_ {i}, \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \Delta \ln \text {halfyearsales} _ {i} = \chi_ {0} + \chi_ {1} \text {weitaonum} _ {i} + \chi_ {2} \Delta \ln \text {credit} _ {i} + \chi_ {3} \Delta \ln \text {bhalfcomment} _ {i} \\ + \chi_ {4} \Delta \ln \text {monthcomment} _ {i} + \chi_ {5} \Delta \ln \text {weekcomment} _ {i} + \chi_ {6} \Delta \text {score} _ {i} + \mu_ {i}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \Delta \ln \text { halfyearsales } _ {i} = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} \text { informative } _ {i} + \delta_ {2} \text { persuasive } _ {i} + \delta_ {3} \text { promotional } _ {i} \\ \quad + \delta_ {4} \text { weitaonum } _ {i} + \delta_ {5} \Delta \ln \text { credit } _ {i} + \delta_ {6} \Delta \ln \text { bhalfcomment } _ {i} \\ \quad + \delta_ {7} \Delta \ln \text { monthcomment } _ {i} + \delta_ {8} \Delta \ln \text { weekcomment } _ {i} + \delta_ {9} \Delta \text { score } _ {i} + \sigma_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

Specifically, for the sales effect of the number of Weitao followers (Equation 4), coefficient $\alpha _ { 1 }$ is our focus. For the sales effect of blog postings, we conducted hierarchical regression to examine the effect of the number of blog postings (Equation 5) and the effect of blog postings’ content types (Equation 6). Therefore, coefficients $\mathbb { \chi } _ { 1 }$ and $\delta _ { 1 } - \delta _ { 4 }$ are our focus.

We constructed Equations (5)-(6) separately from Equation (4) due to the different distributions of the key variables. The number of Weitao followers, lnfollower<sub>i</sub>, follows a normal distribution, and we used OLS regression in the first step of the control function method (see below for details). In contrast, for the number of blog postings, weitaonum<sub>i</sub>, which is overdispersed (i.e., a much greater variance than its mean), we used a generalized negative binomial model.

The above models are subject to the endogeneity issue. That is, the key independent variables, i.e., the number of Weitao followers and the number of blog postings, can be influenced by store sales, i.e., the dependent variable. To address this issue, we constructed instrumental variables. Specifically, we used store i’s competitors’ number of Weitao followers, i.e., lnfolloweriv<sub>i</sub>, as the IVs for store i’s number of Weitao followers and the competitors’ number of blog postings, i.e., weitaonumiv<sub>i</sub>, as the IVs for store i’s number of blog postings. The definition of “competitors” and the construction process of the two sets of IVs are as follows. First, we reviewed the search results when we searched cosmetics and stationery stores on Weitao’s mobile search page for the treatment group. Stores with similar rankings in the search results are similar in many aspects, including store age, level of activity, credits, and ratings. Thus, they are competitors. Here, we define the three stores that were listed right above the focal store i in the search results and the three stores that were right below store i as “competitors.” Second, we used the number of Weitao followers of these six competing stores as the IVs for store i’s number of Weitao followers. That is, there are six IVs for the endogenous variable lnfollower<sub>i.</sub> Similarly, we used the number of blog postings of these six competitors as the IVs for store i’s number of blog postings.

The instrumental variables fulfill both requirements (Wooldridge, 2002). First, the competitors’ number of Weitao followers (and blog postings) are highly correlated with the focal store i’s number of Weitao followers (and blog postings) due to the similarity and competition among competitors. For our sample, the model-free evidence shows significant correlations (p < 0.001) between the IVs and store i’s number of Weitao followers (and blog postings). Second, when applying the six IVs individually, some competitor stores (e.g., the third one above the focal store and the third one below) are different from the focal store i; thus, their numbers of followers and blog postings would be expected to have limited influence on the focal store i’s sales. We conducted the weak IV test and the Sargan overidentifying test for the two sets of IVs, and the results (available upon request) show that both sets are valid.

To estimate Equations (4)-(6) using these two sets of instrumental variables, we conducted the control function method (Wooldridge, 2015). For example, for the control function estimation procedure for Equation (5) to determine the number of blog postings, we first used a generalized negative binomial model to estimate Equation (7) in order to obtain $\widehat { \omega } _ { i }$ (we used OLS for the number of followers); then, we added $\widehat { \omega } _ { i }$ to Equation (5) and estimated the coefficients using OLS. The estimation procedures for Equations (4) and (6) are the same as that for Equation (5).

$$
\begin{array}{c} w e i t a o n u m _ {i} = \exp (\varphi_ {0} + \varphi_ {1} w e i t a o n u m i v _ {i} + \varphi_ {2} \Delta \ln c r e d i t _ {i} + \varphi_ {3} \Delta \ln b h a l f c o m m e n t _ {i} \\ + \varphi_ {4} \Delta \ln m o n t h c o m m e n t _ {i} + \varphi_ {5} \Delta \ln w e e k c o m m e n t _ {i} + \varphi_ {6} \Delta s c o r e _ {i} + \omega_ {i}). \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

## PSM Results

We first report GBM propensity score estimation results along with fit statistics. Table 3 shows the relative influence of each predicting variable. As can be seen, the store activity level (particularly lnbhalfcomment and lnmonthcomment) and store age (lnshopopentime) are more powerful in predicting a store’s decision to open a Weitao account for both the cosmetics and stationery subsamples.

We performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching based on estimated propensity scores to obtain the control group. After matching, we conducted a series of examinations to ensure the matching quality. First, we compared the “standardized bias” before and after matching for each predicting variable in the GBM. The standardized bias is the percentage difference of the sample means between the treatment and control groups divided by the square root of the average of sample variances of the treatment and control groups (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1985). Compared with statistical inference such as t-tests, our method is more appropriate to measure the matching quality because it is a quantity similar to the effect size and is not influenced by sample size (Harder et al., 2010). As can be seen in Table 4, the matched groups are balanced as the standardized bias gets smaller. For matching to be acceptable, the absolute standardized bias after matching should be less than 20% (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1985). For our sample, the bias is less than 18% for all predicting variables, which indicates that there is no significant pretreatment difference between the treatment and control groups.

Second, we performed an F-test on the joint significance of all covariates and a pseudo $R ^ { 2 }$ before and after matching to measure the overall balance. Sianesi (2004) suggested reestimating the propensity score on the matched sample and comparing the pseudo $R ^ { 2 }$ with that derived before matching. After matching, there should be no systematic difference in the distribution of the covariates between the two groups. Therefore, the pseudo $R ^ { 2 }$ after matching should be fairly low. As shown in Table 5, this is true for both the cosmetics and stationery subsamples. The results of the F-tests point in the same direction, indicating a joint significance of all regressors before but not after matching. This indicates that the matched sample is much more balanced than the original sample.

Third, we conducted a stratification test, following Dehejia and Wahba (2002) to ensure that the GBM model specification is appropriate for estimating the propensity of a store to open a Weitao account. The stratification test procedure is as follows. We divided each subsample into four intervals of the same length based on the estimated propensity scores. The interval was further split into halves if any of the covariates for the store’s decision to open a Weitao account were significantly different between the treatment and the control groups within the interval. This yielded 11 intervals for the cosmetics subsample and 8 intervals for the stationery subsample. The results (not presented due to space limits but available upon request) show that there is no significant difference between the treatment and control groups within each interval for all covariates.

Fourth, we performed a visual check by comparing the kernel density distribution of propensity scores before and after matching, as shown in Figure 1. As can be seen, there are significant differences between the treatment and control groups before matching but the differences decrease considerably after matching.

Fifth, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess the PSM assumption of ignorable treatment assignment, following Rosenbaum (2002) and McCaffrey et al. (2004). We used sensitivity analysis to check whether there was a hidden bias issue in the PSM, i.e., whether there were unobserved variables related to whether a store opened a Weitao account.

The presence of a hidden bias would mean that stores with the same value as the covariate vector would have different odds of opening a Weitao account. To conduct the sensitivity analysis, we defined the sensitivity factor (i.e., the strength of hidden bias) as .  = 1 means that a hidden bias is present at the current level; thus, larger values of  indicate a larger hidden bias. We let  increase from 1 to see how the outcome changed. If inferences about the probability of opening a Weitao account remained unchanged as  increases, we can conclude that our results are robust even if it were possible to obtain and account for additional variables. According to Lin et al. (1998), if results hold when  is equal to or greater than 2, confidence in the results should be quite strong. Based on our sensitivity analysis, the values of  are 2.1 for the stationery subsample and 1.3 for the cosmetics subsample, suggesting that there is no hidden bias issue in the stationery subsample but that there may be a hidden bias problem in the cosmetics subsample. We solved this problem using the control function method (see description in the next section).

Finally, it is important to examine pretreatment differences between the treatment and control groups because the validity of the DID approach hinges on the comparability of the two groups. In addition to the standardized bias analysis and the visual check described above, we conducted a pretreatment trend analysis. We obtained observations of store sales at seven time points, i.e., accumulated store sales at six months, one month, and one week before the Weitao account was opened; at the time the store opened the Weitao account; and one week, one month, and six months after the Weitao account was opened. Taking store sales at the first point as the baseline, the points in Figure 2 represent the sales trend over the following time points for the treatment and control groups; the vertical dashed line indicates the time the store opened the Weitao account. We present 95% confidence intervals of the six time points for both groups. Along with the results of ttests, we see in Figure 2 that for the cosmetics subsample, the difference in store sales between the treatment and control groups is insignificant before the Weitao account openings but becomes significant after Weitao account openings (the confidence intervals of two groups have less overlap after Weitao account openings).

## DID Results for Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Adoption

Our DID results for the sales effect of firm Weitao adoption for the full sample are presented in Table 6. Model fit $( R ^ { 2 } )$ is 0.941. Our focus is the coefficient of the interaction term IfweitaoAweitao, which is 0.189 and significant at the 1% level, indicating a positive and significant Weitao sales effect. The results for other variables are consistent with expectations. We computed the sales elasticity of firm social media adoption, according to the Kennedy (1981) method. The sales elasticity for the full sample is 20.67, which means that stores have a mean 20.67% sales increase after opening the Weitao account. Overall, H1 is supported.

We then conducted the subsample analysis for the industry difference based on product involvement. To address the previously identified hidden bias issue in PSM, we used the control function method, following Wooldridge (2015). In the situation of hidden bias, a store’s decision to open a Weitao account, i.e., $I f w e i t a o _ { i }$ , can be written as a function (see Equation 8) of all exogenous CVs that influence both the store’s decision to open a Weitao account and store sales. The sales that the store received before and after opening the Weitao account can be formulated as Equation (9).

$$
I f w e i t a o _ {i} = 1 \left[ \Omega C V _ {i} + e _ {i} \geq 0 \right],\tag{8}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln h a l f y e a r s a l e s _ {i} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} I f w e i t a o _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} A w e i t a o _ {i} + \\ \gamma_ {3} I f w e i t a o _ {i} \times A w e i t a o _ {i} + \Pi C V _ {i} + \tau_ {i}. \end{array} \tag {9}
$$

The above equations assume that e is independent of exogenous variables and follows a normal distribution with $E ( \tau \vert e ) = \rho _ { 1 } e$ . τ is an unobserved factor, and an econometric problem arises because τ is not independent of $I f w e i t a o _ { i }$ . To address this problem, the idea behind the control function method is to derive a proxy variable that is conditional on the part of the endogenous variable ???????????????? that depends on the error term τ. If this can be done, the remaining part of $I f w e i t a o _ { i }$ will be independent of τ, and the traditional estimation will be consistent.

We used instrumental variables for the endogenous ???????????????? in the control function model. In the treatment group, we used store i-1’s competitors to instrument for store i. Store i-1 and store i were ranked based on the PC Taobao store search results. When we prepared the pool of control group, we searched cosmetics (or stationery) stores on PC Taobao store search page. In the search results, it had both stores that had opened a Weitao account (i.e., Weitao store) and stores that had not (i.e., non-Weitao store). The Weitao stores comprised the treatment group and their numbering i was based on the sequence in the search results. Therefore, Weitao store i is the next treated firm after Weitao store i-1 in the PC Taobao store search results. But the stores could be far from each other in the search results since there might have non-Weitao stores listed between them. Therefore, for store i, we constructed four IVs from store i-1; that are the percentages of stores that had opened Weitao among $\pm 5 , \pm 1 0 , \pm 1 5 , \pm 2 0$ stores of store i-1 in the search results (± means listed right below and right above), i.e., ???????????????????? ${ \mathsf { s } } 5 _ { i } ,$ ?????????????????????? $\begin{array} { r } { . 0 _ { i } , } \end{array}$ ?????????????????????? $1 5 _ { i }$ and ??????????????????????20<sub>??</sub>.

The four instrumental variables satisfy the IV requirements. For relevance, the percentage of stores that opened Weitao accounts reflects a trend of Weitao account opening in the search period. Therefore, competitors of store i-1 and competitors of store i had similar rates of Weitao account openings. For the exclusion restriction, we checked the store search results and found that our treated firms were widely dispersed in the search results. That is, store i-1 and store i were separated in the search results with quite a few non-Weitao stores listed between them. When ranked differently in the search results, store $i \mathrm { - } 1 \mathrm { \bar { s } }$ competitors (i.e., its neighbors) were different from store $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ competitors in terms of store characteristics (such as store activity level, credit, and ratings). ## Results for Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Efforts

We examined the sales effect of firm social media efforts in the number of Weitao followers, the number of blog postings, and different content types. The results for the effect of the number of Weitao followers and blog postings on the full treated sample are presented in Table 8. For the number of Weitao followers, the coefficient of lnfollower is 0.027 (significant at 10% level), suggesting that, in general, stores with more followers have a greater sales effect. However, for the sales effect of the number of blog postings, the coefficient of weitaonum is insignificant. Overall, H3 is supported but H4 is not supported. Meanwhile, we compared the difference between the two industry subsamples, finding insignificant results.<sup>10</sup>

We further explored the sales effect of Weitao blog content. The results are shown in Table 9. For the full treated sample analysis, the coefficient of informative is 0.337 (significant at the 10% level); the coefficients of persuasive and promotional are insignificant. This suggests that, overall, firm informative social media content is more significant in contributing to store sales than persuasive content, thus supporting H5. We also conducted a subsample analysis and found that informative content is significant for the sales of high-involvement cosmetics stores (coefficient of informative is 0.508, significant at the 5% level), promotional content is significant for the sales of low-involvement stationery stores (coefficient of promotional is 0.685, significant at the 10% level), and persuasive content is insignificant for both subsamples.

To compare the difference between the two subsamples, following Wooldridge (2002) described above, we added the dummy variable industry (1 for cosmetics; 0 for stationery) and interaction terms informative×industry, persuasive×industry, and promotional×industry in the regression. The coefficient estimates are as follows: informative×industry = 1.367 (significant at the 1% level), persuasive×industry is insignificant, and promotional×industry = -0.735 (significant at the 5% level). Overall, this indicates that the effectiveness of content types is significantly different for firms selling different types of products. Specifically, informative content is more effective for the sales of selling high-involvement products (cosmetics stores), whereas promotional content is more effective for the sales of low-involvement products (stationery stores). We provide potential mechanisms to explain these results in the discussion section.

For the above analyses, we tested the validity of the two sets of instrumental variables (i.e., lnfolloweriv<sub>i</sub> and weitaonumiv<sub>i</sub>). The corresponding coefficients of the IVs in the first stage of the control function method are all significant for both subsamples $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The strength of the IVs was assessed with a first-stage Sanderson-Windmeijer multivariate F-test of excluded instruments $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ), the Cragg-Donald Wald Fstatistic, and the Kleibergen-Paap Wald F-statistic (both higher than the critical value of Stock-Yogo weak ID at the 10% level for both subsamples). We also conducted the Sargan-Hansen overidentifying test for the validity of the two sets of IVs. For IVs of lnfollower, the Hansen J statistics are 5.503 (p-value = 0.358) for the cosmetics subsample and 5.385 (p-value = 0.371) for the stationery subsample. For IVs of weitaonum, the Hansen J statistics are 5.888 (p-value = 0.317) for the cosmetics subsample and 5.463 (p-value = 0.362) for the stationery subsample. ## Robustness Checks

## Matching with Alternative Algorithms

We conducted various other matching algorithms of PSM to provide robustness to the 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching in the main analysis, including Mahalanobis distance matching, Euclidean distance matching, coarsened exact matching, radius matching, nearest-neighbor matching with caliper (1:3), and kernel matching. The results are largely consistent, ensuring the similarity between the treatment and control groups. The results also passed balance checks. In addition, DID results for the sales effect of Weitao adoption are positive and significant for all matching algorithms except coarsened exact matching. For coarsened exact matching (CEM), the effect of Weitao adoption is also positive but insignificant. This indicates that the sales effect of Weitao exists; however, some store information is lost due to the fact that CEM prunes both treatment and control units (Blackwell et al., 2009). Overall, our results are robust to the different matching algorithms. The results of these analyses are provided in Appendix D.

## Dynamic Sales Effect of Firm Social Media Adoption

To understand how the social media sales effect is generated and sustained, we analyzed the time-varying sales impact of Weitao adoption. To analyze store sales data, we obtained seven time stamps: accumulated store sales (since store opening) at six months, one month, and one week before the Weitao account was opened; at the time the store opened the Weitao account; and one week, one month, and six months after the Weitao account was opened. These allowed us to examine the short- and long-term sales impact for one week, one month, and six months.

The seven time points constitute six time periods, i.e., three before and three after the opening of the Weitao account. Taking the third time period (i.e., one week before the Weitao account was opened) as the baseline, we specified the model as:

$$
\ln \text { sales } _ {i t} = \alpha + \sum_ {l = 1 (\neq 3)} ^ {6} \left(\text { Ifweitao } _ {i} \times \text { time } _ {t}\right) \beta_ {l} + \kappa_ {t} + \mu_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{10}
$$

where dependent variable lnsales<sub>it</sub> denotes the sales that store i received from the first time point to the end of time period t $( t = 1 , . . . , 6 )$ , and time<sub>t</sub> is a dummy variable that indicates the $t ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ time period. Therefore, the interaction term coefficients $\beta _ { 4 } ,$ $\beta _ { 5 } ,$ and β<sub>6</sub> are our focus, which gives us the sales effect of the opening of the Weitao account one week, one month, and six months, respectively. <sub>t</sub> is the time effect, and <sub>i</sub> is the store effect. With the uneven time periods, we added a day effect instead of a time period effect to the model.<sup>11</sup>

Our results of the dynamic sales effect of Weitao adoption are shown in Table 10. For the time periods before the opening of the Weitao account, the coefficients are insignificant for both subsamples, which further suggests no systematic pretreatment difference between treatment and control groups. For the dynamic effect in the cosmetics subsample, coefficients of the interaction terms are positive and significant for all periods after the opening of the Weitao account and increase over time. That is, opening a Weitao account generates an effect after adoption, and the long-term effect is larger than the short-term effect. For the stationery subsample, the coefficient of the interaction term for the sixth time period is significantly positive, which suggests that the effect of opening a Weitao account starts after a period of time. Overall, the sales effect of opening a Weitao account increases and is sustained over time.<sup>12</sup>

## Discussion and Conclusion

Using a unique research setting, this study adds to the literature on the value of social media on firm performance. Specifically, we investigated the impact of FGC on sales performance, answering “whether,” “what,” and “how” questions.

The launch of the social media function (Weitao) on the Taobao e-commerce platform enabled a quasi-experiment in which we were able to trace both firm (Taobao store) social media activities and the corresponding sales impact. Using PSM and DID methods, we found that stores had an overall mean sales increase of 20.67% for a six-month time window after they adopted Weitao, thus answering the “whether” question.

We addressed the “what” question by examining firms in terms of which industries might benefit more from social media adoption, thereby contributing to the literature. However, most prior studies are based on a single industry (or single firm) or consider the overall effect without investigating industry heterogeneity. Taking product involvement as a means to differentiate industries, we found that high-involvement industry stores (cosmetics in our sample) had a relatively lower sales increase from Weitao adoption than low-involvement industry stores (stationery in our sample). This is contrary to our theoretical prediction. Due to the nature of the high-involvement industry, consumers face higher uncertainties when purchasing cosmetics products (than when buying stationery products). We found that although firm social media content aims to address uncertainties in the consumer decision-making process, for the cosmetic stores in our sample, it was insufficient to fully eliminate consumer concerns. This may indicate a learning process or an FGC strategy for firms in high-involvement industries in terms of figuring out how to exert social media efforts to create better performance. Our follow-up examination also confirms this idea.

To enrich this study by providing more practical insights, we investigated how social media efforts can yield more benefits, thus answering the “how” question. By looking at the extensiveness, intensiveness, and content of firm social media communications with consumers, we examined the sales effect of Weitao followers, the volume of blog postings, and FGC content types, respectively. We found that firm social media efforts in extensive communications (i.e., the number of followers) led to greater sales performance. However, our findings indicate that intensive communications (i.e., the number of blog postings) do not have a significant effect— likely because of a Weitao restriction limiting the number of blog postings that a Weitao account could post per day during our study period. Meanwhile, we found no significant difference between the cosmetics and stationery subsamples for the effect of extensive and intensive communications, suggesting that attracting more followers or posting more microblogs does not differentiate between high-involvement industries and low-involvement industries in terms of generating better sales performance.

To make a difference, firms need to conduct more nuanced tactics and consider the firm or industry nature in order to address consumer purchase uncertainties. We believe that content strategies offer a solution in the social media FGC context. Indeed, our content analysis reveals that highinvolvement industry stores could benefit from posting more informative content in order to eliminate consumer uncertainty; in contrast, low-involvement stores would benefit more from promotional content. We propose some potential mechanisms for these interesting findings: First, informative content is effective for selling high-involvement products where consumers often face information deficiency and greater uncertainties. Due to the nature of high-involvement industries, when purchasing products such as cosmetics, consumers need more information about product features. Therefore, firms can provide additional information about brands and products through social media channels. Tangible and reliable information helps address consumer purchase uncertainties and is provided by informative content. Overall, we found that informative content is effective for highinvolvement purchases. Second, promotional content, such as price promotions and free-shipping information, is an important FGC content type for firms in low-involvement industries. Consumers in low-involvement situations have little motivation to process information. In such situations, promotional information would thus be attractive to consumers. In fact, we found that this is a commonly used content practice in the social e-commerce setting that we studied. Third, persuasive content seems to be not as effective as the other two content types. Persuasive content tends to differentiate the focal brand or product from competitors’ brands and products. However, such messages from sellers (FGC) appear to be less convincing. It is even possible that persuasive content could be interpreted as a persuasion attempt, leading to some level of distrust (Darke & Ritchie, 2007). Overall, our findings suggest that while, on average, it is relatively harder for firms in high-involvement industries to gain a sales benefit from social media adoption, they can reap higher returns with effective content strategies, particularly those that address consumer uncertainty.

The overall results were generated by a set of methods that ensure validity and rigor. In addition to the above-noted PSM-DID, we also employed the control function method to address the hidden bias issue in PSM, instrumental variables to evaluate the endogenous store decision to open a Weitao account and store social media efforts (while checking their validity), and a standard and rigorous content-coding process for text mining. We also conducted a series of balance checks to ensure PSM matching quality and used parallel assumption testing and various other matching algorithms to double-check our main results. We believe that this study provides a more complete understanding of firm social media engagement and that the empirics bolster the validity of our conclusions.

## Theoretical Implications

This study contributes to the literature on the value of social media FGC and has a number of theoretical implications. First, with our overarching theoretical framework combining consumer decision process theory and social presence theory, this paper broadens the scope of consumer decision process theory to the context of firm social media management and extends social presence theory by showing how it takes effect in different stages of the consumer decision-making process. It delineates the consumer decision-making process in the context of social e-commerce and improves the understanding of the mechanisms of how FGC affects the stages of the consumer decision-making process by creating social presence.

Second, through examining the effect of FGC on the consumer decision-making process, this study brings a theoretical perspective of product involvement to the understanding of industry differences. Indeed, product involvement is part of the consumer decision-making process and serves as an angle through which industries can be differentiated. The previous literature on this topic has not examined how product involvement theory functions in the social media context.

Third, this study enriches communication theory by investigating the content that firms communicate with consumers. We not only confirm that content is a significant aspect of communication but also identify a new type of content, promotional content, that can help FGC influence consumer purchases, especially for low-involvement firms.

## Managerial Implications

Firms have devoted substantial resources to promoting their brands and products on social media. Most managers, however, are unclear about whether and how social media engagement pays off. This study provides a more complete perspective. Based on our findings, we offer the following suggestions.

First, it is beneficial for firms to embrace social media. Our results show a generally positive and significant sales impact of firm social media adoption at the firm level. Our conceptual framework and empirical methodology can help firms more accurately evaluate the performance of their social media investments, allowing them to more effectively tailor their efforts. Second, firms need to realize that the nature of their industry determines whether and how much they could benefit from social media engagement. We find that, in general, firms in low-involvement industries have a greater sales impact from using social media, but highinvolvement industries can benefit from proper content strategies. This implies that firms should manage their social media differently, based on the nature of the firm. Third, having a general positive sales impact does not mean that firms need to simply open a social media community and wait for the benefits to arrive. It is important to put effort into generating optimal content and attracting consumers. Our results suggest that firm informative content is effective for increasing sales in general, and especially for selling highinvolvement products; promotional content is an effective strategy for selling low-involvement products; and firms should deemphasize the use of persuasive content. Overall, our study provides practical guidance on how to optimize firm social media efforts to improve performance.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has limitations that could be addressed in future research. First, we were only able to choose one product category each for the high- and low-involvement industries. It would be valuable to expand the data collection to other product categories and to generalize our findings of industry differences. Second, future research could explore the role of consumer characteristics and other industry or firm characteristics (including firm promotional activities) on firm decisions regarding social media adoption and on the impact of social media engagement. Doing so would generate more nuanced findings and implications. Third, while we focus on the impact of social media on sales performance, it would be interesting to examine the firm social media learning process to identify the best practices. Fourth, it would be valuable to investigate potential Weitao spillover effects, such as how Weitao product promotion might influence other products or the indirect effect from other Weitao accounts—for example, the individual accounts of key opinion leaders. Finally, future research could extend the examination to a later stage of Weitao development when the social media function had become richer to potentially reveal diverse effects.

## Five Steps of the Content-Coding Process

## Orientation Period

Based on prior studies of FGC content and ad content (e.g., Abernethy & Franke, 1996; Lee et al., 2018), we started with 20 attributes to classify informative content and persuasive content. Informative content includes all factual data on the nature of sellers’ products or services, such as product brand, origins, and components, that could reduce purchase uncertainty (Abernethy & Franke, 1996). Persuasive content refers to content that convinces consumers of product benefits (Lee et al., 2018). Three research assistants who majored in information systems were hired to conduct content coding. To ensure coding quality, we explained the meaning of these 20 attributes to them and conducted two pretests.

Specifically, we collected 99 Weitao blog postings (that contained these attributes) posted by stores that opened Weitao accounts earlier than our sample stores as the pretest sample. We selected 10 to show as examples of attributes to the judges. For the remaining 89, we conducted two pretests (43 blog postings for the first one, and 46 blog postings for the second one). In the first pretest, we asked the judges to read every blog posting and to answer whether it had the 20 attributes, which was a Yes or No for each attribute. This pretest lasted about three hours, and the three judges coded independently without communication during the process. They could refer only to the 10 examples. After that, coding disagreement was discussed until a consensus was reached, and we answered questions from the judges about the coding process. Then, the judges conducted the second pretest. We calculated the Fleiss’s kappa among the three judges. Fleiss’s kappa is an interrater agreement measure that extends the often-used Cohen’s kappa for evaluating the level of agreement among multiple judges with a binary scale. It expresses the degree to which the observed proportion of agreement among raters exceeds what would be expected if all raters made their ratings completely random. The mean of our Fleiss’s kappa was 0.73, and the Fleiss’s kappa value for each attribute was larger than 0.6. This indicates a substantial level of reliability (Fleiss, 1971; Landis & Koch, 1977). Given that Fleiss’s kappa may return low values even when agreement is actually high, we also calculated the percentage of agreement as a robustness check. The mean percentage of agreement was 92%, which is also satisfactory.

## Developing Training Set

The three judges were asked to read and judge the contents of a randomly selected sample of 300 blog postings from our final sample. Again, the judges coded the contents with no communication, but they could refer to examples we supplied from the previous stage. The 300 blog postings constitute our training set. Overall, the mean interjudge percentage agreement was 91%, and the mean value of Fleiss’s kappa was 0.72, which indicates a substantial level of reliability (Fleiss, 1971; Landis & Koch, 1977).

## Text Mining on Training Set

Based on the training set obtained from the previous step, we employed a support vector machine (SVM) with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel for the text classification. It was performed with the Python package sklearn.

To prepare the text data for classification, we first removed irrelevant information from the blog posting content, including store names and generic advertisements. Then, we conducted Chinese word segmentation for each blog posting and filtered stop words. It is worth noting that we loaded special domain lexicons in the word segmentation, including the Taobao lexicon, the cosmetics lexicon with brand names and product names, and the stationery lexicon with brand names and product names. Blog posting content after text processing was presented by vector space, and we developed word frequency matrices. The word frequency matrices were used in the SVM text classification for each attribute.

As for the text classification for a specific attribute, we set the C parameter as 10 and the gamma parameter as 0.001 for the RBF kernel SVM. To evaluate the accuracy of the classifier, we employed a cross-validation method with five folds. This approach uses four folds to build the model and the remaining fold to evaluate the model. This procedure is repeated five times until each of the five folds is used for validation. The above procedure was repeated once for each attribute. Thus, we repeated this process 20 times. We obtained a text classifier from this procedure to classify the final sample.

This procedure provided us with a confusion matrix for each attribute. Based on that, we calculated accuracy to evaluate the performance of the RBF kernel SVM. Overall, the mean accuracy of these 20 attributes was 84.92%, which is a satisfactory level.

## Content Classification

We proceeded to the final coding process based on the SVM text classifier obtained in the previous step. Thus, we had values of the 20 attributes for each Weitao blog posting.
