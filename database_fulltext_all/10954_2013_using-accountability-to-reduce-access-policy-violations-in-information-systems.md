Ab stra ct: Access policy violations by organizational insiders are a major security concern for organizations because these violations commonly result in fraud, unauthorized disclosure, theft of intellectual property, and other abuses. Given the operational demands of dynamic organizations, current approaches to curbing access policy violations are insufficient. This study presents a new approach for reducing access policy violations, introducing both the theory of accountability and the factorial survey to the information systems field. We identify four system mechanisms that heighten an individual’s perception of accountability: identifiability, awareness of logging, awareness of audit, and electronic presence. These accountability mechanisms substantially reduce intentions to commit access policy violations. These results not only point to several avenues for future research on access policy violations but also suggest highly practical design-artifact solutions that can be easily implemented with minimal impact on organizational insiders.

Key word s a nd p hra ses: access policy violations, accountability, accountability theory, awareness, evaluation, factorial survey method, identifiability, information security, monitoring, social presence.

A p ersistent p rob lem in informa tion security is the threat of malicious insiders, or members of an organization who are entrusted with access to information resources but abuse that trust [133]. This problem is particularly pernicious because many security countermeasures are designed to protect against attackers from outside an organization and are therefore often ineffective against insiders [100]. Moreover, compared to outside attackers, insiders possess considerably higher levels of knowledge, resources, and access, all of which represent a greater risk to an organization [70].

A common insider threat is an access policy violation, which occurs when an insider accesses sensitive information in a way that is contrary to the policies of the organization [129, 137]. Access policy violations are committed for various reasons, including fraud, identity theft, disclosure or sale of sensitive information, theft of intellectual property, and imprudent curiosity [93, 99]. Not only are these violations serious in and of themselves, but they also expose organizations to civil lawsuits, regulatory sanctions, and damage to public relations [137]. The Verizon 2010 Data Breach Investigations Report disclosed that 48 percent of data breaches were attributable to organizational insiders—double the amount reported in previous surveys [126]. The CSI Computer Crime and Security Survey 2010/2011 reported that 29 percent of respondents were aware of instances of unauthorized access by insiders in their organization in the past year [88]. Most recently, the Ponemon Institute [76] found that negligent insiders are now the top cause of data breaches (39 percent), partially because insider use of portable storage devices and mobile computing are making organizations more susceptible than ever before. Furthermore, organizational costs of malicious data breaches are more costly than other kinds of breaches, and 33 percent of malicious data breaches are attributable to malicious insiders.

A long-standing solution to mitigate access policy violations is the principle of least privilege, which holds that “every privileged user of the system should operate using the least amount of privilege necessary to complete the job” [95, p. 389]. Although this principle is reasonable, it poses a number of problems for organizations. First, the principle of least privilege does not prevent abuse of access privileges that are properly granted. Second, given the dynamic and changing nature of organizations, it is difficult for organizations to maintain minimal access privileges for employees. As a consequence, “overentitlement” of access privileges is the norm in many organizations [137]. According to one hospital administrator, “There are just thousands of people who have access—and need to have access—to confidential information, and to try to change their behavior is a challenge” [93, p. D1].

One promising means for modifying the behaviors of users with broad access privileges is through accountability, which is “the implicit or explicit pressure to justify one’s beliefs and actions to others” [112, p. 8]. The construct of accountability has received substantial attention in psychology and organizational behavior [54]. Prior research has consistently found that a person’s expectation that he or she will be held accountable reduces the likelihood that the person will behave in socially unacceptable ways [103]. Although the ability of information technology (IT) systems to monitor insiders has long been recognized [35], to date no research has examined the psychological effects of accountability within the information systems (IS) field. The research question of the proposed study is therefore the following:

## RQ: How can features of an IS be designed to increase perceptions of accountability by insiders and thereby deter access policy violations?

In answering this research question, this study makes several contributions. First, we introduce accountability theory to the IS field, and by doing so, we ascertain four system mechanisms that can heighten an individual’s perception of accountability: identifiability, awareness of logging, awareness of audit, and electronic presence. Second, we introduce the factorial survey to the IS field, a scenario-based method with compelling advantages for the study of information security policy (ISP) violations and computer abuse generally. Third, we perform a test of our theoretical model to show that all four accountability mechanisms are effective in reducing intentions to commit access policy violations. Finally, we offer empirical evidence to demonstrate that system artifacts can be designed to raise insiders’ perceptions of accountability and decrease access policy violations. Thus, our results offer organizations a practical solution to complement existing approaches of reducing insiders’ access policy violations.

In this paper, we first review background literature on access policy violations and accountability. We then introduce accountability theory and propose hypotheses for our context. We next describe our methodology and present the results of our analysis. Finally, we discuss our results, contributions, and limitations and discuss avenues for future research.

## Background on Access Policy Violations and Accountability

## What Are Access Policy Violations?

The d ep end ent va riab le of interest in this stud y is access policy violations—acts in which insiders access organizational information contrary to stated policies for how access rights may be used in the organization [129]. For example, an organization might stipulate that personnel records within a system can be accessed only when they are legitimately required to complete a task. If a person with legitimate access rights thus snoops through personnel records to find out the salary of a friend, such an event would constitute an access policy violation. Here, it is not a question of whether someone has rights within the system to access the information but whether someone is using the access rights in accordance with organizational policy.

Access policies are a form of information security policies, formal guidelines that insiders are required to follow to safeguard the information resources of their organizations [11]. The phenomenon of ISP violations has received increased attention from IS scholars [40, 46, 109]. Violations of ISPs can be unintentional or deliberate; in the latter case, individuals consciously violate the expressly stated norm of the organization [109]. This study will examine access policy violations that are intentional, although our findings can likely help curb unintentional violations as well.

We propose a novel way to decrease access policy violations, particularly with systems that require broad access rights: to design user-interface elements such that they increase perceived accountability in end users. Before we explain how this can occur theoretically, we will explicate the construct of accountability.

## What Is Accountability?

A challenge for studying accountability is that the concept is used broadly in a variety of fields, including philosophy, ethics, political science, and organizational behavior (for examples, see [26, 68, 97, 107]). In addition, authors tend to create their own definitions of accountability, which are rarely compatible with each other [10]. As a result, extant literature treats accountability in a fragmented and inconsistent manner.

A useful way to understand accountability is to distinguish between its two most prevalent uses: (1) as a virtue and (2) as a mechanism [10]. As a virtue, accountability is seen as a quality in which a person displays a willingness to accept responsibility, a desirable trait in public officials, government agencies, or firms; hence, in this use, accountability is a positive feature of an entity. As a mechanism, accountability is seen as a process in which a person has a potential obligation to explain his or her actions to another party who has the right to pass judgment on the actions as well as to subject the person to potential consequences for his or her actions. For the purposes of this paper, accountability will be discussed more in line with this second usage: accountability is a key form of organizational governance that cannot exist without mechanisms that promote it. Such mechanisms are typical in employee–manager relationships and include overt monitoring and evaluation, two items we build upon in the next section as the basis for design artifacts that promote accountability. This idea of accountability as a mechanism, along with the construct’s supporting theory, undergirds our research.

## Accountability Theory and Hypotheses

Our resea rch focuses on fostering the mecha nism of a ccountab ility—through design artifacts—on the individual end-user level to decrease access policy violations.

Therefore, the key theory we use to explain and predict how design artifacts can foster individual-level accountability is accountability theory.

In a review of the accountability literature, Bovens [10] found that much of the ambiguity in the literature is the result of authors talking about accountability without studying it rigorously. A notable exception is the work of Tetlock and colleagues, who developed a theory of accountability to explain individual behavior in organizations [115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 120, 121, 122, 123]. Consequently, we leverage Tetlock’s research as the basis of our research model. Accountability theory explains how the perceived need to justify a person’s behaviors to another party causes that person to consider and to feel accountable for the process by which decisions and judgments were reached [119]. This need to account for a decision process and outcome in turn increases the likelihood that a person will think deeply and systematically about his or her procedural behaviors—as opposed to using automatic or heuristic processing to consider his or her actions.

Heuristic processing involves superficial cognitive processing of a decision that results in use of simple rules to make a decision [17, 62]. In heuristic processing, a person does not consider much factual evidence and instead focuses on superficial cues when making a decision [17, 62], which leads to suboptimal decisions [134]. In contrast, systematic processing involves deep cognitive processing and elaboration to make decisions [17, 18, 62]. A person who thinks systematically in making decisions about systems use will therefore consider more input factors and think more carefully to derive an optimal decision that he or she uniquely owns [62], that is, a person will go through a “thorough, in-depth, complete, and well-advised processing of all given information” in making optimal decisions [134, p. 780]. An increased focus on systematic processing consequently increases the emphasis on creating effective outcomes for which the person is accountable [101].

The increased awareness in decision making fostered by systematic processing creates an accountability both to process and to outcome that is more likely to increase prosocial behaviors [28], increase conformity to expected behaviors [123], increase conservatism [110], and decrease risk taking [98]. Importantly, negative emotions such as anger and anxiety can increase heuristic processing and result in greater risk taking and deviant behavior in organizational contexts [31, 89, 94]. Our theoretical context innovatively focuses on proposing design artifacts that can increase perceptions of accountability in end users who are working with systems. Given the above explanation of the causal mechanisms of accountability, we posit that those who perceive themselves to be accountable for use of their access rights will be more likely to experience the systematic processing and awareness that will increase conformance. These accountable end users will be less likely to commit access policy violations than those who feel less accountable. Likewise, accountable end users are less likely to make decisions based on emotions and heuristic processing that could cloud judgment of optimal outcomes—and are therefore less likely to take unnecessary risks with access policy violations. In summary, the fundamental assumption in our theoretical use of accountability theory is that increases in perceived accountability decrease a person’s intention to commit access policy violations.

## Using Design Artifacts to Increase Accountability

Now that we have explained the foundation of accountability theory for our context, we expand on specific manipulations tied to accountability theory that can be linked to user-interface design artifacts to complete our predictions. Accountability theory is especially useful for our objective to reduce access policy violations through system artifacts because this theory highlights several mechanisms that increase accountability perceptions. For example, Lerner and Tetlock noted that “even the simplest accountability manipulation necessarily implicates several empirically distinguishable submanipulations” [54, p. 255], including the presence of another, identifiability, evaluation, and reason-giving.

Three of these four—identifiability, evaluation, and presence of another (i.e., social presence)—are particularly amenable to manipulation by system-related artifacts because of the potential of IT to monitor and record behavior [8, 22, 35, 61]. For these reasons, we focus on how design manipulations of identifiability, evaluation, and social presence directly increase systematic processing, thus manipulating accountability and deterring access policy violations. We depict our proposed theoretical framework in Figure 1. Next, we explain each core manipulation in the following subsections.

## Identifiability Increases Accountability

Identifiability is a person’s “knowledge that his outputs could be linked to him” [132, p. 309], which is in direct contrast to anonymity [73, 102]. Because of this linkage to self [102, 132], identifiability is a potent deterrent of antisocial behaviors such as social loafing [50, 53, 132], flaming [85], and related group discontinuities [78, 102]. Accordingly, identifiability is a necessary facilitator of accountability because this mechanism causes a person to know that his or her actions can be traced back to him or her and that he or she can therefore be made responsible for those actions [54].

When a person is thus performing behaviors that are identifiable, he or she is more likely to engage in systematic processing to ensure he or she performs only behaviors for which he or she is willing to be responsible for. Conversely, if a person is not identified, he or she has less incentive to engage in systematic processing to accept responsibility for an outcome.

Identifiability is closely related to the construct of deindividuation, a person’s belief that his or her actions within a group cannot be associated with him or her individually as he or she becomes immersed in a collective group [29, 84]. Numerous studies have found that when people feel deindividuated they are less restrained in their normal behaviors [29, 84]. Conversely, when individuals sense that they are individuated—that is, distinguishable within a group—antisocial behaviors are curtailed [25, 29, 84]. A classic example from social psychology literature shows that during the U.S. holiday of Halloween, children who trick-or-treat unmasked or unaccompanied by other children are much less likely to take more than then their fair share of candy compared to children who trick-or-treat masked or in groups [25]. A common example of deindividuation in the IS context is the practice of shared login credentials [109]. When more than one user shares the same login credentials, it is unclear at any given time which user is using the system. Accordingly, users of the system are deindividuated and thus attribution of actions made in the system is difficult to assign.

Deindividuation also commonly occurs in computer-mediated communication (CMC)—whether in groups or even in individual use. The mere use of computers to communicate—even if technically identified—still leads to deindividuation because the mediation effect of computer use can lead to a sense of “disembodiment” by users [114]. This deindividuation leads to willingness to engage in riskier communication and decisions online or in CMC contexts. For example, in collaborative group discussions, anonymity leads to more critical comments (e.g., flaming) [85], and an increased willingness to engage in counter-normative argumentation [49]. Anonymity and associated deindividuation are likewise associated with an increase of intimate self-disclosure online [19, 79]. Finally, recent research based on controlbalance theory shows a positive link between deindividuation and anonymity with computer-based crime and online deviance [131].

Given this theoretical background, we posit that identifiability will increase perceptions of accountability, whereas a lack of identifiability will decrease perceptions of accountability. We thus predict in our context that the more identifiability features that are built into a system in which access rights are held, the higher the perception of accountability by users of the system. Subsequently, this increased sense of internal accountability will lead to a decrease in access policy violations. In contrast, the less identifiability that is built into a system, the lower the perception of accountability will be. Subsequently, a lower perception of accountability is more likely to be associated with an increase in access policy violations. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1: User-interface design artifacts that foster identifiability will decrease intent to commit access policy violations.

## Evaluation and Monitoring Increase Accountability

Evaluation is the belief that a person’s “performance will be assessed by another [party] according to some normative ground rules and with some implied consequences” [54, p. 255]. Notably, much of the information used for evaluation often comes from the process of monitoring, and thus they naturally complement each other. Monitoring is the process of watching or tracking a person’s activities [8, 38]. Our research therefore focuses on two fundamental constructs: (1) logging or observing a person’s behavior by an organization (monitoring) and (2) the person’s expectation of being evaluated based on the behavior observed or recorded.

Studies have shown that awareness of evaluation increases socially desirable behaviors [45, 54] and deters socially undesirable ones [103]. This is because awareness of evaluation can create evaluation apprehension [32], a state of mind in which self-focused attention is increased and performance is modified in the presence of those who can disapprove or approve of actions. In this state, self-focus heightens perceptions of discrepancies between actual behaviors and espoused standards [103] and fosters a desire to follow espoused standards. Accordingly, the need to maintain a good impression in the minds of others demands that negative evaluations be avoided that would undermine self-presentation efforts [5].

For a person to have evaluation awareness, he or she must first be aware that his or her performance or actions might be observed either directly or indirectly by others. This is why evaluation is typically coupled with monitoring. The two together then foster the systematic processing necessary for accountability to be increased. Likewise, systems can be particularly effective in enhancing perceptions of evaluation and also of accountability due to the capability of systems to track behavior at a granular level [33]. Monitoring, for example, has been used in ISP compliance contexts as an effective method for helping evaluate and thereby encourage ISP compliance [8, 44] and general computing policies [22, 30]. Such monitoring and evaluation is effective when it is part of the normal ISP and controls and creates the expectation within insiders that they will be monitored and evaluated for their compliance actions [8, 51]. Similarly, monitoring, evaluation, and enforcement are effectively used in system-based audits to encourage adherence to data protection laws [48].

A crucial element of evaluation, and one common in the IT realm, is the logging of an individual’s actions within a system, including recording Web sites visited, records changed, e-mails sent and received, and a variety of other behaviors. However, unless someone is made aware that he or she is being logged, evaluation apprehension and subsequent systematic processing will not occur, and the effects of evaluation on promoting accountability will not be evident. Ultimately, evaluation awareness must be salient if negative behaviors are to be deterred.

For these reasons, monitoring behavior and evaluating behavior are natural complements that are typically conducted together [38]. We propose that to make these most effective, a user needs to be made aware that both monitoring and evaluating behavior are going to occur. Again, this awareness is normally accomplished through ISPs, training, or a combination of the two. Our goal is to demonstrate that these mechanisms can be further represented as design artifacts that further heighten a user’s belief that his or her behavior is being monitored and evaluated. Summarizing this section, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2: User-interface design artifacts that foster monitoring awareness will decrease intent to commit access policy violations.

Hypothesis 3: User-interface design artifacts that foster evaluation awareness will decrease intent to commit access policy violations.

## Social Presence Increases Accountability

We embrace the definition of social presence as the awareness of others in a CMC interaction [87, 127]. Whereas social presence research often places a heavy emphasis on interpersonal relationships (e.g., [87, 127]), Lerner and Tetlock include the “mere presence of another” as an empirically distinguishable submanipulation of accountability [54, p. 255].

This claim is supported by extensive research regarding the effects of the presence of another individual. For example, Guerin [39] reviewed more than 280 studies regarding “mere presence” and its social facilitation effects. Social facilitation “concerns the effects on behavior caused by the presence of other persons” [39, p. 38]. One basic concept of social facilitation is that in the presence of another, an individual has a higher probability of resorting to his or her dominant response [135]. This results in increased performance when executing simple tasks but reduced performance when performing complex or unfamiliar tasks [38]. However, the effects of social facilitation are numerous, depending on situational dynamics and other factors as demonstrated in Guerin’s review [39]. Recent studies have demonstrated these effects and offered theories explaining the causes.

For instance, Zajonc [136], elaborating on his earlier work, suggested that social facilitation effects are a product of uncertainty. Since the presence of another implies likely interaction, a person must be constantly prepared to react and respond to any changes in the social environment. Because being called upon to justify one’s actions to another (accountability) is a possible, albeit uncertain, outcome of interaction, a person is more likely to feel the need to be prepared to respond than if no presence exists. In this manner, the mere presence of another increases systematic processing, which in turn increases accountability, decreases intention to violate social norms, and often inhibits socially undesirable behavior. Several experiments have shown that in the presence of an observer, a person suppresses behaviors that are viewed as socially unacceptable or that could invite disapproval [6, 64].

Moreover, Guerin’s [39] review of mere presence effects shows that social facilitation effects can result from the passive presence of another, especially when the observer’s behavior cannot be watched. Bond and Titus [7], who conducted another large meta-analysis on social facilitation effects of social presence, similarly found that observers who are not readily present still produce social facilitation effects and that these observers might have greater impact on a person than observers who can be seen physically.

But how does this theory fare in the digital realm? Are similar effects observed when physical presence is replaced by an electronic substitute? In a study of the effects of social presence (or lack thereof) with CMC, Sia et al. [106] showed that CMC combined with anonymity led to greater group polarization (the phenomenon of a group taking a more extreme position or extreme thinking following group discussion than would normally occur). They pinpointed that group polarization was created partially due to anonymity and partially due to the removal of verbal cues, but it could be most strongly attributed to the removal of visual cues that promote social presence. Lowry et al. [59] more recently showed that small, anonymous groups that used a shared group editor (thereby experiencing social presence and awareness of others) were facilitated into implicit coordination and higher levels of productivity and quality than anonymous groups that experienced no social presence. Hence, it is clear that social presence can also create positive social facilitation effects in the digital realm. Summarizing this section, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4: User-interface design artifacts that foster awareness of social presence will decrease intent to commit access policy violations.

## Methodology

To test our hyp otheses, we used a fa ctoria l surv ey method [47, 91], a variation of the scenario method increasingly used to study ISP violations and computer abuse [3, 22, 40, 109]. The next sections describe the scenario and factorial survey methods.

## The Scenario Method

The scenario method presents respondents with “written descriptions of realistic situations and then request[s] responses on a number of rating scales that measure the dependent variables of interest” [124, pp. 127–128]. The scenario method is useful for studying access policy violations for two major reasons. First, scenarios can incorporate situational details thought to be important in decisions to behave unethically [52]. Because “the situational context of decision making and the information being handled will vary greatly among offenses” [20, p. 6], scenarios can enhance the realism of decision-making situations by providing contextual detail while simultaneously ensuring that these details are uniform across respondents [1]. Second, scenarios afford an indirect way of measuring intention to commit unethical behavior, which is difficult to measure directly [124]. Because scenarios describe another’s behavior in hypothetical terms, the respondent might feel less intimidated to report an intention to act similarly to the person described in the scenario [43]. For these reasons, the scenario method is the one most commonly applied when studying ethical issues [69] and is increasingly being used to study computer abuse and ISP violations [3, 40].

## The Factorial Survey Method

The factorial survey differs from typical scenario-based surveys in that textual elements within the scenario are experimentally varied. Our application of this methodology itself is an important methodological contribution to the literature because this application has not been previously used in IS research, although it has many potential useful applications—particularly to the study of ISP violations and computer abuse. This technique combines the large number of factors afforded by field surveys with the control and orthogonality provided by experimental designs [47, 90]. The factorial survey has been called the “gold standard” for accessing ethical beliefs and normative judgments [42, 104, p. 931]. It has also proved useful for explaining white-collar crime [71], a context similar to ISP violations such as unauthorized access [133].

Like experiments, factorial surveys are constructed by designing dimensions, or factors of theoretical interest. In turn, each dimension comprises several levels, which are analogous to treatments within an experimental factor. These dimensions and levels are then textually incorporated into the vignettes. The method is called a factorial survey because a full factorial of all possible combinations of levels and dimensions is obtained, forming a Cartesian product. The full factorial ensures the levels are orthogonal, with correlations at or near zero, thus overcoming the problem of multicollinearity [47]. In our study, orthogonality is useful for testing our hypotheses because it allows us to distinguish clearly the different effects of our accountability manipulations. Using a traditional survey, it would be difficult or even impossible to eliminate multicollinearity between our manipulations [91].

Like traditional surveys, factorial surveys adopt the technique of statistical random sampling. From the population of the full factorial, a random sample of vignettes (called a pack) is obtained, which typically contains between 10 and 60 vignettes [47]. Each survey respondent receives his or her own pack and gives a response (e.g., reports a level of intention) for each vignette contained in the pack. Estimates of dimensions in the full factorial population are made from the sample of responses to vignettes received in the pack.

In this way, estimates for a wide variety of factors can be obtained. Traditional experimental designs can support only a factorial of only a few dimensions with a few levels each before becoming impractically complex [91]. Factorial surveys do not share this limitation. Because random sampling is used, many factors and levels can be used to create a factorial of hundreds, thousands, or even millions of unique vignette combinations [47]. This provides a rich set of factors that more closely approximate the real world.

## Design of the Factorial Survey

To create a factorial survey to test our hypotheses, we first developed a set of six scenarios that described situations of access policy violations informed by the literature. To establish face validity and to ensure the scenarios described common and important violations in practice, we solicited qualitative, open-ended feedback from two system managers responsible for maintaining and enforcing access policies in their organizations. We also obtained qualitative feedback about the realism and relevance of the scenarios with five employees who were end users of financial and personnel records systems with broad access rights given to users. Based on this feedback, we selected the best three of the six scenarios for use in our instrument (for the full scenarios, see Table 1).

In addition, the explicit statement that the scenario described an access policy violation (e.g., “Gary believes doing so may be a violation of university policy”) was also varied randomly so that this statement was present in the scenario for some respondents and not for others. Based on our proposed model from accountability theory, we developed descriptions of eight system features that would be likely to heighten perceptions of accountability and decrease violations of the access policy: (1) logging user activities, (2) providing users a log of their activities in the system, (3) identifying users of the system by user ID, (4) warning users that the system is regularly audited, (5) providing the user a log of audits of the system, (6) requiring justification from users before access is permitted, (7) making all users’ logs viewable to all users of the system, and (8) prompting the user that an action to be executed will be linked to the user’s user ID. These system features became the eight factorial dimensions of the pretest. With two levels for each of the eight dimensions (present or not), this resulted in 256 possible combinations.

Next, 20 short vignettes describing a system profile were randomly selected from the 256 possible configurations of the textual elements. Each vignette represented a system profile for the user. The order in which the accountability manipulations (e.g., “unique user account,” “user activity recorded”) appeared in the vignette was also randomized to negate ordering effects.

## Pretest

The instrument was pretested [9] using a sample of 102 accounting majors in three sections of an accounting IS course. This sample was deemed appropriate because of the students’ exposure to access controls and IS security. Each subject rated 20 vignettes each, which yielded a sample of 2,040.

Based on qualitative feedback on the pretest, the instrument was refined to improve clarity and readability. Moreover, based on analysis of the pretest data, we were able to consolidate our eight system features into four dimensions: (1) identifiability, (2) evaluation expectation (logging), (3) evaluation apprehension (audit), and (4) social presence. Each dimension had two levels each: either (1) the system feature was described in the scenario or (2) it was not. Together, these dimensions and levels yielded a factorial of 16 unique combinations (2 × 2 × 2 × 2). The final dimensions and levels are summarized in Table 2.

## Primary Data Collection

The primary sample consisted of 96 IS majors in two sections of a course on IS business processes and internal control. Importantly, the subjects were familiar with the topic of IS security policies, access control, and computer abuse. Following standard practice with factorial surveys, the level of analysis was not the participant, but the vignette [47, 111]. The final sample thus consisted of 960 observations, comprised of 96 participants rating 10 vignettes each. Approximately 90 percent of the participants were male, and the average participant age was 23 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 1.7 years.

## Analysis

The d ep end ent va riab le for the a na lysis was the participant’s reported intention of violating the access policy just as the scenario character did, given the system profile described in the vignette. The four system features described in Table 2 served as the predictors and were analyzed as categorical variables.

Because each respondent rated multiple vignettes, the observations were not independent. Unobserved differences (i.e., heterogeneity) in respondents constituted a fixed individual effect that biased the vignette ratings for each respondent [37]. Because ordinary least squares (OLS) does not handle mixed models of fixed and random effects, an alternative means of analysis was necessary [71, 74]. To control for the individual effect, we selected the SAS PROC MIXED procedure [96, 108], a generalized form of the standard linear model, which allows for fixed and random effects [64]. In contrast to OLS, the PROC MIXED procedure uses maximum likelihood estimations [96], as does covariance-based structural equation modeling techniques [34]. More importantly, PROC MIXED uses maximum likelihood estimates of variances. These variance estimates adjust for the correlation that exists in our data. That correlation comes from the repeated measures (i.e., each subject rating 10 scenarios). Thus, each subject’s 10 ratings are correlated with one another—constituting a fixed individual effect [71]. Typical least squares analysis does not account for such correlation, and, notably, the variance estimates used for these statistical tests are incorrect, yielding inaccurate results. Conversely, the maximum likelihood estimates from PROC  MIXED are accurate estimates, accounting for the correlation in the data [55].

After controlling for individual effect, we then examined the data to identify individuals who reported a zero probability of intention to violate the access policy for all 10 system profiles, effectively self-censoring their responses to zero [71]. Because these individuals cannot be made to violate the access policy any less through accountability mechanisms, the individuals were removed from the sample. Of the 96 participants in the sample, 15 were removed, leaving a final sample of 81. The final sample size was 810 observations (81 participants with 10 vignettes each).

## Control Variables

Following other scenario-based studies, five control variables were used in the analysis: (1) scenario type received, (2) whether a policy violation was explicitly stated in the scenario, (3) respondents’ reported perceived realism of the scenario, (4) respondent’s gender, and (5) respondent’s age. As mentioned previously, in terms of demographics, 90 percent of respondents were male and the average age was 23 (SD = 1.7). The average reported realism for the scenarios was 8.1 (SD = 1.7) on a scale from 0 to 10, indicating that the respondents generally believed the scenarios were realistic. Moreover, the more realistic a respondent perceived the scenarios to be, the more likely the respondent was to report an intention to violate the access policy. On a scale from 0 to 10, the average reported intention was 4.04 (SD = 2.9). However, of all these control variables, only realism significantly influenced intention $( F = 4 . 0 0 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ .

To obtain the control variable model with the optimum fit statistics, the control variables that were not significant were dropped from the model. The final control variable model (see Table 3) yielded fit statistics of 3,597.1 (Akaike’s information criterion [AIC]) and 3,601.9 (Schwarz’s Bayesian information criterion [BIC]). Both statistics are functions of the log likelihood for a given model. Moreover, both statistics penalize a model for including terms that do not contribute substantially to the fit of the model. The statistics differ in the size of the penalty. For both statistics, a lower value indicates a better-fitting model with fewer unnecessary terms. Therefore, the goal is to minimize either the AIC or BIC when comparing rival models [83]. Thus, these scores established baseline fit statistics that our theoretical model needed to improve on to demonstrate predictive ability (lower AIC and BIC scores are better).

## Theoretical Effects

Next, the theoretical accountability effects were added to the model (see Table 4). The fit scores for the theoretical model were substantially improved, with AIC and BIC significantly lower than those of the control model $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The test for significant difference between models in terms of either AIC or BIC is a likelihood ratio test. The test statistic is distributed as a chi-square distribution, and p-values are calculated using the statistic relative to its degrees of freedom [55]. Therefore, we conclude that the theoretical model explains intention to violate the access policy substantially better than does the control model.

Our testing of the model showed that all four hypotheses were significantly supported (see Table 4). The effect size can be understood in terms of the scale of the dependent variable, the range of the difference of the change in accountability, and the parameter estimate (–0.187) in the above model. For every unit increase in the change in accountability, intention was reduced by –0.187.

We obtained effects sizes for our theoretical variables by calculating the difference in the mean of the dependent variable with the effect present or not. This difference was then divided by 10 (the maximum scale value) to obtain a percentage decrease in the dependent variable. These calculations resulted in effect sizes of 0.076 for identifiability, 0.085 for awareness of logging, 0.078 for awareness of audit, and 0.041 for electronic presence. Following Cohen [21], effect sizes of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 are considered small, medium, and large, respectively. By this standard, the sizes of all the effects can be considered small to medium. However, the cumulative effect for all four system design features decreased intention to violate the access policy by 0.253, a medium- to large effect.

## Interaction Effect Model

Finally, for exploratory purposes, interaction effects were tested to see if combinations of accountability system design features were effective in decreasing intention to violate the access policy. The only significant interaction was between awareness of logging and electronic presence $( F = 4 . 6 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which had an effect size of 13 percent, approximately a medium-size effect. In addition, adding the interaction between awareness of logging and electronic presence to the model further decreased AIC and BIC to 3,489.5 and 3,494.3, respectively $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This finding is intuitive because it indicates that the effect of electronic presence is amplified when a person is also made aware of logging.

## Discussion

The p rima ry p urp ose of our stud y was to leverage accountability theory to explain and predict how intentional use of interface design artifacts could positively influence perceived accountability in end users. To test our theoretical model, we used a novel application of the factorial survey method to create 810 cases of design vignettes to test our theory. Overall, our theory was supported, resulting in significant implications for research and practice. In the remainder of this section, we first summarize the results of our data collection and analysis. We then explain the specific implications for research and practice that derive from our results. We next propose how researchers and practitioners can create design artifacts of identifiability, monitoring awareness, evaluation awareness, and social presence to further leverage our theory and results. Finally, we discuss limitations and future research opportunities for this research.

## Summary of Results

All four of our proposed hypotheses were supported. The system feature of identifiability (a person’s “knowledge that his outputs could be linked to him” [132, p. 309]), increased perceived accountability and decreased intention to violate the access policy (H1). The system feature awareness of logging (used specifically to increase monitoring awareness) also increased perceived accountability and decreased intention to violate the access policy (H2). The system feature of auditing (specifically designed to increase evaluation awareness and evaluation apprehension, a state of mind that results in greater self-focused attention and concern about the evaluation of behaviors) increased the desire to engage in “approved” actions and thus perceived accountability and also decreased intention to violate the access policy (H3). Finally, the system feature electronic presence (used to emulate social presence) increased sense of accountability and decreased intention to violate the access policy (H4). Overall, we show that these design-artifact modifications can likely positively influence users to display more accountable behaviors.

## Contributions to Research and Practice

The results of this analysis provide several contributions to research and practice. First, this study breaks new ground by applying accountability theory to the IS field. Although accountability has received substantial attention in psychology [54], this theory has not been examined in IS to our knowledge. Moreover, we proposed a theoretical model that shows how key design-artifact choices—namely, identifiability, monitoring awareness, evaluation awareness, and social presence—can each positively increase accountability and can each negatively decrease intention to violate access policy. Should this theory and its results hold in other settings, our theoretical model provides a systematic explanation and prediction for design artifacts that can increase perceived accountability in users and thus decrease access policy violations.

Second, to test our theory, we employed the factorial survey method, considered the methodological gold standard for accessing ethical beliefs and normative judgments [42, 104]. Although the factorial survey method is widely used in criminology, psychology, and ethics, again, to our knowledge, this is the first application in IS research. This contribution is particularly relevant given the increasing use of the scenario method in studying ISP violations and computer abuse [22, 40, 46, 109]. The factorial survey method can be understood as a more sophisticated form of the scenario method because textual elements of the scenario of theoretical interest are experimentally varied. By applying the factorial survey method to other forms of ISP violations and other IS phenomena, researchers stand to gain deeper insights into the influence of contextual factors as well as the theoretical factors that affect respondents judgments [47].

Third, the empirical test of our model found that design artifacts of an IS can be manipulated to increase perceptions of accountability and thereby deter access policy violations. All four of our hypothesized accountability effects significantly decreased intention to violate the access policy. In addition, when the four accountability design features were used together, we found they decreased intention to violate the access policy by more than 25 percent. Hence, system design artifacts that increase accountability have strong, positive interactions with each other. These findings are particularly useful in practice because the design artifacts that we provided in our study can be applied to most systems in a simple and cost-effective manner. Furthermore, these features are minimally invasive and are not heavy handed. This is important because research has shown that more heavy-handed approaches to stopping computer abuse, such as access policy violations, can sometimes backfire and result in more undesired behaviors [61, 77].

Fourth, we provide practitioners with empirical evidence that accountability mechanisms can be a useful supplement to the principle of least privilege, sanctions, and employee training in preventing access policy violations. Our results indicate that access policy violations can likely be decreased simply by manipulating the design artifacts of the system. By increasing accountability within systems, practitioners might be able to ease access policy restrictions on users, enabling greater efficiency and flexibility of system users. This is particularly important because in practice the principle of least privilege is often poorly implemented for pragmatic reasons [137], and the need for broad access privileges within systems will continue.

## Future Research for Designing Accountability Artifacts

Although our study supports our underlying theory, more work needs to be done to further expand on the four design artifacts we studied to benefit research and practice. Thus, we summarize below our extensive literature review on how accountability could be further evoked through interface design considerations.

## Design Artifacts to Invoke Identifiability

For a richer consideration of design artifacts that support identifiability, we first turn to human–computer interaction literature that has studied these features, which are also known as cues to identity [114]. The basic idea of identity cues is that these interface features reveal or give correct personal information about a user that can be linked to the user [114]. To create cues to identity, Tanis and Postmes [114] effectively manipulated digital photographs of participants and personal information (e.g., first name) in an experimental setting and found that these cues indeed increased individuation. Photographs have similarly been shown to be effective cues to identity in similar studies [113, 128]. Photographs are likely to be especially effective identity cues because humans have a highly evolved sensitivity in recognizing the individuality and differences in facial appearance that indicate unique identity [66].

In contrast, several interface designs invoke deindividuation and can thus decrease accountability. Chief among these is allowing for anonymous or shared group log-ins and any other features that focus on group contributions without individual identification, such as anonymous electronic brainstorming [72]. Use of altered or manipulated photographs that misrepresent actual physical appearance would likely cause similar issues because humans are highly attuned to recognizing even the slightest features in faces [66]. In addition, features that allow users to take on alter egos via avatars that misrepresent who they are (e.g., become an animal) should also result in deindividuation because such avatars do not represent one’s true self. These possibilities point to interesting future research avenues.

## Design Artifacts to Invoke Monitoring and Evaluation Awareness

In reviewing the literature, we suggest that several user-interface design artifacts can be used to foster monitoring and evaluation awareness. We propose that visually depicting representations of a user being monitored and evaluated will be effective in increasing the expectation that he or she is being monitored and evaluated. Monitoring can be visually incorporated by using history features such as bread crumb trails [16], transaction logs [92], visual depictions of data history, Web logs [23], audit trails [2], and the like. If these visual artifacts are thus incorporated into system design, they should work to increase accountability.

## Design Artifacts to Increase Social Presence

Of all the possible operationalizations of design artifacts, social presence (the awareness of others in a CMC interaction [87, 127]) presents the widest variety of possibilities for system design artifacts because of the many ways that social presence can be demonstrated through system interaction. This is why one basic way that presence or awareness has been widely integrated into systems design is through features that invoke interactivity [57, 60, 125]. Such features typically include those that invoke two-way communication, control, and synchronicity, which are further described in several research papers [12, 13, 41, 56, 60, 65, 82].

Similarly, presence or telepresence [24] can be designed into systems in several other ways. For example, in instant messaging, presence is invoked by indicators of whether someone is online and available to chat and similar awareness features [58, 105]. Three-dimensional avatars that represent people online can also help increase a sense of presence [27, 80, 81]. Also, while not likely as useful as live voice, textto-speech capabilities have been shown to improve a sense of presence in online interactions [80, 81].

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations that point to promising research opportunities. First, although we found support for four different forms of accountability design artifacts, other design artifacts could also increase accountability perceptions among users. The previous section overviewed several possible ways to implement the various accountability artifacts. Further research needs to test these as well as identify other relevant accountability factors.

Second, this study examined intentions using hypothetical scenarios rather than actual behaviors within a real system. However, this method of focusing solely on intentions is accepted widely in studies involving questions of unethical, antisocial, and criminal behavior because it is difficult to receive completely honest responses through direct questioning [69, 75, 109, 130]; it is also established in the IS field in studying computer-related abuse and ethics issues for the same reasons [4, 22, 63, 67, 109]. The literature, as thoroughly reviewed by Siponen and Vance [109], has established several methodological advantages for using this method versus tracking disclosures or actual negative behavior. Importantly, by asking participants how they feel about particular scenarios, we could receive more honest answers than if we asked the participants to confess if they had personally violated the same policies in the scenarios. Still, despite the proven nature of our method, the predictive link between intentions and behavior is not perfect, and more can be learned by measuring actual behaviors with real systems. Although computer abuses (e.g., access policy violations), like other forms of socially undesirable behavior, are difficult to measure directly [109], a field study of an actual system in use—with tracking of actual abusive behaviors—would likely provide valuable insights.

Third, our study used student participants to test our theoretical model, even though student samples can sometimes poorly generalize to other populations [36]. Furthermore, when the purpose of an experiment is to test theory, convenience samples can effectively falsify whether a theory is applicable to a larger population [14]. Finally, our sample was culturally homogeneous. Although homogeneity is desirable for theory testing [15], cultural differences could affect intention to commit access policy violations. However, we chose to use student participants because it provides a more stringent test of the theory—homogeneity of a subsample provides a more severe test of theory by reducing statistical error that could obfuscate systematic violations of theory [15]. We further assert that students are an appropriate and relevant data target because they often use systems with broad access privileges as students and as part-time employees. Moreover, the students we used were specially trained in IT policy issues, and they will become employees faced with access policy issues in the future [86]. Nevertheless, it is unclear how well these results generalize to other contexts with more mature working professionals in which increased maturity could naturally decrease intention to violate access policies. Differences are also possible depending on differences in industry practice and regulations (e.g., HIPAA [Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act] in health care versus Sarbanes–Oxley in finance).

## Conclusion

Orga niza tions a re fa cing increa sing p rob lems with access policy violations, which are worsening because of the business need for broad access within systems and the proliferation of USB (Universal Serial Bus) devices and mobile computing. Because traditional security methods alone cannot resolve these security issues, this study presented a new approach to reduce access policy violations. We used accountability theory, which explains the perceived pressure to justify a person’s actions to others, to identify system mechanisms that raise perceptions of accountability. Our test of our model demonstrates that four accountability mechanisms—namely, identifiability, awareness of logging, awareness of audit, and electronic presence—substantially reduce intentions to commit access policy violations. Given these promising findings, we call for additional research in creating more sophisticated design artifacts to increase accountability in organizational insiders.
