# Beyond Transaction Cost Economics: Towards an endogenous theory of Information Technology Outsourcing

Mary C. Lacity <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Leslie P. Willcocks <sup>b</sup>, Shaji Khan <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Missouri-St. Louis, One University Boulevard, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA

Article history: Available online 14 June 2011

## a b s t r a c t

In our review, we coded 73 empirical findings from 31 journal articles that applied Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) to study Information Technology Outsourcing (ITO). As Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) note correctly, the empirical results of TCE tests in the context of ITO are mixed. We found that only 49% of the empirical ITO findings supported TCE logic. We found only slightly better support for TCE when it is used as a normative theory (54%) than when it is used as a predictive theory (47%). The main difference between Karimi-Alaghehband et al.’s (2011) contribution and our contribution to the debate focuses upon what to do next. Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) argue that ITO researchers need to apply TCE more faithfully. We argue that we are asking too much of TCE—the ITO phenomenon is more complex than can be accommodated by TCE. We argue that ITO research has matured to the point that we should be building our own endogenous ITO theory. We offer observations and insights on what such a theory might entail. In moving ITO research forward, we first critique TCE assumptions and provide alternative assumptions that seem to fit ITO observations well. We draw on our review of 741 ITO empirical findings (Lacity et al., 2010) to provide a detailed theoretical framework to advance further study that suggests the most promising constructs to use in an endogenous ITO theory.

## 1. Introduction

Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) has been the most frequently appropriated theoretical framework for the study of Information Technology Outsourcing (ITO) (Klein, 2002; Dibbern et al., 2004). TCE is a theory specifically addressing make-or-buy decisions and has therefore been viewed as a strong theoretical base for analyzing ITO decisions (e.g. Aubert et al 1996) TCE, has enioved an abundance of empirical and theoretical academic attention in other disciplines, which may have also influenced its appeal to ITO researchers (Anderson, 1994; Bowen and Jones, 1986; Griesinger, 1990; Hennart, 1991a b: Hesterly et al. 1990: Hill 1990: Joskow, 1985 1991: Lieberman 1991: Malone, 1987: Malone et al. 1987: Pisano 1990; Robins, 1987; Walker and Poppo, 1991). In addition, this is a theory we have used and applied in our own empirical work (Lacity and Willcocks, 1995: Poppo and Lacity, 2002: Willcocks and Lacity, 2009). Given its common adoption for the study of ITO, it is both relevant and timely to review the empirical applications of TCE to the ITO context.

As Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) note correctly, the empirical results of TCE tests in the context of ITO are mixed. Their interpretation of the mixed empirical results assumes ITO researchers frequently misappropriate the theory. We agree with much of their interpretation; we agree that TCE often operates better as a normative theory (Poppo and Lacity, 2002, 2006) and that ITO researchers frequently ignore interaction effects. We complement their contribution by offering additional insights into the mixed TCE results based on our review of the empirical ITO literature. In our review, we coded 73 empirical findings of TCE found in 31 ITO articles. Of these, slightly less than half of the empirical findings supported TCE logic. We categorize the reasons authors provided for the anomalies into four categories: research methods, boundary conditions, TCE assumption violation, and alternate theory explanations. With research method explanations, authors do not assume that their data provides evidence counter to TCE logic. Instead, authors attribute lack of empirical support of TCE to measurement problems, or to some TCE effects overpowering other TCE effects. Boundary condition explanations attribute lack of empirical support of TCE to the distinctive context of ITO, such as the distinctive nature of IT, the distinctive research setting (e.g. such as public sector IT), or the distinctive attributes of the data collected. TCE assumption violation explanations argue that TCE’s explicit or implicit assumptions are unsupported. Finally, alternate theory explanations suggest that other theories are more powerful in explaining ITO.

The main difference between Karimi-Alaghehband et al.’s (2011) contribution and our contribution to the debate focuses upon what to do next. Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) argue that ITO researchers need to apply TCE more faithfully. We argue that we are asking too much of TCE—the ITO phenomenon is more complex than can be accommodated by one decision-making theory. We have argued (Lacity et al., 2010) and continue to argue here that ITO research has matured to the point that we should be building our own endogenous theory rather than continuing to rely heavily on reference discipline theories. By endogenous here, we refer to the several dictionary definitions of ‘deriving or originating internally’, ‘arising from within the generating structure’ and ‘generated by internal factors’.<sup>1</sup>

The paper proceeds with some commentary on TCE as a theory. Because Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) have provided a good explanation of TCE, we will not replicate an explanation of the theory in this paper. We then discuss the method we used to review and code the empirical ITO literature that appropriated TCE as a theoretical basis to derive propositions, hypotheses, or arguments. Next, we discuss the empirical anomalies by offering research methods, boundary conditions, TCE assumption violations, and alternate theory explanations of anomalies. We conclude by arguing that ITO research may be best served by moving towards developing our own theory of ITO. To derive an ITO specific theory, we believe we can retain, recombine, and reconfigure important constructs from other theories that have been empirically tested and robustly found to be significant in the ITO context. The retained constructs need to be coherently organized, with a consistent set of assumptions that suggest why these constructs are the best predictors of ITO decisions or ITO outcomes. A beginning is made below with the development of a theoretical framework for ITO studies (see Fig. 1).

## 2. Reviewing TCE constructs

TCE’s main tenet is ‘‘to align transactions, which differ in their attributes, with governance structures, which differ in their costs and competencies, in a discriminating (mainly, transaction cost economizing) way’’ (Williamson, 1991a, p. 79). In this sense, TCE clearly focuses make-or-buy decisions on the constructs of transaction attributes, governance structures, and costs. These main constructs of TCE are well explained by Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011). Specifically, they discuss transaction attributes (asset specificity, uncertainty and frequency), types of costs (Production Costs and Transaction Costs, i.e Governance costs), and TCE’s behavioral assumptions (Bounded Rationality and Opportunism). In particular, they provide a good explanation on the differences between Behavioral Uncertainty and Environmental Uncertainty. We wish to add only a few notes to their review of TCE. First, although Williamson (1991b) calls Opportunism and Bounded Rationality assumptions of TCE, other authors have argued these are testable independent variables (Ghoshal and Morgan, 1996; Macher and Richman, 2008; Miranda and Kim, 2006). In our review, we considered empirical tests of Opportunism and Bounded Rationality. Second, Williamson (1991a) and other researchers (e.g. Alchian and Demsetz, 1972; Poppo and Lacity, 2002, 2006) consider Ease of Measurement to be a fourth transaction attribute:

Transaction cost economics regards the transaction as the basic unit of analysis (Commons. 1925, 1934) and maintains that the principal dimensions (in transaction cost economizing respects) with respect to which transactions differ are frequency, uncertainty, and asset specificity (Williamson, 1975, 1979, 1983), to which ease of measurement should probably be added (Barzel, 1982; Kenney and Klein, 1983; Alchian and Woodward, 1987; Holmstrom, 1989). (Williamson, 1991a, p. 79).

In our review, we considered empirical tests of Ease of Measurement (usually operationalized as ‘‘Measurement Difficulty’’) when authors specifically invoked TCE logic to include this construct as a transaction attribute (Poppo and Zenger, 1998, 2002; Loebbecke and Huyskens, 2006; Mayer and Salomon, 2006). We did not include Ease of Measurement or Mea surement Difficulty when Agency Theory was used to justify the construct, such as in Oh et al. (2006).

Finally, our review includes both Decisions and Decision Outcomes as valid, testable dependent variables of TCE. The traditional dependent variable in TCE is the make-or-buy ‘‘Decision’’, initially conceived as a binary option (market or hierarchy) (Williamson, 1975). TCE logic expanded to include multiple forms of sourcing options including the hierarchy, long-term contracts, franchising, joint ventures, and market (Williamson, 1991b). Some researchers have argued that true appropriations of TCE must use the ‘‘Decision’’ as the only legitimate dependent variable, while other researchers have argued that as a normative theory, the “Decision Outcome" is an important dependent variable (Poppo and Lacity. 2002. 2006; Karimi-Alaghehband et al., 2011). When Decision Outcome is used as the dependent variable, the research question becomes: Do managers who follow TCE prescriptions make better sourcing decisions than managers who do not follow TCE prescriptions (Poppo and Lacity, 2002)? We considered both dependent variables in our review. Table 1 lists and defines the constructs from TCE we considered in our review (Asset Specificity, Transaction Frequency, Uncertainty, Measurement Difficulty, External Production Cost Advantage, Transaction Costs, Opportunism, Bounded Rationality, Decision, and Decision Outcome).

## 3.1. Coding independent and dependent variables

Two of the authors independently coded 73 findings from the 31 articles. Appendix A lists all the main effects that were coded for each article. For each article, we mapped the TCE terms the authors used (called ‘‘Study Independent Variable’’ and ‘‘Study Dependent Variable’’ in Appendix A) to the TCE generic terms we defined in Table 1 (called ‘‘Study Independent Variable Coded As’’ and ‘‘Dependent Variable Category’’ in Appendix A). In coding independent variables, the ‘‘Study Independent Variable’’ term was identical to the ‘‘Study Independent Variable Coded As’’ term in 32 of the 73 findings coded. For example, Ang and Straub (1998) use the term ‘‘Transaction Costs’’ which is identical to the TCE term ‘‘Transaction Costs’’. In other cases, minor interpretations were needed. For example, Watjatrakul (2005) called a construct ‘‘Environmental Uncertainty’’ which we mapped to ‘‘Uncertainty’’. We always made sure that we coded what the authors actually defined and measured. For example. Loebbecke and Huyskens (2006) “Study Independent Variable" is called “Transaction Uncertainty’’. They define this variable as, ‘‘Transaction Uncertainty is traditionally determined by performance measurement complexity (Poppo and Zenger, 1998)’’ (p. 418). We thus coded the ‘‘Study Independent Variable Coded As’’ term as ‘‘Measurement Difficulty’’, not as Uncertainty.

In coding the dependent variables, we mapped the ‘‘Study Dependent Variable’’ terms to two Dependent Variable categories: The ‘‘Decision’’ and the ‘‘Decision Outcome’’. Concerning ‘‘Decision’’, ITO researchers have clearly used a range of dependent variables, including a binary decision (insource or outsource) (e.g. Arnett and Jones, 1994), the degree of outsourcing (e.g. Ang and Straub, 1998), the extent of substitution (e.g. Nam et al., 1996), and decisions about contract type (e.g. Poppo and Zenger, 2002). Concerning ‘‘Decision Outcome’’, ITO researchers have also examined a plethora of ITO outcomes including Degree of ITO Satisfaction (e.g. Poppo and Zenger, 2002), Client Extra Costs (e.g. Dibbern et al., 2008), and Outsourcing Success (e.g. Wang, 2002).

## 3.2. Coding relationships among variables

For this article, we coded main effects between independent and dependent variables. Appendix A has two columns associated with these codes, the empirical ‘‘Findings’’ column and the ‘‘Support for TCE’’ column. The ‘‘Findings’’ column indicates one of three possible values between an independent variable and a dependent variable: ‘‘+1’’, ‘‘-1’’, and ‘‘0’’. We coded a ‘‘+1’’ for positive relationships, ‘‘-1’’ for negative relationships, and ‘‘0’’ for relationships that were studied but not empirically significant. If the study was quantitative, we used p < .05 as the requirement for a significant positive or negative relationship. If the study was qualitative, we relied on the authors’ strong arguments for a significant positive or negative relationship (Lacity et al., 2010). For an example of coding a qualitative study, consider Dibbern et al. (2008). Using qualitative research, the authors collected data about a German financial institution that offshored six software projects to an India-based supplier. They provide strong data and arguments that Asset Specificity, which they operationalized as ‘‘Required Client Specific Knowledge’’, was positively associated with the Decision Outcome, ‘‘Client Extra Costs’’. We coded this Finding as a ‘‘+1’’.

The ‘‘Support for TCE’’ column in Appendix A indicates whether the empirical finding supports the logic of TCE or not. We coded a ‘‘1’’ to indicate that the finding is consistent with TCE logic and a ‘‘0’’ to indicate that the finding is not consistent with TCE logic. For example, Miranda and Kim (2006) found a significant positive effect between Asset Specificity and IS Outsourcing Expenditure, which was counter to one of their hypotheses and is counter to TCE logic. Thus, we coded ‘‘Support for TCE’’ column as a ‘‘0’’ for Miranda and Kim (2006).

In all, we coded 73 main effects. Of the 73 findings we coded, 45 findings (62%) used the ‘‘Decision’’ as the dependent variable and 28 findings (38%) used ‘‘Decision Outcome’’ as the dependent variable. We also examined the few papers that included intergctions effects, but left this analysis for Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011)

## 4. TCE empirical findings in the ITO context

Table 2 provides a summary of the level of support TCE has achieved across 73 empirical ITO findings. Overall, 36 of the 73 findings support TCE logic (49%). The cost constructs show 100% percent support for TCE logic. That is, every time External Production Cost Advantage or Transaction Costs were empirically examined, the findings supported TCE logic. Specifically, the higher the External Production Cost Advantage of suppliers over in-house provision, the more clients pursued outsourcing (Decision) or reported better Decision Outcomes. Similarly, the higher the Transaction Costs, the less outsourcing clients pursued (Decision).

Turning attention to the four attributes of a transaction, the support for TCE logic weakens. Of the 33 times was examined, TCE logic was supported 12 times (36%). This is the most troubling finding because Williamson views Asset Specificity as the most important transaction attribute (Williamson, 1991b) and writes Asset Specificity ‘‘is where the main predictive action resides’’ (Williamson, 2005, p. 7). Of the 16 times Uncertainty was examined, TCE logic was supported eight times (50%). Measurement Difficulty was examined six times and supported TCE logic three times (50%). Transaction Frequency was only studied four times; all four findings failed to support TCE logic. Before offering an interpretation of these findings, we think it is prudent to separate the data by dependent variable. When used as a predictive theory, ITO researchers use Decision as the dependent variable to answer the research question, ‘‘Does TCE predict ITO Decisions?’’ When used as a normative theory, ITO researchers use Decision Outcome as the dependent variable to answer the research question, ‘‘Does following TCE logic lead to better ITO Decision Outcomes?'

## 4.1. Decision as dependent variable

Among the 73 findings coded, 45 used some type of ITO Decision as the dependent variable (see Table 3). When used as a predictive theory, TCE logic was supported 21 times among the 45 times it was examined (47%). Again, the cost constructs are fully supported as explained above. The most troubling finding is the Asset Specificity—in only 30% of the findings was Asset Specificity significantly and negatively associated with the decision to outsource or propensity to outsource. Uncertainty was also supported only 37% of the time, at least as a main effect associated with ITO Decisions.

## 5. TCE anomalies in the ITO context

In this section, we interpret the 37 findings that did not support TCE logic. We first diagnosed the anomalies by reading the reasons or explanations provided by the authors of each article. For 8 out of 37 anomalies, authors merely noted that TCE was not supported and did not offer a further explanation. For 29 anomalies, authors provided at least one explanation. In total, authors provided 60 explanations for 29 anomalies. We categorized these explanations into research method explanations, boundary condition explanations, TCE assumption violation explanations, and alternate theory explanations (see Table 5).

The most frequent explanation (n = 14) offered was measurement problems. The authors argued that the lack of empirical support for TCE logic may be related to the inherent difficulty of measuring core TCE constructs (e.g. Nam et al., 1996; Lacity and Willcocks, 1995; Aubert et al., 2004; Loebbecke and Huyskens, 2006). For example, Aubert et al., (2004) considered that the ‘‘measures used to tackle asset specificity were not adequate’’ might explain the reverse link they found between asset specificity and the outsourcing level (p. 929). We note that these authors used 24 items to measure asset specificity, and question how much more thoroughness we can expect from researchers in the way of measures. Loebbecke and Huyskens (2006) did not find a statistical relationship between asset specificity and outsourcing decisions. They wrote that ‘‘the surprisingly low impact of the TCE variables on netsourcing decisions could be explained by their definition and operationalization’’ (p. 421). Although Loebbecke and Huyskens (2006) do not provide the total number of items, they indicated that they had items for technical specificity, site specificity, and human capital specificity. Measurement problems are not unique to ITO research. A number of reviews of the empirical TCE literature across a variety of disciplines point to the confusion surrounding the definitions, and therefore the measurement of, key TCE variables, as a major hurdle in empirical applications of TCE (Klein, 2005; David and Han, 2004; Macher and Richman, 2008). Even Williamson acknowledges the issue. For example, he writes about obstacles in studying Transaction Costs, ‘‘For one thing, the concept of transaction costs lacked definition. Being a vague and malleable concept, transaction costs came to be invoked as an all-purpose explanation for puzzling practices’ (Williamson, 2005, p. 4). Nevertheless, ITO researchers may take this both as a cautionary note in their future appropriations of TCE as well as an impetus to incorporate consistent and valid measures.

Authors also explained that some TCE variables were not significant because of the way the models were tested. In four instances, authors explained that when multiple TCE independent variables were tested simultaneously, one TCE variable captured most of the variance. In these cases, the authors were not questioning TCE logic, but instead suggested that TCE was supported if different statistical tests were run. For example, Mayer and Salomon (2006) did not find a significant main effect of time specificity on ITO decision when that variable was run with other TCE main effects in the model. The authors noted, ‘‘when we ran the results without the other four hazards, time specificity was negative and statistically significant’’ (p. 953). In three instances, authors explained that main TCE effects were not significant due to TCE variable interactions. For example Chen and Bharadwaj (2009) found that asset specificity had no discernable relationship to contract extensiveness, but they attributed this anomaly to the interaction effect of contract type and asset specificity.

Overall, research method explanations argue that findings are not really counter to TCE logic but instead may be explained by faulty measures or consequences of specific methodological issues.

## 5.2. Boundary condition explanations

When ITO researchers borrow TCE to study ITO decisions, they are doing what Whetton et al. (2009) call ‘‘horizontal bor rowing’’. With horizontal borrowing, researchers use concepts that were formulated in a different social context. ITO researchers expect, or assume, that the prototypic properties of the originating social contexts of TCE are similar to the context of ITO. When anomalies arise, authors suggest that the social context of ITO is distinctive, and thus outside of the boundaries of TCE. Within this broad category, three specific explanations are provided: the distinctive context of ITO, the distinctive research setting (e.g. such as public sector IT), or the distinctive attributes of the data collected.

Authors argued that the distinctive context of the study may explain 10 anomalies. For example, in discussing the positive effects (as opposed to the hypothesized negative effects) of both Asset Specificity and Uncertainty on outsourcing decisions within government organizations, Miranda and Kim (2006) pointed to relative lack of resources (munificence) in the public sector when compared to the private sector as a key explanation of why governments would outsource functions high in asset specificity and under increased uncertainty. Similarly, Loebbecke and Huyskens (2006) maintained that TCE-based variables as defined and operationalized in the traditional ITO research may not have been appropriate in the context of their study on netsourcing, as evident by a lack of support for most TCE-based variables in their study. Authors have explained six anomalies by pointing to the distinctive nature of IT. For example, Lacity and Willcocks (1995) argued that the unique nature of IT—such as IT’s constantly changing requirements and ubiquitous implementations—triggers TCE exceptions and thus operates on the fringe of mainstream TCE. Authors have explained three anomalies by pointing to the distinctive attribute(s) of data. The authors explained that a variable may be lacking enough variance to show a significant effect (e.g. Aubert et al., 2004; Mayer and Salomon, 2006), or the authors simply explained that the lack of TCE support may be an ‘‘artifact’’ of their data (e g, Nam et al. 1996)

We agree that it is important to assess the extent to which the ITO context matches a reference discipline theory’s original contexts. In the example of TCE, it is important to ask: can theories based on evidence from sourcing products be appropriate for studying ITO services? TCE’s main constructs were developed in the 1930s and enjoyed a long tradition of theoretical development and testing on more mature phenomena in manufacturing industries (Coase, 1937). The classic empirical papers on TCE examined contexts such as coal markets (Joskow, 1987), petroleum coke (Goldberg and Erickson, 1987), natural gas (Masten and Crocker. 1985), and cable companies (Williamson, 1976), Perhaps the context of ITO does not quite fit the context of TCE. By questioning the context (and not just the measures) when empirical results from ITO studies conflict with propositions derived from reference disciplines, researchers help define the boundaries of a reference theory’s applicability to ITO. This leads to heightened understanding of both the theory, and also of the ITO phenomena under investigation. It can also lead (as we suggest below) to the search for more endogenously derived theories that better fit the research context.

## 5.3. TCE assumption violation explanations

Some authors explained anomalies by examining TCE’s assumptions. TCE is based on two behavioral assumptions: bounded rationality and opportunism (Williamson, 1991a). Some ITO authors found evidence that TCE behavioral assumptions were violated. Bounded rationality assumes that decision makers intend to make rational decisions but that their rationality is limited by the information they have, the cognitive limitations of their minds, and the finite amount of time they have to make decisions (Simon, 1978). As implemented in TCE, the assumption is that decision makers will base make-or-buy decisions on cost minimization:

‘‘The criterion for organizing transactions is assumed to be the strictly instrumental one of cost-economizing. Essentially this takes two parts: economizing on production expense and economizing on transaction costs’’ (Williamson, 1979, p. 245).

Although Williamson recognizes that politics play a role in organizational processes, he assumes an organization must be economically viable in the long run. He argues that economizing is a more super-ordinate strategy than political strategizing:

I furthermore aver that, as between economizing and strategizing, economizing is much the more fundamental...More importantly, I maintain that a strategizing effort will rarely prevail if a program is burdened by significant cost excesses in production, distribution, or organization. All the clever ploys and positioning, aye, all the king’s horses and all the king’s men, will rarely save a project that is seriously flawed in first-order economizing respects (Williamson, 1991a, p. 75)

Some ITO authors explained anomalies by pointing to evidence that decision makers employed criteria other than cost minimization. For example, Diana (2009) wrote that it is possible that participants in Health Care Delivery Systems make outsourcing decisions based on other criteria. Lacity and Willcocks (1995) identified six reasons driving outsourcing behavior in client firms other than cost minimization, such as eliminating a troublesome IT manager or IT function. Tiwana and Bush (2007) found that sometimes ‘‘strategizing’’ does overpower ‘‘economizing’’ in certain ITO decisions. One thing to note is that the ITO decision was the dependent variable in these studies. Perhaps these decision makers made bad decisions and outcomes would have been better if managers followed TCE prescriptions

Dibbern et al. (2008) questioned the assumption of vendor opportunism. The authors found that higher levels of Required Client-Specific Knowledge (a transaction attribute) increased Client Extra Costs (an ITO Outcome). Both TCE and the knowledge-based view (KBV) predict this relationship, but have different underlying assumptions driving the logic. Using qualitative research, the authors found that the reasons for this finding were much better explained by a KBV assumption than by a TCE assumption. The client’s excess costs were caused more by the need for more knowledge transfer than by the need to safeguard against vendor opportunism. The authors concluded, ‘‘These extra costs most often arose independently from the threat of opportunistic behavior, challenging the predominant TCE logic of market failure (p. 333).’’

TCE has a number of other assumptions besides bounded rationality and opportunism. TCE assumes hierarchies administer rewards and punishments to align managers’ goals with those of the firm, both parties in an exchange may not have the same information (information asymmetry), and the transaction is the best unit of analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989; Williamson, 1991a). Some ITO researchers have found that these other TCE assumptions were violated in the context of ITO. For example, Lacity and Willcocks (1995) argued that their data did not fit well with the assumption that all members of an organization are motivated to make cost efficient decisions because internal rewards and punishments align individual goals with corpo rate goals. TCE really considers only two stakeholders—the client firm and supplier firm. TCE sufficiently warns client firms that suppliers may behave opportunistically. But we found that this assumption ignores the stakeholders within client and supplier organizations, who, we found, rarely share the same unified goals (Lacity and Willcocks, 2001). Lacity and Willcocks (1995) also questioned whether the transaction is the best unit of analysis. TCE assumes decisions have initial entry and exit points defined by the transaction, but we found that sourcing decisions are highly influenced by past outsourcing experiences, prior supplier relationships, and multiple sourcing decisions. These violations of assumptions are examined in more detail in the discussions section.

## 5.4. Alternate theory explanations

Some authors argue that alternate theories are more powerful in explaining ITO results than TCE. Of the 37 TCE findings that were not supported, authors suggested alternate theories 7 times. For example, Diana (2009) found that environmental variables (such as government regulations) are driving ITO decisions in the healthcare industry. Loebbecke and Huyskens (2006) found more support for strategic management variables like competitive relevance of company's software applica: tions and strategic vulnerability. Alvarez-Suescun (2010) wrote, ‘‘Clearer support is found for resource-based arguments than for predictions from transaction cost approaches,’’ p. 640.

## 6. Discussion: towards endogenous theory

So far, we have shown that over half of the empirical tests of TCE have resulted in anomalies in the ITO context. We have categorized how authors explain these anomalies into research methods, boundary conditions, TCE assumption violations, and alternate theory explanations. The question remains, ‘‘what do these findings mean for future ITO research?’’ Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) argue quite legitimately and effectively that ITO researchers need to apply TCE more faithfully. We acknowledge and agree with their assessments on the need to test TCE variable interactions. We, however, think the way forward is develop our own endogenous theory of ITO. We may be asking too much of TCE—the ITO phenomenon is more complex than can be accommodated by one decision-making theory. We have argued (Lacity et al., 2010) and continue to argue here that ITO research has matured to the point that we could and should be building on ITO-specific research findings and developing what we have defined as endogenous ITO theory rather than continuing to rely over-heavily on reference discipline theories. In this discussion section, we further explore the important point that TCE has received mixed empirical results and criticism from other disciplines. Thus, ITO researchers are not alone. Given our vast base of empirical ITO research, we are ready for an endogenous theory of ITO and we describe some foundations for such a theory.

## 6.1. Mixed empirical results and criticisms from other disciplines

Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) make many good points about how TCE may be applied more faithfully and rigorously in ITO empirical work. Their points, together with TCE as a useful lens for examining contracting problems, are positively reinforced in a general review of TCE empirical studies by Macher and Richman (2008). In an impressive study, Macher and Richman (2008) reviewed 900 empirical appropriations of TCE to the domains of economics, health economics and policy, agricultural economics and policy, marketing, accounting, finance, law, organization theory, international business, and strategy.<sup>3</sup> One positive development they point to is the way studies are increasingly combining the predictions of TCE with other theoretical perspectives—for example organization theory, neo-classical economics, competitive strategy, innovation, alternative theories of the firm—to explain better a variety of empirical phenomena. We agree with this direction and indeed have utilized TCE with power, relationship, or resource-based theories ourselves to enhance explanatory and predictive power (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993; Seddon et al., 2007; Willcocks and Lacity, 2009).

More negatively, with some parallels to the observations of Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) on ITO studies, Macher and Richman (2008) conclude that the use of TCE reasoning to inform empirical research has been ‘‘piecemeal’’, and point to a number of theoretical and empirical gaps. Amongst these, in particular, they cite the frequency of relatively crude measurement, especially of asset specificity and opportunism. Also, they cite the failure to explore interaction effects amongst both TCE and non-TCE variables. With Williamson, they also want TCE to have a more rigorous mathematical foundation and fully formal analysis, the idea being that this will force researchers to define with greater precision the concepts central to the empirical analysis. They also cite the failure to investigate the behavioral foundations of opportunism, or its lack, in different institutional, and indeed cultural settings.

Clearly Macher and Richman (2008) do suggest a number of ways in which TCE can be complemented and developed, but the weight of their discussion falls on how to be more faithful to TCE constructs, and be more precise in measurement. This is to underplay the considerable array of criticisms of TCE that have appeared in the many reference fields they have reviewed, though we acknowledge that Macher and Richman’s major focus was only on empirical studies. For example, in Lacity and Willcocks (1995), we pointed to prior criticisms of TCE, such as the criticism that TCE’s unit of analysis (i.e., the transaction) ignores history; the theory disregards the complementarity among multiple transactions; and TCE’s constructs are difficult to measure (Fischer, 1977; Milgrom and Roberts, 1990, 1995). There are many more criticisms to include since Lacity and Willcocks (1995) and a much longer history of mixed empirical support to report. Carter and Hodgson (2006) provide another impressive review of the empirical results of TCE. They also report mixed empirical results. And perhaps most impressive of all, Williamson (2005) directly addressed the ‘‘contested mechanisms’’ of TCE. He acknowledges that ‘‘the economics of governance is an unfinished project’’ (p. 16). Our point is that reference theories such as TCE often have criticisms, limitations, and spotty empirical support. Importing these ‘‘warts’’ should not diminish a research contribution, but rather enhance the depth of understanding to be achieved in new, specifically information systems, contexts.

What is interesting in this focus on more precise definition and measurement is that the weight of most empirical studies seems to fall much more in the direction of corroborating, rather than more neutrally testing TCE. This is particularly true in the IT outsourcing field. At one level it is not surprising for an immaturely disciplined IS field to seek prestigious, external theories that can be utilized relatively quickly. One issue here, as we have argued elsewhere, is that this importing of theory from other disciplines needs to be more critical than it has been (Lacity and Willcocks. 20o9), In practice, for example, within economics. TCE has been the subiect of long-standing and incisive criticisms, but these have rarely been articulated in IS studies that utilize TCE. A more noticeable tendency in IS studies has been to assume that anomalous findings reflect on weaknesses in measurement and research method or that ITO is outside the bounds of TCE. Few ITO researchers questioned the viability of TCE assumptions, conceptions and prescriptions. As we saw in Table 5, 13 of the 60 explanations of TCE anomalies in ITO considered TCE’s assumptions. It is also the case that even where such anomalies may be many, TCE concepts and language are sufficiently loosely calibrated for an explanation to be found within TCE and its exceptions, such as uncertainty, small number of suppliers, or due to ambiguities in TCE language (Lacity and Willcocks, 1995, 2009). Building this point, one also notes what Hodgson (1994) describes as the ‘regrettable malleability’ of TCE constructs. TCE seemed cast as a high level general theory, its uncalibrated constructs permitting empirical data to be fitted to support the theory all too easily. This becomes especially problematic where, as in several IT outsourcing and other IS studies, TCE is assumed to be a fully explanatory theory and few tests of its predictiveness in terms of project success or failure are carried out.

This discussion suggests several ways forward. Scholars should by all means work to sharpen TCE constructs, be more faithful to them, and aim for more precise measurements. But, as we have suggested above, there are sufficient mixed results with applying TCE to the study of IT outsourcing to suggest that TCE can be more usefully adopted and tested together with complementary theories (Cheon et al., 1995; Grover et al. 1997), offering different insights into the same phenomena, or with contesting theories (Klein, 2002) that allow the best explanation to arise, rather than be applied alone. We believe we should recognize the extraordinary richness within the empirical IS studies carried out on IT outsourcing over the last nearly 20 years, and build an endogenous testable theory sufficiently elaborated to capture the distinctive attributes of IT outsourcing phenomena, and able to provide sufficient explanation and description.

## 6.2. Foundations for an ITO theory

From these points, together with the high level of anomalies discovered when reviewing the empirical studies, it seems that IT outsourcing may well be a phenomenon that overflows TCE’s ability to explain and predict decisions and outcomes. Can we build an alternative, more appropriate theory to assist ITO studies? In the rest of this paper we consolidate the known ITO research findings into a theoretical framework for use, testing, and further development in the field.

A formal theory like TCE comprises assumptions, constructs, and hypothesized relationships among constructs. Although developing an actual ITO theory is beyond the scope of this debate, we offer some observations and insights on what such a theory might entail. It is very easy to criticize an existing theory, but quite a different matter to propose a new one. In moving ITO research forward, we offer three assumptions about ITO and provide, in each case, reasons why these may be more appropriate than TCE assumptions. Next, we draw on our review of 741 ITO empirical findings (Lacity et al., 2010) to select the most promising constructs to use in an endogenous ITO theory and propose some sample propositions.

6.2.1. ITO Assumption 1. Management practices are a more important source of IT production cost efficiencies than economies of scale

The studies in this TCE review found that a supplier’s External Production Cost Advantage always significantly affected ITO decisions and ITO outcomes. This is quite a testimony to the predictive power of TCE’s notion of production costs. TCE assumes that suppliers possess inherent economies of scale that allow them to incur lower average costs through mass production efficiencies and labor specialization. Mass production is presumed to reduce average costs by allocating fixed costs over more units of output and by receiving volume discounts on inputs. Labor specialization is presumed to reduce costs by allocating workers to focus on tasks at which they are most adept. We believe, however, the source for an IT supplier’s production cost advantages has more to do with management practices than with size effects.

In Lacity et al. (2010), we reviewed 33 findings of size (client firm size, client IT department size, supplier size, and trans action size) on ITO decisions and ITO outcomes. No patterns emerged for any of these size variables. For example, three empirical ITO studies found larger clients outsource more, five studies found smaller clients outsource more, and six studies found no significance of size whatsoever. No size pattern is evident perhaps because there is simply no pattern to uncover. In our own case study research, size measures did not have any relationship to a supplier’s subsequent ability to reduce client costs in our data. Specifically, in Lacity and Willcocks (1998), we showed how three measures of size (data center size, IT headcount, and IT budget) did not explain whether clients achieved IT cost savings with outsourcing. In the context of ITO, we have a very powerful finding: Managerial practices such as standardization, centralization, technology enablement (like self-service portals), and tight controls contribute more to IT production cost efficiencies than size. We have made this point in several articles and books, including Lacity and Hirschheim (1995), Lacity et al. (1996), Lacity and Willcocks (1998), and Lacity and Willcocks (2009). We are not saying that scale does not matter—we have certainly seen how large ITO suppliers have an important pool of global talent that eludes many internal IT organizations. But we are saying that the preponderance of our data suggests that IT production costs have more to do with management practices than inherent economies of scale. A rider to this finding is that empirically we have found that not only are such management practices often replicable in-house (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1995), but that large client organizations can also often replicate economies of scale achieved by IT outsourcing suppliers (Lacity and Willcocks, 2009; Willcocks and Lester, 1999).

## 6.2.2. ITO assumption 2: ITO decisions are highly interdependent

In Lacity and Willcocks (1995), we first questioned whether the transaction is the best unit of analysis for understanding ITO decisions. TCE assumes decisions have initial entry and exit points defined by the transaction. For TCE the decision begins when an organizational member initiates an outsourcing evaluation for a particular transaction. The decision terminates with a decision to insource (make) or outsource (buy). In the ITO context, we argued that using the transaction as the unit of analysis overlooked the broader historical context which has significant implications for ITO decisions. We think ITO deci: sions are not made around isolated transactions. Instead we find that most ITO clients are in their second, third, and even fourth generation of ITO relationships. A client’s prior history with delivering IT, with prior ITO experiences, and client capabilities are all vital to understanding the ITO decisions a client makes today. Indeed client retained capabilities may well be a major variable that can explain why some outsourcing which does not follow TCE stipulations can still be successful (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998; Willcocks and Feeny, 2006). Some of the more interesting, yet still understudied ITO research, is looking at the dynamic effects of outsourcing over time (e.g. DeDrick et al., 2010; Levina and Ross, 2003; Jarvenpaa and Mao, 2008; Yakhelf, 2009; Willcocks and Feeny, 2006; Willcocks et al., 2010). These authors have begun to discover how capabilities are built over multiple generations of outsourcing, bundled sourcing and multi-sourcing. Some are discovering significant reduced overall Transaction Costs when IT services are bundled, rather than bought in separate transactions (Willcocks et al., 2010).

## 6.2.3. ITO assumption 3. Stakeholders within organizations do not have aligned ITO goals

As noted above, TCE assumes hierarchies administer rewards and punishments to align managers’ goals with those of the firm. In reality, we argue that TCE’s view of only two stakeholders—the client firm and the supplier firm—is too simplistic in the context of ITO. Within client firms, for example, we identified at least six different stakeholders: senior management, IT management, business unit managers, IT staff, end users, and in-house specialists such as lawyers, auditors, and human resource specialists. Various client stakeholders expected different things from IT outsourcing. Senior management frequently expected total cost savings. End users typically focused only on service delivery, regardless of costs, because they don’t directly pay for IT. Client IT staff members focused on the effects of ITO on their careers and on the technical quality and speed of delivery. Client specialists focused on controls (for auditors), contract provisions (for lawyers), and redundancy implications (for HR specialists) (Lacity and Willcocks, 2000; Lacity and Rottman, 2008).

Supplier organizations are equally complex. Suppliers house a myriad of stakeholders, all expecting different things from client relationships. Senior supplier management typically focused on profit margin and revenue growth. The sales team looked to sell big deals fast. The supplier delivery teams wanted interesting work. The supplier engagement managers were in the thorny position of satisfying all the supplier-side stakeholders in addition to satisfying all the client-side stakeholders. In addition, the supplier engagement managers competed with their peers for the supplier’s best human and technical resources for their account (Feeny et al., 2005). To further complicate the stakeholder landscape, clients frequently engaged multiple suppliers. One supplier may be working on an upgrade for the client’s software that is currently hosted and maintained by another supplier. Additional stakeholders may include the client’s external customers or labor unions that may have strong preferences against outsourcing. The complex stakeholder landscape suggests that the success of ITO will largely depend on managing the relationships among a diverse group of stakeholders. We posit that it is dangerous to assume stakeholder alignment within organizations, but rather stakeholder alignment must be actively and aggressively designed anew in the face of ITO.

Having presented alternative assumptions, let us turn to constructs.

## 6.2.4. Constructs for an ITO theory

A start on this can be made through the findings of Lacity et al. (2010), where we reviewed the entire empirical body of ITO literature from 1992 to 2010. In total, we coded 741 relationships between independent and dependent variables. By extracting the best evidence, we developed a theoretical framework of the most important categories of constructs affecting ITO Decisions and ITO Outcomes (see Fig. 1). As can be seen in Fig. 1, the determinants of ITO decisions include categories of constructs associated with motives to outsource, transaction attributes, client firm characteristics, influence sources, and ITO outcomes. The determinants of ITO outcomes include categories of constructs associated with client and supplier capabilities, contractual and relational governance, decision characteristics, transaction attributes, and ITO decisions. Within each construct category, we indicate the constructs that were repeatedly tested and found to affect significantly ITO decisions or ITO outcomes. Because ITO researchers have widely appropriated theories from reference disciplines, theoretical constructs from several reference disciplines are evident in Fig. 1. We thus see that several constructs borrowed from reference disciplines are important in explaining ITO decisions and outcomes. To derive an ITO specific theory, we believe we can retain important constructs from other theories that have been empirically tested and robustly found to be significant in the ITO context. We also believe we can begin to discard constructs from reference dis ciplines that have not been empirically robust in the ITO context, like Asset Specificity from TCE. For example, Aubert and Weber (2001) make a convincing argument to use specialization<sup>4</sup> as a transaction attribute instead of asset specificity.

This is a capability specific to IT.

Thus, when we refer to ‘‘endogenous’’, by this we mean recombining existing theory in new ways and throwing out constructs that do not work discovered as a result of empirically grounded testing with ITO-specific data.

It may be that ITO researchers can even inform reference disciplines based on our empirical results. For example, our empirical domain in ITO shows that clients need a different set of retained capabilities when outsourcing. Reference theories like Resource Based View speak of capabilities that are valuable, rare, non-imitable, and non-substitutable, but we are not aware of theories that argue that different capabilities are needed when outsourcing. In Fig. 1, four of the five client capabilities associated with positive ITO outcomes are about capabilities clients needed to manage ITO: Supplier Management Capability. Contract Negotiation Capability. Cultural Distance Management Capability, and ITO risk Management Capability. (Only a client’s IS Technological and Methodological Capability is about a client’s ability to do IT in-house). When outsourcing IT—and perhaps other business services—client firms need a different set of retained capabilities than when these services are managed in house.

Our main argument here is that we have identified the most robust findings from among 741 ITO findings and these can provide a solid foundation for an endogenous ITO theory.

## 6.2.5. ITO propositions

Although the development of an entire ITO theory is beyond the scope of this paper, we provide some examples of how propositions might be derived from the assumptions above and from the preponderance of evidence from Lacity et al. (2010) depicted in Fig. 1.

Assumption 1 supposes that management practices are a more important source of IT production cost efficiencies than economies of scale. We believe that ITO suppliers with mature capabilities are the reason that ITO suppliers can deliver positive results to clients while still generating positive margins for the supplier firm. In Lacity et al. (2010), we identified from the empirical ITO literature a collection of three supplier capabilities that are associated with positive ITO outcomes: IS Human Resource Management Capability–Supplier, IS Technical and Methodological Capability-Supplier, and Domain Understanding. These capabilities—not supplier size—were correlated with good ITO outcomes. Sample propositions that may be conjectured based on assumption 1 and the empirical data are:

Proposition. IT suppliers with distinctive, mature capabilities deliver better ITO outcomes to clients than suppliers with immature capabilities.

Proposition. IT supplier capabilities predict client ITO outcomes better than IT supplier size.

Assumption 2 states that ITO decisions are highly interdependent. The empirical evidence also points to the interdependency. For example, researchers found that client firms with more recent ITO engagements had higher frequencies of success than older engagements (e.g. Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Rottman and Lacity, 2008). In Table 6 and Fig. 1, we also see that Prior Client/Supplier Working Relationship, the situation in which the client and supplier organizations have worked together in the past, was associated with better ITO outcomes (e.g. Lee and Kim, 1999; Gopal et al., 2003). Researchers attribute these findings to learning curve effects. Researcher found that clients need to master a significant learning curve, and once they do, they start achieving better ITO outcomes (e.g. Carmel and Agarwal, 2002; DeDrick et al., 2010; Kaiser and Hawk, 2004; Rottman and Lacity, 2006, 2008). This empirical finding is depicted in Fig. 1 by the bi-directional arrow between ITO Decisions and ITO Outcomes that shows that current ITO decisions are affected by the outcomes from previous or current ITO engagements. From assumption 2 and empirical data, we can propose:

Proposition. Clients with prior ITO experience, both successful or otherwise, achieve better ITO outcomes than clients with no prior ITO experience.

Obviously, these broad propositions hardly form an ITO theory. Much work needs to be done to create a coherent ITO theory based on ITO specific assumptions and based on the body of empirical ITO literature. Our goal here is merely to encourage ITO researchers to start forging ahead with our own data-driven theories. The advantage of starting to develop an endogenous theory based on the findings from Fig. 1 is that it represents, in summative form, almost all we know, as represented in the empirical peer-reviewed research, about IT outsourcing and related phenomena. A further advantage is that theory based on data cannot be completely refuted by more data (Glaser and Strauss, 1999). A way forward is to develop a coherent ITO theory, test hypotheses, and refine the theory in actual research studies.

## 7. Summary

Transaction Cost Economics has been the most frequently appropriated theory for the study of IT outsourcing (Dibbern et al., 2004; Klein, 2002). In this review, we found that hypotheses derived from TCE for the constructs External Production Cost Advantage and Transaction Costs have received strong empirical support in the ITO context. We seem to have generated enough evidence across multiple studies to at least understand the effects of these two independent variables within the ITO context. However, many TCE transaction attributes including Asset Specificity, Uncertainty, Measurement Difficulty, and Transaction Frequency have produced mixed results. To interpret TCE anomalies, we have documented how ITO researchers question their own measures and methods, argue that ITO is distinctive, or propose that alternate reference theories better explain ITO findings. We have discussed that ITO researchers are not alone in finding mixed results. Prior reviews of TCE in non-ITO contexts also report mixed results (e.g. Bowen and Jones, 1986; Carter and Hodgson, 2006; Macher and Richman, 2008). We have urged authors to consider what the vast amount of empirical research tells us about ITO decisions in terms of operating assumptions and the most important determinants of ITO decisions and ITO outcomes.

With Karimi-Alaghehband et al. (2011) we agree that being more faithful to TCE theory and its constructs, more careful in definitions, and more precise in measurement is certainly worthwhile work. We also agree with the importance of extending work on major concepts, for example examining more deeply the behavioral and institutional foundations of opportunism, something to which Williamson himself seems sympathetic. However, we would argue that there are sufficient mixed results with applying TCE to studying IT outsourcing to suggest that other work may be even more important. Such work would follow from the work we began in this paper, and in Lacity et al. (2010), in developing the foundations—in the form of assumptions, constructs and propositions—of an endogenous ITO theory. Research work would involve strengthening these theoretical foundations, and also testing the theory in the field. One important corollary would be to see ITO researchers, and perhaps the IS field generally, learn to have the confidence to build theory from the phenomena they are observing rather than, as has often happened, to look over their shoulders to other reference disciplines to import theory to corroborate.
