# Information Systems and Task Demand: An Exploratory Pupillometry Study of Computerized Decision Making

## Abstract:

Information systems (IS) play an important role in successful execution of organizational decisions, and the ensuing tasks that rely on those decisions. Because decision making models show that cognitive load has a significant impact on how people use information systems, objective measurement of cognitive load becomes both relevant and important in IS research. In this paper, we manipulate task demand during a decision making task in four different ways. We then investigate how increasing task demand affects a user’s pupil data during interaction with a computerized decision aid. Our results suggest that pupillometry has the potential to serve as a reliable, objective, continuous and unobtrusive measure of task demand and that the adaptive decision making theory may serve as a suitable framework for studying user pupillary responses in the IS domain.

Keywords: pupillometry; task demand; adaptive decision making; eye tracking; cognitive load

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in technology has afforded IS researchers the opportunity to take advantage of specialized hardware and software that allows them to examine physiological measures indicating a change in user behavior. For example, a number of IS studies have used eye movement data to better understand users’ viewing and search behaviors (e.g., Cyr et al. 2009, Djamasbi and Hall-Phillips 2014). Because eye movements reveal a great deal of information about user attention and awareness, eye tracking is becoming increasingly popular in industry research (Djamasbi 2014). As improvements in hardware technologies continue to lower the price of eye tracking systems, a greater number of scholars and industry researchers will be able to use these technologies in their laboratories (Djamasbi 2014, Rotaru et al. forthcoming). This in turn creates a greater need for extending the use of eye movement data in information systems (IS) research. For example, changes in pupil contraction and dilation have been shown to be a reliable source of one’s mental load (Kahneman 1973, King 2009, Klingner et al. 2011, Piquado et al. 2010, Beatty and Kahneman 1966, Beatty 1982, Rosch and Vogel-Walcutt 2013). Because mental effort plays a major role in how people use computers to make decisions (Djamasbi 2007, Gregor and Benbasat 1999, Todd and Benbasat 1994), pupillometry can serve as an effective tool in understanding users’ decision making behavior. Moreover, pupillometry has the added advantage of revealing a user’s subconscious experience of using a computerized decision aid because changes in pupillary responses are mostly regulated by the autonomic nervous system (Gabella 2012).

In this paper, we explore pupillary responses to an IS decision task and argue that the adaptive decision making theory (Payne et al. 1988, 1993) may serve as a suitable theoretical framework for IS pupillometry studies. According to this theory, task demand affects how people go about making decisions. People adjust to the complexity of decision environments by using decision strategies that can help them make good decisions while conserving their limited cognitive resources. Because pupil dilation serves as a reliable indicator of cognitive effort (Beatty 1982, Rosch and Vogel-Walcutt 2013), the decision makers’ flexibility in adjusting their decision strategy to conserve cognitive load is likely to be reflected in the changes in their pupil information.

Using the adaptive decision making theory as a framework, the objective of this study is to examine the link between task demand and pupil data. Hence, the primary focus of our study is different from studies that measure decision quality and consequently the methodology of our study is more similar to studies that focus on other properties of decision making such as decision making processes, judgments and choices (Kamis et al. 2008, Park et al. 2008, Simpson and Hale 1969, Einhaeuser et al 2010, Atalay et al. 2012, Glaholt et al. 2009, Fehrenbacher and Tracy 2016).

Detecting the impact of task demand on cognition unobtrusively via pupil data overtime is an important contribution to IS literature because cognitive demand has a major impact on how effectively computerized decision tools are used (Gregor and Benbasat 1999). Thus, the results of this study can provide insight for the design of advanced adaptive decision aids.

In order to study users’ reactions to task demands we test their pupillary responses under four different task demand treatments. We then examine possible differences in users’ pupil data by analyzing pupil dilation, the trend of pupil dilation over decision time, and the variation in pupil dilation under different treatments.

The paper is organized as follows. First, we provide a brief review of relevant literature. Next, we conduct a decision making laboratory experiment to examine the effect of four different task demands on pupillary responses. Finally, we conclude the paper with the discussion of our results and its implications for IS research and practice.

## 2. Background

Before we can see an object, light has to reflect off the object and travel to our eyes (Djamasbi 2014). The amount of light that is registered by our eyes is controlled by the constriction and expansion of our pupil which is continuously adjusted by our autonomic nervous system (Gabella 2012, Thayer and Brosschot 2005, Thayer and Lane 2009). In addition to controlling the amount of light that enters our eyes, adjustment in our pupil size which is mostly outside our control, has been associated with a variety of our internal states. For example, studies show significant association between pupil size and attractiveness ratings or arousal responses (Hess 1965, Kahneman 1973). In this paper, we focus on the relationship between pupillary responses and cognitive load. Because pupil dilation can be measured continuously during task processing and because it has a low latency (0.3-0.5 seconds) with respect to changes in mental workload, it can provide a sensitive and robust measure for cognitive load (Bailey et al. 2007, Baily and Iqbal 2008, Kramer 1991, Silva 2009).

## 2.1 Pupillary Responses and Cognitive Load

Research shows that changes in pupil dilation can serve as a reliable proxy for people’s cognitive effort and suggests that pupil dilation is influenced by task characteristics. For example, when people try to solve a task, the size of their pupil seems to have a positive correlation with the difficulty of the task (Kahneman 1973). A study by Hess and Polt (1964) shows that pupil dilation increased 22 percent when individuals were asked to calculate 16 times 23, but only 11 percent when they were asked to calculate 7 times 8. Thus, the higher computational complexity of multiplying double digit figures increased pupil dilation. In addition to showing that the harder the problem the larger the pupil size, the work by Hess and Polt (1964) is one of the first to illustrate that tracking pupil dilation permits the researcher to observe cognitive workload continuously at any time during a task.

The work by Kahneman and Beatty (1966) also shows a relationship between pupil size and cognitive effort, that is, their findings show that the size of participants’ pupil diameter and the number of digits in a number that they had to memorize was positively correlated. Similarly, the work by Johnson (1971) provides evidence of a positive relationship between cognitive effort and pupil size. When participants were informed that they no longer had to retain the digits that they were asked to remember, their pupil constricted (Johnson 1971).

More recent evidence using more advanced eye tracking technology confirms and extends earlier research, suggesting that changes in cognitive load are indicated by changes in pupil dilation (e.g. King 2009, Klingner et al. 2011, Piquado et al. 2010). For instance, Klingner et al. (2010) examined effects of aural versus visual task presentation on pupil dilation. They found that the magnitude of the pupil response was greater for aural presentation of memory tasks involving digits and sentences of varying complexity being read out to participants. Similarly, using aural information presentation, Piquado et al. (2010) showed that pupillary response was an effective measure of cognitive load for both younger and older adults.

Oliveira et al. (2009) presented participants with search tasks and subsequently with Google web search results. Search results pages were manipulated to contain both relevant and irrelevant information. The study results showed that on average pupil dilation increased when participants examined the relevant, as compared to the irrelevant information in the interval between second two and second five after stimuli onset. The increased pupil dilation for relevant information indicates users’ increased effort in processing what they deemed relevant to the task at hand and thus confirms the positive link between pupil size and cognitive effort.

In addition to the average size of the pupil, there is indication that variability in pupil dilation may also reveal users’ cognitive load. Rhythmic contractions and dilations of the pupil that occur at rest do seem to disappear during the performance of mental arithmetic (Kahneman and Beatty 1966 in Kahneman 1974). The relationship between cognitive load and pupil dilation variation (PDV) has been supported by a recent IS study that shows a positive relationship between PDV and task difficulty (Buettner et al. 2015). Another recent IS study suggests that PDV may be more sensitive than pupil dilation (PD) in detecting cognitive load in complex problem solving tasks (Shojaeizadeh et al. 2015). Shojaeizadeh et al. (2015) found a significant correlation between pupil dilation variation and the subjective measure of time demand but they did not find a significant relationship between pupil dilation and the same self-report measure of cognitive load.

Buettner et al. (2015) argue that the mere nature of IS tasks make PDV an appropriate and relevant measure of cognitive demand in IS studies. According to Buettner et al. (2015), realistic IS task environments require users to complete a series of subtasks, such as: information acquisition, recall and comparison. Each of these sub-activities place “a recurring demand of mental activity resulting in a variability of the pupil diameter” (Buettner et al. 2015, p. 536).

## 2.2 Pupillary Responses and Decision Making

Because steady gazes represent visual information that is sent to our brain for processing, fixations provide invaluable data for understanding user preference formation, decision and choice behavior (Djamasbi 2014, Shimojo et al. 2003, Atalay et al. 2012, Glaholt and Reingold 2009). Little work has been done however, to examine pupil dilation movement in decision making, particularly in complex decision environments such as those that involve decision support tools. It is reasonable to argue that complex decisions increase individuals’ cognitive load and thus decision making behavior is likely to be traced via pupil information.

A number of studies provide support for the validity of this argument. For example, a study by Simpson and Hale (1969) shows that even simple decisions can impact our pupillary responses. The decision task used in the study by Simpson and Hale (1969) required participants to use a lever which could be moved in two directions. In the decision condition, participants had to decide in which direction to move the lever within a seven second interval, before moving the lever in the decided direction. In the no-decision condition, participants were told in which direction to move the lever within a seven second interval before then moving the lever in the direction that they were instructed. Subsequently, they had to make the response. In the decision condition, pupil size was found to be greater than in the no-decision condition. This was attributed to the higher cognitive activity in the decision condition.

Similarly, another study suggests that decisions increase cognitive load and thus they can be traced by pupil dilation. For example, using three experiments, Einhaeuser et al. (2010) showed that pupil dilation could predict decision making for simple choices. In the first experiment, participants were asked to push a button as a response to a stimulus at any point in time within a 10 second time window. In the second experiment, participants were asked to covertly choose one out of five digits that were presented in consecutive intervals for two seconds each. In a third experiment, participants had to choose one digit in a five-digit sequence, but this time participants were instructed which digit to pick. In all cases, pupil dilation alone predicted the choice the participants made, such that pupil dilation was increased during the two second interval that presented the chosen option.

Pupil dilation as a response to decision making has been also observed in truth telling studies. Lying is cognitively more demanding than telling the truth because individuals need to remember a ‘second reality’. Consistently, in a communications game where the individuals had to decide whether to report the truth or exaggerate it (biased message) Wang et al. (2010) found that pupil dilation increased when individuals decided to send deceptive messages.

## 2.3 Adaptive Decision Making Theory and IS Pupillometry

In this paper, we argue that the adaptive decision making theory (Payne et al. 1988, 1993) can serve as a suitable theoretical framework for understanding user pupillary responses in decision support system studies. According to this theory, decision making is a highly contingent information processing behavior driven by two primary objectives: the decision makers’ desire to make good judgments and their desire to minimize the use of cognitive resources that are needed to make good decisions. Conserving cognitive resources is an intelligent adaptive response as human information processing capability is inherently limited. Challenged by the conflicting desires to increase accuracy and decrease effort, people use different decision strategies to meet the constraints of their decision environments (Payne et al. 1993). In other words, based on the demand from the decision environment, people make adjustments to their effort-accuracy levels. For example, when faced with a time limit, people increase their level of cognitive effort by speeding up their decision making process to make reasonably accurate decisions (Maule and Edland 1997). If this strategy is not sufficient, people accommodate the time constraint by switching to a more efficient information processing strategy, for example, one that allows them to save time by using heuristics focusing on a smaller set of available information. A recent study provides evidence that individuals adjust their decision making strategy to accommodate time-pressure by using heuristics, such as elimination-by-aspect (Fehrenbacher and Smith 2014).

Cognitive load that varies with the demand of a decision environment and the way decision makers adapt to their environment is a key construct in adaptive decision making theory. Because cognitive load is influenced by the task at hand and because pupil dilation is a reliable proxy of cognitive load (King 2009; Klingner et al. 2010; Piquado et al. 2010), the level of task demand is likely to be detected by changes in one’s pupil size (that is, pupil dilation or PD). Additionally, because people adjust to task demand by switching to information processing strategies that can conserve their cognitive resources, their flexible adaptive decision behavior is likely to be detected by the variation in their pupil size (i.e., pupil dilation variation or PDV).

## 3. Method

The objective of this study was to examine the impact of cognitive demand, manipulated as four different task demands, on users’ pupillary responses. In order to achieve the objective of our study, we conducted a laboratory experiment, which provided a suitable setting for collecting pupil data and controlling task demand.

The review of the literature in our paper underlines the notion that both pupil dilation (PD) and pupil dilation variation (PDV) are associated with cognitive load and decision making. Thus, we examined possible differences in PD and PDV in the four different treatments. The literature review also suggested that analyzing PD over time may be useful in understanding decision behavior. For example, both Simpson and Hale (1969) and Einhaeuser et al. (2010) found that pupil dilation over time exhibits consistent patterns such that time periods associated with a decision lead to increased pupil size. Thus, we used the analysis of PD over time to examine whether and under which conditions we find evidence for such associations for a typical IS decision task.

## 3.1 Participants and Design

One hundred and ten (49 male, 61 female) undergraduate business students from a large university participated in this study. Participants were drawn from an information systems class in the business school of the university. Participants took part voluntarily. They could choose to take part either in laboratory studies or in alternative tasks for receiving credit points (e.g. George et al. 2000, Djamasbi et al. 2008, Djamasbi and Loiacono 2008, Fehrenbacher and Tracy 2016). The information systems class covered topics such as ERP, project management and software implementation. Thus, participants had an understanding of the importance of the decision of implementing an Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system and risks, benefits and consequences involved. In particular, the software implementation topic covered subject matters such as vendor selection processes and decisions. The topic regarding vendor selection processes and decisions which is particularly relevant to the experimental task used in our study, was covered in details in the course prior to the experiment, hence all participants were sufficiently trained for the task in our experiment.

63.6 percent of the participants were second year students, 21.8 percent third year, 10.9 percent first year, and 3.6 percent fourth year students. As such, most of the students in addition to the vendor selection training they received in their management information systems class, had also previously undergone training as part of their other classes in decision making methods in business (e.g. investment decisions in finance, reporting decisions in accounting, or advertising decisions in marketing). The time of the experiment was in the second semester of the year. Thus, even the small portion of first year students had received some general business-related decision making training in their first semester. Participants had an average work experience of 1.7 years. As such, the student sample in our study was determined to be appropriate for the task used and is comparable to other experimental studies (Marsden et al. 2002, Park et al. 2008).

Cognitive load was manipulated as task demand in two ways: 1) requiring users to make decisions as fast as they could, and 2) requiring users to provide justification for their decisions. Both features increase task demand (Hendy et al. 1997, Lerner and Tetlock 1999, Paul and Nazareth 2010). Thus, the experiment was designed in a 2 (time sensitive, not time sensitive) X 2 (justification, no justification) fashion. Participants were assigned to one of the four treatments that provided a different task demand (T1: making the purchase recommendation decision was neither time sensitive nor did it require justification, T2: making the decision was not time sensitive but required justification, T3: the decision was time sensitive but did not require justification, T4: the decision was both time sensitive and required justification) using a computerized randomization procedure.

We verified the success of the randomization procedure by testing for systematic differences in gender, age group (categorized using US census categories), year of study and work experience across conditions. Our results did not show systematic differences among the treatments. The results of a chi-square test indicated no significant differences in the distribution of gender, age group and year of study (gender chisquare = 6.238, p > .10; age group chi square = 1.058, p > .78; year of study chi-square = 10.739, p > .29) in our four different treatments. Similarly, the results of ANOVA did not indicate a significant difference in work experience (F = 1.212, p > .30) in the four different experimental conditions. Thus, these tests demonstrate that randomization likely provided sufficient indirect control. Randomization and the tests also provide sufficient assurance of the independence of unobserved variables (e.g. skill) from treatment variables (Friedman and Sunder 1994).

In addition, since eye tracking data collection is typically limited to one or only a few participants at a time in a session, this can pose a problem because participants can potentially share information about the experiments between sessions. To alleviate this concern, we asked participants to not discuss the experiment with others until a specific date when the experiment was completed. Participants were told that by doing their best, regardless of their performance, they would provide invaluable information for the study. Additionally, we examined the data to see if there was an overwhelming preference for a specific solution. If knowledge sharing was a systematic problem in our study, we were likely to see an overwhelming preference for a particular solution. Gaze information was also likely to show that participants in later sessions showed a different pattern of behavior than participants in earlier sessions (e.g., the participants in later sessions would make a decision without adequately reviewing the provided criteria). We did not observe such systematic differences in behavior.

## 3.2 Task

In order to mimic the ambivalence of many judgment and decision making tasks in practice, as in many prior research (e.g., Fehrenbacher and Tracy 2016, Atalay 2012, Kamis et al. 2008, Park et al. 2008), we used a task that did not involve an optimal solution. The task used in our study required participants to assume that they were IS business analysts responsible for selecting a new ERP system in their company. They had to make a decision for acquiring one of the two pre-screened software solution candidates (System A and System B) that differed along eight dimensions: initial purchase price, maintenance cost, duration of implementation, usability, underlying infrastructure, functionality, security, and speed of system. Pre-screened information about the systems was provided in forms of individual scores for each of the eight dimensions as well as a total score, which represented the sum of all eight scores. Each individual score was a numerical value on a 10-point scale with 1 denoting very poor and 10 denoting very good as it is usually done in a point scoring analysis (Simkin et al. 2012). Thus, the total point score for each system could range from 8 to 80.

Participants were informed that the sum of the two systems’ scores was equal and that they would need to weigh the different criteria in order to make a decision. This required participants to judge both quantitative information reflected in the ratings as expressed in numbers as well as qualitative information reflected in the categories such as security or functionality. Participants were instructed to make their decision without the use of any decision aids including paper and pencil. The complete instructions for the task used in this study are provided in Appendix A.

Participants in the time sensitive treatments (T3, T4) were told that the purchase decision was time critical and therefore they had to make a recommendation as fast as possible. Generally, time sensitivity and task demand are positively related, that is, time sensitivity makes decisions more demanding (Payne et al. 1993). Similar to prior research (Kadous and Sedor 2004, Libby et al. 2004), participants in the justification treatments (T2, T4) were told that they would need to state reasons for their recommendation. Justifying one’s action increases task demand and typically involves more effortful processing and increased cognitive load (Evans 2008, Lerner and Tetlock 1999, Tetlock 1999, Payne et al. 1993). Participants in the least demanding condition (T1) did not have any time sensitivity requirement, nor did they have to provide justification for their decisions. The case was used previously by Fehrenbacher and Smith (2014).

Table 1 shows that generally decision times in this experiment tended to be rather short between 34.98 seconds in T4 and 44.80 seconds in T1. Many judgment and decision making tasks in research and practice only take a short amount of time (e.g. Atalay et al. 2012, Horstmann et al. 2009). However, the

nature of results may substantially change if a task involves longer time duration and the processing of several tabs and screens. As such, while already being much more complex than many other pupillometry studies (e.g. Simpson and Hale 1969, Beatty 1982, Kuchinke et al. 2007, Bijleveld et al. 2009, Einhaeuser et al. 2010), for simplicity reasons, we employed only one rather simple decision screen (Figure 1).

As mentioned above, the task did not contain an optimal solution. While such tasks can represent important aspects of realistic decision making environments in a laboratory experiment, they do not allow researchers to see how participants’ judgments fare against optimal solutions. The direct comparison of participants’ decisions against a normative solution can help check for two important methodological questions: 1) were participants provided with adequate training for the task? and 2) were participants adequately engaged in the task? Decisions with reasonable quality typically can alleviate both of these concerns (Djamasbi and Loiacono 2008). Because the lack of adequate training and/or task engagement can affect the results of the experiment, when the task does not have an optimal solution, researchers must take extra care to ensure that participants receive adequate training for the decision task. Researchers must also use different methods to assess whether participants took the task seriously. Subsequently, we outline our assessment regarding the two methodological questions.

First, in Section 3.1 we explained how we ensured that our participants had sufficient training for the task. We described that the majority of our participants (89.9 percent) had at least one year of courses that covered business decision making methods. Even the 10.9 percent first year students in our study had at least one semester of such business courses. Moreover, our demographic data showed that those who participated in our study had on average 1.7 years of work experience, which often is regarded as favorable when considering the suitability of student populations for business related decision making tasks in experimental studies (Marsden et al. 2002, Park et al. 2008). Additionally, we recruited our participants from the same information systems course and conducted the experiment toward the end of the semester to make sure that all the topics involved in the task were covered in this course in detail prior to the day the experiment was started. This allowed us to make sure that all participants in the study received the same basic training and practice necessary to complete the task meaningfully (e.g., lecture, notes, homework, class exercises, tests, etc.).

Second, we also looked at manipulation check results. If participants had not taken the task seriously, we were likely to detect similar patterns of behavior regardless of task conditions in the four different treatments. We looked at task completion times (for all treatments) and examined the provided responses for decision justifications (for treatments T3 and T4). Table 1 shows the task completion times by treatment conditions. In the time sensitive conditions (T3 and T4) the average time until a decision was submitted was 35.99 seconds (Std. Dev. = 14.25). In conditions without time sensitivity (T1 and T2) the average task completion time was 47.98 seconds (Std. Dev. = 26.45). A t-test indicated that these means were significantly different $( \mathbf { t } = 2 . 9 7 6 , \mathbf { p } < . 0 1 )$ , implying that participants in the time sensitive conditions completed the tasks at a significantly faster speed compared to those participants that did not receive instruction about the time sensitivity of decisions.

Next, we examined the written responses given to justify decisions in the treatments that required justification (T2, 28.9 words and T4, 23.4 words, see Table 1). On average participants wrote 26.1 (Std. Dev. = 26.26) words to justify their decisions. The justifications always included references to the criteria along which the systems were ranked.

We also inspected participants’ gaze videos during the time they were making a judgment to assess whether our participants took the time to review the provided information. These videos showed that participants fixated on provided pieces of information on the decision screen before making a judgment. Fixations are reliable indication of attention and thus they are often used to examine whether provided information is processed by participants (Djamasbi 2014, Rotaru et al. forthcoming). The inspection of individual gaze videos provided evidence that participants were adequately engaged in processing the decision task.

## 3.3 Pupil Data

In order to collect participants’ pupil data for the study, we used a commercially available remote eye tracker, Tobii T120. This eye tracking device which has been used in many prior IS studies, collects the information about a user’s eye movements unobtrusively at the rate of 120 samples per second (e.g., Fehrenbacher and Smith 2014, Djamasbi and Wilson 2013). Before starting the task, eye trackers were calibrated for each recruited participant. Due to the anatomy of their eyes, often a few participants are unable to complete this process successfully. In our study, six people could not successfully complete the calibration process. Hence, their data was not included in the study (i.e., the actual number of participants recruited in this study is 116). Compared to other eye tracking studies conducted by the authors, this was a typical rate for the calibration process.

The experiment was conducted in four different cubicles located in the Behavioral Laboratory of one author’s university. Thus, data was collected for four participants at a time. The light conditions remained constant in the cubicles. This is particularly important in pupillometry studies because of the influence of illumination on pupil size (Pomplun and Sunkara 2003, Bailey and Iqbal 2008). The experimental material in all four conditions used the same font size and font color. The material was shown on the same type and size of computer screen (17 inches), with the same resolution (1280 x 1024 resolution). The same eye trackers, Tobii T120, were used to collect eye movement data. All participants took part in the study during regular business hours. They were asked to leave their belongings outside of the cubicles in the main room of the Behavioral Laboratory in order to avoid other disturbances such as mobile phone messages. Similar to prior pupillometry studies (e.g., Hess 1965, Simpson and Hale 1969, Bailey and Iqbal 2008), random assignment of participants to treatments was used, as described earlier in the paper, to mitigate potential biases resulting from individual differences (e.g., mood, visual acuity) that may affect an individual’s pupillary response.

## 4. Results

The pupil data reported in this section is the average of pupil information for right and left eye. For each participant, pupil data was analyzed for gazes that fell within the decision task area, i.e. the 794 x 828 pixel rectangular area covering task related information on the decision screen (Figure 1). To examine the impact of task demand on pupillary responses, we compared the mean of PD and PDV values in various treatments. We also examined changes in dilation and constriction of pupil sizes over time (Simpson and Hale 1969, Beatty 1982, Einhaeuser et al. 2010).

Due to the exploratory nature of our investigation, we did not only look at the overall differences in pupillary responses among the four different treatments but also examined pairwise differences between conditions even when the overall results were not significant. Such comprehensive exploratory analysis will help to gain more insight about the impact of various treatments on pupillary responses and thus help refine the design of future investigations in this area.

## 4.1 Pupil Dilation

We looked at the mean of pupil dilation in each of the four treatments. As shown in Table 2, the average pupil size was largest (2.93 mm) in the time sensitive condition (T3) and smallest in the two treatments that required justification (T2 and T4, 2.73 mm and 2.72 mm respectively). The increased average pupil size in the time sensitive treatment (2.93 mm), as compared to average pupil size (2.79 mm) in T1, the least demanding decision environment, is consistent with the results of the studies discussed in the background section of this paper, which suggest a positive relationship between cognitive load and pupil dilation. However, as displayed in Table 2, the average pupil size of participants in the justification condition (independent from whether they were instructed that their decisions were time sensitive or not) was slightly smaller than the average pupil size of participants in the condition that had the least demanding environment (2.73 mm and 2.72 mm vs. 2.79 mm). The decrease in average pupil size in the more demanding conditions is not consistent with the interpretation that increased cognitive load will result in increased pupil dilation. However, using the adaptive decision making theory, one can argue that the more demanding conditions required participants to switch to a strategy that could better conserve the limited cognitive resources needed for completing the task at hand.

## 4.2 Time Analysis of Pupil Dilation

As in prior research, we analyzed the changes in pupil dilation over time (Simpson and Hale 1969, Beatty 1982, Einhaeuser et al. 2010). Naturally, the number of data points in such analyses is determined by the task time. Hence, to facilitate the comparison of trend analysis in various conditions, studies often equalize the number of data points by designing the task in a way to have pre-specified time windows (e.g., Simpson and Hale 1969, Beatty 1982, Kuchinke et al. 2007, Bijleveld et al. 2009, Einhaeuser et al. 2010). This approach, however, can affect the ecological validity of decision tasks, such as the one in our study, that naturally do not have a pre-defined time. In these situations, different participants will have different number of data points. To overcome this problem we applied a method often used in economics and finance literature to normalize time series with a different number of data points (see Juhn et al. 1993, Andersen et al. 2003).<sup>1</sup> We standardized task times across participants by partitioning each participant’s dilation recording time into percentiles. In other words, we standardize each participant’s recording time so that it was composed of 100 equal time intervals. Then we calculated the average pupil data for each of the 100 intervals. This process allowed us to have an equal number of pupil data points for each participant regardless of participants’ task completion times, which in turn allowed us to compare the trend of pupil data over time, as it has been done in previous studies. This method is explained more thoroughly in Appendix B.

As in prior research (Einhauser et al. 2010), in order to allow for better comparison across conditions and participants, pupil dilation information for each treatment was normalized to zero mean. Figure 2 displays time analyses of pupil dilation data in each of the four different treatments .

## 5. Discussion

This study examined whether cognitive demand, manipulated as time sensitivity and decision justification, placed upon a software selection decision task results in differences in pupil dilation, dilation trend, and pupil dilation variability. We found some evidence that time pressure increased pupil dilation when completing a decision task. This is consistent with the notion that the cognitive load on human information processing increases with time pressure (Hendy et al. 1997, Kuo et al. 2009). As such Hendy et al. (1997) found that time pressure determines subjective estimates of workload. Importantly, we find this evidence in instruction-based time pressure environments and not in environments where the ratio of time necessary to make a decision and the time allowable to make a decision is varied.

We further found that requiring decision makers to justify their decision decreased their eye dilation. Certainly, such a requirement does not decrease mental processing because it requires decision makers to reason why they choose one alternative over another in order to justify their decision. One way to explain this phenomenon is by arguing that the justification requirement cued our users to change their decision strategies. Decision makers are adaptive and choose alternative decision making strategies depending on context (Payne et al. 1993). Such a shift in decision making strategy is necessary to reduce cognitive load in order to conserve the valuable yet limited cognitive resources needed during the decision making process. As such, the momentary eye dilation may not necessarily increase with a justification requirement.

In an effort to explore different cognitive load stages for our software decision task, we examined pupil dilation trend for a standardized time series. We observed that at the beginning of the decision period and at the end of the decision period (outer part of time series) pupil dilation tended to be larger than in the middle (inner part of time series) of the decision period. This trend indicated that cognitive load was higher at the beginning when new information was processed for the first time and at the end when individuals finalized their decision as compared to the middle part of the decision where individuals had already gained some familiarity with the task and still took time to finalize their judgments. Interestingly enough, the pupil dilation values at the beginning and at the end of the decision period were significantly higher than the pupil dilation values in the middle of decisions in all but the least demanding treatment. This suggests that increased task demand may affect the distribution of cognitive effort during a decision making task. In particular, increased task demand may affect the experience of cognitive load at the beginning and the end of decision periods more profoundly. Looking at this data through the lens of adaptive decision making theory, our trend analysis suggests that people used different strategies during the decision period. The data suggests that people used more effortful strategies at the beginning and the end of a decision period.

Finally, we turned our attention to pupil dilation variation, a relatively unexplored measure in pupillometry research. We found evidence that task demand decreased average pupil dilation variation. In particular, the most demanding task had the lowest PDV. Our results are consistent with prior research indicating that rhythmic variations of the pupil that occur at rest do seem to disappear during the performance of cognitively demanding tasks (Kahneman 1974). However, our results are not consistent with a recent study by Buettner et al. (2015) that shows a positive relationship between task complexity and PDV. One explanation is that the direction of the relationship (positive or negative) between PDV and task demand depends on the task. The task used in our study was different from the one used in Buettner et al.’s study. Our task demands were instruction-based (time, justification) and did not differ in the number of different inputs that had to be processed in order to reach a solution, as it was the case in Buettner et al.’s study (2015).

This explanation is consistent with the adaptive decision making theory because it emphasizes the impact of task demand on selecting a decision strategy. Looking at Buettner et al.’s (2015) results using the adaptive decision making theory, one can argue that increased PDVs in response to task difficulty may have partly stemmed from participants’ flexibility in adapting to the demands of the decision environment. In order to conserve their cognitive resources needed to complete the task, participants in Buettner et al.’s study (2015) may have switched back and forth from more effortful to more efficient decision strategies that could allow them to process the various input sources in their tasks thereby optimizing the effort-accuracy trade-off. The negative association between PDV and task demand in our setting may also imply that our participants used their strategies more consistently. The results of our study and Buettner’s study together underline the need for and importance of developing a theoretical framework that can explain pupillary responses with regard to decision strategies. Understanding the link between pupillary responses and the selection of a decision strategy can help design decision support systems that improve decision quality. Because people select a strategy that can conserve cognitive load while providing reasonably good decisions, designing tools that can reduce cognitive load is likely to encourage the use of more elaborate decision strategies and consequently help people to deliver more accurate decisions.

Additionally, from a broader design point of view, understanding user experience of cognitive load through the non-conscious measure of pupil data can provide invaluable insight for designing successful systems. Cognitive effort can have a negative impact on how we use computerized tools (Todd and Benbasat 1994, Gregor and Benbasat 1999, Djamasbi 2007). As such Gregor and Benbasat (1999, p. 497) contend that information from intelligent systems will not be used “if the user has to exert ‘too much’ effort”. Our results show that the analysis of PD, PDV, and time series can provide a more complete picture of decision behavior. In particular, the results indicate that PDV may serve as a particularly useful measure of cognitive load in IS pupillometry studies. Our results showed a consistent inverse relationship between PDV and increased task demand but we did not observe such a consistent pattern of relationship between PD and increased task demand. Our findings are consistent with a recent study that suggests PDV may be more sensitive than PD in detecting cognitive load during complex problem solving tasks (Shojaeizadeh et al. 2015).

By providing evidence that adaptive decision making theory may serve as a suitable theoretical framework for explaining the relationship between cognitive load and pupil data, our study does not only address the call for incorporating non-subjective measures in IS research (Marsden et al. 2006), but also provides a theoretical direction for future IS pupillometry studies. The adaptive decision making theory suggests a variety of task contingencies that influence how individuals respond to task complexities and predicts that people adopt decision strategies that provide the best effort-accuracy tradeoff for the situation at hand (Payne et al. 1993). Using this framework in IS pupillometry studies can help to examine users’ reactions to task conditions at physiological levels and thus provide a more nuanced support to meet user needs. For example, our results showing higher pupil dilation at the beginning and at the end of a decision task calls for design elements that can help reduce cognitive effort during these specific decision periods. For example, better information presentation (e.g., showing explanations when hovering over a criteria and ability to mark choices, to rank them, or make notes about them) is likely to help reduce cognitive effort at the initial stage or the end stage of decision making in tasks similar to the one used in our study.

Using the effort-accuracy framework of adaptive decision making theory in pupillometry IS studies also has an implication for the design of advanced adaptive decision aids (Barkhi et al. 2005). Advances in computer technologies make it more and more possible to envision seamless collaborations between human and intelligent information systems in everyday situations (Shah et al. 2011). ## 6. Limitations and Future Research

As with any laboratory experiment, our results are limited by the task used in the study. First and foremost, we need to note that despite being more complex than other tasks examining pupil data (Simpson and Hale 1969, Beatty 1982, Kuchinke et al. 2007, Bijleveld et al. 2009, Einhaeuser et al. 2010), the task used in our study was relatively short and it was displayed on a single decision screen. Hence, response times in our study were brief. It is important for future research to explore the impact of task condition on pupillometry in longer decision tasks involving more complex task material presented on multiple screens or dashboards. The introduction of more dynamic elements and interfaces (e.g. Watts et al. 2009) and the involvement of several decision makers (e.g. Paul and Nazareth 2010) are further ways of extension. It is also possible that task type can influence the experience of cognitive load manipulated via task conditions. For example, in this study we used a task that did not have a single optimal solution. Future research using tasks that facilitate direct measurement of decision quality can help to test such a possibility. In this study we focused on the links of cognitive demand, pupillary responses and choices. Future research using tasks that facilitate direct measurement of decision quality can help to verify and/or extend our results to include decision quality. The use of cognitive load theory (Schroder et al. 1967) may inform such future studies.

Our results suggest that adaptive decision making theory may be a useful theoretical lens for examining pupil data in decision support systems. However, more research is needed to investigate the intricate link between pupillary responses, task demand, and strategy selection. For example, our results showed a negative relationship between PDV and task demand while Buettner et al. (2015) found a positive relationship between task complexity and PDV. We argued that this inconsistency may be due to task type which in turn affects the choice of decision making strategy. Future studies are needed to explore these possibilities and test the generalizability and thus the robustness of adaptive decision making theory in IS pupillometry studies. Another way forward could be to ask individuals about their use of decision making strategies in a post-experimental questionnaire. This can add to the question on whether objective measures and individual perceptions are complements. However, individuals may not always respond knowingly or in an unbiased manner (Furnham 1986).

Another bias may have been introduced because participants did not all take the experiment at the same time. As stated above, four participants at a time completed the experiment. Thus, participants had the possibility of talking to each other between sessions. In order to mitigate this concern, we asked participants to not disclose information to other participants about the study until the experiment was concluded. Participants were told that by doing their best to complete the task, regardless of their performance, they would provide invaluable information for the study. Probably as a consequence, we did not observe such information exchange. However, even if participants had talked to each other, in part because there were no optimal solution, there was little to be gained by exchanging information about the experiment. And as we report above, the manipulation checks indicate that the experimental treatments worked. Further, we did not observe systematic differences in the data regarding preferred solution and gaze pattern between participants taking the experiment earlier and later.

Finally, we did not use a performance based reward mechanism in our study. However, examining the link between pupillary responses and performance incentives, particularly when linked to decision quality, are both relevant and interesting. Because performance incentives can urge people to more intensely focus on the task, they may also have an impact on cognitive load. Future studies are needed to explore these links.

## 7. Conclusion

The idea of the human being as a capacity-limited processor is widely accepted in the economics, psychology and information systems literature (Simon 1982, Hendy et al. 1997, Payne et al. 1993). The competitive landscape of today’s business environment continually challenges us to improve the quality and timeliness of decisions by developing effective decision support systems that can help people make faster and smarter decisions. Because cognitive effort influences our choice of decision strategy (Payne et al. 1993) and because it can negatively impact how we use computerized tools, a better understanding of cognitive load can help us design computerized decision support systems that are used more successfully (Gregor and Benbasat 1999, Djamasbi 2007).

Despite evidence that pupil data can serve as a reliable measure of cognitive effort, little work has been done to incorporate pupil data in IS research. Our study begins to fill this gap by an exploratory examination of the impact of four different types of task demands on pupil dilation and its trend during a decision period. We also examined the impact of these task demands on pupil dilation variation, a less explored metric, during decision making. Our results provide evidence that task demand can be observed via pupil dilation, pupil dilation trends, and pupil variation. These results have important implications for future IS pupillometry studies. First, the results indicate that the adaptive decision making theory may serve as a suitable theoretical lens for such studies. Second, the results show that trend analysis of pupil dilation can provide useful information about the distribution of cognitive effort during various stages of decision making. Third, the results suggest that pupillometry is likely to serve as a useful and sensitive way of detecting flexibility in decision making behavior. Thus, our study provides evidence that detecting pupil responses to task demand can be a productive area of investigation for IS researchers from theoretical and practical perspectives. As such it has the potential to improve the design of computerized decision aids.

## Highlights

 Task demand in computerized decision making can be observed via pupil dilation, pupil dilation trends, and pupil variation

 Trend analysis of pupil dilation provides useful information about distribution of cognitive effort during various stages of decision making

 Adaptive decision making theory may serve as a suitable theoretical lens for such pupillometry studies

 Pupillometry has the potential to serve as a reliable, objective, continuous, and unobtrusive measure of task demand to improve adaptive decision support systems
