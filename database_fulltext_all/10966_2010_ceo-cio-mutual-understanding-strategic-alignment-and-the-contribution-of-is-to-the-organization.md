# CEO/CIO mutual understanding, strategic alignment, and the contribution of IS to the organization

Alice M. Johnson <sup>a,</sup>\*, Albert L. Lederer <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Business and Economics, North Carolina A&T State University, Greensboro, NC 27411-0002, USA <sup>b</sup> Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, USA

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 September 2007 Received in revised form 1 September 2009 Accepted 1 December 2009 Available online 18 January 2010

Keywords: Mutual understanding of the role of IT Strategic alignment IT strategy IS contribution

## A B S T R A C T

Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO is thought to facilitate the alignment of an organization’s IS with its business strategy, and thereby enhance the contribution of the IS to business performance. A survey of 202 pairs of CEOs and CIOs was taken to investigate the relationships between them. Mutual understanding was measured as the role of IS in the organization, using the perspectives of both executives. Strategic alignment was measured as the fit between the CEO’s assessment of eight STROBE dimensions and the CIO’s assessment of the analogous STROEPIS dimensions; both the CEO and CIO evaluated IS contribution. Mutual understanding of the role of IT led to seven alignment dimensions whereas six alignment dimensions led to IS contributions. Our study extended the theory of IT strategic alignment and provided direction for CEOs and CIOs interested in improving the IS contribution of their organization.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations invest a large percentage of their resources in IT. However executives have frequently stated that they have not realized value from this investment [7]. Observers have suggested that organizations benefit from their IT only when alignment exists between the firm’s business and IT strategies; IS resources must target areas critical to the success of the organization [6].

Alignment is a complex, multi-dimensional construct, and organizations have historically experienced difficulty in achieving it. They have therefore formed committees of business and IS executives and communicated their strategic direction through them. Perceptual differences between executives have, however, been found to influence the strategic planning process, and thus many studies have suggested that mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO is a prerequisite for IT strategic alignment.

We therefore posed two questions:

\- Does mutual understanding of the role of IT between the CEO and CIO lead to IT strategic alignment?

\- Does IT strategic alignment lead to better IS contribution to the organization?

We treated alignment as the fit between the sets of dimensions of STROBE (strategic orientation of business enterprises) [13] and

STROEPIS (strategic orientation of the existing portfolio of IS applications) [4]. See Appendix 2 for a brief description of these. Failure to find an impact of CEO/CIO mutual understanding about the role of IT in the dimensions, or failure to find an impact of the dimensions on the IS contribution to organizational performance would suggests that previous assumptions about the need for alignment were flawed.

We employed a postal survey of 202 pairs of CEOs and CIOs from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and other directories in two adjacent states of the U.S. We used an instrument, constructed and tested in previous IS research, with individual sets of survey items for the role of IT, business strategy, IS strategy, and IS contribution to the organization. Our study combined the pairs of CEO and CIO perceptions of the role of IT to assess mutual understanding, and the pairs of CEO responses about business strategy with the CIO responses about IS strategy to assess IT strategic alignment. It also used the CEO’s view of the organizational contribution of IS. Multivariate analysis was used to test the hypotheses.

## 2. Mutual understanding, IT strategic alignment, and IS contribution

## 2.1. Mutual understanding

Mutual understanding refers to a degree of agreement between individuals on a topic [5]. It is valuable to organizations because of its effect on the firm’s success. In the IS context, it favorably influences the development of a shared vision of the role of IS and thus the contribution of IT to the organization, making strategic planning less onerous and leading to alignment between an organization’s CSFs and IT capability [10]. It has not, however, been shown to lead to IT strategic alignment.

Raghunathan et al. [9] suggested an instrument for assessing the role of IT by both the CIO and CEO. They stated that responses by both executives to questions about the role could help to identify gaps in mutual understanding about the importance of IT to the business, and thereby facilitate consensus building and alignment of IT use. Because our research sought to assess similar mutual understanding, we used that instrument.

## 2.2. IT strategic alignment

This can be viewed as the extent to which IS planning supports business planning [1].

We used the business strategy dimensions of the STROBE model to determine the fit by defining the analogous STROEPIS dimensions. The authors considered alignment as realized rather than planned, and defined it as the fit between each of the eight business and IT strategy dimensions.

\- Aggressiveness is the extent to which an organization attempts to improve its market position and outperform competitors. A firm which competes to increase market share is following an aggressiveness strategy.

\- Analysis is the extent to which an organization requires factual, comprehensive information for decision-making. A firm which attempts to identify the cause of problems and generates alternative solutions is following an analytic strategy.

\- Internal defensiveness is the extent to which an organization engages in activities to improve its efficiency of business operations in an effort to preserve its prospective domain. A firm that competes by reducing cost would be practicing internal defensiveness.

\- External defensiveness is the market or environmental activities that help a firm preserve its domain. Competing by establishing and maintaining strong relationships with customers is externally defensive.

\- Futurity is the extent to which an organization’s decisions or activities reflect long-term considerations. Competing through extensive forecasting and tracking environmental trends exemplifies futurity.

\- Proactiveness is the extent an organization searches for new market opportunities and business ventures. A firm that responds to a changing environment its competitors practices proactiveness.

\- Riskiness is an organization’s willingness to engage in business practices with an uncertain outcome, but potentially high return. A firm that competes by investing in a new subsidiary with a low probability of high returns may be practicing riskiness.

\- Innovativeness is the extent a firm applies creative and imaginative solutions to business problems. A firm that adopts new products and services before competitors is practicing innovativeness.

We used STROBE and STROEPIS to measure IT strategic alignment between the factors for each dimension.

## 2.3. IS contribution to the organization

Premkumar and King [8] viewed IS contribution in terms of the impact of overall systems and developed an instrument to assess it using five items; we used this instrument, which measures IS Contribution to:

1. Customer satisfaction as a top priority for business and IT executives. A one-point increase in an organization’s customer satisfaction index resulted in a very significant increase in the firm’s market value; as a result, many organizations have increased their investment in CRM.

2. Sales revenue is often the primary motivation for investing in IT. It was linked to at least \$30 million in revenue increases at Phillips 66 in the 1980s. FedEx’s recent annual revenue of more than \$19 billion has been attributed to its effective use of IS.

3. Market share can determine long-term profitability. Firms that possessed a larger share of the market are likely to be more profitable. Numerous examples of the contribution of IT to increased market share have been reported.

4. Return on investment is used to help a firm evaluate its strategy. With such huge expenditures, ability to realize ROI for IT investments and validate the strategy is critical.

5. Operating efficiency is a top priority for IS and business executives; this has resulted from the deployment of EDI, CRM, ERP, data warehousing, and many other technologies.

## 3. Hypotheses

3.1. The relationship between mutual understanding of the role of IT and alignment

Mutual understanding has long been recognized as a key antecedent to behavior; it may be expected to lead to many positive outcomes [3], but it is necessary to explain why such change is expected. Mutual understanding through team cohesiveness leads to better decision-making, and therefore managers with stronger agreement about IT management issues would have stronger agreement in developing a high-quality set of business and IS plans.

Uncertainty reduction theory predicts that, under less mutual understanding, communicators have greater uncertainty about organizational issues, actions, and attitudes. Although uncertainty increases information seeking to reduce the uncertainty, reduce stress, strengthen cohesiveness, and improve effectiveness in accomplishing goals, the presence of prior mutual understanding implies less uncertainty, less information seeking, less stress, greater cohesiveness, and more effectiveness. Moreover, information seeking can be time consuming, costly, and inefficient.

Uncertainty reduction theory justifies the expected effect of CEO/CIO mutual understanding of the role of IT on IT strategic alignment. When they share an understanding of the role of IT on the organization, the CIO would have less need for information seeking, and they would work well together. In contrast, the information seeking motivated by less prior mutual understanding would be time consuming, costly, and inefficient, delaying implementation and impeding alignment.

The development of common views among top management about IS utilization may be essential for the successful creation of IS strategy. Tan and Gallupe [12] reasoned that mutual understanding leads to better decision-making, and therefore managers with stronger agreement about IT management issues would develop a high-quality set of interrelated business and IS plans.

Hence, we proposed:

H1. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to aggressiveness alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing analysis, the organization makes greater use of comprehensive, factual information for decision-making in order to outperform its competitors. When the CIO and CEO share an understanding of the role of IT, the CIO would have less need to seek critical, related information, and the two would have less stress and greater cohesiveness in working together. Hence:

H2. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to analysis alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing internal defensiveness, the organization tries harder to improve business operations efficiency. With mutual understanding, the CIO would have less need to seek critical, related information and the two would have less stress, greater cohesiveness and thus have more internal defensiveness alignment. Hence:

H3. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to internal defensiveness alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing external defensiveness, the organization makes greater efforts to build strong relations with customers and suppliers. With mutual understanding, the CIO would have less need to seek information and thus have more external defensiveness alignment. Hence:

H4. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to external defensiveness alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing futurity, the organization makes a greater effort to conduct activities that reflect long-term considerations. With mutual understanding, the CIO would have less need to seek information. Hence:

H5. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to futurity alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing proactiveness, the organization makes greater effort to find new market opportunities. With mutual understanding, the CIO would have less need to seek information. Hence:

H6. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to proactiveness alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing riskiness, the organization participates more in business practices with an uncertain outcome but a high return. Hence:

H7. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to riskiness alignment.

In a business strategy emphasizing innovativeness, the organization makes greater effort to apply creative and original solutions to business problems. Hence:

H8. Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO about the role of IT is positively related to innovativeness alignment.

## 3.2. The relationship between alignment and IS contribution

IT strategic alignment facilitates the IS contribution to the organization in terms of the deployment of relevant IT, which then has a major impact on the competitiveness of an organization by lowering costs, providing product differentiation, using electronic links, creating market entry barriers, leveraging unique capabilities, enabling existing business strategies, and creating new business strategies. Conversely, without alignment, completed projects may be unrelated to business objectives and strategy [11]. Thus, by ensuring that IS resources are not dedicated to low-impact projects, alignment would positively influence IS contribution to organizational performance, such as improved customer satisfaction, market share, sales revenue, return on investment, and operating efficiency, used as multi-item IS contribution measures in our study.

IT strategic alignment has been shown to impact organizational performance, as well as to improve the impact of IT investment on business performance [2]. We conceptualized alignment as having eight dimensions. Therefore, eight hypotheses were proposed.

H9. Aggressiveness alignment is positively related to IS contribu tion to organizational performance.

H10. Analysis alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

H11. Internal defensiveness alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

H12. External defensiveness alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

H13. Futurity alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

H14. Proactiveness alignment is positively related to IS contribu tion to organizational performance.

H15. Riskiness alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

H16. Innovativeness alignment is positively related to IS contribution to organizational performance.

## 4. Methodology

Our methodology involved a relatively large-scale postal survey of paired CEOs and CIOs with the data analysed using multivariate statistical analysis. The approach was chosen because the study of alignment is well enough advanced that a qualitative approach was deemed unnecessary, because others had already done this, and because analytic modeling and simulation techniques were deemed infeasible.

## 4.1. Questionnaire development

The authors developed two postal questionnaires: one for the CEO of the organization and one for the CIO or top IT executive. Both questionnaires contained the same eight items about the role of IT in the organization in order to measure CEO/CIO mutual understanding. The absolute values of the differences between the CEO and CIO response for each item served as indicators in a construct representing CEO/CIO mutual understanding. Smaller differences therefore indicated better mutual understanding.

The CEO questionnaire contained the twenty-seven items about the organization’s business strategy extracted from the STROBE instrument. The CIO questionnaire contained thirty items about the organization’s IT strategy extracted form from the STROEPIS instrument. All of the items used a Likert scale of 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) with the extent to which they represented the portfolio of IS in the organization. Because an interaction perspective performed well where two variables combined to impact a third, strategic IT alignment was calculated for each dimension as the product of the mean of the items for its business strategy dimension and the mean of the items for the corresponding IT strategy dimension.

Both instruments contained the same five items on a scale of 1 (no extent) to 5 (great extent) concerning the extent to which IS contributed to RoI, sales revenue, market share, operating efficiency, and customer satisfaction. These measured contribution of IS to organizational performance. Because the CEO may be seen as somewhat more knowledgeable and impartial about the IS contribution, the CEO data were used to measure the dependent variable, whereas the CIO data were used merely for validation and comparative purposes. Although our study used items identical to those in the original instruments, thus taking advantage of their previous validation, our study still had extensive validation. First, the content validity of each questionnaire was examined prior to mailing of the survey. Five professors of IS in a university business school with undergraduate, MBA, and PhD programs having IS concentrations initially reviewed each instrument. Next, five pairs of executives (one CEO and one CIO) pilot-tested the surveys. The senior author met with the subjects individually while they completed the survey after which they provided feedback which was used iteratively to improve the wording of the survey.

## 4.2. Data collection

The participating organizations were headquartered in two adjacent states of the U.S. The Chamber of Commerce in the largest city in the sampling area endorsed the research; it actively solicited and encouraged organizational executives and Chamber members to participate, and it provided its membership list.

In addition, two other resources were used to identify potential participants: a database of major employers in the sampling area and the American Business Index. Organizations headquartered in the sampling area were selected as potential subjects if they had at least fifty employees or if the Chamber data indicated the presence of an IT function in the firm. These criteria resulted in a potential base of 1011 organizations.

CEOs of these organizations were then invited to participate as subjects in our research via an initial letter on Chamber of Commerce stationary signed by a Chamber official. The package included a prepaid return postcard to be completed by the CEO. Those willing to participate were required to write the name of their top IT manager on the postcard. Surveys were then mailed separately to each CEO and top IT manager with cover letters emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation and strict confidentiality of the responses.

Although our study relied on the CEO to identify the top IT manager, surveys were mailed directly and thus independently to each informant, therefore requiring no personal contact between them, thus reducing the likelihood of ‘‘socially acceptable’’ responses. 521 CEOs (52%) returned the postcard and 228 (23%) agreed to participate. Subjects then returned 204 matched surveys for a response rate of 20%. Two of the matched surveys were unusable due to incomplete responses, so 202 pairs were used in our analysis.

In addition to their high level of experience and education, the CEOs and CIOs generally agreed about the contribution of IS to the organization. A multivariate analysis of variance of the IS contribution variables showed no significant differences between their responses (Wilks’ lambda = 0.31, p = 0.28).

Table 4 shows the descriptive statistics for the variables and constructs. Interestingly, the pairs of executives exhibited the greatest mutual understanding (i.e., lowest score) for ‘‘IS breakdown for extended periods will affect organizational activities severely’’ (RIT2), not a surprise given the contemporary organizational dependence on IS. They had the least mutual understanding for ‘‘IS breakdown will affect our database access’’ (RIT5), not a surprise given the CIO’s more technical concerns. Both executives rated internal defensiveness as the strategy most practiced and proactiveness as the least. Apparently, organizations use IT substantially to reduce costs, but much less to search for new market opportunities and business ventures.

A time-trend extrapolation was used to assess non-response bias; in it, non-respondents are seen as more similar to late respondents than early ones. Therefore, using the first 25% of the returned surveys as early respondents and the last 25% as surrogates for non-respondents, a multivariate ANOVA of the role of IT variables and IS contribution variables tested whether differences in response time were associated with different responses. The results indicated no significant differences for the CEOs (Wilks’ lambda = 0.89, p = 0.37) or CIOs (Wilks’ lambda = 0.82, p = 0.29). Further assessment of non-response bias showed no difference in the revenue of a randomly selected set of respondents (based on their answers to demographic questions) and non-respondents (based on information in a publicly available database) using a t-test (p = 0.53).

## 5. Analysis

## 5.1. Reliability and validity

One confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to validate the role of IT, business strategy, and IS contribution constructs that contained the data collected from the CEO, and a second was used to validate data from the CIO. The maximum likelihood method of parameter estimation and robust statistics were employed: in this a model is deemed a good fit if the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } )$ is less than 3, the non-normed fit index (NNFI) and comparative fit index (CFI) are at least 0.90, the LISREL adjusted AGFI is greater than 0.80, the standardized RMS residual (RMR) is less than 0.10, and the RME error of approximation (RMSEA) is less than 0.08.

Fit indices were observed for each execution. The indices indicated that re-specification was needed for only one change during a single execution.

Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability scores were used to assess internal reliability. Convergent validity was assessed by examining factor loadings, and a variance extracted test was used to assess discriminant validity.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

## 5.2.1. Final model specification

PLS Graph version 3.0 was used for hypothesis testing. The CEO/ CIO mutual understanding of the role of IT construct was an exogenous variable. Strategic alignment and IS contribution were endogenous variables. The mutual understanding and IT strategic alignment constructs were both operationalized as formative, thus each emerged as a result of its indicators. The individual indicators were weighted according to their relative importance in forming the construct. After an initial run to test hypotheses, nine organizations where the mean of the CEO business strategy items fell at least two standard deviations below the mean of the entire sample were dropped—to remove those without a proper business strategy. Table 9 shows the weights for the mutual understanding construct.

This shows that the CEO/CIO mutual understanding construct was primarily formed by the extent to which the company relied on IS for efficient operation and the extent to which extended IS breakdowns affected organizational activities with weights of 0.92 and 0.86, respectively. (Weights were not shown for the IT strategic alignment constructs because each was a single calculated variable.)

IS contribution was operationalized as a latent variable with reflective indicators because each was thought to measure the same underlying phenomenon (IS contribution). The loading for each indicator reflected the extent to which it explained the latent variable. Table 10 shows the loadings for the IS contribution construct.

## 6. Discussion of findings

## 6.1. Discussion of mutual understanding effect on alignment

Our study supported hypotheses that mutual understanding between an organization’s CEO and CIO about the role of IT lead to IT strategic alignment in six of the eight dimensions of strategy: aggressiveness (H1), analysis (H2), internal defensiveness (H3), external defensiveness (H4), futurity (H5), proactiveness (H6), and innovativeness (H8).

Mutual understanding between the CEO and CIO did not lead to IT strategic alignment for the dimension of riskiness (H7). CIO and CEO phone interviews were conducted by the authors to find out why this hypothesis was not confirmed. The responses suggested that a riskiness strategy differed from the others because the CEO may feel less comfortable informing other executives that such a strategy is being followed. The pair of executives might have mutual understanding of the role of IT in the organization, but the CIO may be unaware of the extent of the riskiness of the business strategy; the CEO of one firm stated that her organization’s riskiness strategy ‘‘might not be shared throughout the organization.’’ A bank CIO similarly asserted that he had not been ‘‘aware of the role we in IT played in supporting a risky business strategy until the news hit the press.’’

## 6.2. Discussion of alignment effect on IS contribution

Our study supported the assumption that alignment would be positively related to IS contribution for analysis (H10), external defensiveness (H12), futurity (H13), proactiveness (H14), riskiness (H15), and innovativeness (H16), but did not support (H9) and (H11). According to one CIO, a longer time delay may be needed to realize the IS contribution of a strategy of aggressiveness, while another expressed the same idea by stating that aggressiveness might cause the organization to implement IT applications that it could not support and internal defensiveness alignment did not lead to IS contribution. One CEO, attributed the IS contribution of a new IT to users who learned how to apply the IT for cost reduction by saying, ‘‘I attribute the company’s Internet success to the users and am interested first in the area where the minimization occurred, and second in the method used to cause the minimization.’’ A CIO added that the financial outlay for new IT may overshadow its savings.

The analysis dimension had the strongest effect on IS contribution to the organization. Ironically, mutual understanding thus facilitated alignment on three dimensions (aggressiveness, internal defensiveness, and futurity) that did not have the greatest impact on IS contribution to the organization.

## 7. Implications

CEO/CIO mutual understanding about the role of IT led to greater IT strategic alignment for seven of the eight dimensions.

The findings confirmed the importance of the efforts of CIOs to cultivate a mutual understanding with the CEO on the potential role of IS in the organization, as well as the importance of the CEO’s efforts to establish the role of IS/IT in the organization. While informal opportunities abound, the presence of both executives on business and IS strategy committees is important. On the other hand, mutual understanding did not lead to IT strategic alignment for the dimension of riskiness.

IT strategic alignment generally led to higher IS contribution to the organization, though mutual agreement on aggressiveness and internal defensiveness did not lead to IS contribution; through follow-up interviews, we found that IT was less helpful in supporting aggressiveness and may even motivate excessive expectations and efforts to apply IT beyond its capabilities; we also found that managers and users attributed the contribution of internal defensiveness alignment to the user department rather than to the IS. CIOs might thus devote more effort to educating CEOs and others about how the IS contributes to the efficiency by reductions in labor or materials that offset the costs of the IT itself.

## 8. Limitations

Our study had potential limitations. First, the sample consisted of CEOs and CIOs from organizations headquartered in two adjacent states of the USA. Bias may therefore exist due to regional attitudes and economic conditions, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the relatively rigorous instrument validation process resulted in some dropped items.

## 9. Conclusion

Our study used previously validated IS instruments for measuring the role of IT, business strategy, IT strategy, and IS contribution, and demonstrated the impact of CEO/CIO mutual understanding on various dimensions of IT strategic alignment. It demonstrated the richness of the dimensions and the complexity of the impact of mutual understanding by showing that such understanding led to some, but not all, dimensions of alignment.

We also investigated the impact of alignment on IS contribution. Some dimensions lead to IS contribution while others did not, substantiating the importance of understanding alignment in terms of its dimensions.

## Appendix 2. Brief description of STROBE and STROEPIS

The STROBE (strategic orientation of business enterprises) instrument conceptualized the theoretical underpinnings of the business strategy construct and operationalized it with multiple dimensions. Because the purpose of Venkatraman’s study was to establish the dominant dimensions of business strategy via analytical methods, those dimensions were specified a priori. The study identified eight dimensions of strategic orientation (aggressiveness, analysis, internal defensiveness, external defensiveness, futurity, proactiveness, riskiness, and innovativeness). Each dimension defined a characteristic or orientation with respect to an organization’s marketplace behavior.

He derived the items for each dimension from a literature review, and collected data to test the model in two phases. The first used a questionnaire mailed to presidents and CEOs of 250 strategic business units; this was used to refine the questionnaire. The second employed the refined questionnaire to collect data from 450 additional subjects. The response rate for the two combined phases exceeded 30%. He combined the data from the phases into a sample size of 202 subjects after ensuring the lack of significant differences between them.

Confirmatory factor analysis within the LISREL framework assessed the measurement properties of STROBE. The results generally supported the reliability, validity, and unidimensionality of the instrument.

He suggested that researchers should replicate and refine the instrument by using it as an anchor in other research, evaluate the degree of error attributable to hierarchical levels with responses to the instrument from multiple informants, and apply the dimensions to develop a business strategy taxonomy.

## STROEPIS

Chan et al. built on the STROBE instrument to create STROEPIS (strategic orientation of the existing portfolio of IS applications) to model IT strategy. Because it was developed specifically to parallel STROBE, it used the same eight dimensions. Each STROEPIS dimension assessed the extent to which an organizational IS facilitated the tasks included in the corresponding STROBE dimension. The authors then characterized strategic alignment as the fit between STROBE and STROEPIS.

This study proposed relationships between: (1) business strategic orientation and business performance, (2) IS strategic orientation and IS effectiveness, (3) IS effectiveness and business performance, (4) IS strategic alignment and IS effectiveness, and (5) IS strategic alignment and business performance. Thus, they developed measurement models (STROEPIS, IS strategic alignment, business performance, and IS effectiveness) to conduct their research.

After pilot testing, a survey was mailed to financial services and manufacturing industries to test the measurement and structural models. Executives within the same firm completed the survey. Data from 164 firms was used in the analysis. The hypothesized factor structure for constructs generally received empirical support. Al research instruments showed good convergent and discriminant validity. Scale reliabilities exceeded 0.70.

Chan et al. suggested that future researchers investigate the applicability of STROEPIS in industries other than manufacturing and financial services, focus on issues that facilitate alignment, and investigate other relationships that utilize the IS strategic orientation construct.
