# A field study of corporate employee monitoring: Attitudes, absenteeism, and the moderating influences of procedural justice perceptions

Michael Workman

Nathan Bisk College of Business, Florida Institute of Technology, 150 W. University Blvd, Melbourne, FL 32901, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 4 October 2008 Received in revised form 20 June 2009 Accepted 23 June 2009

Keywords: Personnel monitoring Procedural justice Psychological contract Organizational security policy

## a b s t r a c t

Managers are responsible for creating and enforcing company policies governing organizational practices, and one practice that is on the rise in organizations involves monitoring of employees for security purposes. The research literature on security behaviors has focused almost exclusively on compliance with or obedience to such policies; however, compliance with prescribed behaviors is not complete in terms of organizational performance. People may comply with policies with which they disagree, but harbor resentments and exhibit counterproductive and even destructive behaviors in protest. We conducted a field study of organizational monitoring policies and practices using factors from the threat control model and found that perceptions of threat, self-efficacy, and trust in the organization were key factors in attitudes about monitoring, and that these factors interacted with employee perceptions of organizational procedural justice such that high perceptions of organizational procedural justice moderated negative attitudes toward corporate monitoring, and better attitudes about monitoring was found to associate with reduced employee absences from the job.

## 1. Introduction

Managers carry special responsibilities for stewardship over personnel and organizational resources through enforcement of company policies and practices. In the execution of their stewardship, they may be involved in the gathering of information about employees such as their performance measurements compared to their objectives and other work-related activities; but also, increasingly, managers are called upon to gather information about employees and enforce organizational policies that include various security practices such as monitoring access to vital corporate resources (Thomas, 2004). Monitoring is the physical or electronic observation of someone’s activities and behavior (Ball & Webster, 2003).

These practices are particularly acute in organizations that are regulated by government legislation, such as in the healthcare industry in the United States (US) through the Health Insurance Porta bility and Accountability Act (HIPAA) or those that deliver products and services to a government agency or are regulated in the European Union (EU) or the United Kingdom (UK). Thus in most cases, organizations must gather information about employees to identify and authenticate them for access control, and then monitor their behavior as they conduct their work, which produces a large cache of employee-specific data (Mueller, 2007). Because of the severity of the consequences of security breaches, as well as levied as punitive measures by regulators for non-compliance, organizations are increasing their technological monitoring practices, but research into the organizational impacts from these practices is lagging behind (Workman, 2008a).

While a significant amount research has investigated issues related to company policy compliance, we contribute to the literature in several ways. First, we fill a gap in the literature by studying the ef fects of forced compliance, and second, while studies (e.g. D’Urso, 2006) have begun to assess the psychosocial impacts on people from monitoring, the findings are mixed – some studies (e.g. Johnston & Cheng, 2002; Wigan & Clarke, 2006) indicating deleterious effects, while others (e.g. Acquisti & Grossklags, 2005; Allen, Coopman, Hart, & Walker, 2007) often finding acceptance among workers for such practices. Our research helps to explain these contradictory findings. Finally, we addressed whether organizational procedural justice practices might offset some of the negative effects such as absenteeism.

## 1.1. Monitoring: the unblinking eye

The unlinking eye (Castells, Mireia-Fernandez, Qiu, & Sey, 2006) is a reference to the constant monitoring of physical or electronic movements and activities of people. In most cases people are aware of company monitoring activities in the US, EU, and the UK However; this is not always the case as was exposed about the covert monitoring of employees at Deutsche Bahn and Deutsche Telekom and other companies (c.f. DW Worldwide, 2009). Post the ‘‘US-9/11 attacks” as it is known, there are new legal protections for corporate monitoring in the US, UK, and EU (Keck, 2005; Mueller, 2007), and the prac tices have continued to expand in both range and depth (D’Urso, 2006; Workman, 2008a).

In an organizational setting, it has become common practice for instance to allow the electronic observation of web surfing activity, monitoring emails, and telephone call monitoring of office employees (e.g. ‘‘for quality assurance purposes”), along with increasing use of global positioning satellite systems (GPS) and radio frequency identification (RFID) for tracking mobile workers (Aiello 1993; Workman, 2008b).

Evidence of this escalation was shown by Vasterman, Yzermans, and Dirkzwager (2005) that indicated in 1999, 67% of employers electronically monitored their employees, but by 2001 that number had grown to 78%, and that 92% of employers indicated the use of electronic monitoring by 2003. Most of the employers surveyed (Harvey, 2007) said they monitored employee web surfing, more than half reviewed email messages and examined employees’ computer files, and roughly one-third tracked content, keystrokes, and time spent at the computer. In addition, employers are increasingly adding video monitoring to their monitoring repertoire (Fairweather, 1999). Of companies surveyed (Harvey, 2007), only 18% of the companies used video monitoring in 2001, but by 2005, that number had climbed to 51%, and 10% of the respondents indicated that cameras were installed specifically to track job performance.

## 1.2. Monitoring: attitudes and organizational behavior

The growing pervasiveness of the information collections has combined with increasing technolog ical sophistication, allowing companies to monitor the actions of employees more invasively also (Akdeniz, Walker, & Wall, 2000; Fairweather, 1999). Software that covertly monitors computer activities and monitoring hardware devices are being fashioned to blend into the environment by hiding them in pens, clocks, or bookends (Bajcsy, 2005). These may dampen employee monitoring awareness regardless of whether employers notify employees of such monitoring, which can create a psychological double bind on employees who know they are being monitored, but the unobtrusiveness of the technology may make them seem innocuous (Akdeniz et al., 2000). This creates a double bind because it carries the implied presumption that everyone is ‘‘potentially guilty” (Langenderfer & Linnhoff, 2005).

Nevertheless, employees state that maintaining their privacy is an essential element in how they feel about their jobs and their employers (Allen et al., 2007). They assert that monitoring negatively impacts their attitudes and their work-related behaviors such as absenteeism (Lee & Kleiner, 2003; Monahan, 2006). Important questions then are: How do employees perceive monitoring practices in organizations, given that privacy is such a critical issue to them (Huddy, Feldman, Taber, & Lahav, 2005)? What effects do these perceptions have on organizational behaviors such as absenteeism? And what might be done to mitigate if the perceptions and behaviors are negative?

## 2. Theory framework

The escalation in monitoring has been sparked in part by the increasing focus on security threats to individuals and to organizations (Fischer, Greitemeyer, Kastenmuller, Frey, & Oswald, 2007). Protection motivation theory (Rogers, 1975) has been used to examine the individual level threat assessments and behavioral coping responses, primarily in regards to healthcare issues (e.g. Boer & Seydel, 1996). Extending from this line of research, Workman, Bommer, and Straub (2008) developed a threat control model (TCM) and tested it in relation to why employees, who are aware of security threats and the countermeasures, often choose not to implement discretionary security protections. Working from this previous research, the present study investigates how those factors related to monitoring of employee behaviors.

## 2.1. Monitoring, attitudes and absenteeism

Organizations espouse treats in order to gain compliance with security policies that include having employees give up personal information and subsequently allow monitoring of them (Workman, 2008b). In this regard, the term commercial fear has been used in reference to the manipulation of strong emotions to neutralize negative attitudes toward something undesirable (Workman, 2008a). Importantly in this process, employees have expectations that the leadership in their organization wil treat them fairly in the process, and they develop tacit expectations that the organization will act in the best interest of the emplovees while conducting its stewardship over organizational resources including personnel – the concept is that of a psychological contract (Rousseau, 1994).

A psychological contract reflects people’s tacit beliefs about the reciprocal obligations between employees and their employer (Argyris, 1960). The bases of a psychological contract thus are trust and reciprocity (Conway & Briner. 2002). It advises that people expect to engage in reasonably predictable give-and-take exchanges with their employers, for example, an employee may expect to give time and effort in exchange for pay, and management may expect to provide pleasant and safe working conditions in exchange for employee loyalty (Argyris, 1960).

A breach of a psychological contract can be defined as an individual’s perception that another party has failed to fulfill promised obligations (Conway & Briner, 2002). If an employee believes his treatment is inequitable compared to others, he or she may be motivated to seek justice through organizational systems (Adams, 1965; Stephenson, 2008). If employees perceive that justice is not served with respect to their grievances, their attitudes and work-related behaviors are negatively impacted (Fassina, Jones, & Uggerslev, 2008; Guest, 1998). Attitudes are dispositional factors that comprise positive or negative evaluations of performing chosen behaviors; such as doing ‘‘X’’ is a ‘‘good’’ thing to do (Ajzen, 2002). Negative attitudes have been associated with organizational consequences such as low productivity and counterproductive behaviors such as increased absenteeism and tardiness (Terry & Hogg, 1996; Umpress, Labianca, Brass, Kass, & Scholten, 2003).

In terms of monitoring then, the psychological contract suggests that employees expect that espoused security threats are real and the monitoring of activities are justified, and that managers would act with due care and due diligence to protect the information gathered about employees and use the information and conduct monitoring for good purposes. Based on these assumptions and drawing from the TCM (Workman et al., 2008), our theoretical framework consisted of percep tions of vulnerability to an espoused security threat, the perceived severity of an espoused threat, an employee’s level of self-efficacy, the perceptions of the company’s effectiveness in taking preventive security measures, and general levels of trust. Accordingly, we developed hypotheses and exam ined the moderating effects of organizational procedural justice as follows.

## 2.2. Threat perceptions, severity and likelihood, and monitoring

The perception of threat is the anticipation of a psychological (e.g., assault), physical (e.g., battery), or sociological (e.g., theft) violation or harm to oneself or others, and which may be induced vicariously (Lazarus, 1991). From a security perspective, the focal stance in organizations has relied heavily on a theory of deterrence (Jervis, 1989) to try to prevent people from carrying out threatening acts by fear of damage, punishment, or retaliation (Straub, Carlson, & Jones, 1993). In organizations, deterrents are applied in two ways: internal threats of punishments for misbehaviors, and warnings of external threats to gain compliance with rules or policies (Straub & Nance, 1990). Security policies are organi zational instruments that establish the rules and punitive sanctions regarding security behaviors. Increasingly, employment is conditioned upon employee agreements that elicit promises to comply with security policies including allowing the organization to collect personal data and monitoring their activities (Straub & Welke, 1998; Workman & Gathegi, 2007).

If deterrents fail, organizations try to intercept the actions of people either on the outside or on the inside of a company before they can carryout a threat by monitoring their behaviors (Jones, 2007; Monahan, 2006). Recent research has even suggested using behavioral analysis to try to predict the potentiality of misbehavior by employees and establishing acute monitoring interventions of potentia ‘‘suspects” (Schultz, 2002; Schultz, 2008). In order for organizations to implement such controls from the pervasive collection of information about people and the invasive monitoring of their behaviors – and to approve budgets and spending and gain the compliance of its employees, managers may try to mobilize personnel by attempting to evoke strong emotions to neutralize negative attitudes toward monitoring (Workman, 2008a) and get them to relinquish their personal information and certain aspects of their privacies (Allen et al., 2007; Workman, 2008b).

In this process, companies may use tactics to make vigilant an organization’s workforce making even remote threats seem severe and imminent but mitigated under the auspices of due care of the organization to assuage negative attitudes (Fischer et al., 2007; Monahan, 2006; Vasterman et al., 2005). Security awareness and training programs, security standards and compliance audits, some types of mandatory access controls, and issuing warnings such as popup dialogs in user interfaces are just some of the ways that organizations elicit fear appeals (Workman et al., 2008). Fear appeals must constantly reenergize employees because over time their effects begin to deplete. Unless people are continuously mobilized against a threat, they eventually become complacent (Fischer et al., 2007; Pyszczynski, Greenberg, & Solomon, 1997). This is important in terms of ensuring security policy compliance and security-conscious behaviors because it has been found for example that people may ignore or disable security measures when they are perceived as intrusive or ineffective, unless they are constantly placed in a psychological state of hyper-vigilance about severe impending threats (Langenderfer & Linnhoff, 2005). However, this is also significant because employee attitudes about monitoring of their behavior in order for organizations to provide security initiatives depend on their perceptions of the severity and likelihood of a threat occurrence (Rogers, 1975).

Nevertheless, people differ in their assessments of threats in response to fear appeals (Wilde 2001). For example, some people have a greater risk or threat tolerance than others (Lauriola, Leven, & Hart, 2007; Sitkin & Pablo, 1992). When a threat is perceived, people behave according to the amount of risk they are willing to accept – a risk homeostasis. Risk behavior thus relies on the perceived severity of the potential damage levied by the threat, such as financial costs of repairs, against a person’s perceived payoff for ignoring the risk or threat (Grothmann and Reusswig, 2006). Therefore, people tend to adjust their behavior in response to the likelihood and extent of the damage the threat may cause compared to potential rewards versus punishments for ignoring it (Pyszczynski et al., 1997; Rogers, 1975).

In summary, the perceived severity and likelihood of threat and the associated acceptance of risky behavior are based on the premises that: (1) people place a certain intangible value on $" \mathrm { l i f e } "$ , ‘‘liberty”, and ‘‘property”; (2) they have a threshold level of risk they will accept, tolerate, prefer, desire, or choose; (3) this produces a ‘‘target level” of risk that they will accept before acting depends on the perceived advantages or benefits versus the disadvantages or costs of safe or unsafe alternatives; and (4) this will affect employee attitudes regarding an organization’s monitoring practices in order to take precautionary measures to try protect them (Rogers, 1975; Wilde, 2001). Consequently:

$H _ { 1 } ;$ People who perceive greater levels in terms of perceptions of threat vulnerability will have more positive attitudes toward monitoring by their organizations.

$H _ { 2 } ;$ People who perceive greater levels in terms of perceptions of threat severity will have more positive attitudes toward monitoring by their organizations than those who perceive lower threat levels

## 2.3. Efficacy and monitoring

On the one hand, fear appeals may serve to mobilize people for taking actions against threats, but they can have a reverse effect – they might engender fatalistic attitudes in some people (Fischer et al., 2007; Pyszczynski et al., 1997). The coping mechanisms people may take toward espoused threats rely in part on their perceived confidence in their abilities, or self-efficacy (Bandura, 1978), to take alterative courses of action compared against their personal assessments of the costs of those actions (Boer & Seydel, 1996; Rogers, 1975; Wu, Stanton, Li, Galbraith, & Cole, 2005). In context of our study, this ranges from availing themselves of adjudication channels or quitting compared to conceding to monitoring practices and suppressing their resentments. This is because compared to people with low selfefficacy, people who possess high self-efficacy tend to perceive more control over their outcomes, such having a greater say in organizational practices, and having the ability to quit and find other employment when working conditions become unsuitable (Tangirala & Ramanujam, 2008; Wiesenfeld, Swann, Brockner, & Bartel, 2007).

A second aspect of coping behavior depends on how effective people perceive the available preventive measures, including the security of the security information collected by means of monitoring (Pechmann, Zhao, Goldberg, & Reibling, 1993). For example, some researchers (c.f. Sasse, Brostoff, & Weirich, 2004) have stated that many of the security methods might be akin to the cliché about a locked door keeping honest people honest, and the point has been made repeatedly in the security literature that little can be done to stop a determined attacker (Theoharidou, Kokolakis, Karyda, & Kiountouzis, 2005). Still others (Davis & Silver, 2004) have charged that extant systems have virtually ignored the creativity with which people find ways around any given security system, citing that the ability of attackers to contravene security continues to elude the ability to defend against them (Bresz, 2004).

When the efficacy of precautionary measures is perceived as high, people have more positive attitudes about security policies and practices that include the collection of personal information and monitoring of their behaviors (Hsu & Kuo, 2003; Thomas, 2004). Alternatively, if the efficacy of the precautionary measures is low or is perceived to deliver only a small incremental degree of benefit compared to the personal costs, people have poorer attitudes about such policies and practices (Pechmann et al., 1993).

To summarize, to a degree the use of security measures such as the collection of personal information from monitoring for the purposes of identification and authentication may lend to general perceptions of security, but there are instances where these measures may psychologically undermine attitudes as people are or may be placed under scrutiny by ‘‘fighting back” (Bennett & Regan, 2004). This can affect employees in unintended ways. At a minimum, people may repress and internalize the emotional impact of the simultaneous effects of feeling under constant threat and under constant scrutiny if they perceive their self-efficacy or the efficacy of the prevention measures are inadequate which may result in poor attitudes and avoidance behaviors such as absenteeism (Hansen, 2002). Therefore,

$H _ { 3 } { \mathrm { : } }$ People who perceive greater self-efficacy will have more positive attitudes toward monitoring by their organizations than those who perceive lower self-efficacy

$H _ { 4 } \mathrm { : }$ People who perceive greater efficacy of preventive measures will have more positive attitudes toward monitoring by their organizations than those who perceive lower efficacy of preventive measures.

## 2.4. Trust and monitoring

As indicated before, the extent to which employee expectations of what the organization will provide and what they owe the organization in return forms the basis of a psychological contract (Ivancevich, Konopaske, & Matteson, 2008). People will tolerate monitoring and give up certain of their privacies so long as they perceive that the psychological contract is maintained (Umpress et al. 2003). While some research has suggested that age may factor into perceptions of the psychological contract (and hence should be controlled in hypotheses tests) there are significant relationships be tween a psychological contract breach and work-related outcomes (Bal, De Lange, Jansen, & Van der Velde, 2008).

A key ingredient in emplovee attitudes concerning the psychological contract is trust (Stephenson 2008). Some research (e.g. Walczuch & Lundgren, 2004) has defined various forms or types of trust for different targets and situational contexts; but regardless of various contexts, some people have a greater general propensity to trust than others – that is, they may have a more ‘‘trusting nature” (Charbaji & Jannoun, 2005; Chen & Barnes, 2007; Krishnan, Martin, & Noorderhaven, 2006). When people are very trusting, this can ‘‘limit the cognitive efforts of (people) when they consider their broade environment. . . and the cognitive comfort that trust brings also limits variety of thought and action and attentiveness” (Krishnan et al., 2006, pp. 894–895) making one more accepting of monitoring regardless of how the monitoring information might actually be used (Wigan & Clarke, 2006).

That personal information gathered on the job is owned not by oneself but by corporations and by governments (Keck, 2005) places employees in vulnerable situations that rely on trust (Marx, 2006). One way trust may be challenged is through leakage of personal information from information repositories or by other means, which exacerbates both the potential and severity of the damage to individ uals. As an example, a letter was recently circulated by the Administaff Corporation that read in part: ‘‘An Administaff laptop computer containing personal information including social security numbers, names and addresses of current and former Administaff worksite employees was reported missing. . . The laptop computer is password protected; however, the personal information was not saved in an encrypted location which is a clear violation of company policies ” (c.f. Workman et al., 2008).

In other cases trust might be challenged by the organizational aim to thwart attacks by extensive monitoring-related information gathering and then through data mining techniques. try to identify patterns of misbehavior that emerge from the collection of behavioral data and then closely scrutinize personnel (Schultz, 2002; Schultz, 2008). This has meant the use of technologies that enable data collection and storage of a massive information cache within many organizations about employees tha offer the potential to be used or even misused (Monahan, 2006).

If due care and safeguards are not properly taken in organizations to protect employee information, employee trust will be challenged (Stephenson, 2008). Maintaining information on laptops that can be lost or stolen, or the outsourcing of personnel data collection and storage has caused some (Borrull & Oppenheim, 2004) to assert an abrogation of organizational due care responsibilities (Jørgensen, 2006). Hence, organizational members are likely to observe or be exposed to a variety of cases where due care is taken and also instances of carelessness by other employees and management with sensitive information, including their own private security data (Marx, 2006).

In summary then, some employees trust the organization that the information collection and monitoring will increase the odds of thwarting attacks as espoused and that due care will be taken in the protection and rightful use of the personal information, while others remain more skeptical abou those assumptions and are suspicious of the organizational motives and use of the information, which affect their attitudes and their behaviors such as avoidance of the antecedent (Castells et al., 2006; Davis & Silver, 2004; Huddy et al., 2005). Consequently, we hypothesize that:

$H _ { 5 } { \mathrm { : } }$ People who are more generally trusting will have more positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations than those who are generally less trusting.

## 2.5. Procedural justice interactions with TCM components

The literature posits many influences among people in their compliance with security policies, which establish the rules for governing their behaviors (e.g. Workman & Gathegi, 2007). However, whether or not people simply comply with policies is incomplete in explaining organizational results. Employees may comply with the policies but harbor resentment or exhibit other (possibly subtle) counterproductive behaviors if they consider the policies unfair (Calluzzo & Cante, 2004; Proctor, Kim, Vu Schultz, & Salvendy, 2002; Umpress et al., 2003). For example, the literature reports that persistent fear appeals and protracted hyper-vigilance can have negative consequences for psychological states of mind such as attitudes (c.f. Albright, 2007), and suggests negative effects from systemic monitoring on pro-social behaviors of the law abiding (Calluzzo and Cante, 2004; Proctor et al., 2002).

In their meta-analysis of electronic monitoring in the workplace, Johnston and Cheng (2002) identified an association with employees who have had their performance technologically monitored and increases in absenteeism. turnover and decreases in productivity. They also found that many of the monitored employees described their environments as toxic and that it tended to undermine employee morale and created divisions between employees and management. Further, in cases where management used electronic monitoring as a means to improve consistency in employee performance evaluations, employees frequently questioned the fairness of their employer’s use of electronic monitoring in the reviews, and that the perceived lack of procedural fairness in monitoring sometimes led to negative employee reactions such as withdrawal, sabotage, resignation, or some other form of diminished organizational citizenship. Thus the question begs, how might negative attitudes be mitigated by the higher perceptions of procedural justice in the execution of security practices involving monitoring (Allen et al., 2007)? We therefore proposed interactions of the factors in our study.

Building on Adam’s (1965) equity theory, the concept of organizational procedural justice has become an important research element in understanding how people respond to administrative demands (Dietz, Robinson, Folger, Baron, & Schultz, 2003; Luo, 2007). Procedural justice is perceived when the process used to make the decision was deemed fair. There are a number of conditions that lead people to perceive justice in the process. First, people want to be able to have a say or voice in any decision that might affect them. Further, people want to know that managers and those with power in the organization are suspending their personal biases and motivations from decisions and are relying on objective data, to the greatest possible extent. Finally, procedural justice is perceived when people are presented with a mechanism for correcting perceived errors or poor decisions, such as having an appeal process (Baldwin, Bommer, & Rubin, 2008).

The organizational use of fear appeals in justifying the collecting of personal information and behavior monitoring levies an impact on employee beliefs about the motivations, benevolence, and integrity of the organizational leadership and affects the extent to which employees are willing to take risks (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995).

People have optimal levels of risk that they tolerate from their environments, and they try to adjust their behavior so that it closely approximates their own risk optimums or risk homeostasis (Eysenck, 1994). When the management in an organization presents its case for the requirements for information collection and monitoring, employees assess the requirements according to their views of fairness and whether they perceive they have input into the process and outcomes (Umpress et al., 2003).

If organizational mechanisms allow for people to express dissent, or if they have avenues to assuage their concerns, for example, replacing the use of a social security number with a different identifier (Albrechtsen, 2006), this moderates employee threat-risk assessments (DeDreu, Giebels, & Van de Vliert, 1998). If employees are not permitted input into a process that affects them or if they fee that management is arbitrary in their collections of information and monitoring of personnel, and that employees must remain silent under the threat of punishment, this negatively impacts their attitudes (Tangirala & Ramanujam, 2008).

Next, when employees perceive that they have the self-efficacy and that organizational processes are just and effective, they have more positive attitudes than they do when organizational procedural justice is perceived badly regardless of efficacies (Mayer, Nishii, Schneider, & Goldstein, 2007; Wiesenfeld et al., 2007). When high procedural justice is perceived, it improves the interactions between leaders and organizational members because employees believe that remedies exist for any breaches or wrongs that may be encountered (Coleman, 1990). The willingness to take risks relies on not only one’s own abilities, but also the integrity of the processes that broker the relationships between parties (Mayer et al., 1995). In that sense, this interaction reflects a ‘‘psychological state comprising the intentions to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of an other” (Rousseau, Sitkin, Burt, & Camerer 1998, p. 395).

Similar to threat assessment, efficacy and risk taking, procedural justice and trust intersect at the juncture where there are mutual binding responsibilities that must be upheld and are tested through the course of the transactions exchanged. When management issues fear appeals, people have different reactions to them in terms of whether they assess the appeals as authentic or whether management is attempting to manipulate them by constantly ratcheting up the espoused severity and frequency of the threats in their rhetoric (Albright, 2007)

Moreover, when no remedies exist for violations of the psychological contract, employees develop negative attitudes toward the organization and its practices (Zhao, Wayne, Glibkowski, & Bravo, 2007). The degree of trust is reinforced by the perceived fairness or unfairness in organizational processes (DeDreu et al., 1998). That is to say, perceptions of fairness and procedural justice in a transaction moderate trust levels (Lind, Kray, & Thompson, 2001) and this affects employee attitudes either positively or negatively (Fassina et al., 2008).

Finally, when people have poor attitudes about their workplace, they exhibit greater avoidance behavior such as having increased absenteeism (Somers, 1995), and conversely, when work attitudes are more positive, people tend to have more positive attendance records (Nielsen, 2008; Terry & Hogg 1996). Therefore,

$H _ { 6 } \mathbf { : }$ Procedural justice will interact in the relationship between threat vulnerability perceptions and atti tudes such that greater procedural justice will amplify positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations.

$H _ { 7 } \colon$ Procedural justice will interact in the relationship between threat severity perceptions and attitudes such that greater procedural justice will amplify positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations.

$H _ { 8 } \mathbf { : }$ Procedural justice will interact in the relationship between self-efficacy and attitudes such that greater procedural justice will amplify positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations.

$H _ { 9 } \colon$ Procedural justice will interact in the relationship between efficacy of preventive measures and atti tudes such that greater procedural justice will amplify positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations.

$H _ { 1 0 } \mathrm { : }$ Procedural justice will interact in the relationship between trust and attitudes such that greater procedural justice will amplify positive attitudes about monitoring by their organizations

$H _ { 1 1 } \colon$ People who have more positive attitudes about personal monitoring by their organizations will have lower absenteeism.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Participants

To gain a bounded population with qualities that would yield data to address our research question, we selected and gained entrée into a multinational company with offices in the US, throughout Europe, throughout the UK, and in various locations in India. We made this selection so that the sampling validity could be ascertained while preserving the multinational attributes important to our study. The company was involved in a range of product development and services, conducting quality assurance, delivering customer support and services, and performing marketing and sales functions – and we sampled across the lines of business to help preserve ecological validity while controlling for variations in organizational contexts and to avoid cross-organizational validity issues. Using a company employee list frame, we randomly sampled 624 employees from a population of approximately 102,000, and we received a 62% response rate, or 387 respondents giving us a 95% sampling confidence level ±5% at a standard error of estimate of 0.05. We then collected absenteeism data for these participants in number of hours over a 20 month period.

## 3.2. Instrumentation

In order to properly assess during our manipulation checks the codification of the data, we asked the respondents what information the company collected for the purpose of monitoring and that they considered ‘‘personal”. Responses included, Meyers–Briggs type indictors (given to all employees), ‘‘4S” Selling Styles inventory (given to the sales department), yearly credit checks, pre-employment background checks including reference checks, drug tests, educational records and criminal histories, social security numbers, fingerprint scans (for some employees who access computer server rooms), military service records, performance evaluations, demographics and addresses, health information (including disabilities), various information collected about their activities during the monitoring such as what Websites they visited and emails they sent, and information including biometrics used for identification and authentication.

We also asked respondents to define monitoring in order to operationalize the term. Definitions of monitoring were, monitoring email, RFID tracking of personnel movements through certain buildings, monitoring Web surfing, examining files on hard drives and attachments in email, video monitoring o those who worked in server rooms or movements through corridors; and in customer support listening in on phone conversations ‘‘for quality assurance purposes”.

As recommended by Straub et al. (1993), we used pre-existing scales in all cases, both for their validation characteristics and for efficiency. Items for the questionnaire on a 7-point scale in positive directions were chosen as follows: threat severity, vulnerability, and response efficacy items were adapted from Rippetoe and Rogers (1987) and modified according to Milne and Orbell (2000) recommendations. Self-efficacy items were adapted from Bandura (1978) modified for the target population according to Compeau and Higgins (1995) guidelines. Trust items were adapted from Workman (2008b) and Gefen and Straub (2004). Items for Procedural Justice were adapted from Baldwin et al. (2008) and Lind et al. (2001), and the dependent measures items were developed according to Ajzen’s (2002) recommendations for measuring attitudes.

We initially screened the data by using a combination of scatter plots and the examination of Mahalanobis distances (Tabachnick & Fidell, 1996) to ensure the removal of outliers (one was found and discarded) and determine the data properties. For construct validity, we conducted a factor analysis rotated with Varimax. With eigenvalues set at 1.00, we retained items with communalities more than .70 and cross-loadings below .35 (Stevens, 1991) according to Kaiser’s rule (Tabachnick & Fidell, 1996). There were some statistically significant correlations among independent variables raising some concerns about multicollinearity. We addressed this problem on two levels. First, we cleansed the data by removing items until multicollinearity tolerances were above .70 (Allison, 1999). Second, as discussed in the Results section, we centered the data in deviation score form according to Aiken and West (1991) and Allison's (1999) recommendations for conducting the hypotheses tests.

## 3.3. Procedures

Corporate executive sponsors facilitated our entrée once researchers had signed a nondisclosure and confidentiality agreement and had obtained approvals from the institutional IRB. The researchers were provided with company directory of the population under study, including location and email addresses. The corporate sponsors sent each participant a message, with an acknowledgement flag set, in which they were informed that researchers were studying a problem involving how best to address employee concerns and simultaneously respond to company security concerns.

The randomly selected participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses in the message. The researchers then contacted participants via email with an attachment and a cover letter once again issuing a statement regarding confidentiality, along with an announcement of the Web address (URL) of the online data collection instrument. Also, each participant each received an authentication password. When participants took the questionnaire, the authentication password was used to produce a message digest to keep track of who had completed the questionnaire and to ensure that the questionnaire was taken only once by each participant. Participants accessed the survey Web page distributed as a link embedded in an email message sent by the researchers. The text of the message contained the consent form and cover letter explaining the data collection instrument and instructing them on how to access it. Radio buttons and drop down lists controlled for data entry errors.

We took an initial step to compare respondents on their perceptions about the collection of infor mation for employment purposes compared to perceptions of information collections from monitoring. It was interesting to note that the mean scores were significantly different $( t = - 3 5 0 . 6 0 , p < . 0 0 1 )$ The information collection for employment mean was 4.6 (on a scale of 7) indicating a rather benign view of the practice, but monitoring had a mean score of 3.2 indicating a rather unfavorable view of the practice. Therefore, the separation of these data for the dependent variable and the moderation test became crucial in our interpretation of the results. For testing the hypotheses, we used moderated hierarchical regression analyses. The controls were assessed in the first step followed by the assessment of the main effects of the regression equations, while the moderated hypotheses were evaluated consistent with the recommendations of Stone and Hollenbeck (1989) and Aiken and West (1991). As such, the data were centered in the analysis to reduce multicollinearity among the interaction terms and their individual components (Aiken and West 1991). ## 5. Discussion

People may comply with company policies and procedures they may disagree with, but harbor resentments about them that may lead to counterproductive behaviors. The literature (Derene, 2007; Hillenbrand, 2008) has been highlighting concerns about employee monitoring and calling for further study. We sought to fill three gaps in the literature: (1) to investigate the effects of forced compliance with the policy of employee monitoring, (2) to help explain discrepancies in the literature that show some accepting of employee monitoring while others expressing concerns, and (3) determine ways organizations might mitigate negative effects.

We found in our study that people who had greater perceptions of vulnerability to security threats were more amenable to monitoring. However, while perceptions of the severity of those threats were not a factor in people’s attitudes, their attitudes (which were relatively negative) did improve when they had more positive perceptions of organizational procedural justice. We also found that greater self-efficacy resulted in more positive attitudes about monitoring, and that greater perceptions of company security efficacy led to more positive attitudes about monitoring, and that greater levels of trust produced more positive attitudes about monitoring. In all cases, attitudes were improved by higher perceptions of procedural justice, and that as more positive attitudes about monitoring was associated with lower absenteeism. The findings of this research should be of interest to human resources practitioners, organizational developers, organizational behaviorists, industrial and organizational psychologists, and managers in organizations alike.

When interpreting the results of our study, some limitations are noteworthy. While participants were randomly selected, we could not randomly assign them to groups. We chose a field study over a laboratory experiment for the purposes of gaining ecological validity and to achieve ‘‘real world” practices that tend to be more generalizable, along with the feasibility of gathering a larger participant population to test. Next, while we did conduct a random selection, and the response rate was reasonably good and well within the good sampling validity parameters for a ±5% sampling confidence at a standard error of estimate of 0.05, we cannot be completely sure that those who did not response are not substantially different from those who did in some important aspect.

## 6. Conclusions

The trajectory of the security stance in many if not most organizations leans toward broadening and deepening the collection of personal information and monitoring people as preventive measures against security threats. There are increases in security threats and organizations are obliged to address these threats proactively through the use of identification, authentication, and monitoring practices. As a result, some employees may be constantly placed in a psychological state of hyper-vigilance about severe impending threats. This fear may help to neutralize negative attitudes about practices that people otherwise find objectionable, such as invading their privacy. However, the act of monitoring can result in counterproductive behaviors if people feel simultaneously under constant threat and constant scrutiny (Tangirala & Ramanujam, 2008). For example, if threats are perceived as imminent and severe, people will often trade their privacies for even the illusion of safety (c.f. Davis & Silver, 2004; Joslyn & Haider-Markel, 2007), but these tradeoffs may create a psychological resistance that can lead to counterproductive behaviors such as absenteeism, and even to subversive behaviors (Gass & Seiter, 1999).

We note that security practices are optimized when they are commensurate with the needs of the organization as well as the needs of the employees. Using due care and due diligence in security practices, such as taking precautions against information leakage, helps to raise employee perceptions of organizational efficacy. Transparency in the measures undertaken for protecting personal information and how monitoring is done and for what purposes improves the attitudes employees have about such practices. Finally, important attitudinal gains are achieved when employees perceive a procedurally just organization, and that they are empowered to address concerns and grievances with management over the monitoring practices.
