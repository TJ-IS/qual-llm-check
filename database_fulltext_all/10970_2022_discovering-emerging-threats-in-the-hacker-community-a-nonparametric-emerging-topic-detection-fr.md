# DISCOVERING EMERGING THREATS IN THE HACKER COMMUNITY: A NONPARAMETRIC EMERGING TOPIC DETECTION FRAMEWORK<sup>1</sup>

Weifeng Li Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA U.S.A. {weifeng.li@uga.edu}

Hsinchun Chen Department of Management Information Systems, Eller College of Management, University of Arizona Tucson, AZ U.S.A. {hsinchun@arizona.edu}

The prevalence and rapid growth of cybercrime are largely attributed to hacker communities on the dark web, where cybercriminals extensively exchange hacking resources, share hacking knowledge, and organize cyberattacks. Such streams of hacker-generated content constitute an invaluable data source for developing threat intelligence that can inform organizations of cybersecurity risks and facilitate proactive cyber defense. Drawing upon the design science paradigm, we propose a novel nonparametric emerging topic detection (NPETD) framework for detecting emerging topics in streams of hacker generated content. Our framework extends the state-of-the-art nonparametric topic model to inductively model topics without having to specify the number of topics a priori. Moreover, our framework features an efficient algorithm to jointly infer topics and detect topic emergence. We conducted experiments to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of our framework in comparison with the state-of-theart baseline methods. Our framework outperformed the baseline methods in detecting the listings of emerging threats in darknet marketplaces on recall, F-measure, topic coherence, and processor time. The practical utility of our framework is further demonstrated in a major hacker forum, where we identified several notable emerging topics with important implications for victim companies and law enforcement. The proposed framework contributes to cybersecurity, topic detection and tracking, and design science.

Keywords: Cybersecurity, hacker community, topic detection and tracking, emerging topic detection, nonparametric topic model, design science

## Introduction

The prevalence and the growth rate of cybercrime have become a major concern for organizations and society. Companies experienced an average of 270 cyberattacks in 2021, an increase of 31% from 2020 (Bissell et al., 2021). In 2020, the FBI received a record number of 791,790 cybercrime reports from the general public with reported losses exceeding \$4.2 billion (FBI, 2020). Numerous reports have attributed cybercrime to the hacker community on the dark web, where cybercriminal supply chains grow and enable various cybercrimes including ransomware attacks, carding fraud, and data breaches (Europol, 2021; Microsoft, 2021). Past studies have also noted the abundance of the content generated by hackers in such communities to exchange hacking resources (e.g., ransomware, breached data), share hacking knowledge, and organize cybercriminal activities (Benjamin et al., 2019; Yue et al., 2019).

Hacker-generated content in these communities constitutes an invaluable data source for developing threat intelligence that can inform organizations of cybersecurity risks and facilitate proactive cyber defense (Mahmood et al., 2010), and this data source has motivated the recent development of computational methods for hacker community-based threat intelligence (Benjamin et al., 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2016; Samtani et al., 2022). Prior methods have commonly focused on categorizing hacker-generated content into certain threat categories, which enable cybersecurity stakeholders to efficiently digest the massive amount of content available in hacker communities. Despite significant progress, these methods mostly rely on human-labeled data and are not designed for detecting threats beyond previously known threat categories. Since new targets, vulnerabilities, exploits, and hacking resources have emerged in hacker communities, detecting such emerging threats is of particular value to organizations’ cyber risk assessment and proactive cyber defense strategies.

The topic detection and tracking (TDT) methodology (Allan, 2002) has the potential to advance emerging threat detection by identifying the emerging topics in hacker communities. Nonetheless, when applied to the exploration of hacker communities for emerging threats, extant TDT methods have two major limitations. First, existing methods mostly assume a fixed number of emerging topics, which could be problematic in exploring hacker communities, where emerging topics are often unknown a priori. Inaccurate prespecification of emerging topics could result in topic misrepresentation, affecting the discovery of emerging threats. Second, the topic inference algorithms of most methods are either inherently time-consuming (e.g., Gibbs sampling) or predicated on restrictive assumptions (e.g., one document can have only one topic). There is a need to further improve computational efficiency to enhance proactive cyber defense. Taken together, ineffective and inefficient emerging topic detection in hacker communities diminishes organizations’ situational awareness and timely response to cyberattacks.

Drawing upon the design science paradigm (Hevner et al., 2004), we propose the design of a novel framework, called nonparametric emerging topic detection (NPETD), to detect emerging topics in streams of hacker community content. The methodological contribution of the proposed framework is twofold. First, our framework extends the state-of-the-art nonparametric topic model, the hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP), which specializes in inductively modeling topics without having to prespecify the number of emerging topics. Second, our framework features an efficient algorithm to enhance proactive cyber defense. Our algorithm couples stochastic topic inference with statistical emergence detection to expedite the discovery of emerging topics. To evaluate the proposed method, we developed our research testbed based on two major forms of hacker communities. Our experiments on listings from the darknet marketplace revealed that, in comparison with the state-of-the-art baseline methods, the flexibility introduced by HDP improves the effectiveness of detecting listings of emerging threats, as measured by precision, recall, F-measure, and topic coherence. Moreover, our proposed algorithm was able to detect the listings of emerging threats much faster than the best-performing baseline methods, while maintaining consistent and competitive effectiveness. Furthermore, we demonstrate the practical utility of our framework in a major hacker forum, from which we identified several emerging topics with important implications for compromised companies and law enforcement. These findings, along with the proposed method, contribute to several important research areas, including cybersecurity, topic detection and tracking, and design science.

## Related Work

## Hacker Community-Based Threat Intelligence

The hacker community on the dark web provides a virtual platform for tens of thousands of hackers worldwide to exchange various hacking resources, such as malware, exploits, spamming, POS/ATM skimmer, remote access tools, stolen accounts, and many more (Benjamin et al., 2019; Mahmood et al., 2010; Yue et al., 2019). In addition, hackers share hacking knowledge and organize cybercriminal activities through such communities (Benjamin et al., 2019; Thomas et al., 2015). Hacker communities generally exist in two prevalent forms. Forum-based hacker communities allow hackers to hold conversations in the form of posted messages, through which hackers can exchange hacking resources, discuss hacking ideas, share hacking knowledge, and connect with others (Hui et al., 2017). Marketplace-based platforms, such as carding shops and darknet Marketplaces, are online black markets for anonymous transactions involving hacking resources, where hacker sellers can post listings that describe the major features, functionalities, and pricing of their products and services for sale (Ebrahimi et al., 2022).

The hacker-generated content in such communities presents a unique opportunity for developing threat intelligence that can inform organizations of cyber risks and facilitate proactive cyber defense (Mahmood et al., 2010). Hence, a growing body of research has developed computational methods for developing threat intelligence from hacker communities (Benjamin et al., 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2022; Li et al., 2016; Samtani et al., 2022). Two related streams of such methods are threat categorization and emerging threat detection. Threat categorization methods focus on classifying hacker-generated content into a set of predetermined threat categories, such as carding malware and services (Li et al., 2016), exploits (Samtani et al., 2022), and hacker assets (Ebrahimi et al., 2022). Threat categorization methods are mostly supervised in nature, leveraging cybersecurity knowledge to determine a fixed set of threat categories and subsequently training meticulously designed models on the datasets labeled with these categories. This stream of methods has enabled cybersecurity researchers and practitioners to efficiently identify known threats from the rich content in hacker communities.

Equally important is the discovery of emerging threats outside of the existing cybersecurity knowledge base. New targets, vulnerabilities, exploits, and hacking resources are constantly appearing in hacker communities, and threat categorization methods are not equipped to detect these previously unseen threats. Accordingly, the second stream of methods focuses on detecting relevant content indicative of emerging threats (Benjamin et al., 2019). Most of the existing methods focus on detecting a set of emerging keywords, such as victim names (Almukaynizi et al., 2018) and threat terms (Benjamin & Chen, 2015). In practice, since multiple emerging threats are often appearing the hacker communities at the same time, dissecting the set of emerging keywords into semantically coherent themes is important for detecting individual emerging threats. Existing keyword-based methods rarely capture semantic associations between keywords and therefore cannot group keywords of each threat. To that end, topic detection and tracking (TDT) methodology presents enormous potential in developing threat intelligence about emerging threats through detecting emerging topics from hacker communities.

## Topic Detection and Tracking

Topic detection and tracking (TDT) is a body of research that develops systems to discover topical structures in document streams, such as news articles and social media feeds (Allan, 2002). Major TDT tasks include topic segmentation (Hu et al., 2013), topic tracking (Kawamae, 2018), topic link detection (Bejan & Harabagiu, 2014), and emerging topic detection (Atefeh & Khreich, 2015). Both supervised and unsupervised approaches have been used to address these tasks (Franz et al., 2001; Schwartz et al., 2001). Supervised approaches specialize in the detection and tracking of specified types of topics labeled in the ground truth training data (Atefeh & Khreich, 2015). In many real-world applications, the topic spaces for labeled data and unlabeled real-world data could be different due to the emergence of new topics or the drift of existing topics (Zhang et al., 2011). Consequently, unsupervised approaches are suitable for exploring unlabeled real-world data to identify previously unknown topics.

State-of-the-art unsupervised TDT methods build upon the probabilistic topic representation, which extends the traditional keyword-based topic representation that characterizes each topic as a set of keywords (Aiello et al., 2013; Choi & Park, 2019; Kleinberg, 2003). Probabilistic topic representation captures not only keywords but also their semantic relationships empirically from the data, which further informs the exploration of the unlabeled real-world data (Blei et al., 2003). Probabilistic topic representation has been extensively applied in the hacker community research. Examples include characterizing illicit activities on the deep and dark web (Tavabi et al., 2019), analyzing discussions of distributed denial of service attacks (Yue et al., 2019), clustering carding forum discussions (Kigerl, 2018), understanding the implementations of hacker assets (Samtani et al., 2017), and more. We next review two major probabilistic topic models: Bayesian parametric topic modeling and Bayesian nonparametric topic modeling.

## Bayesian Parametric Topic Modeling: Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)

LDA builds upon a set of probabilistic assumptions about how a set of latent variables (e.g., topics) generate observed documents (i.e., prior distributions) and subsequently infers how these assumptions (e.g., topics) manifest in the data (i.e., posterior distributions). Formally, LDA first assumes ?? latent topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { 1 } , \dots , \pmb { \beta } _ { K }$ to be shared across documents ${ \pmb w } _ { 1 } , \dots , { \pmb w } _ { D } \colon ( 1 )$ each topic $\pmb { \beta } _ { k }$ sets a multinomial distribution over words, and (2) the word composition of each document $w _ { d }$ is governed by a latent multinomial mixture of topics $\pmb { \theta } _ { d } = ( \theta _ { d } ^ { 1 } , \dots , \theta _ { d } ^ { K } )$ , where $\theta _ { d } ^ { k }$ is the proportion of topic $\pmb { \beta } _ { k }$ in document $w _ { d }$ . By fitting the corpus data to these assumptions, LDA infers the posterior distributions to capture the distribution of latent variables in the data. For example, the posterior topics $\beta _ { 1 } , \ldots , \beta _ { K } | w _ { 1 } , \ldots , w _ { D }$ capture the topics in the corpus.

One practical challenge facing LDA is topic prespecification (Arun et al., 2010). That is, LDA assumes a fixed number (i.e., ??) of topics, and this number must be prespecified for LDA. The misspecification of the number of topics can lead to erroneous posterior distributions, which can further result in topic misrepresentation: While an underestimated number of topics causes underfitting, where each resulting topic is convoluted with several actual topics, an overestimated number of topics leads to overfitting, where multiple resulting topics overlap on the same actual topics. Topic misrepresentation also raises issues when interpreting discovered topics for cyber threats and can subsequently affect the discovery of emerging topics. While preliminary model selection techniques have been proposed to determine the appropriate number of topics (Arun et al., 2010; Griffiths & Steyvers, 2004), estimating the number of topics can be challenging for exploring hacker communities on the dark web.

## Bayesian Nonparametric Topic Modeling: Hierarchical Dirichlet Process (HDP)

In Bayesian nonparametric modeling, latent variables can be modeled as sample distributions drawn from an overarching stochastic process (i.e., a distribution over distributions), thereby allowing for a potentially unlimited number of latent variables (e.g., topics). Specifically, nonparametric topic models use the Dirichlet process (DP) as its main building block, which is a stochastic process known for having a wide coverage over topics and tractable posterior inference. With the concentration parameter ?? and base distribution ??, the DP is represented as $\mathsf { D P } ( \alpha , H )$ . DP is selected for topic modeling due to its clustering and infinity properties. Specifically, a sample distribution from DP, $G \sim \mathrm { D P } ( \alpha , H )$ , is a discrete distribution over an infinite number of unique samples $\begin{array} { r } { \widehat { \pmb { \beta } } _ { 1 } , \ldots , \widehat { \pmb { \beta } } _ { \infty } { \sim } { \cal H } \colon G = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \infty } \pi _ { k } \delta _ { \widehat { \pmb { \beta } } _ { k } } } \end{array}$ with probability one (Sethuraman, 1994).<sup>2</sup> Draws from the sample distribution $\beta _ { 1 } , \dots , \beta _ { n } { \sim } G$ can form a flexible number of clusters, where $\pmb { \beta } _ { i } \ ' _ { \mathrm { { s } } }$ with the same value of ${ \widehat { \pmb { \beta } } } _ { k } { \sim } H$ belong to the same cluster. DP introduces considerable flexibility to topic modeling where the correct number of topics is inductively estimated a posteriori.

The hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP) is a stacked DP, where the base distribution for the document-level DP is a sample distribution drawn from the corpus-level DP (Teh et al., 2006). Specifically, each document is associated with a distribution over topics drawn from the document-level DP: $G _ { d } { \sim } \mathrm { D P } ( \alpha , G _ { 0 } )$ , where $G _ { d }$ is the document topic distribution that further generates a topic $\pmb { \beta } _ { d n } \sim G _ { d }$ for each word $w _ { d n } \sim \pmb { \beta } _ { d n }$ within the document ??. The corpus topic distribution $\begin{array} { r } { G _ { 0 } = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \infty } \pi _ { k } \delta _ { \widehat { \pmb { \beta } } _ { k ^ { \prime } } } } \end{array}$ \~Dirichlet(??) contains a potentially unlimited number of topics. To summarize, HDP’s main technical improvement is allowing for the modeling of a flexible number of topics and the actual number of topics to be inductively inferred from the posteriors. This improvement helps HDP avoid the topic misrepresentation issue (Wang & Blei, 2011).

## Probabilistic Emerging Topic Detection

Emerging topic detection primarily seeks to capture previously unseen topics with an increasing appearance in the document stream (Atefeh & Khreich, 2015). Specifically, documents arriving in temporal order are first partitioned into time slices, and the topics from documents of neighboring time slices are modeled and compared to identify emerging topics in the subsequent time slice (Atefeh & Khreich, 2015). The design of probabilistic emerging topic detection methods is generally based on three major considerations: the baseline model for modeling topics from documents of each time slice, the inference algorithm for extracting topics for each time slice, and the emergence detection mechanism for comparing topics from neighboring time slices. ## Baseline Model

Existing methods mostly extend parametric LDA and LDA variants (e.g., DTM, online LDA, bursty biterm topic model, TopicSketch) as the baseline model for detecting topics at each time slice. Two categories of extensions have been proposed to accommodate emerging topics. In the first category, the LDAbased topic model with the same number of topics is applied to documents of each time slice, and the resultant topics from neighboring time slices are compared to detect emerging topics having significant changes in topic composition (AlSumait et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2018; Lau et al., 2012). For example, online LDA (AlSumait et al., 2008) adopts an LDA to model documents of each time slice, and for each time slice, builds the prior of topics upon the resultant topics from previous time slices. As the number of topics is fixed for all time slices in such extensions, detected emerging topics are primarily based on the preexisting topics. The second category allows for additional topics between neighboring time slices by extending the LDA model to explicitly accommodate emerging topics (Huang et al., 2017; Soleimani & Miller, 2016; Takahashi et al., 2012; Xie et al., 2016; Yan et al., 2015; Yin et al., 2013). For example, Soleimani and Miller (2016) extended the number of topics from K to K+1 to allow for the emerging topic in incoming documents. Nonetheless, the number of emerging topics in these models has to be prespecified and fixed a priori, which could be difficult in analyzing hacker communities. Nonparametric HDPbased models have been rarely studied, with the exception of Ding and Chen’s (2014) study, which shows promising results of HDP outperforming LDA-based methods due to HDP’s flexibility in accommodating emerging topics.

## Inference Algorithm

The three types of model inference algorithms are the Gibbs sampling-based inference, singular value decomposition (SVD), and expectation maximization (EM). The Gibbs sampling-based inference is the standard algorithm for inferring probabilistic topic models, which involves iteratively sampling from the conditional distribution of all random variables in the model (AlSumait et al., 2008; Ding & Chen, 2014; Lau et al., 2012; Lee & Sohn, 2019; Yan et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2015). The major limitation of the Gibbs sampling-based topic inference is its computational efficiency. Even with the improvements to accommodate document streams (e.g., collapsing word variables into a bag-of-word vector), this type of algorithm still requires over 500 iterations of sampling from the entire set of documents (Huang et al., 2017). To that end, SVD-based algorithms provide a relatively efficient solution by formulating the topic modeling problem as matrix factorization (Xie et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). In particular, the matrix of documents characterized by bag-of-words vectors is viewed as the multiplication between the document-level topic composition matrix and the topic matrix. However, this type of algorithm generally imposes strong assumptions on the data-generating process, limiting the applicability of the method to the hacker community context. For example, Xie et al. (2016) assumed that each document could only be associated with one topic, which is reasonable for microblog data such as tweets, but could be problematic when applied to hacker-generated content. EM algorithms achieve a balance between computational efficiency and generalizability by formulating the probabilistic topic model problem as an optimization problem (Soleimani & Miller, 2016; Yin et al., 2013). For example, Soleimani and Miller (2016) proposed a generalized EM algorithm for optimizing the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to achieve enhanced performance and computational efficiency.

## Emergence Detection

One major challenge in prior emerging topic detection is false positives, which are vague topics caused by noise in social media. Emergence detection filters out false positives by examining the significance of each emerging topic. The first approach for detecting topic emergence uses thresholds on measures of topic emergence. One common measure is the similarity of the same topic between two neighboring time slices (AlSumait et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2017; Lau et al., 2012). In particular, divergence measures, such as Kullback-Leibler divergence and Jensen-Shannon divergence, are employed to evaluate topic similarity and find topics with significant changes exceeding certain thresholds. However, such measures are rarely used to detect new emerging topics that have never appeared before. The second approach draws upon the infinite-state automaton to model topic emergence as a state transition (Kleinberg, 2003). In the infinite-state automaton, the states represent different levels of topic appearance within a time slice and a topic transitioning into a higher appearance state indicates the emergence of the topic (Takahashi et al., 2012; Yin et al., 2013). Similarly, the infinite state automatonbased approach primarily captures the emergence of preexisting topics and has been mostly applied to a retrospective analysis of historical topic emergence. To address this issue, the third approach extends the LDA model to accommodate new topics explicitly, with additional topic variables to capture potentially emerging topics (Yan et al., 2015). Nonetheless, the emerging topics captured by the additional topic variables may be random noise. As such, Soleimani and Miller (2016) proposed conducting a bootstrap-based significance test, which enhances the performance of finding new topics that significantly contribute to explaining incoming documents. While such statistical tests cannot be directly applied to the nonparametric model, these methods have demonstrated promising performance in detecting topic emergence.

## Summary

Our review of the past research has identified four important research gaps. First, emerging topic detection has unrealized potential for improving the discovery of emerging threats from hacker communities to inform cyber risks and facilitate proactive cyber defense. Second, there is a lack of flexibility in modeling the number of emerging topics, which is important for analyzing hacker communities. The modeling flexibility afforded by the nonparametric HDP model can improve the applicability of emerging topic detection in hacker communities. Third, while emerging topic detection necessitates computationally efficient model inference, most inference algorithms are either inherently time-consuming or predicated on restrictive model assumptions. Extending the EM algorithm has the potential to achieve both computational efficiency and emerging topic detection effectiveness in hacker communities. Lastly, the emergence detection mechanism can be further improved to detect previously unseen topics while filtering out random noise. We are motivated to leverage statistical testing for enhancing emergence detection.

## The Nonparametric Emerging Topic Detection Framework

We propose the nonparametric emerging topic detection (NPETD) framework to detect emerging topics in hacker communities. Consistent with existing literature (AlSumait et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2018), the proposed framework assumes that the documents ?? (e.g., posts in hacker forums, listings in darknet marketplaces) in hacker communities arrive in temporal order and are partitioned into time slices, $t \in$ $\{ 1 , \ldots , T \}$ . Within each time slice ??, a set of topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ can be modeled from the newly arrived documents $D _ { t } =$ $\{ d _ { 1 } , \dots , d _ { N _ { t } } \}$ . Our framework primarily seeks to detect emerging topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t } ^ { \ast }$ by comparing the topics between adjacent time slices, $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ and $\pmb { \beta } _ { t - 1 }$

As shown in Figure 1, our framework consists of two major components: the topic modeling component and the emergence detection component. The topic modeling component aims to model topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ underlying the newly arrived documents of each time slice $D _ { t }$ , and the emergence detection component identifies whether there are emerging topics by comparing topics from adjacent time slices $( \mathrm { e } . \mathrm { g } . , \pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ and $\pmb { \beta } _ { t - 1 } )$ . For each time slice, a conditional hierarchical Dirichlet process (conditional HDP) is leveraged to model topics in the newly arrived documents by using the learned models from the previous time slice as a prior. Drawing upon the nonparametric topic model, our proposed framework is capable of inductively extracting a flexible number of existing and emerging topics as documents arrive over time. For each time slice, we couple the topic model inference algorithm with the emergence detection to enhance the computational efficiency of our proposed framework. Specifically, the inference of topics is based on the state-of-the-art EM inference framework, stochastic variational inference. Our framework utilizes the Bayes factor test, a statistical test amenable to fundamental Bayesian nonparametrics’ assumptions of infinite dimensionality, to compare topics of adjacent time slices in order to detect the emergence of topics.

## Topic Modeling: Conditional Hierarchical Dirichlet Process

The topic modeling component primarily seeks to model topics from the newly arrived documents of each time slice to facilitate emerging topic detection between adjacent time slices. As the number of emerging topics in hacker communities is difficult to estimate, we propose leveraging the nonparametric topic model, which can inductively extract a flexible number of topics from documents in hacker communities.

However, the traditional hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP) does not consider the temporal ordering of documents and is therefore unable to compare topics between adjacent time slices. Inspired by AlSumait et al. (2008) and Gao et al. (2018), we extend the traditional HDP to accommodate the temporal ordering of documents and enable the comparison of topics between adjacent time slices.

In particular, we propose a conditional HDP model that inductively extracts a flexible number of topics from documents in each time slice to compare topics over time. From the documents in a given time slice, a conditional HDP is used to learn topics. The learned topics of each time slice are used as a prior for conditional HDP at the successive time slice when new documents are available. Specifically, while the traditional HDP draws its corpus-level topic distribution $G _ { 0 }$ from a Dirichlet process (DP) whose base distribution is a symmetric Dirichlet distribution Dir(??), the conditional HDP draws its overall topic distribution $G _ { t }$ for time slice ?? from a Dirichlet process (DP) whose base distribution is the overall topic distribution $G _ { t - 1 }$ from the previous time slice ?? − 1. Conditioning $G _ { t }$ on $G _ { t - 1 }$ enables existing topics learned from the previous time slice to be carried over to the successive time slice; therefore, emerging topics can be identified by comparing topics from adjacent time slices. The posterior model $\mathrm { c H D P } _ { t }$ captures all topics in documents $D _ { t }$ of time slice ?? and is then compared with the posterior model $\mathrm { c H D P } _ { t - 1 }$ using topics learned from documents $D _ { t - 1 }$ of the previous time slice $t - 1$ . If the newly arrived documents $D _ { t }$ do not have emerging topics, cHDP will have the same topics as $\mathrm { c H D P } _ { t - 1 } .$ , because ${ \mathrm { c H D P } } _ { t - 1 }$ already has all the necessary topics to model the newly arrived documents. If the newly arrived documents contain emerging topics, the posterior model cHDP will learn a flexible number of additional topics from newly arrived documents. As such, the emergence detection component is designed to compare cHDP and ${ \mathrm { c H D P } } _ { t - 1 }$ for detecting the emerging topics of time slice ??.

## Emergence Detection

Similar to traditional social media, the hacker community is also filled with various forms of noise that could cause falsepositive topics. The emergence detection component seeks to compare topics from neighboring time slices and identify significant emerging topics using statistical testing. Statistical tests used in past research are unsuitable for nonparametric models because these tests are not amenable to the infinite dimensionality of the conditional HDP model. Hence, the Bayes factor test is leveraged for evaluating the significance of candidate emerging topics. The Bayes factor test is a Bayesian hypothesis test that compares the likelihood probability of two competing models to examine whether one model has significantly increased predictability over the other (Gelman et al., 2013). In particular, the Bayes factor test compares cHDP against ${ \mathrm { c H D P } } _ { t - 1 }$ to examine whether including candidate emerging topics significantly increases the likelihood probability of modeling newly arrived documents $\mathrm { D } _ { t }$ . If $\mathrm { c H D P } _ { t }$ achieves a significantly higher likelihood probability than c $\mathrm { H D P } _ { t - 1 } ,$ , the candidate emerging topics are confirmed to have statistical significance; otherwise, the candidate topics are considered to be random noise. Specifically, Bayes factor test statistic ?? is defined as,

$$
K = \exp (\ln p (D _ {t} | c H D P _ {t}) - \ln p (D _ {t} | c H D P _ {t - 1}))
$$

The critical value of the Bayes factor test statistic is 3 (Kass & Raftery, 1995): when $K > 3$ , the cHDP model has a significant fit, suggesting that the candidate topics are true positives. A major computational challenge in conducting Bayes factor tests is calculating the likelihood probability log $p ( \mathrm { D } _ { t } | \mathrm { c H D P } )$ , which requires integrating over all latent topics and topic distributions:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln p (\mathrm{D} _ {t} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t}) \\ = \ln \iiint p (\mathrm{D} _ {t} | G _ {t}, \boldsymbol {G} _ {t, d}, \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {t}, \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t}) p (G _ {t} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t}) \\ \times p (\boldsymbol {G} _ {t, d} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t}) p (\boldsymbol {\beta} _ {t} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t})   d G _ {0} d \boldsymbol {G} _ {t, d} d \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {t} \end{array}
$$

This integration is intractable due to the high dimensionality of the overall topic distribution of time slice $G _ { t } ,$ topic distributions of documents $\pmb { G } _ { t , d } = \{ G _ { t , d } \}$ , and topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ . To address this challenge, we leverage the stochastic variational inference (SVI) framework (Hoffman et al., 2013), the state-of-the-art online model inference framework. Specifically, we derive the evidence lower bound (ELBO) to approximate the intractable likelihood probability, which is the tractable lower bound on the logarithm of the intractable likelihood probability:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\mathcal {L} _ {q} (\mathrm{D} _ {t} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t}) \triangleq \mathbb {E} _ {q} \big [ \ln p \big (\mathrm{D} _ {t}, G _ {t}, \boldsymbol {G} _ {t, d}, \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {t} \big | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t} \big) \big ]} \\ & {\qquad - \mathbb {E} _ {q} \big [ \ln q \big (G _ {t}, \boldsymbol {G} _ {t, d}, \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {t} \big | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t} \big) \big ]} \\ & {\qquad \approx \ln p (\mathrm{D} _ {t} | \mathrm{cHDP} _ {t})} \end{array}
$$

where $q \big ( G _ { t } , \pmb { G } _ { t , d } , \pmb { \beta } _ { t } \big | \mathrm { c H D P } _ { t } \big )$ is the variational distribution of latent variables that approximates the posterior distribution, and $\mathbb { E } _ { q } \mathrm { i s }$ the expectation under the variational distribution. Approximating the likelihood probability using ELBO has at least two major strengths compared to other alternatives. First, ELBO allows for computationally efficient optimization that contributes to the scalability of analyzing document streams. Second, the resulting variational distribution $q \big ( G _ { t } , \pmb { G } _ { t , d } , \pmb { \beta } _ { t } \big | \mathrm { c H D P } _ { t } \big )$ can provide statistical properties of latent variables, such as the topic distribution of time slice $G _ { t } ,$ document topic distribution $\pmb { G } _ { t , d } ,$ and topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ , informing the interpretation of emerging topics (Hoffman et al., 2013).

As summarized in Figure 2, we instantiate the SVI framework to develop an efficient algorithm for maximizing $\mathcal { L } _ { q } ( \mathrm { D } _ { t } | \mathrm { c H D P } _ { t } )$ . Traditional variational inference maximizes $\mathcal { L } _ { q } ( \mathrm { D } _ { t } | \mathrm { c H D P } _ { t } )$ using an iterative expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm in which local variables of the entire set of newly arrived documents are estimated (E step) before global variables are optimized (M step). Following SVI, our algorithm iterates over a minibatch of a random subsample from the newly arrived documents $\mathrm { D } _ { t }$ and can therefore expedite convergence (Hoffman et al., 2013). Specifically, the E step in our algorithm performs coordinate ascent on local variables such as topic distribution $G _ { t , d }$ and word topics $\pmb { \beta } _ { t }$ for each document in the minibatch of the random subsample. The M step first generalizes the estimations for newly arrived documents $D _ { t } ,$ , and then performs natural gradient ascent on the overall topic distribution of time slice $G _ { t }$ to maximize $\mathcal { L } _ { q } ( \mathrm { D } _ { t } | \mathrm { c H D P } _ { t } )$ . Replacing a full iterative scan over the entire set of newly arrived documents with a scan over a random subsample, our SVI-based algorithm reduces the computational cost of each E-M iteration to enable online learning.

## Evaluation

Consistent with the design science paradigm (Hevner et al., 2004), we rigorously evaluated the effectiveness, efficiency, and utility of our proposed framework in the context of hacker communities. Specifically, we first conducted experiments to compare the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed framework against the state-of-the-art baseline methods in detecting emerging threats from darknet marketplace listings. Moreover, we demonstrate the practical utility of the proposed framework by detecting emerging topics from a major hacker forum.

## Experiment Design

Following prior literature (Soleimani & Miller, 2016), we designed an experiment to examine the effectiveness and efficiency of our framework in the emerging topic detection task on the darknet marketplace (DNM) testbed. In particular, the experiment primarily evaluated our framework in detecting DNM listings with emerging topics, in comparison with the state-of-the-art baseline methods. All methods were evaluated in two neighboring time slices. In the first time slice, each method learned existing topics from a set of training listings, and in the second time slice, each method detected listings with emerging topics from a set of unseen testing listings.

However, a DNM benchmark testbed with the ground truth knowledge of emerging topics is lacking. In alignment with prior literature (AlSumait et al., 2008; Soleimani & Miller, 2016), we built a testbed based on the listings collected from the Alphabay DNM for emulating the emergence of listings with previously unseen topics. Alphabay was selected because the marketplace was the largest DNM that grouped listings based on their categories, which could be leveraged for developing the ground truth knowledge of emerging topics. Specifically, six categories of listings related to hacking products and services were selected as positive listings with emerging topics, including exploits, hosting services, botnet and malware, socks, VPN, and security software. To build the testbed, we first collected all listings from Alphabay between 2014 and 2016 and preprocessed these listings by concatenating the title and the listing description, applying tokenization and stemming, removing stop words, and filtering listings with short descriptions. Our preprocessing resulted in 6,777 listings with 163 positive listings and 6,614 negative listings from nonhacking-related categories (e.g., weapons and illicit drugs). We randomly selected 4,280 (about 60%) negative listings into the training set of the first time slice, and the remaining negative listings and all positive listings into the testing set of the second time slice. The proportion of positive listings in the testing set reflects the actual composition of emerging topics in hacker communities.

Our baseline methods comprised state-of-the-art emerging topic detection methods, including adaptive online LDA (AOLDA) (Gao et al., 2018), anomalous topic discovery (ATD) (Soleimani & Miller, 2016), TopicSketch (Xie et al., 2016), and the bursty biterm topic model (BBTM) (Yan et al., 2015). Specifically, we implemented stochastic variational inference for BBTM and AOLDA to speed up the execution time of these baseline methods on our testbed. Consistent with prior literature, we quantified effectiveness using precision, recall, and F-measure on detected listings with emerging topics. Moreover, we gauged the quality of detected emerging topics using topic coherence. For each topic, topic coherence calculates the co-occurrences of the most probable words in a benchmark corpus. We selected the standard UMass topic coherence (Mimno et al., 2011) because this intrinsic evaluation measure primarily uses the given corpus as the benchmark instead of an external corpus (e.g., Wikipedia), which is amenable to our exploratory context. The UMass topic coherence is a scalar metric, and a higher value suggests higher topic quality. Furthermore, we used processer time to empirically evaluate the computational efficiency. Processor time, also known as CPU time, gauges the time a CPU spent on processing the task (Hoffman et al., 2010; Wang & Blei, 2011). All experiments were conducted using a 16-core 3.0GHz processor paired with 32G RAM.

## Experiment Results

Table 2 summarizes the macro-average performance of NPETD and baseline methods in detecting listings with emerging topics. On a high level, our proposed framework (NPETD) outperformed baseline methods on recall, F-measure, and topic coherence, while achieving the second-highest precision. In particular, NPETD outperformed the best baseline method (ATD) by 0.022 on precision, 0.109 on recall, 0.087 on F-measure, and 10.69 on topic coherence. Among baseline methods, ATD achieved the best recall and F-measure, followed by BBTM, which achieved the best topic coherence. The performance difference between ATD and AOLDA could be attributed to their modeling difference in treating emerging topics. While AOLDA mainly tracked topic changes over time, ATD used an additional topic to capture the emerging topics. On our testbed, the emerging topic of hacking assets was significantly different from other preexisting topics; therefore, using a dedicated topic to capture the hacking asset semantics turned out to be more effective than updating preexisting topics. Moreover, the performance improvement of NPETD was likely because the listings of hacking assets might contain more than one topic. By using nonparametric modeling principles, NPETD could leverage a flexible number of topics to capture emerging hacking topics. As reflected in the topic coherence, this flexibility resulted in enhanced quality of the detected emerging topics. Overall, the macro-average results confirm that the nonparametric topic modeling component in our framework can effectively accommodate emerging topics in DNM listings.

Figure 3 illustrates the plot of the F-measure against processor time for each listing category, where the horizontal axis represents the processor time in seconds on a log scale and the vertical axis indicates the F-measure. The plus sign in each panel represents our proposed NPETD framework, whereas the other signs denote baseline methods. Overall, our proposed framework achieved the best performance in detecting listings of exploits, hosting services, socks, and VPNs, and outperformed most baseline methods in computational efficiency. In particular, the F-measures of NPETD on listings of exploits, hosting services, botnet and malware, socks, VPN, and security software were 0.909, 0.9, 0.918, 0.824, 1, and 0.809, respectively. While ATD outperformed NPETD on listings of botnets and malware and security software, this baseline method had the lowest F-measure of 0.4 on hosting services listings. In comparison with baseline methods, NPETD consistently achieved an over 0.8 F-measure across various listing categories. Moreover, NPETD was much faster than the best-performing baseline method (i.e., ATD) in detecting listings of botnets and malware (51 seconds vs. 24 minutes) and security software (23 seconds vs. 8 minutes). In other listing categories, NPETD demonstrated better computational efficiency than most baseline methods. Taken together, NPETD showed consistent and competitive effectiveness and computational efficiency among the state-of-the-art emerging topic detection methods.

Detecting emerging topics in the forum has significant cybersecurity implications as the discovery of new carding trends (e.g., new carder, new batches of breached accounts, new carding techniques) could help law enforcement and security professionals better understand the landscape of carding crime and strategize countermeasures.

## Conclusion

The abundance of hacker community content has presented opportunities for developing threat intelligence to inform cyber risk management and facilitate proactive cyber defense. In this study, we develop a novel nonparametric emerging topic detection framework (NPETD) to detect emerging topics in hacker communities. The proposed framework is intended for exploring streams of hacker-generated content to identify emerging topics that could suggest new cyber risks for organizations. Our framework incorporates the state-of-the-art nonparametric topic model to inductively extract topics and accommodate emerging topics in hacker communities. To test the efficacy of our framework, we conducted evaluation experiments on a darknet marketplace testbed to compare our NPETD method against the state-of-the-art baseline methods. Our evaluation results suggest that the proposed framework outperformed the baseline methods in detecting listings containing emerging hacking-related topics and maintained consistent performance across various listing categories. Moreover, in comparison with baseline methods, our framework achieved better computational efficiency without compromising the effectiveness of emerging topic detection.

Our research makes several notable contributions. Our first contribution is the design, development, and evaluation of our emerging topic detection framework. This framework can help to develop threat intelligence with enhanced emerging topic detection capabilities that sift through hacker communities for new topics potentially related to vulnerabilities, attack vectors, and potential targets. Our second contribution is our practical implications for the IS cybersecurity research community. In response to the call for “black hat research” in Mahmood et al. (2010), we built our research design for application in hacker communities and were able to identify emerging threat topics by analyzing hacker-generated content. Our research not only confirms the urgent need for such research, but also demonstrates its practical value to stakeholder groups including organizations, threat analysts, and law enforcement.

Moreover, since topic modeling is extensively applied in various IS contexts, our emerging topic detection framework could be extended to other contexts where the number of topics cannot be readily estimated and emerging topics need to be detected promptly. For example, new technologies and designs could manifest through patent documents as emerging topics, the identification of which could benefit organizations investment decisions on research and development (Tiwana, 2014). The increasing mention of certain symptoms in social media feeds could present as several emerging topics. The detection of such emerging topics might help predict epidemic outbreaks (Brynjolfsson et al., 2016). As the proliferation of big data creates opportunities for organizations to gain business insights and foresight (Chen et al., 2012), our proposed framework has the potential to empower organizations with exploratory analytics that detect topics related to new ideas, opportunities, and threats to enhance organizations’ strategic foresight and improve organizational agility. In addition to their practical significance, emerging topics are also important for research that takes a temporal perspective to examine dynamic phenomena with underlying temporal shifts in constructs and concepts of interest in an attempt to understand the processes that lead to their emergence, as well as the antecedent causes and their consequences (Berente et al., 2019).

Hsinchun Chen is Regents Professor and Thomas R. Brown Chair in Management and Technology in the Management Information Systems Department at the Eller College of Management, University of Arizona. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from New York University. He is the author/editor of 20 books, 300 SCI journal articles, and 200 refereed conference articles covering digital library, data/text/web mining, business analytics, security informatics, and health informatics. He founded the Artificial Intelligence Lab at The University of Arizona in 1989, which has received \$50M+ in research funding from the National Science Foundation, National Institutes of Health, National Library of Medicine, Department of Defense, Department of Justice, Central Intelligence Agency, Department of Homeland Security, and other agencies (100+ grants, 50+ from NSF). He is a fellow of ACM, IEEE, and AAAS.
