# Talk Before It’s Too Late: Reconsidering the Role of Conversation in Information Systems Project Management

Ste fano Mas tro gia com , Step hanie Misone r, And Ri cca rdo Bona zzi

Abs tract: Effective team coordination is essential for the information systems (IS) projects’ success. We present a four-year study, based on design science research, to develop and instantiate a conceptual model—called Coopilot—to improve real-time coordination in IS projects. Coopilot is a simple conversational guide to help IS project managers minimize the number of coordination surprises that arise for teams during their project meetings. Drawing on coordination literature outside the IS research field, we have adapted and instantiated the theory of joint activity developed by psycholinguist Herbert Clark. The results illustrate the value Clark’s theory can add to the IS field and both the importance of conversation intended as a new theoretical construct in IS team project coordination as well as the importance of reaching a sufficient level of understanding. Project managers involved in this study who used Coopilot reported both higher levels of confidence that their projects were on a successful path and overall higher levels of team motivation.

Key words and phras es : conversation for coordination, IS project management, IS teams, project management, team coordination.

## Introduction and Motivation

Bob is perplexed.

As an information systems project manager at a firm called ACME, his assignment is to create a new intranet site for the company. He has nine months to get the site up and running. The project is fully aligned with the company’s business strategy, and the outline already has been clearly communicated. Despite this apparently favorable backdrop, some doubts arise in his mind as the result of two brief conversations that took place earlier in the day.

At 9:00 a.m ., Anne, the project’s coordinator for a participating business unit, called Bob:

Anne: “This intranet project just means creating an internal Google-like system.”

Bob: “Well, it will be more than a search engine; it will be a revamped version of our current system—which is called Group Information—with all of its data.” He then offered a long explanation.

At 12:00 p.m . Bob saw Cliff, a systems engineer, rushing around fixing technical problems. Cliff presented a hurried explanation of a problem he had encountered.

Cliff: “I know I promised you a server workload analysis for last week, but I’ve gotta run—the server NWSR110002 just crashed.” Bob barely had time to remind Cliff of the increasing urgency of the analysis that Cliff had promised.

What puzzles Bob is that earlier he had been able to rally a majority of participants during the kickoff meeting, at which he had given a two-hour Power-Point presentation, explaining the intranet project in every detail. Everyone had been given a copy of the project charter that Bob had drawn up carefully, in accordance with PMI [Project Management Institute] methodology.

Now, Bob is wondering if he should move on to the next phase and prepare the detailed planning as he had imagined, or instead whether he should attempt something else, taking into account the brief, informal conversational feedback from earlier in the day.

Lik e any inform ation s ys tems (IS) project m anager (PM), Bob is experiencing coordination surprises [32], events that do not make sense in terms of one’s own beliefs. Effective coordination among project team members is recognized as a central issue, specifically in IS projects [2, 18, 36, 48, 50], and presents numerous challenges [2, 49, 54, 61]. Research has revealed that effective coordination increases project performance [28, 54], and that failures of IS projects often result in part from problems with coordination [18, 36]. Despite these facts, coordination has not been the subject of extensive study in the IS literature [50]. With that in mind, we can legitimately ask how we can help IS PMs such as Bob cope with coordination surprises and take appropriate actions.

When dealing with coordination issues, the first instinct of researchers in the IS field is to turn to Malone and Crowston’s [40, 42] coordination theory, a powerful approach to addressing coordination problems from an activity perspective. Using coordination theory, Bob cannot be sure that a detailed analysis of activities and their dependencies will really help him understand why Anne asked questions about matters that she was supposed to know, because coordination theory does not take full account of the cognitive aspects of situations.

In search of an answer for Bob, we looked for coordination approaches that would encompass both cognition-related and activity-related aspects of coordination. This led us to explore approaches of coordination that are unusual for the IS field, examining the work of Herbert Clark [12] in psycholinguistics. Clark described how people use language to coordinate joint activities (i.e., activities carried out by an ensemble of people acting in coordination with each other). Clark’s approach specifies the cognitive conditions necessary for effective coordination and the linguistic acts by which coordination is accomplished. Klein et al. seem to share Clark’s ideas: “Though previous accounts of team coordination (e.g., [31, 42, 70]) have identified features of effective coordination, Clark’s description of joint activity during conversations seems to provide a much stronger basis for understanding team coordination” [32, p. 143]. Therefore, we begin our Clarkian journey by considering that an IS project can be viewed as a series of joint activities in which coordination plays a crucial role and “joint performance depends mostly on participants’ coordination abilities, as cognitive work is distributed among different team members” [32, p. 139]. In Clark’s terminology, Bob and the other project participants need to establish and maintain a sufficient level of common ground: the set of knowledge, beliefs, and suppositions people believe they share [12, p. 93]. Bob’s situation shed light that something in their common ground is broken, and our proposal is that he must find some means of reconciling team members’ understandings if he does not want the future outcomes to be compromised by these misunderstandings. Unfortunately, Clark’s theory does not offer a set of prescriptions to help Bob, and it has never been applied in IS research.

With this in mind, and taking into account the apparent lack of practitioners’ interest in IS project management research [6, 56], our aim is to extend Clark’s theory to IS research and instantiate it into an artifact for PMs. More precisely, we aim to equip Bob (or any PM) with a simple tool that is compliant with Clark’s linguistic concepts [12] and that is designed to improve team coordination in real time (i.e., during project meetings), so as to facilitate early detection and repair of coordination problems. This will prevent them from growing and avoid jeopardy to the entire project that could result from the progressive accumulation of coordination surprises.

Hence, this study is motivated by the following research question:

RQ: How can we adapt Clark’s theory to help PMs reduce, in real time, coordination surprises so as to affect positively the course of a project?

In responding to this question, our study is anchored in design science research [29], so as to make Clark’s approach accessible to IS project management. Design science seeks to provide relevant solutions to management problems [25]. Consequently, the current paper describes the development of a new conceptual model to improve realtime team coordination in IS projects, and it provides specifications of an artifact, a conversational guide we named “Coopilot” (COOrdination PILOT) that can assist PMs as they address coordination problems in real time during meetings.

We build on prior research regarding coordination in IS project teams and show the ways in which our research must step beyond what already is known (second section). We thus turn to Clark’s [12] joint activity theory (third section). We describe the research method and explain how we developed a prescriptive model (fourth section). We provide background for our adaptation of Clark’s theory, along with its instantiation (fifth section) and evaluation (sixth section) in three main projects within three organizations from various sectors. Our findings demonstrate the usefulness of the artifact to PMs, helping them to be more proactive in the resolution of daily coordination problems (seventh section). Before concluding, we identify the main contributions of our research to the literature and to practice (eighth section).

Foreshadowing those contributions, we first propose a new design theory for IS research following the guidelines of Gregor and Jones [25]. Second, the success of the artifact designed on the basis of Clark’s theory demonstrates the potential of this theory and the value it can add to the IS field. As applied here, the theory stresses both the importance of conversation, intended as a new theoretical construct in IS team project coordination, and the importance of reaching a sufficient level of understanding. Consequently, our research offers new theoretical insights for IS researchers by (1) deeply investigating the role of conversation and the role of evidence of understanding in real-time coordination and (2) providing a tool to guide conversations so as to improve real-time coordination.

## Literature Review: Team Coordination in IS Projects

Res earch into organizational s cience [21, 46, 66] in general and IS projects in particular [2, 18, 36, 39, 48, 49, 50] has stressed the importance of coordination when undertaking complex activities. Such coordination is quite challenging when project team members need to collaborate on common activities about which there is significant uncertainty, for which there are extreme time constraints, and concerning which capabilities and culture are not necessarily shared [7, 9, 11, 24, 61, 62].

A majority of these studies has been carried out on the impact of coordination on IS project success [2, 10, 36, 48, 50]. Most of these studies are based on the classical perspective of coordination taken from organization literature, such as coordination theory [41, 42], the information processing model [22], and the fit and contingency model [38, 64]. Such research has posited that task interdependency and uncertainty determine the use of modes of coordination. It has revealed that predefined mecha nisms, such as a “mechanistic” coordination strategy that uses “task programming mechanisms” and an “organic” coordination strategy that uses team communication, can be designed to meet various contingencies. For instance, in the context of software project management, Nidumolu focused on the “important practical problem of managing software projects effectively” [48, p. 192]. His study revealed that even though vertical coordination mechanisms (coordination through a strong PM) and horizontal coordination mechanisms (coordination through meetings and one-on-one contact) play different roles in the IS development process, higher levels of both vertical and horizontal coordination can lead to higher levels of overall project performance. Similarly, Chen et al. [10] carried out a survey of 323 IS professionals, and found that both horizontal and vertical coordination have a direct, positive effect on final project performance.

Leaving aside the cognitive aspect of coordination situations, various studies have completed and enriched this view of “explicit” coordination mechanisms (used purposely to coordinate) by determining that in addition to explicit components, implicit components of coordination (i.e., those used without a conscious effort to coordinate) are necessary for a project to succeed [8, 19, 45]. Recent research in team cognition has revealed some mechanisms for implicit coordination; these broaden our understanding of how team members coordinate their contributions in the midst of interaction [8, 19, 54, 67]. Such implicit coordination has been referred to as the “synchronization of member actions based on unspoken assumptions about what others in the group are likely to do” [69, p. 3]. To manage their dependencies more effectively, members use cognitive mechanisms [17, 18, 34] such as shared knowledge [1, 8, 47], team members’ common ground for a given project (what participants know they share about the project), and team awareness [16] of who is available and who previously has done what, where, and for whom. Shared knowledge about the team or the task helps team members anticipate what others are likely to do and what the task requires from them [8, 33], thus helping them coordinate implicitly. In addition, when team members know that they share knowledge, their communication is more effective because they have more common ground [14, 35]. Espinosa et al. [20] and Strode et al. [62] stated that implicit coordination encompasses five components: “know why,” “know what is going on and when,” “know what to do and when,” “know who is doing what,” and “know who knows what.” For example, “know why” is based on each individual working on the project having both an understanding of the overall project goal and an understanding of how a task contributes to that overall goal.

Consequently, the integration of both explicit and implicit mechanisms as well as both activity-related and cognition-related aspects of coordination is essential to our purpose insofar as we aim to help PMs better detect and manage coordination problems in real time. Nevertheless, although prior research has established that effective coordination implies that team members know they share knowledge, several questions remain unanswered in that regard. For instance, how can team members know that they share knowledge sufficiently enough to minimize false predictions and coordination surprises? How do we ascertain that team members understand their roles and the project goals? And our favorite question: How do we know who knows what? In order to respond to these questions, and thus improve real-time coordination, it appears necessary to go further by considering how the cooperative work is carried out and by paying closer attention to coordination problems [63]. Our main assumption is that conversation is the main mediating mechanism between individuals, specifically in terms of how they understand each other’s intentions as the project unfolds. Previous studies in organization science [37, 38, 66] have recognized conversation as an essential factor in team performance, but to our knowledge, there are no studies in the IS field that have deeply investigated the roles of conversation and of evidence of understanding in real-time coordination. From this statement, we moved toward another perspective, one that exists outside the field of IS. That perspective comes from the work of Clark [12], and we used it as our theoretical foundation.

## Our Theoretical Foundation: Clark’s Joint Activity Model

The rel evance of Cl ark ’s [12] joint activity theory, which crosses boundaries of disciplines, has been noted by several researchers,<sup>1</sup> including Bechky [5] in organization studies, Dillenbourg and Traum [15] in computer-supported collaborative learning, and Klein et al. [32] in psychology. According to Clark, coordination is concerned with what team members do when they solve coordination problems, mainly using language. Two people have a coordination problem whenever they have common interests or goals, and each person’s actions depend on the actions of the other. The issue is interpredictability: participants must infer what actions they can expect from each other in their joint effort to reach the common goal. To solve coordination problems, people need to establish and maintain a sufficient level of common ground: the set of knowledge, beliefs, and suppositions that people believe they share [12, p. 93]. The concept of common ground is different from “shared knowledge” or “common understanding” insofar as it includes the recursive notion that participants are aware that they share any knowledge they have [68].

All too often, as Klein et al. [32] pointed out, people discover a serious loss of common ground. In other words, they experience coordination surprises. Such surprises occur when something happens that does not make sense in terms of their beliefs. For example, Bob is surprised by Anne’s and Cliff’s remarks because both were present at the project kickoff meeting, where Bob supposed they received all the relevant information. Another example is a project team member who is supposed to be responsible for carrying out a specific task but who claims not to be aware of that responsibility. Coordination surprises in projects largely originate in what Klein et al. [32] described as the fundamental common ground breakdown (i.e., when there is confusion about who knows what). At such times, “team members can lose their calibration without knowing it, and continue to interact until they run into a coordination surprise” [32, p. 171]. This concept appears very useful in the context of IS projects insofar as there is a constant need for teams to update their common ground. For example, project information spaces constantly evolve under the pressures of changing requirements, shifting roles, the need to integrate new constraints or new participants, or the emergence of new risks. If a team’s common ground is not sufficiently updated with this new information, participants start making decisions based on obsolete information, thus triggering a domino effect that leads to coordination surprises and potential drift in terms of scope, time, quality, and cost.

To reduce the number of coordination surprises, some proactive action should be taken by PMs to keep their team’s common ground fully operational during project meetings. One way to tackle this challenge is to help PMs “artificially” raise the level of evidence of understanding among team members, what Clark and Schaefer [13] call the grounding criterion (i.e., when participants reach the mutual belief that they understand each other sufficiently for current purposes). “Artificially” means that it is not sufficient for participants to nod, or say “okay” or “yes,” when asked if they understand what is being said in the course of a project meeting. Instead, if there is any doubt about participants’ understanding, PMs will ask, in real time, specific questions intended to repair potential discrepancies in common ground. In other words, we decided to design a conversational guide in terms of questions and answers of this type:

PM: “I request that you now provide evidence of your understanding of x.”

Team members: “We hereby provide evidence of our understanding of x.”

The question remains of what x should contain, at a minimum, to maintain the common ground of a team running a project in an operational state. For that purpose, we used Clark’s definitional requirements for joint projects. Clark [12, p. 191] defines a joint project as a joint action projected by one of its participants and taken up by others. Clark continues by pointing out that joint projects require joint purposes.

For A and B to commit themselves to joint purpose r, it is necessary to satisfy four conditions:

1. Identification: A and B must identify r.

2. Ability: It must be possible for A and B to play their part in fulfilling r.

3. Willingness: A and B must be willing to play their part in fulfilling r.

4. Mutual belief: A and B must each believe that 1, 2, 3, and 4 are part of their common ground.

This definition is a cornerstone of our research. For Clark, a joint project is the equivalent of an interaction for most of us, whereby A proposes something to B, and then B responds positively. In IS projects, considerable division of labor occurs in the form of Clarkian joint projects. In our research, we hypothesized that IS project failure could be an expression of defective division of labor at this joint project level, where participants implicitly believe that basic requirements are met when in fact they are not. These concepts, quite unfamiliar for an IS project management audience, are the foundations we used to design our tool.

## Research Method

In this s tudy, we wis h to devel op a pres criptive m odel ; therefore, we have chosen to perform design science research [26, 27, 43, 59]. Design science seeks to obtain new theories through the design of artifacts [43, 59] that serve human purposes. It also seeks to provide relevant solutions to management problems [25]. Different approaches and methodologies [27, 29, 51, 58] have been proposed in design science. We decided to align our work with the process put forward by Holmström et al. [29] because it covers the entire spectrum, from the design research problem through to iterations and final theory development. Moreover, although we acknowledge that design science has experienced significant traction in the field of IS since the publication of Hevner et al. [27], we found the model of Holmström et al. [29] slightly more appropriate for an audience that covers a wider spectrum of disciplines within management studies. The process proposed by Holmström et al. [29] starts with the development of new ideas (solution incubation), then moves on to tested ideas (solution refinement), mid-range theory (explanation I), and formal theory (explanation II). Considerations about the development of a formal theory will be presented at the end of this paper, as part of the discussion for future research. Finally, evaluation is a crucial element in design science [27]; thus, we have also used strategies for design science research evaluation developed by Pries-Heje et al. [52]. For each phase of the process, an explanation is given as to what was evaluated, how it was evaluated, and when.

## Phase 1: Solution Incubation

The present research originated from the experiences of one of the authors, who has worked as PM in various IS projects in the banking and finance industry over the past 15 years.

## Objectives

Having experienced the role of PM in some projects and that of participant in others, one of the authors perceived that many agreements were formed on the basis of ambiguous objectives and abstract vocabulary. Consequently, he made inquiries as to the “impact” of these ambiguities on the overall course of the project.

## Means

During the researcher’s investigations, he identified this as a coordination problem that needed to be addressed during project meetings. He elaborated a solution design using abductive reasoning [29] by combining the strong theory of Clark [12], with its own project management expertise, to obtain a set of questions to ask to project participants.

## Knowledge Interest and Research Approach

The ability to combine these insights is closely related to the researcher’s professional skills in project management and his background as a researcher in coordination theories.

## Evaluation

In 2009, the alpha prototype was evaluated with a naturalistic evaluation [52] in two organizations, after the development of the initial version (ex post). Ten PMs in the first organization and eight managers in the second organization used the alpha prototype to solve everyday project management problems (coordination during interactions) with real users (400 users). The performance indicator was frequency of utilization, intended as a proxy for users’ acceptance of the artifact, which was on average once a week. In our case, the technical specifications obtained by the first iterations concerned (1) the managers’ need for model simplicity and (2) the managers’ need for a prescriptive model.

## Phase 2: Solution Refinement

## Objectives

According to Holmström et al. [29], solution refinement consists of the refinement of the initial solution design—it solves the problem. The rudimentary solution design is subjected to empirical testing.

## Means

In December 2011, two university researchers joined the project and introduced the notion of real time and ease of use. The resulting beta prototype derived from Clark’s theory was a set of four questions, visualized on a paper card. Finally, a methodology to use the beta prototype was formalized and presented in a set of slides.

## Knowledge Interest and Research Approach

In the second stage, the research approach was still pragmatic and practice-oriented, whereas the process of development of the artifact was more structured than in the previous phase.

## Evaluation

The second evaluation took place after development of the beta prototype and it was an artificial evaluation, in the sense that the presentation of the problem and the creation of a solution were not based on a real-time situation. Indeed, the beta prototype was presented to a community of project management professionals during a project management conference in Switzerland (April 11–12, 2012). The second evaluation resulted in positive feedback with respect to managers’ need for model simplicity and managers’ need for a prescriptive model, whereas the causal effect among constructs was partially achieved.

## Phase 3: Explanation I—Substantive Theory

## Objectives

This phase establishes the theoretical relevance of the solution design [29]. In this phase, the researcher seeks theoretical justification as well as demonstration of theoretical utility.

## Means

In May 2012, we refined our model by integrating the IS project management literature on project success and failure. Furthermore, we introduced a new dependent construct (a project’s potential for success). Soon after the presentation in April 2012, we were contacted by PMs who attended the presentation who were willing to implement the beta prototype in their own projects. The selection of candidates was driven by their willingness to cooperate and to make information available to the researchers.

## Knowledge Interest and Research Approach

In the third stage of the study, we aimed at developing a mid-range theory. During the third iteration, we followed the guidelines of Gregor and Jones [25] to develop a design theory. Table 1 presents how we followed each guideline.

## Evaluation

Two organizations joined the study with projects in IS, whereas the third one joined with a new product development project (see Table 2). We used this new product development project as a control case to verify whether or not our model was limited to IS projects. Data were collected in three steps (from May 2012 to September 2012). First, an initial interview was carried out to obtain understanding of the context of the project and present our coordination support model. The length of each semistructured interview ranged from 120 to 180 minutes. Second, a contact was made every two weeks (lasting, on average, 30 minutes) using semistructured interviews to provide understanding of the use of the model, its eventual usefulness for detecting a problem without knowing the exact nature of the problem, its eventual usefulness for repairing any problems detected, the overall feeling of the project, and potential improvements to be made to the model. Third, a closing interview (lasting, on average, 120 minutes) was carried out with each set of participants to confirm their comments on these three points and ask them if they wanted to stop using the model or continue. All of the interviews and meetings were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed.

## Adaptation of Clark’s Theory and Instantiation

In this s ection, we des crib e how we adapted Cl ark ’s theory for use by PMs in a conceptual model we have named Coopilot. Later, we explain how we turned that into an instantiation for the purpose of our experiment.

## Adaptation of Clark’s Concepts for Project Managers: The Conceptual Model

To define what types of content PMs should monitor and update during project meetings, we started with Clark’s four requirements of joint purposes: identification, ability, willingness, and mutual belief. The results of the translation and adaptation of these concepts for PMs are the four variables that constitute our conceptual model (see Table 3).

At the level of an entire IS project, we interpreted requirements 1 to 3—identification, ability, and willingness—as four variables that had to be constantly kept up to date in team members’ common ground. These are joint objectives, joint resources, joint risks, and joint commitments. These variables represent four different families of knowledge, presuppositions, and beliefs that participants need to share to act jointly in their IS project. We assumed that PMs had to continuously monitor these variables, from project inception to project termination, if they wanted their participants to act as a team and make their individual contributions harmoniously converge into a joint outcome through proper and consistent division of labor. The fourth requirement, mutual belief, was interpreted as the setting in which these four variables had to be monitored: project meetings.

Joint objectives describe the amount of common ground (CG ) shared by the project’s participants about what they are supposed to achieve together, joint resources are the amount of CG about the resources available to that end, and joint risks are the obstacles that could prevent participants from playing their part. Finally, joint commitment describes how much is shared about what to expect from whom. For reasons of both choice and pragmatism, we assumed that participants’ willingness was reflected in their commitments and decided not to overtheorize about the relationship between willingness and commitment. We know that this area requires further investigation, but it is beyond the scope of this research.

Clark’s fourth requirement for joint purposes, that of mutual belief, was interpreted as the setting in which these four variables must be managed by the PM: project conversations. Indeed, conversation is the basic site for human coordination [12, p. 8], and therefore is the best medium for establishing evidence of mutual understanding. Meetings are more than just simple “talking shops.” It is quite popular nowadays to criticize meetings and complain about “meetingitis,” a term used to describe a situation in which there are so many unnecessary meetings that it is not possible to proceed with work effectively. We claim that the issue here is not the meetings themselves but, rather, what is said in these meetings.

## Testable Propositions for the Conceptual Model

In order to answer our research question, we used a set of propositions that express the relationships between three concepts that were operationalized as follows:

1. Coopilot: This concept is operationalized with both the frequency of use of the conceptual model during project meetings and the usage of its components.

2. Coordination surprise: This concerns, first, coordination surprise perceived by the PM before the use of Coopilot; second, CG breakdowns detected and repaired; and, third, coordination surprises perceived after the use of Coopilot by the PM.

3. Potential for success: This is operationalized with, first, the PM’s belief that the project will be successful in achieving its outcomes; second, both the PM’s perception of team motivation and the team’s satisfaction with the ongoing course of the project; and, third, the ability of the project to meet its requirements as measured by metrics such as EVM or PPC. For the concept of “potential for success,” after considering previous research on IS evaluation and IS project management, we decided to focus on the perceptions of PMs (our primary audience in this study). Nevertheless, in order to provide more evidence for this concept, we also observed indicators such as e-mail from other team members and project performance indicators such as the EVM and metrics such as PPC, as used in the Last Planner system [3], for instance. Indeed, research underlines the difficulty of objectively determining a project’s success or failure [23], concluding that a “comprehensive understanding of IS success thus remains elusive” [55, p. 1849]. Success or failure is “a moving target” [37, p. 396] that depends on the time at which the evaluation is carried out as well as by whom.

By assuming that Clark’s theory makes real-time coordination more effective, our two propositions are:

Proposition 1: The use of Coopilot by the PM decreases team coordination surprises, as perceived by the PM.

Proposition 2: Reduction of coordination surprises positively affects the project’s potential for success, as perceived by the PM.

## Findings

Our conceptual m odel and its ins tantiation were depl oyed and evaluated in three different organizations.

## Project Context

Organization 1 is an international, independent, medical humanitarian organization that delivers emergency aid to people affected by armed conflict, epidemics, natural disasters, and exclusion from health care. Coopilot was implemented and used in one of the most strategic international projects carried out by the organization. This project aims to develop and implement a common human resources database using enterprise resources planning (ERP) in order to share a common vision of the human resources deployed all over the world. The project can be qualified as having a high level of uncertainty because it is in an initialization phase; thus, the scope of the project and its requirements are not fixed. The first meeting was held in March 2012. The ERP implementation was to begin in 2013, but the start date had already been pushed back to 2014. In this project, as is the case generally in projects in Organization 1, the coordinator faces great difficulties in terms of time management and priorities. This project concerns operating centers all over Europe, and its project team members are distributed throughout various countries. Organization 1 has a strong democratic policy in the way it functions. For each global decision, each operating center must provide agreement. As a consequence, the process of decision making takes time, and finding a consensus is a necessary but tedious process.

Organization 2 is an insurance company in Switzerland with 300 employees. Each PM manages five projects on average; nevertheless, consolidation at a program management level does not exist in this organization. PMs struggle with the adoption of a global project management culture, and project management is relatively new to this firm. Coopilot was implemented in a data warehousing project. This project was divided into four main phases. At the time of the study, which began in early 2012 and relates to additional functionalities and 20 deliveries. The third phase was due to be completed in November 2012. Within this organization, the main area of concern for this project was the PM’s workload, which was considered to be too high because of the number of active projects. Team members are also engaged in other projects, so only 40 percent of their operational time is devoted to this particular project. As a consequence, planning and resources management have become problematic, particularly because this project was not seen by the organization to be strategic.

Organization 3 is a Swiss manufacturer of wristwatches and accessories. The company is one of the largest single luxury watch brands in the world, producing about 2,000 watches per day. Coopilot was implemented and evaluated in a project that is highly strategic for the company. The aim was to develop a revolutionary movement for a new watch. This project, which started in 2007 from scratch, contains a high level of technical complexity. The project had clear objectives, with retro planning, and a fixed date of launch on the market. With 6,000 employees, Organization 3 is organized on several hierarchical levels. As a consequence, according to the PM, the language used and the perception of the project and the product are usually different at each level, partly because everyone involved has different expectations. With 40 participants spread over two sites—site 1 for the watch’s movement and site 2 for the case and the strap—multiple PMs have been involved, each focusing on the development of a component of the product. To ensure coordination of these subprojects, common methods and documents can help frame and support all the steps of the project. These include weekly meetings with the PM of each subproject. The project was planned to end at the close of 2012, and it effectively finished on time in December 2012.

## Use of Coopilot

In all three projects, the cards were used for coordination on a frequent basis. In Organization 1’s project, the coordinator used them every two weeks starting in June 2012, and after a few weeks, she used them once a week, during each meeting. It is interesting to note that we observed two cases where the PMs developed an unintended personal use of Coopilot. The cards were targeted for facilitation of team meetings, and we did not provide any other instructions. The PM of Organization 1 used the four variables to structure internal reports and provided written guidelines to participants, each variable being presented in the form of a chapter. The PM of Organization 3 regularly used the four variables to structure his thinking when driving in his car, imagining the next relevant action to be carried out with his project participants. With regard to the usefulness of the components, the PMs of all three organizations stated that all four variables were equally important to them but that one appeared to be more problematic in their specific project. The problematic variable, which they thought required more of their attention than others, was different in the three cases (see Table 4). Also, it is necessary to stipulate that, of the three PMs, only one (Organization 1) made it clear to his or her team that he or she was testing a new research model.

## Coordination Surprises

The findings about coordination surprises (the second construct of our conceptual model) are summarized and illustrated in Table 5. These consisted of (1) coordination surprises before use of Coopilot, (2) CG breakdowns detected and repaired after the use of Coopilot, and (3) coordination surprises after use of Coopilot.

## Coordination Surprises Before Use of Coopilot (Before May 2012)

All the organizations’ PMs experienced coordination surprises in their projects before the implementation and use of Coopilot. In Organization 1, the PM encountered a coordination surprise in April 2012, when she learned by chance that the scope of the project had “suddenly” changed and she had not been informed. In Organization 2, the PM underlined that rumors were the main problem in her project, including misinformation within and around the project with regard to the schedule and workload as well as changes to the scope of the project. In Organization 3, the PM explained that since the beginning of the project, his main difficulty was having to deal with fluctuating levels of commitment among project stakeholders.

## Common Ground Breakdown Detection and Repair After Use of Coopilot (May–September 2012)

All of the PMs reported that, thanks to Coopilot, they were able to discover and manage “potential problems.” These potential problems were CG breakdowns, which involved different variables. In each case, the PMs initiated actions to repair the breakdowns.

In Organization 1, a CG breakdown was detected with joint objectives during the middle of June 2012. In a follow-up interview, the PM explained that, during a coordination meeting, Coopilot helped her realize that although every member seemed to understand the objectives of the project, they disagreed on what had to be done. At the end of the meeting, she helped the team frame the areas of disagreement and presented them to the project sponsors. She brought their answers back to the next meeting, and the team brainstormed and identified that the major problem was that the project’s objective was too generic and created different expectations among participants. Consequently, they decided to split the global objective into clear sectoral objectives. At the following meeting, convened two weeks later, the PM noted that the participants commitment was higher; she felt that the participants had taken over the global and sectoral objectives, and that this resulted in higher levels of participation.

In Organization 2, a CG breakdown was detected by the PM at a meeting. She felt that something was wrong with two variables of Coopilot—joint commitments and joint resources—and she was right. After a few questions, she discovered that three members believed they had to work on a task, but this was not the case. Their misunderstanding led to a perception of work overload. In a later meeting, another CG breakdown was detected and repaired. Coopilot helped the PM detect a problem with the variable joint risks: The team members realized that they did not share any of the same perceptions.

## Coordination Surprises After Use of Coopilot

In September 2012, at the end of our evaluation period, all of the PMs acknowledged that they had not encountered any major coordination surprises. The PM of Organization 1 noted that although she knew about every variable of Coopilot before we introduced her to the artifact, the simplicity of the model allowed her to see meetings in a new light. In particular, she could clearly see which variable was a problem, and where and how to act. She stated, “The model helped me ensure that all members of the meeting have the same vision and have identified the same potential problems” (August 2012). In Organization 2, no coordination surprises were identified by the PM since she started using Coopilot with her team. The PM explained how useful it was for her to frequently ask her team members simple questions such as “What resources do you need this week?” As a result, commitment rose within the team. Finally, in Organization 3, the PM noted that the use of the artifact improved productivity during and between meetings. He observed that during long gaps between meetings, participants were clearer about the things they had to do and why they had to do them. Project closure often tends to be neglected; in his case, however, people knew exactly what they had to do for the others and what the others were supposed to do for them. The PM also realized the necessity of shortening the gaps between meetings. Coopilot helped him realize that frequency of meetings was more essential than he had previously imagined. He also noted that in other ongoing projects, he would increase the frequency of meetings because those projects were in their “warm period.”

## Potential for Success

In the three projects, our findings revealed that Coopilot changed PMs’ perceptions about the successful trajectory of their projects and the motivation of their teams (see Table 6). In Organization 1, the PM claimed that “Coopilot helps me ‘feel’ the project.” She explained that the model helps her acquire knowledge about the key topics to be discussed before individual team members start working on their parts of it. She stated that she sees the project as a future success because “People have a very good understanding of what has happened and what is going to happen.” She acknowledges that motivation of team members has increased since she began using Coopilot. In fact, indicators support such a perception: The rapidity of e-mail responses has increased, and contents of the feedback of each team member are more elaborated. In addition, when the PM asked if team members wanted a backup person to whom they could delegate some of their work if necessary, they answered that it was not necessary; they wanted to stay involved with and be an important part of this project.

In Organization 3, the PM recognized that Coopilot could function “as a guide to pilot his projects now.” We observed that the PPC of the project increased five weeks after the implementation of Coopilot. In Organization 1, Coopilot became “a structure” through which the PM could guide the project. “In my previous meetings, I have always addressed the risk, but I haven’t stressed enough the importance of joint objectives and joint resources. Yet it is unthinkable for me not address these points during a meeting now” (MIS [management information systems] Coordinator, June 19, 2012). In Organization 2, the PM acknowledged that using Coopilot led to greater transparency and clarity among team members. The PM in Organization 3 saw Coopilot as “a project guide that makes the implicit explicit.” As an indication of a strong contribution of Coopilot to their projects, all of the PMs asked to continue with Coopilot after the end of the experiment, requesting additional copies of the cards to distribute to all team members, other teams, and other departments, such as quality control for Organization 3 and HR for Organization 1.

These findings reveal that the use of Coopilot confirms our predictions and the robustness of the underlying assumptions. The first proposition, that “the use of Coopilot by the PM will decrease team coordination surprises as perceived by the PM,” was validated within all three organizations. These evaluations also suggest that the frequent use of Coopilot decreases team coordination surprises from the point of view of the PM. In addition, because of this reduction in coordination surprises, PMs improved their perceptions of their projects’ potential for success. This seems to confirm our second proposition, namely that “reduction of coordination surprises positively affects the project’s potential for success as perceived by the PM.”

## Contributions

Our m ain ob jective in this paper was to adapt Cl ark ’s theory to improve team coordination by reducing, during project meetings, coordination surprises and to have a potential positive effect on the course of a project. We thus developed a conceptual model: Coopilot. It was instantiated as a tool (Coopilot cards) and implemented in three projects. Our main contributions and their implications are presented below.

## Theoretical Contributions

## Contribution 1: A New Design Theory for IS Research

As shown in Table 1, we followed the guidelines of Gregor and Jones, who believe that a “more rigorous approach to specifying design theory should assist with the development of cumulative design theory that is relevant to industry and with raising our discipline above the craft-level” [25, p. 331]. We specifically chose that approach in order to find utility for practitioners while trying to offer new theoretical insights for IS researchers beyond those interested in design science. We see the Coopilot cards as a prototype serving a communicative purpose in illustrating the design principles that are embodied within our design theory. Moreover, we recognize that design mutability is a special component of an IS design theory, and we have taken into account possible evolutions of the shape of our instantiation (e.g., paper cards versus a mobile application on a smartphone, explained in our conclusion). We tried to maintain context-independence for the four variables used to assess CG breakdown, so that they could be used by a wider range of PMs.

## Contribution 2: From Clark’s Theory to IS Research

As we are reminded by Holmström and Truex [28] and Truex et al. [65], calls continue for “good theory” in IS as scholars explore facets of novel theories and how they should be adapted to IS research. Drawing on previous IS project coordination literature, we extended Clark’s model to fit IS research and we instantiated it to provide an artifact for PMs. Our findings, acknowledging the usefulness of the artifact, reveal the added value of Clark’s approach to team coordination in the field of IS project management. Although previous studies have explored both implicit and explicit coordination mechanisms in IS projects [17, 18], to our knowledge, no study in the IS field has deeply investigated the impact of conversation and evidence of understanding on successful team coordination, and possibly even on the success of an entire project. We believe that Clark’s theoretical model can open a new creative space for IS project management. His model encompasses crucial elements such as activity, language, time, intention, commitment, and decision processes, and it has the potential to become a solid basis for both IS researchers and practitioners.

## Contribution 3: From IS Research to Clark’s Theory

This research brought Clark’s concepts into the field of IS project management. The relevance of the artifact designed from his principles demonstrates that this theory can be extended, since it can be “scaled up” from a single conversation to an entire project with dozens of related conversational episodes. The same principles still apply. To some extent, a project can be viewed as a long conversation, punctuated both by long and short interruptions and by changes in participants [44]. Moreover, we have proven that the model can be instantiated. Coopilot is one of the first attempts (maybe the first) to design a tool to manage the CG of teams in real time. Such adaptation and instantiation might appear challenging at the beginning, since Clark’s coordination model is purely descriptive and by no means prescriptive, but we hope to have added useful constructs to Clark’s theory.

## Practical Contributions

## Contribution 1: An Artifact That Is Easy to Use and That Delivers Prescriptive Advice

Following a design science approach, we developed a simple instantiation of our design theory that helps PMs improve team coordination in real time. Our evaluation of Coopilot indicated that frequent use of Coopilot over a period of several months reduces the number of coordination surprises, by aiding PMs in detecting issues among the four dimensions of our model (joint beliefs, namely joint objectives, joint commitments, joint resources, and joint risks) and repair them in real time by picking and asking one of the proposed questions we coined as conversational guides.

## Contribution 2: Conversation as a Central Element of Project Management

Many PMs we encountered believe, wrongly, that meetings are a waste of time. Meetings are not the problem; what is problematic is the lack of consideration of what is said, and how, during meetings. Our findings are in line with those put forward by Klein et al., who noted that “there are practices that can be engaged in proactively in order to make it easier to detect a breakdown before a possibly harmful surprise. One of these practices is to elaborate—to the extent possible—all the steps of the ‘joint action ladder’ during coordination events” [32, p. 159]. Such practices are actively promoted by the usage of our tool.

## Contribution 3: Implications for Project Management Methods

This research is an illustration of, first, how our conceptual method and its instantiation can be a complement to existing project management methods and, second, how existing project management methods could enrich the bodies of knowledge and tools available to PMs in order to improve their real-time coordination skills. On the one hand, Coopilot cannot substitute for existing methods (such as process-driven methods like PMI [53] or PRINCE 2 [PRojects IN Closed Environment]) or “lean” and “agile” methods (e.g., Last Planner [3] and Scrum [57]). Indeed, Coopilot focuses exclusively on the facilitation of real-time coordination during meetings by providing simple conversational guidelines that aim to facilitate the process and to help team members review their CG before leaving each other. On the other hand, lean methods such as Last Planner and Scrum already give a greater importance to team coordination, and they can benefit from Coopilot: Presence in a meeting can be used as a support for mutual understanding. More generally, we hope to help further stress the importance of coordination and conversation stated in most how-to books and methods, such as the IPMA (International Project Management Association) competencies baseline [30] and the PMBOK (Project Management Book of Knowledge) [53] from PMI. As noted by Klein et al., “one of the key aspects of joint action is the process of sustaining common ground to enable coordination. . . . This includes the role of shared experience and expertise, and poses a limitation to those who think coordination can be manufactured through procedures and explicit guidelines” [32, p. 171]. Our hope is that we have presented enough evidence to show that communication, contrary to some approaches and popular perceptions, is not a linear, uncomplicated process of sending and receiving messages. Pragmatic considerations such as how to set up and maintain mutual understanding appear to be taken as self-evident. Communication and conversation are a coordinated activity as well as the primary means for coordinating other activities [4]. In this respect, Coopilot is part of a new generation of tools because it was designed under such assumptions.

## Conclusion and Future Research

Concretel y, returning to the exam pl e that b egan this paper, should Bob move on to the next phase, or instead attempt something else with regard to his project and the other people involved in it? We are now well equipped to help Bob make a decision. We recommend that he talk before it is too late, while the resources allocated to the project are still available and have not disappeared as the result of a progressive accumulation of coordination surprises. Bob should not move on to the next phase until he organizes a series of one-to-one and group meetings to put everyone back on the same page. For example, Anne’s remark illustrates a CG breakdown related to joint objectives and Cliff’s remark shows a CG breakdown concerning joint resources. Our model can assist Bob during his meetings by helping him identify where the CG breakdowns are so that he can repair them in real time through use of the sample questions. PMs who had used Coopilot from the beginning could address most of these perception gaps during meetings (Proposition 1), and Bob’s project would now be in better shape and benefit from a greater potential for success (Proposition 2).

The current version of our conceptual model was implemented and evaluated in three case studies. The PMs concerned were involved on a voluntary basis. For the present study, the period of analysis was four months. Future research is needed to corroborate the extent to which our model is generalizable. In particular, with respect to the four phases of our methodology, we developed a new method for running Coopilot on smartphones and we are currently recruiting teams for large-scale testing.
