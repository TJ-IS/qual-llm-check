# Logic Pluralism in Mobile Platform Ecosystems: A Study of Indie App Developers on the iOS App Store

Yixin Qiu,<sup>a</sup> Anandasivam Gopal,<sup>b</sup> Il-Horn Hann <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Health Integrity LLC, Easton, Maryland 21601; <sup>b</sup> Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742

Contact: joqiu1999@gmail.com (YQ); agopal@rhsmith.umd.edu (AG); ihann@rhsmith.umd.edu (I-HH)

Received: November 5, 2012 Accepted: June 15, 2016 Published Online in Articles in Advance: March 6, 2017

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2016.0664

Copyright: © 2017 INFORMS

Abstract. The software development field has inherently been identified with two fieldlevel institutional logics, i.e., logic of the profession and logic of the markets. Traditionally, information systems development methodologies have been used to reconcile the competing demands from these two logics. In this paper, we study how these two logics manifest in a platform-based software ecosystem where significant entrepreneurial opportunities are created for independent third-party app developers (indies). Specifically, we study how indie developers manage the two logics on the iOS platform ecosystem under the influence of the platform owner Apple. We first identify practices of indie developers consistent with the two field logics across three entrepreneurial domains, i.e., app ideation, execution, and marketing. Second, we identify areas where the two field logics may be in conflict as well as in coexistence. We show that indie developers enact logic conflict through disagreement and denunciation of the opposing logic. When logics that are inherently opposed appear to coexist, we investigate how app developers manage these opposing demands through a process we call logic synthesis. Using a grounded theory approach, we propose a model of field-level market and professional logics operating in the mobile app platform ecosystem and the practices reflecting such logics in the indie developer community. Our work contributes to the growing literature on platform ecosystems and the processes adopted by third-party app developers in such ecosystems, in addition to furthering the study of institutional logics in technology contexts.

## 1. Introduction

The software development industry has undergone many transformational changes in the past decade as technology platforms and customer needs have evolved. The latest transformation is the emergence and growth of platform-based software ecosystems, often referred to colloquially as the “app economy” (MacMillan et al. 2009, Wasserman 2010). The most notable platform ecosystems are observable in the mobile software sphere where apps are specifically developed to be implemented on mobile platforms, such as Apple’s iOS and Google’s Android, and sold through digital marketplaces also owned by the platform provider such as Apple’s App Store and Google’s Google Play. Unlike traditional software development, wherein software firms develop large-scale information systems based on clients’ requirements, the platform ecosystems “leverage the expertise of a diverse developer community, with skills and an appreciation of user needs that platform owners might not possess, to creatively develop new capabilities unforeseeable by the platform’s original designers” (Tiwana et al. 2010, p. 675). The resulting software products, or apps, tend to be small in scale, but provide consumers with diversity in terms of functionality, and displaying significant innovation and originality on the part of the developers (Boudreau 2012). Third-party app developers have thus become instrumental in ensuring the success not only of their own apps but also of the software platforms on which they operate. In this paper we focus on these third-party app developers; specifically, we study their interactions with the platform owner and consumers, as well as the processes they adopt in competing on the platform marketplace. Our goal is to enhance understanding of the workings of the app economy.

The term “platform” has been used in multiple contexts in the literature leading to considerable variation in the connotations attached to it. As a first step, following Tiwana et al. (2010, p. 675), we formally define the software-based platform that is the focus of our work here as the “extensible codebase of a softwarebased system that provides core functionality shared by the modules that interoperate with it and the interfaces through which they interoperate.” Furthermore, a module or app is defined as “an add-on software subsystem that connects to the platform to add functionality to it.” From this perspective, the software platform’s ecosystem represents “the collection of the platform and the modules specific to that platform.” While this definition addresses the technical artifacts present, we use a more expansive definition of the platform ecosystem to include not only the software artifacts but also the various stakeholders, such as the platform owner, developers who simultaneously compete and co-operate, end-user consumers who purchase software, advertisers and content providers, and the complex social interactions that occur among these players (Yoo et al. 2012). Because the mobile platform ecosystem was a relatively new phenomenon when this research was conducted, we adopted the grounded theory methodology with a broad research objective of understanding the processes behind how app developers operated in the iOS ecosystem context.

An early theme that emerged in our fieldwork, and that influenced the course of our investigation, pertained to the observation that app developers were constantly juggling between, on one hand, adhering to the professional standards of software engineering and, on the other hand, navigating through the business demands and competitive pressures of the iOS market. As our fieldwork progressed, we made linkages between these themes emerging from the field and extant work in the literature suggesting that the field of software development has inherently been identified with two imperatives, i.e., the professional imperative and the market imperative. The professional imperative in the field is shaped by the principles of software engineering, associated with a focus on quality, reliable engineering practices, well-identified processes, and the appropriate methodologies to be used in creating and maintaining good quality software (Pressman 1993, Jones 2009, Boehm 1984). However, it is also true that much software is developed keeping in mind a dominant business environment, with its attendant market constraints and opportunities. Early work in the field of software development recognized that market parameters such as costs, efort, and scale eficiencies were equally important as the best development principles (Boehm 1984, Maxwell et al. 1999, Banker and Slaughter 1997, Henderson and Lee 1992). Thus, various information systems development (ISD) methodologies built on reconciling the conflicting demands such as the software development lifecycle (SDLC), structured design, and the Capability Maturity Model (CMM) were developed. Later refinements of methodologies, such as the agile methodology and rapid prototyping, emerged to handle increasing complexity in software projects (Pressman 2005, Ramesh et al. 2012). These approaches of managing conflict have remained in the software development field even in the new era, where third-party developers write software for the platform and publish in the platform-owned marketplace. Thus, our broad research questions are: How do these two sets of conflicting imperatives manifest in developers on platform ecosystems? How do third-party developers respond to and manage the interaction of the two imperatives under the influence of platform technology and policies?

As we continued the fieldwork and concurrent lit erature reviews, we further inferred the associations between the theoretical lens of institutional logics (Friedland and Alford 1991, Thornton et al. 2012) and the field data we were gathering. Several institutional logics have been developed along the lines of sources of legitimacy, authority, and identity; basis of norms, attention, and strategy, and so on in the literature (Thornton 2002). While more granular level logics can be constructed closer to the context, we chose to leverage the professional and market logics defined by the institutional logics literature in studying the two imperatives of the software development industry. Our choice was driven by the realization that the core elements of the institutional logics literature such as institutional pluralism and multilevel logics, discussed in more detail in Section 2, were particularly apposite given the practices and dynamics described by the participants in our study. Using the institutional logics lens to study the platform software ecosystem also addresses a significant gap in the literature. Current work (Lee and Raghu 2014, Wasserman 2010) has limited the discussion on platforms to two perspectives, i.e., economics, which represents the platform as a two-sided market, and engineering, which conceptualizes the platform as a technical architecture. The organizational aspects of platforms, in terms of how third-party developers work their interactions with consumers and the platform technology and policies, remains understudied. Indeed, these communities are characterized by social and organizational practices that are unique to platform ecosystems that rely on third-party complementors as sources of innovation (Gawer 2014). The logics lens helps us achieve this goal in that institutional logics provide legitimate guidelines for behaviors for entities in societal, organizational or individual contexts (Friedland and Alford 1991) while also allowing ample room for the individual agency. By way of addressing this gap in the literature, we present an analysis of the working practices of Apple’s iOS app developers and their interactions with the platform owner Apple, as they ideate, execute, and market their apps on the App Store.

The platform ecosystem model of software development that we study difers from the traditional context of software development in several ways. First, the platform owner (Apple, in our case) is not only the technology provider but also the market-maker. This is a significant departure from past research, where the platform owner only provided the technological resources, while allowing the market to influence all other actions on the part of the software developer. By contrast, as our findings suggest, the platform owner not only reinforces selected norms about quality but also influences the form and degree of competition through its policies, thereby directly influencing developer strategies and processes.

A second diference relates to the observed democratization of third-party innovations to the platform, which has allowed independent software developers (i.e., indies) to compete on a level playing field with large software firms. While indie developers are independent and have considerable agency, two factors are particularly important. First, indies tend to be members of an informal cultural collective where a specific social hierarchy based on artistic or artisanal capabilities emerge, and where communities with specific guidelines and norms develop (Van Meeteren et al. 2010). Second, indie developers are nascent entrepreneurs, i.e., individuals who commit their own time and resources to founding a new firm in the form of a start-up (Wagner 2007). The emergence of indie developers in the software platform ecosystem has implications for management of logic conflict. In large development firms, clearly specified boundary spanners are tasked with resolving conflicts that routinely arise between diferent members of the organization (Berente and Yoo 2012). This is not true for indies. By virtue of co-ownership of the firm and its production systems, the efects of both logics are manifested on indie developers in full. Furthermore, unlike ISD where the full force of conflict is likely felt in the development stage (client management is typically exercised separately), indie developers experience logics across the full entrepreneurial life cycle, from product conceptualization to eventual market release.

Finally, the traditional ISD literature suggests that balance can be achieved between cost and quality objectives especially when viewed through the lens of software processes (Krishnan et al. 2000). By contrast, the logics literature maintains that in many cases, logic conflict cannot be reconciled and that such contradictions are structural (Berente and Yoo 2012, Dunn and Jones 2010). Our work here provides for a middle ground where we observe conflict and reconciliation. At the individual level, there is variation in the extent to which developers could and are willing to reconcile the conflicting logics they experience. In addition, it is here that we observe the full force of the platform owner’s influence, across all activities undertaken by indie developers, thus providing for a richer description of logics pluralism.

The above diferences between the context in which traditional ISD is carried out, and the newer form of platform-centric ecosystems, suggest large gaps in terms of how prior work from ISD applies directly to the new context. In this paper, we therefore ask the following research questions: Given the observation that indie developers operate in a platform ecosystem where multiple institutional logics exist, how do these developers manage these logics in their concurrent roles as software developers and nascent entrepreneurs? How do the platform technology and policies influence the indie developers’ practices? Exploring these processes forms the primary contribution of our work here. While conflict reconciliation in ISD may not be new, a description of the form and processes by which it is performed by indie developers, as provided in our work here, remains underexplored in the literature.

We conducted this research through a grounded theory study on iOS indie developers. Using over 90 hours of field observations, and in-depth interviews along with secondary data collected from 19 indie app developers, we first explicate the technological, market, and cultural environment exercised by the platform owner in which indie app developers operate. Next we characterize what practices of app developers reflect professional and market logics and how these practices are manifested in developers’ specific entrepreneurial areas. Then, we describe how developers tend to encounter logic conflict in app execution and marketing, while addressing logic coexistence in all app entrepreneurial activities. We further delineate in detail how developers balance the professional and market logics where they may coexist through mechanisms of logic synthesis in relation to platform influence. We thus open the black-box of social processes by which innovation is created on software platforms, thereby responding to a significant gap in the literature (Gawer 2014).

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. Multilevel Logics, Institutional Pluralism, Embedded Agency, and Situationism

The theory of institutional logic builds on the premise that society consists of a set of interdependent yet contradictory institutional logics. Institutional logics may be defined as the socially constructed, historical patterns of cultural symbols and material practices, including assumptions, values, and beliefs, by which individuals and organizations provide meaning to their daily activity, organize time and space, and reproduce their lives and experiences (Friedland and Alford 1991, Thornton and Ocasio 1999). In our theorizing, we draw on four specific elements in the logics context that are relevant to our study.

First, we argue that institutional logics operate at multiple levels. The societal-level logic is assumed to influence the logics acting on the lower level, such as the field or the industry (Thornton et al. 2012). As an example, Thornton’s (2002) analysis of the highereducation publishing industry shows that societal logics of markets and professions have parallel analogs in the lower-order field of publishing, in the form of editorial and market logic. Practices and beliefs observed at the field level reflect higher logics operating at the societal level. Without a societal counterpart, identifying field-level logics is incomplete (Thornton et al. 2012). After field-level logics reflecting societal logics are identified, do certain logics dominate or can fields reflect the influence of multiple logics concurrently? To address this question, we introduce the concept of institutional pluralism.

In professions where multiple field-level logics may operate concurrently, these may be contradictory and occasionally complementary (Dunn and Jones 2010, Purdy and Gray 2009). Thus, the notion that multiple logics can jointly influence a field, i.e., institutional pluralism, has replaced the assumed dominance of a single logic in the field (Thornton 2002). In institutional pluralistic fields, multiple logics can be in conflict or coexist; typical analyses of such contexts discuss how actors enacting varying logics interact (Reay and Hinings 2005), how actors respond to environments that reflect certain logics (Eikhof and Haunschild 2007), and purely historical accounts of logics in fields such as medicine (Dunn and Jones 2010). When multiple logics coexist in a field, actors reconcile these conflicts using a variety of practices such as developing collaborative relationships between the prevalent logics (i.e., bridging) (Reay and Hinings 2009), loose coupling strategies (Berente and Yoo 2012), and mechanisms such as logic transformation, adaptation, and grafting (Purdy and Gray 2009). A summary of the literature is in Table 1. The majority of this work has considered settings where organizations or fields operate in institutionally pluralistic contexts, with little or no work considering how individuals respond to multiple logics in their field. Do all individuals respond similarly to such logics, or is there scope for diverging strategies adopted by individuals in such settings? We link logics pluralism in the field to individual behavior using a third theoretical concept, i.e., embedded agency.

Embedded in an institutionally pluralistic field, individuals leverage opportunities provided by multiple logics and adopt practices in response to the constraints applied on them by virtue of each logic (Battilana 2006). Constraints and opportunities work by focusing the individual’s attention on acceptable and legitimate activities while highlighting domains where divergent or entrepreneurial behavior may be possible. For instance, Berente and Yoo (2012) describe embedded agency in NASA, where organizational actors use loose coupling to reconcile institutional conflicts arising from Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) implementation.

While embedded agency explains how individuals respond to multiple logics, are there specific triggers in the pluralistic environment that lead to idiosyncratic individual behaviors? Here we introduce the fourth concept, i.e., situationism. When individuals are exposed to multiple logics, the concept of situationism (Thornton et al. 2012) helps explain when and which of the logics are likely to be triggered. Situationism thus refers to the influence of the situation’s characteristics, in time and place, on individual behavior, thereby including the immediate social context and interactions with the material properties of the situation.

Using these four concepts from the institutional logics (multilevel logics, institutional pluralism, embedded agency, situationism), we study the processes and behaviors of indie app developers as they work on Apple’s iOS platform ecosystem. We first use multilevel logics and the concept of pluralism to argue that indie developers enact field-level versions of the societal logics of the market and the profession. We argue that iOS developers operate in environments where elements of market and professional logics co-exist, consistent with prior ISD research that has discussed methods by which conflicting demands on development teams may be reconciled (Iivari et al. 1998). Subsequently, we theorize about how, through embedded agency, individual app developers are likely to follow diferent paths in terms of how they manage the multiple logics operating on them in the iOS platform ecosystem. Finally, we identify the strong hand of the platform owner, as technology provider and marketmaker, in setting the situational context.

## 2.2. Institutional Pluralism in Software Development: Logic of the Market and Logic of the Profession

App developers are, first and foremost, software developers. Hence, we first examine the extent to which the institutional logics operating in the field of software development may be associated with higher-order societal logics. Among the various multilevel logics that have been discussed on logic pluralism, one pair of societal logics that has received extensive study, and is also of particular relevance to our work here, is the logic of the professions and the logic of the markets (Thornton 2002). Originally conceptualized as part of inter-institutional system ideal types along with four other logics,<sup>1</sup> these logics are defined and distinguished by categorical characteristics such as sources of legitimacy, authority, and identity; basis of norms, attention, and strategy, and so on. The literature discusses the dynamics between these societal logics as including logic conflict and logic coexistence. Logic conflict here arises when norms clash, such as when artistic value meets economic value in cultural industries such as musical orchestras (Glynn 2000) or theatre (Eikhof and Haunschild 2007). By contrast, professional and market logics co-exist in fields where social actors’ practices are more guided by pragmatic goals (Tschang 2007, Jain et al. 2009).

We begin by delineating, as ideal types, the “pure” form of professional logic in the software development field. The professional logic of the field is deeply rooted in the software engineering profession as well as the accepted norms of usability and good design. The IEEE Computer Society’s Software Engineering Body of Knowledge defines software engineering as the “application of a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to the development, operation, and maintenance of software” (Abran et al. 2004, p. 1). In a survey of software professionals on what knowledge is most critical, elements of software engineering such as programming languages, analysis and design methods, and algorithms were the top choices (Lethbridge 2000).

Increasingly, the professional logic for software products transcends utilitarian software to include creative and aesthetic appeal. Recent developments in defining the software development profession have termed these activities as “creative” endeavors, analogous to the performing arts due to the iterative creative process typically observed therein (Harris et al. 2009). According to the UK Department of Culture, Media, and Sports, the software sector is classified as a creative industry, alongside film, architecture, music, and the performing arts, highlighting the technical nature as well as the aesthetic component attached to software development.<sup>2</sup>

In addition, the professional logic for the software development field includes a strong enjoyment-based intrinsic motivation to adhere to creativity in task achievement (Lakhani and Wolf 2005, Shah 2006). This is reflected in the literature on the depiction of developers as “hackers.” Building and creating innovative software is the center of a hacker’s life (Carmel 1997), backed by a strong sense of “hacker” identity and “hacker” ethics (Raymond 1996, Levy 1984). Drastically distinct from the later derogatory meaning associated with the term, the original term “hacker” referred to those who were enthusiastic about programming, enjoyed the intellectual challenge from programming work, mistrusted authority and business objectives, and believed in meritocracy. The above attributes, combined, form the critical elements of the ideal type of professional logic for the field of software development.<sup>3</sup> The main elements of this ideal type are shown in Column 1 of Table 2.

Similar to the professional logic described above, it is possible to find elements of market logic in the software development field. “Pure” market logic here would be reflected in an overarching focus on market or commercial considerations in the field, where the traditional levers of business play a dominant role. In such a setting, prices obtained in the market for software would be the primary consideration for any development activity, implicating market share and profitability as the most desirable goals for the organization or individual. By contrast to the elements of aesthetics, quality, and the “hacker” ethic described before, pure market logic would emphasize financial and market goals, with commercialism being the dominant source of identity (Thornton 2012). Indeed, many of these considerations have been explicitly modeled and studied in software development organizations; prior work has discussed the importance of costs (Jorgensen and Shepperd 2007), cycle time or durations (Harter et al. 2000, Cohen et al. 1996), profitability (Gopal et al. 2003), and revenues (Arora et al. 2001). ## 2.3. Logic Conflict, Reconciliation, and the Role of ISD Methodologies

While these ideal types delineate “pure” organizational forms, from the early days of software development, organizations have evolved strategies and methods to find ways to reconcile the competing demands of the professional and market logics (Adams 2007). Thus, the two field-level logics have co-existed and been in conflict in diferent stages and forms in the field. In terms of co-existence, a variety of software development methodologies and techniques in ISD have emerged to help organizations manage quality considerations, while achieving market outcomes.

Early approaches include the seminal works by Boehm (1984), Jones (1978), and Humphrey (1988). Later work explicitly modeled trade-ofs between development costs, on one hand representing market considerations, and quality, representing elements of professional logic (Krishnan et al. 2000, Harter et al. 2000, Slaughter et al. 1998) on the other hand. This work showed that reconciliation between the conflicting demand of the market and the profession may be reached through use of software development methodologies, thereby ensuring financial and market performance (Gopal et al. 2003, Telang and Wattal 2007).

As the field of software development became more complex and distributed, newer methodologies and techniques evolved to accommodate the traditional conflicts between market considerations and quality. Harris et al. (2009) show that as uncertainty in the development project increases, the traditional set of controls applied on teams must be modified to allow for more emergent forms of control, thereby allowing teams to pay attention to quality as well as eficiency on the project. Similarly, the use of the “agile” methodology has been shown to be particularly efective when requirements change often, a bane of software development projects in most modern settings (Maruping et al. 2009, Agerfalk et al. 2009). Developing this theme further, Ramesh et al. (2012) show that agile methodologies, attractive when requirements change, are at odds with control needed when development is geographically distributed (in ofshoring, for instance). In such settings, organizational ambidexterity, i.e., “the ability to balance the tenets of diferent development methodologies” (p. 326) became particularly useful in addressing conflicting demands. The advent of component-based development (Schmidt 2006), where individual components can be reconfigured into fullscale software products to address a specific set of requirements, adds further complexity to the development process, making it harder to manage the costquality trade-of. In studying this issue, Subramanyam et al. (2012) show that the size of the individual components (i.e., granularity) and the quality inherent in each component have significant efects on efort and costs, again providing insight on the trade-ofs between development efort and quality. Almost all of this work is in the context of large-scale development in organizations, by contrast to the indie context we study. Nevertheless, developing strategies to reconcile conflicting demands on the development team placed by the market, on one hand, and quality considerations, on the other, is not without precedent in ISD.

Lest we conclude that market and professional logics in software development are successfully reconciled based on ISD methodologies, there is enough evidence of significant continuing conflict as well. The literature discusses how the cultural norms of autonomy and freedom, which programmers typically cherish (parts of the original hacker culture) clash with the market orientation of firm strategy, deadlines, and cost orientation (O’Mahony and Bechky 2008). The emergence of the free and open source software movement is partly attributed to market practices followed by proprietary software firms that insisted on strict intellectual property protection, by contrast to earlier practices where operating systems such as Unix were distributed under open licensing schemes (Lakhani and Wolf 2005). Thus, in the institutionally pluralistic field of software development, prior work suggests that there remain ongoing conflicts between market and professional logics.

Recognition of the institutionally pluralistic field of software development has, over the last three decades, led to the emergence of institutional players that have been instrumental in promoting methodologies and techniques for reconciling conflicts between the market and the profession (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Gopal et al. 2005). The teaching of development methodologies, such as agile development, the software development life cycle, rapid prototyping, and the CMM (to name just a few), in formal software engineering curricula has allowed reconciliation between quality, on one hand, and costs, on the other, to be more easily incorporated into the field (Pressman 2005). Similarly, development of institutions such as the Software Engineering Institute and the IEEE Computer Society, focused on researching and developing methodologies for the management of complex software development projects, has led to the institutionalization and legitimization of market and professional logics in the field (Boehm 1984).

## 2.4. Embedded Agency and the Situational Influence of Platforms

How do indie developers maneuver through these conflicts between field logics, in the presence of the institutional pressures applied by the platform owner? Embedded agency builds on the observation that even though all agents operate in the same institutional framework, facing the same set of conflicts between diverging institutional logics, they vary in terms of their espoused strategy and their actions (Battilana 2006). Thus, entrepreneurial activities are not stamped out in the presence of strong institutional norms arising from field logics; rather, embedded agency allows individual behavior to deviate from norms in fruitful ways (Garud et al. 2007). Because we are interested in studying individual app developers, the embedded agency lens allows us to associate individual behaviors and practices with field-level logics (through the ideal type) but also allows us to establish the situational influence of the platform owner (Thornton et al. 2012).

For indie app developers, a crucial situational characteristic is the influence from the platform owner, Apple in this case. Assuming two roles, Apple first serves as the technology provider, and also the owner of the sole distribution channel for apps, where it curates the content and quality for iOS apps through its review process, the selection of the featured apps in each category, and its promotion activities for new apps. Thus, it exercises a significant level of control and coordination over its third-party developers (Boudreau 2010, Eaton et al. 2015). Hence, the situational characteristics imposed by the platform owner add to the two institutional logics in the field that contribute to shaping the developers’ identities and practices in the platform ecosystem. By juxtaposing these influences on individual app indies, we observed the individual practices that each developer may use to navigate conflict and coexistence between the logic of the market and the profession, but molded by the platform owner’s technological and cultural influence.

## 3. Research Methods

We conducted a grounded theory study (Birks et al. 2013, Walsh et al. 2015) to answer the research questions. Data collection began in November 2009 and ended in June 2011 from multiple data sources, i.e., field and online observations and semi-structured interviews. Because we were interested in nascent app entrepreneurs, we made a general sampling rule where we restricted our interviewees to those who developed apps for revenue generation purposes (as opposed to those who developed apps for leisure or strictly for their own consumption), had published at least one app on the App Store, and had built bootstrapped ventures with no external financing support. Because the workplace of indie iOS app developers is geographically spread out, we relied on Mac OS X and iOS developer meet-ups and events in the Washington, DC metropolitan area for field observations and interviewee recruiting.

To gain access to iOS developers, the first author began by attending a local Mac OS X indie meet-up group, information for which was posted on a tech events website frequented by developers. In the first visit, she introduced herself by explaining the general purpose of the research, and was welcomed by the group. Composed of a mixture of iOS and OS X developers, this group had an informal atmosphere, where developers would get together every week, code on their computers, and converse informally on issues of mutual interest. Several visits in, the first author acquired a significant understanding of the context in which these meet-ups occurred and also the prevalent social norms. The first interview for this paper was conducted with the group leader of this meet-up. Subsequently, based on our criteria for inclusion, our sample of developers snowballed to other members of this meet-up group. The interview process was initiated with a general purpose of understanding indie app developers’ App Store experience. Over the course of subsequent interviews, we noted that a strong identification with Apple culture, where app quality dominates the development philosophy, emerged as a very salient theme in the interviews. At the same time, several developers reported experiencing an “Aha!” moment when they realized that merely writing a good app was not enough; other activities such as marketing are equally important. Indie app developers’ attention to such craftsmanship in their entrepreneurial process and developers’ “Aha!” moment served as a rich discovery (Agar 1996) for the first author and prompted her to go back to the literature to search for theorizing on craft-based industries; Thornton’s (2002) work on institutional logics in the higher-education industry proved to be particularly well matched. In her paper, the discussion on professional and market logics resonated well with the observed salient points emerging in the interviews. As a result, we adopted the lens of institutional logics as our high-level theoretical guide; subsequent micro-level theorizing on indie app developers’ practices on managing professional and market logics was conducted in a grounded and inductive fashion (Birks et al. 2013).

Data analysis began immediately after data collection started. With the guidance of the institutional logics literature, we began the coding on developers’ logic anchoring for their App Store practices. Constant comparison (Glaser and Strauss 1967) was used in deriving labels and conceptual elements reflecting developers beliefs, identities, values, and business practices. On the completion of several interviews, we realized that all of the developers interviewed thus far strongly anchored on professional logic. Following the theoretical sampling approach (Glaser and Strauss 1967), with the goal of increasing the variance of the logic with which developers identify, we deliberately sought to locate developers who mainly enacted the market logic by attending other business-oriented meet-up groups on mobile development in the same geographic area.

One of those groups was focused on mobile app development specifically and included developers from multiple platforms, although at the time the majority of the members were iOS developers. Another occasion with a more business-oriented flavor was a one and a half-day event focused on iPad development. Both groups had a formal structure, with organized activities such as developers’ app presentations and panel discussions on the technical, business, and legal aspects of the mobile app industry. Several interviewees were recruited based on the content of their presentations, because developers would often disclose (sometimes involuntarily) whether their practices on the App Store were primarily driven by the professional or market logic.<sup>4</sup> Other times, an informal conversation with developers provided similar information. The first author also attended a gathering for the mobile app industry which attracted not only app developers but also marketers, content providers, and funders. We reviewed the logics literature concurrently with our fieldwork. Because the literature had also introduced the concept of variation in the relationships between logics in the pluralistic field, we added questions about logic dynamics to our interviews. Pre vious interviewees were subsequently followed up on these dynamics as well. Thus, the purpose of attending these events was to identify interviewees who would maximize the variance on their logic anchors and logic management practices, as well as help us recruit developers to replicate the characteristics on these two dimensions. In total, we conducted over 90 hours of field observations,<sup>5</sup> with 122 pages of singlespaced field notes taken. Twenty-six face-to-face semistructured interviews were conducted with 19 indie app developers, while follow-up interviews were conducted with 3 developers. Each interview, lasting one to two hours, was recorded and transcribed. Extensive memos were generated during the interview transcribing process, which also involved revisiting field notes. In doing so, the first author drew mental maps and diagrams to make sense of the emerging themes and their relationships. Constant comparison was used in developing categories reflecting developers’ logic anchoring and logic management, as well as their properties and dimensions (Strauss and Corbin 1998). In total, 352 pages of singled-spaced transcripts and 130 pages of field notes with memos were produced.

Besides formal interviews, the first author held informal conversations with the informants whenever possible at various meet-ups. In these informal conversations, our interpretations of the interviews were verified. In addition to interviews, online resources such as company websites, blogs, user forums, Facebook fan pages, as well as the apps themselves, of the 19 developers were examined in detail. Besides being used to triangulate the interview data, these secondary data helped develop further understanding of interviewees’ company philosophy, design and development style of apps, and user support strategy. Towards the later stage of data collection, the first author began participant observations via a 10-month internship at an iOS mobile software startup. Through day-to-day work activities and interactions, the first author gained a deeper understanding of the development life cycle of mobile apps and the decision-making process of app entrepreneurs. While not part of the analyzed data, this experience was used to validate internal and face validity of the model developed (Adler and Adler 1987). Table 3 provides an overview of collected data. We start presenting our findings by describing the platform ecosystem enabled by Apple’s technological, market, and cultural characteristics and the indie developers’ reactions to these characteristics. Our research proposes that logic conflict and co-existence manifest in iOS indie app developers’ practices. Subsequently, we discuss developers’ practices when navigating logics where they appear to co-exist. ## 4.1. The Situational Characteristics of Indie App Developers: Apple as a Platform Owner

As the platform owner, Apple influences indie app developers in three distinct ways. First, as a software platform provider, Apple provides the technical tools, documentation, and guidelines for third-party developers wherein the latter produce platform-compatible applications complementary to the platform. Developers enunciate the importance of following Applerelated news and updates to keep abreast of the latest changes in platform technologies.

The second and arguably the most important aspect of the platform owner’s influence emerged from its role as the creator of the market exchange, i.e., the iOS App Store. This influence is a double-edged sword. Lack of appropriate distribution channels for indie software products had traditionally been a major obstacle in the field. Besides lack of access to consumers, indie developers typically did not have adequate resources for activities such as generating and enforcing licenses, maintaining payment systems, and marketing. The advent of the App Store removed this significant infrastructure hurdle for all developers and allowed a wider market reach. The ability of the App Store to level the playing field for indie developers also generates significant empowerment for developers, in addition to allowing them to compete with larger and more reputed studios and firms. In some cases, developers with popular apps expressed their positive sentiments towards the App Store using strong terms, as seen here in a statement from our fieldwork:

“One thing I will say, I have been a developer for almost 30 years, from punch cards on mainframes to the latest languages. In those 30 years, this is the first time as a developer I have a “Rock Star” response when people find out I write apps for the iPhone. It’s like, wow, I never realized people actually write that stuf! You are so COOL!;” (Fieldwork)

While the positive aspects of the App Store are evident, it is also clear that Apple’s store design and governance policies, as well as the competitive environment, pose significant challenges for indie developers. Apple is well known for its gatekeeper role on the App Store through its control of the app publishing and review process. Apple reviews every app published on the App Store for content and functionality. While such reviews are intended primarily for quality assurance, at times developers’ apps are rejected or pulled from the marketplace with little or no reasonable explanation, as described by an interviewee:

“It was the number one audiobook (app on the App Store), so one day we got a phone call from Apple.

Fighting for visibility and the risks of failure so became a challenge even for developers with a good track record:

“Visibility is really really hard on the App Store. Most apps you never see, most apps get buried. One approach that Apple as the platform owner uses to address the visibility challenge is through “Top Charts,” which are driven by download numbers and revenue amount, as well as “Featured Lists,” which are curated by Apple staf. However, getting on these charts often requires a significant marketing budget, which is hard for indie developers to come by, especially those just starting out on the App Store. Furthermore, the design of the “Top Charts” creates what developers refer to as the “technical barrier,” i.e., “how many clicks it takes to get to your app. In the iTunes store, it displays 25 per page. From 26 to 25, it’s a barrier” (TM) These design decisions on the part of the platform owner give rise to the nature of competition described as “hit-oriented”:

“One of the things that came over with the App Store, like to the music industry, you get a hit song, you get up the popularity chart, then it dies of, it might become classic . . . same with the iTunes, which is very ‘hit’ oriented, so you need to push it to all the reviewers, magazines, have people talk about it on Twitter, Blog, because you only have several weeks, to pop on the chart.” (JV)

Developers also noted their belief that polished and well-designed apps are more likely to be featured by Apple. This belief is driven by the recognition that Apple has traditionally emphasized aesthetics and form, an appreciation for polished design, a focus on user experience and product quality. This tradition, starting with the Mac OS X application, has been extended to mobile applications on the App Store.

A critical aspect of the design of the App Store is that it has greatly changed the way in which developers interact with end-users, by contrast to traditional forms of feedback present in the software product industry. Through access to the distribution channel and the review mechanisms on the App Store, the feedback loop between developers and users has become quick, informative, and influential, while allowing developers to take ownership and pride in their work:

“While I know that my apps have reached tens of thousands (and in one instance hundreds of thousands) of users, I really take great pleasure in every single customer email I get (good or bad), because that means someone out there really cares about what I do. While I got a lot of emails about the failed update, most users were quick to write that they liked the app, and that they were very disappointed that it was broken. This is the kind of feedback that makes the hard work worthwhile. Getting feedback is something that you rarely experience as a software developer working in a large cooperation.” (Fieldwork)

While such feedback is useful and easily available in the marketplace, the design of the marketplace makes it hard for the developer to reach the individual user directly. This is due to the anonymous nature of the review system implemented by Apple, which poses barriers for the developer in terms of providing user support, a critical component in software development:

“The frustrating thing is unlike on TouchArcade, on iTunes, you can’t respond to their reviews. So there is absolutely no way to reach the person. And sometimes they are complaining maybe about a bug, and they hope you would fix, but you can’t talk to them, they’re like there is this problem, it’s terrible. We’re like, why don’t you email us? Because we have all these emails, email us and we’ll help you. Those are frustrating.” (NL)

Yet another challenge for developers, and indies in particular, in interacting with users on the App Store is “liability misattribution.” Easy access to apps on the App Store even to those with limited technical experience often leads to wrongful attribution of errors to the developer, especially in cases where the fault lies elsewhere in the technical ecosystem:

“Sometimes they’ll complain about things that are beyond our control, like the application doesn’t download or doesn’t install properly, and we have to refer them to Apple, or that they aren’t able to make the purchase transaction and again we’d have to refer them to Apple for stuf like that.” (JS)

The third and final aspect of the influence that Apple extends to indie app developers is the presence of the strong Mac Indie developer community and their identification with Apple’s culture. These developers write third-party applications for Apple’s desktop operating system, Mac OS X, initiated in the early 2000s. The Mac indie community has embraced and pursued Apple’s culture of emphasis on design and quality, and the community itself regards these goals as worthy of peer recognition in the community. When iOS was first made available for third-party development, Mac indie developers were among the first to start writing applications for this platform, given their knowledge of the operating system and programming language as well as understanding of the customer base from the Mac OS X products. Over time, Mac indie developers have influenced new iOS developers with their values and beliefs through socialization activities that occur online and ofline. The presence of an influential, pre-existing indie developer community and platform identification adds another layer of influence on iOS developers that is particularly characteristic of Apple.

These three elements together form the situational context that exerts influence on how indie iOS developers operate on the App Store. In Section 4.2, we describe the actual practices that developers use as they navigate conflict and coexistence between the two logics and how the three aspects of the platform owner influence illustrated above impact these practices. Note that Apple’s influence on indie app developers’ practices in diferent entrepreneurial areas is not equally distributed. While the impact is profound in some areas, it might be moderate in others, allowing developers to more fully enact field-level logics. We examine these issues in more detail next by considering individual practices consistent with the logics.

## 4.2. Professional and Market Logic Practices Within Entrepreneurial Areas

4.2.1. Developers’ App Store Practices Enacting the Professional Logic. We identify practices that emerge through our qualitative coding based on three entrepreneurial areas, i.e., app ideation, app execution, and app marketing, consistent with the entrepreneurial literature. Accordingly, developer practices and selected evidentiary quotes in the three entrepreneurial areas are presented in Table 5. We start with practices that reflect the field-level professional logic of software development and characteristics of the iOS ecosystem.

The first phase of the entrepreneurial process is app ideation when developers make decisions on the types of applications to be developed and features to be included. A recurring theme from developers identifying with professional logic is the choice to write software that fulfills their own needs or relates to their own passion. In our interviews, indie developers stated that when they work on familiar domains or on apps they personally need, they tend to use the app more often and constantly improve it. Specifically, developers design the user interface they found satisfactory, create the flow of the app that best matched their use habits, and improved the innate engineering quality of the software by finding and fixing bugs themselves. The second stage of activity, app execution, is about development efort on release, maintenance, updates, and app portfolio strategy. Developers anchoring on professional logic set high engineering standards before shipping apps. While software training institutionalizes the engineering aspect of the software, Apple’s tradition of appreciation for design and quality concurrently influences developers’ decisions. Apple provides design guidelines for third-party app developers and recognizes well-designed software with their annual design awards. Developers also look up to Apple products for design inspiration. Participant JV admires the detail-oriented aspect of Apple’s design language through socialization with the Mac indie community, and was aiming for the same in his own app. While working on a drawing app at the time our fieldwork took place, he was eager to show of what he had accomplished in terms of app details, being meticulous about app quality: “I might have to make it brighter, the paper would probably have texture to it, the ink will look like ink.” Developer NS also emphasized this:

“When we release an app, we say, it’s gotta have a beautiful user interface, it’s gotta be intuitive, and it’s gotta be crisp. When someone looks at it, they have to say “this looks nice.” You also have to look at what Apple produces, and just say is this something Apple would release, does this look good enough to be an Apple product.” (NS)

Within app marketing, developers influenced by and enacting professional logic rely on peers to achieve marketing goals through peer support and peer recognition. Their philosophy is to market the apps among fellow developers first, and then have these power users and early adopters drive further downloads through positive word of mouth. During fieldwork, we observed that developers would endorse each other’s apps through online channels such as blogs and Twitter as well as ofline venues, such as meet-ups and social events. Developers value not only peer recognition but also recognition from high-status peers. Positive recommendations from popular or elite developers convey honor and the possibility of app visibility and sales. Developers’ dependence on high-status peers in their marketing endeavors is grounded in their belief in building high quality apps, consistent with professional logic. In such cases, app quality tends to be closely tied to developers’ personal reputation, and reputation is key in community-based networks such as those observed among indies.

The flipside to this marketing approach is that developers tend to extend such quality-based peeracceptance marketing strategy to the developer-user relationship. Assuming that end users have the ability to discover a good product on their own, developers believe that customer acquisition will automatically follow in an organic manner, and refrain from initiating communication with end users unless specific support needs arise.

4.2.2. Developers’ App Store Practices Enacting the Market Logic. During ideation, developers enacting market logic focus on developing apps with a massmarket appeal, by contrast to a focus on their personal needs. The rationale is that these apps are more likely to achieve wide adoption and subsequently climb onto top charts, which feed more downloads. For instance, such developers are likely to spend a considerable amount of time keeping track of the top-chart apps and researching current app trends in the App Store, and use this information to drive their own choice of apps to develop. Some developers also practice “copycatting,” i.e., learning from and mimicking existing hit apps. In the App Store, it is common to see “cheats” and “walkthroughs” for top-ranking games, which provide users with guides and tricks for the game play to advance levels or achieve high scores, and apps with deceivingly similar names and content to hit apps.

With respect to app execution, developers identifying with market logic embrace the idea of eficiency and time to market, which are distinct from developers enacting professional logic with its attendant focus on quality, design, and aesthetics. Given the scale of competition and the number of new apps that are introduced over time, fighting for visibility on the App Store is extremely challenging. Therefore, many developers engage in quick development and try every means to claim a spot in a new development platform or a less crowded app category. The rapid rate of technological change provides many opportunities for the nimble developer to exercise a “rush” strategy. For instance, the introduction of the iPad allowed indie developers to quickly port existing apps to the new platform, even if the app was better suited to the mobile phone. In addition to fierce competition on the App Store, Apple’s policies also influence developers’ app execution strategies. Because Apple reserves the right to reject or pull apps in seemingly capricious ways as part of its gatekeeper role, there is significant recognition of the unpredictability of app sales on the App Store, leading to excessive experimentation on the part of developers. This involves frequent app launch and limiting the level of efort on each app launched. As to apps’ design, instead of creating original graphics or well-thought-out user interfaces (UI) through significant and painstaking attention to details, market logicinfluenced developers tend to purchase stock images for app icons and make do with less well-developed aesthetics and UI design.

During app marketing, developers are influenced by market recognition, a market logic value. Essentially, the objective here is to introduce an app and achieve a “hit,” i.e., obtaining visibility through the top charts on the App Store in the first few days, and extending the app’s top chart lifetime once it reaches the top chart. To achieve this, developers aggressively use competitive pricing, advertising, and solicit influential bloggers and review sites. Subsequently, they release a stream of constant updates to the apps to encourage positive customer reviews. Finally, they try to increase the discoverability of the app through techniques such as in-app advertising, App Store Search Engine Optimization, and cross-app promotions.

Another significant component of the market-logic based approach to marketing involves proactive interaction with end users. As mentioned above, the communication mechanism between the developer and users provided by Apple is limited by design. In addition, end users in general lack knowledge about the relationships between third-party developers, Apple, and additional service providers. Recognizing this, and realizing that direct communication with end users may remedy user confusion and enhance trust, savvy indie developers find creative ways to circumvent Apple’s store design. These include directing end users to developers’ websites, social networking sites, and influential blogs. Some developers are also active participants in user forums, directly responding to and providing support to users. A positive outcome of such open communication is that end users take on a participatory role in the development of the apps wherein they suggest feature updates, evaluate such updates, and even issue clarifying information on behalf of the developer to other users, thereby benefitting the market-focused app developer and the app’s downloads.

The preceding discussion clearly suggests the diverging approaches taken by developers in each entrepreneurial area, as well as the potential for contradiction. For instance, the “hit marketing” approach in market logic is inconsistent with the “passive marketing” approach adopted in professional logic. In some areas, this contradiction is more pronounced and apparent while in others, the diferences coexist. Below we describe these dynamics between the two logics.

## 4.3. Logic Conflict and Its Impact on App Developers’ Practices

We find that logic conflict is reflected in developers’ disagreement or disapproval of practices associated with the opposing logic. Our fieldwork suggests this to be particularly salient in app execution and marketing.

During app execution, while professional logic suggests polished app design and development as well as continual quality improvement, the uncertainty of sales on the App Store could easily render this practice costly, suboptimal, and unrewarding. Developer DS neatly vocalizes the inherent conflict:

“I think there are a lot of people are more design purist, or engineering purist. Their goal is to make something beautiful, or make something cool, like technically, the code is really cool inside the way they do it. My goal in this is not like aesthetic, my goal is to make a living and support my family and support my employees’ family on the apps we make, so that changes our priorities, that’s why we release things so quickly, we write simpler apps. It’s not just developing for developing’s sake.” (DS)

On the other hand, app execution informed by market logic suggests many quick apps, jettisoning apps that get no traction in the marketplace and quick updates, each of which saves valuable developer time and efort by allowing the market to respond quickly. These strategies, however, tend to fall significantly short on core software engineering standards as well as Apple’s design guidelines for third-party developers, a sentiment voiced by several of our interviewees. “Releasing an app with just a couple of wallpapers?”, Developer

TM was dismissive of a wallpaper app, costing 99 cents without substantive originality, representative of excessive experimentation.

During the app marketing phase, developers enacting professional logic tend to market apps to the developer community rather than end users as they value peer support and recognition. This practice is deemed inefective by those who identify with the market logic. Developer JS expressed his opinion:

“I’ve noticed people buy advertising on sites like iphoneDevSDK. I never understood that, unless you’re making a product that’s oriented towards developers. I don’t see a lot of value in doing promotion of the applications to the developer community itself. Developers more than anybody are keenly aware of what’s out there on the App Store, what’s doing well and what’s not. So I think it’s way more important to be promoting a product to consumers than to developers.” (JS)

By comparison, the “hit” marketing approach consistent with market logic is perceived to be opportunistic behavior by those enacting professional logic. Within the developer community, excessive hit-oriented marketing does not provide adequate legitimacy to the developer who uses these methods, as developer JV stated:

“I don’t want to have anything to do with it. I don’t think it’s common, not even possible to make 3 or 4 apps a year. I don’t want to be dependent on the hits; you have to have shorter cycle. Hopefully you will make a good hit out of every cycle. If I take a year to develop an app, if it’s not a hit, then I’m screwed.” (JV)

While the “hit-marketing” strategy in particular stems from the competitive App Store environment, developers identifying with professional logic have systematically voiced mistrust of this strategy, and indeed of the need for marketing activities in general. App developers who are influenced by professional logic ideologically resist the idea of active consumer marketing, interpreting it as superficial compared to more tangible work such as programming and coding. Participant NS provided this viewpoint, vocalizing the implicit contradiction manifest between the professional and market logics:

“My experiences have led me to believe that most of the time marketing is not helpful to a company. It feels all gimmicky to me, and maybe it’s just as a developer, I’m susceptible to those things more than other people, but I notice gimmicks, like I can see all this person did is tweet this contest again where they were giving out a Macbook Air, great. Another person flooding my stream on Twitter of content I don’t care about. We’re trying not to be gimmicky, and we try to just produce an awesome product that hopefully people will love, and that’s like our philosophy.” (NS)

## 4.4. Logic Coexistence and Its Impact on App Developers’ Practices

Not all conflict inherent between the two field logics in the App Store context results in vocalized instances of conflict. In many cases, disagreement and misalignment between logics operating on the embedded agent are reconciled through what we call the process of “logic synthesis,” reflecting logic coexistence. We define logic synthesis as the processes in which indie app developers engage as they compromise or revise their practices from their focal logic to the opposing logic. Synthesis can also occur through the grafting of practices of the opposing logic on those practices that the developer enacts by virtue of adhering to her focal logic. In addition, we argue that synthesis can occur from both focal logics in the App Store context. In both these cases, the developer is seen to move to a more hybrid position with respect to focal logics. We discuss developers’ synthesis practices using this working definition across the specific entrepreneurial areas. All developers do not feel the need to manage logic coexistence in equal measure. Therefore, in addition to identifying synthesis practices, we delineate factors that appear to facilitate or inhibit synthesis practices. Both factors are shown in Figure 2.

4.4.1. Factors Facilitating and Inhibiting Logic Synthesis Under the Influence of the Platform Ecosystem. With regard to factors facilitating logic synthesis, the literature on logic conflict management indicates that entrepreneurs operating in institutionally plural environments tend to be motivated by entrepreneurial pressures, defined as the necessity to ensure the survival and performance of the entrepreneurial venture (Tschang 2007). In our fieldwork, we also find that entrepreneurial pressures compel developers to adopt practices that may, hitherto, not have been viewed as legitimate or necessary. In addition to entrepreneurial pressures, our analysis reveals that entrepreneurial learning and community knowledge acquisition need also work to enhance the practice of synthesis. Entrepreneurial learning, echoing the experiential learning and search framing in the organizational learning literature (Levitt and March 1988), addresses entrepreneurs’ incremental adaptations in beliefs, practices, and routines in response to feedback about outcomes from their external environments over time. By contrast, community knowledge acquisition need denotes the communal knowledge-sharing characteristics of indie app developers, viewing software developers as a community of practice.<sup>7</sup>

In terms of logic synthesis inhibitors, our fieldwork helped to identify two factors, both pertaining to app marketing. First, we were surprised to observe that iOS developers’ socialization with the Mac indie developer community tended to inhibit synthesis in iOSrelated app marketing. Because the Mac indie market is still dominated largely by professional logic, where personal networks and relationships are critical, Mac developers who also develop iOS apps, or iOS developers with deep linkages to the Mac community find it less important to adopt or graft practices of market logic in their marketing activities:

“The first thing I did is that I announced it in the Chicago C4 Conference in front of about 200 people. One of the tech bloggers for the Mac Space John Gruber was at this conference, he saw it, he linked it to his site, just from this guy, and at this weekend, I got 2,000 hits for my site. So I think there are two diferent ways, there’s I want to build a bunch of buzz about my app, trying to get in the top 10 of the App Store, and ride that wave out. And there’s another that no, I’m going to focus my attention in building a good quality product. The idea is that if you focus on building something that’s tight, that’s quality, that has a good crafted experience, all you need to do is get one of these people in these big sites to use it, love it, and then write about it. And in that sense you’re not propositioning people, and they are doing it for you.” (RR)

The other factor inhibiting synthesis arose from growing opportunities in the labor market for iOS developers. The App Store environment allows many indie developers to create apps on a part-time basis, while maintaining full-time positions. The rapid growth of the app economy created significant consulting opportunities for developers with iOS development skills, thereby dampening the need to synthesize conflicting logics on their own apps. Developer JR had a popular app WZ, but did not face a compelling need to engage in App Store marketing through “crisis management,” given alternative opportunities:

“Now on the App Store it only shows the reviews for the current version. So for developers you really have to stay on top of that. WZ, last time I checked, there are 2 reviews for the current version. People are disgruntled because it lacks something that it doesn’t claim to have. So people see those 2 reviews and it really impacts the downloads. If I were a better businessman, I would be pushing out a new update as soon as possible to get those bad reviews of, I would be doing everything in my power to get some reviews. I need to get those two of that page, I need to manage that. I’m not, and it’s a problem.” (JR)

Note that the platform owner, specifically in the case of Apple, plays a role in the presence of synthesis inhibitors. While we only observe that socialization with the Mac indie community inhibits logic synthesis in app marketing, the influence of this community is potentially felt in app execution as well. The Mac indie community’s values and beliefs about quality, peer recognition, and status, strongly afect how apps are executed, preventing developers from considering market-logic based practices for execution. Similarly, when considering the impact of alternative opportunities for app development, these are closely linked to Apple’s leadership position in the mobile sector. Apple’s success here breeds opportunities for app developers, allowing them to avoid the need to synthesize, if any. In Section 4.4.2, we embed these factors into the discussion of logic synthesis across three entrepreneurial areas, as shown in Figure 3.

## 4.4.2. The Mechanisms of Synthesis.

Synthesis in App Ideation: Inner, Outer Evaluations and “Eating Your Own Dog Food.”<sup>8</sup> In the app ideation phase, some developers allow the process to be equally influenced by developing apps to fulfill personal needs as well as by addressing market needs. This results from a process of synthesis which, by our definition, occurs from both focal logics. For professionallogic driven developers, entrepreneurial pressure and learning push them towards logic synthesis in app ideation, in the form of “inner” and “outer” evaluations. Market-logic driven developers synthesize, alternatively, primarily through a process informally termed “eating your own dog food,” under the influence of entrepreneurial pressures.

Inner evaluation is realized through being self-analytical or peer critiquing. Synthesizing developers enacting professional logic compromise on their preferred ideas or functionalities to work on, to reduce the potential risk of developing apps with little or no market appeal. Such pressures forced developer TM to think twice about what to develop before starting the project:

“I think everyone has ideas. You need to be able to filter out your own ideas. I had A LOT OF ideas. And I look at them and I go, wait a minute, that one wouldn’t have mass appeal, why would I do that. I mean I’ve definitely thought and definitely heard of ideas that I thought were good ideas, but you know, would a lot of people buy it, if I say no, then I just don’t think it’s really worth the efort.” (TM)

Similarly, having his first two apps fail, developer JS realized that the market’s tepid response may have been due to his lone-ideation approach. Learning from failure thus provided him with the motivation to revise his strategy by heeding his peer developers’ advice for his future apps:

“For my next app, I’m going to get a couple of people, I think you can fool yourself about what’s the best way to do things if you’re the only one deciding that. And if other people think your ideas aren’t great enough to wanna work on them, they probably aren’t that great. In any case, they’re not gonna put in the extra hour. Examined by more than one peer advice will be really helpful.” (JS)

Outer evaluation synthesis consists of leveraging users input through testing before releasing the app to the general public. User feedback at this stage can improve software usability, enhance those functionalities that appear more attractive, and thereby ensure loyalty from early adopters. However, engaging users early also requires developers to modify the software if needed, which may mean letting go of features that developers initially planned or were committed to. Developer RR shared this viewpoint:

“Testing is really the counterbalance between developer’s needs and consumers’. Think about who you wanna sell it to, and get it in front of those people as soon as possible, because you won’t be able to guess what they want, they will always surprise you. You want this, gotta be this way. They will tell you the other way . . . feedback is the key.” (RR)

By contrast, synthesizing developers enacting the market logic emphasized that while it is critical for an app to address a market need, it is also important to build something that developers themselves like or find useful. This practice of synthesis is an instance of grafting elements from professional logic to market logic; developers refer to this as “eating your own dog food.” Doing so leads to systematic use of the app by the developer himself (similar to a user), which facilitates bug and usability fixes early on. On the App Store, every new app gets its moment to shine on the “new release” chart because getting on this chart earns eyeballs from a massive user base. However, the benefits from this exposure are short-lived due to the large number of new apps released every day. Any impressions of bad quality or inadequate attention to programming defects noted by users, leading to poor reviews, could nullify all of the development efort on the app. Therefore, recognition of this entrepreneurial reality is cited in encouraging developers to fully use their own apps, thereby testing it in the manner that an average user would, as expressed by a developer of broad-based calendar and alarm apps:

“We’re doing a Calendar replacement. I’m using this as my main app for calendar, obviously looking for bugs and stuf, but I’m kind of living out the bugs so that my customers eventually don’t have to. And my developers are having good test cases because I have more calendar events than anyone in the universe. They are like no one is having more events like you do.” (KY)

Synthesis in App Execution: Emotional Detachment with Own Technology and Increased Emphasis on Design. In the app execution phase, we find elements that are simultaneously in conflict and coexistence. We find that developers vocalize criticism of excessive experimentation, or an excessive focus on quality to the detriment of cost, as execution strategies. However, we also observe that developers are willing to synthesize between the conflicting demands of app quality, on one hand, and business or economic eficiency, on the other. Based on our definition of synthesis through focal logic compromise/revision or opposing logic grafting, developers enacting professional logic synthesize through emotionally detaching from their own technology and features, while those enacting market logic do so through greater attention to app design and aesthetics.

Entrepreneurial pressure and learning motivate developers enacting professional logic to synthesize, and this synthesis is manifested in three stages in the execution phase, i.e., app development, app release, and app update decisions. During app development, synthesis occurs around developers’ decisions to build apps from the ground up versus leveraging existing technologies. The latter helps increase development eficiency, whereby developers graft elements from market logic. Developer TM observed this on his very first app. He wrote the building blocks of the app by himself even though reusing frameworks provided by Apple could have saved much efort and time. In some cases, developing every aspect of an app from scratch is preferred (and may even be eficient) since adopting new APIs (Application Program Interfaces) or functionality requires mastering new material. However, not leveraging well-maintained and widely adopted technologies can lead to high in-house maintenance costs later on. Logic synthesis here implies that indie developers relinquish the “not-invented-here” approach and leverage technologies from the larger developer community to achieve business eficiency.

During the app release stage, developers face the question of when to stop development and publish the app on the market. The professional logic ideal type suggests that the app be published when it is polished and of high quality. However, for full-time developers without other sources of income, entrepreneurial pressures compel them to forego new features to ship the app sooner:

“Like the WM application, it was pretty much done, and I was just working on supporting rotations, trying this way, this way, it doesn’t rotate correctly. I spent like 2 weeks working on it. At the end, I’m like, you know we had to cut out rotation . . . I wanna ship things that are good, but sometimes you have to see the forest through the tree. Sometimes you focus so much on this one little thing, and then you look back and you just spent thousands of dollars trying to get this little one thing right. So maybe think bigger, more strategically.” (NL)

Developer SJ, who was propelled by entrepreneurial learning and market pressure, also exhibited logic synthesis by cutting app features. He was involved in a trademark dispute for six months, and it was finally settled with SJ selling his app name, with significant losses of cost and time. Learning from this episode about trademark dispute costs, for his next app he decided to jettison less critical features and focus on time to market:

“Basically the first person who has it in commerce, they are the first person who gets the name. And if somebody names their app PP tomorrow and I hadn’t put it out, and also I gotta change my name and everything. So, and again it’s all because of the trademark experience I had with MQ, and so I was like, you know what, I gotta put it out. It doesn’t matter what it looks like. I just gotta get the name. So what I did, I ripped of all the voiceover stuf, and there are some screens, they didn’t look so good going out, but they didn’t need to be there, so I ripped out whatever didn’t look good, to kind of put it out, and people loved it.” (SJ)

In addition to app development and release, logic synthesis takes place during the software update decision-making process. Here, for developers enacting professional logic, synthesis generally implies a “move-on” attitude, wherein developers opt to stop responding to their own desires for enhancement on an existing app with limited market demand, contrary to the professional logic ideal type. Developer JN explained this:

“I spent a lot of time to get network work properly after the initial release, because now people can compete head to head, and it’s something people will like. And it was really dificult . . . so I spent A LOT of time getting it to work right, and it works really well, but almost no pump in sales, something I thought people are going to appreciate, they don’t really appreciate that much . . . was very large time investment for very little return.” (JN)

Developers identifying with market logic also engage in synthesis practices in app execution, though for identifiably diferent reasons, i.e., their experience with platform owner’s policies and user interactions. Specifically, they move away from the “rush” strategy salient in market logic practices, and invest resources in product design, detailed UI, and aesthetics, to extents deemed unnecessary in the market logic ideal type. Developer TM’s original strategy “was to release as fast as you can, and update as fast as you can,” was a manifestation of the market logic. He stated: “Before, if I had a few bugs, I’d just send it up there; I would cut corners and constantly add features.” However, in November 2009, Apple changed a key policy on the App Store: it began to only allow the first version of the app to appear on the release chart instead of every new version.<sup>9</sup> This change put crudely engineered apps at risk since user response to the first version became critical for the app’s subsequent performance. The developer community stressed this repeatedly—post-policy change, all apps got one shot only at being viewed on the new-release chart. For market-logic driven developers, this necessitated a process of synthesis wherein elements of design, aesthetics, and quality had to be incorporated into decisions around app execution, as expressed by developer TM: “I do more initial development now; you need to spend more time, polish it up, test it, market it, appropriately.”

In addition to learning from the platform owner’s policy change, end-user feedback motivates marketoriented developers to consider software design and aesthetics, thereby enabling synthesis. As discussed earlier, the developer-user feedback loop is made possible by active user participation; a responsive developer can gain considerably by following up on quick feedback. Developer KR’s recording app was popular thanks to its mass-market appeal and powerful features. However, the icon design and user interface generated many complaints, forcing him to reconsider his app execution strategy:

“I did everything on my own. Now that I’m making more money, I think I’m gonna hire a graphic artist to clean up the user interface, change all the graphics and design better icons. I’ve got several reviews saying that the user interface is kind of clunky.” (KR)

To a large extent, the iOS development landscape itself is dialectic, requiring practices aligned with professional and market logics. On one hand, competition and Apple’s unpredictable policies on the App Store prevent indie app developers from investing beyond a certain point in terms of polish and aesthetics. On the other hand, Apple’s appreciation of polished apps, in addition to the feedback provided by end users, reward developers’ efort towards thoughtful design and aesthetics. Thus, a simple yet acceptable synthesis strategy for developers, at least during app development and release, is to make “simple yet polished apps” (TM), or “a fully-polished product that doesn’t include all of the features you expect to include in the product eventually” (JS).

Synthesis in App Marketing: Peer Partnership, Niche Marketing, and Peer Community Contribution. In the app marketing stage, recall that the “hit” marketing and “organic” marketing strategies tend to be in conflict. However, in certain cases, developers can strike a balance between organic word-of-mouth marketing based on peer recognition, on one hand, and a consumeroriented marketing strategy based on market recognition, on the other. Again, as logic synthesis occurs from both logics here, we see that professional logic-driven developers synthesize through engaging peer partnership and niche marketing, while market logic-driven developers synthesize through peer community contribution.

The App Store’s promotion structure, such as top charts, highlights the importance of in-App Store marketing to achieve visibility and sales. One synthesis strategy used by professional-logic enacting developers is to partner with peers who have popular apps on the App Store. In doing so, developers still rely on support from the developer community, a feature observed in the professional logic ideal type. However, the collaborative partnership allows their product to reach the mass market on the App Store, in accordance with market logic. Thus, under entrepreneurial pressure to succeed, these developers graft practices of market logic without compromising entirely on their own professional logic. Developer DC shared his experience:

“One of the things we partner with FS company who has a significant presence in the market place is they have a network of head-to-head play games, so by partnering with them, we can sort of use their existing distribution network, and their network of players, as long as the game is of similar quality, and meets the interests of their customer base, we should be able to be successful, should be able to step on their foundation.” (DC)

Peer marketing, however, can sometimes backfire when there is potential conflict with functionality provided by the platform. Developers whose apps compete in content with Apple’s native apps face questions of legitimacy from both the developer community and the general consumer base. App Store legitimacy becomes particularly problematic among peers who strongly identify with Apple’s culture or those who view stepping on the platform’s turf as risky behavior. One of our interviewees, Developer RH, wrote a music play-list app intended to replace native functionality on Apple’s iPod. This move led to dissenting voices in the developer community, suggesting that the developer “doesn’t get the platform” and that “You don’t redesign Apple’s stuf.” When sharing the app with his developer peers, RH did not receive the support he expected from his peer marketing activities. Having learned a lesson from this experience, RH chose to target his app to hardcore music fans only. Even though such niche marketing reduced sales potential, the app was marketed towards the consumer base for whom it was built. Even better, the app received very positive reviews on the App Store. In such cases, synthesizing professional logic-driven developers partly relinquish the activities with which they identify (peer marketing practice here), and engage in niche marketing to reach the target user.

Market-logic driven developers engage in synthesis during app marketing through contributions to the peer community. Software development as a profession traditionally relies on teams and communities of practice for knowledge sharing due to the range of technologies involved and the pace of technological advancement. On the App Store, the need for knowledge sharing is particularly salient given the rapid change of technology and the App Store business environment, as stated by developer KY:

“I think the biggest thing is that it’s so brand new that things change like every single day. There’s just all constant state of flux, we’re all trying to figure out what’s the best way to do this. The other thing is it does require complete diferent mindset to design and create user experiences for mobile applications. It’s not click. It’s tapping, it’s pinching and zooming, it’s swiping right or left.” (KY)

Several participants in our study lamented that the indie work style lacks knowledge exchange opportunities with peers on a regular basis. Therefore, interacting with a peer community (online or of-line) becomes particularly critical for business success. Developer DS exemplifies this type of synthesis: He pursues a “hit-oriented” marketing strategy by focusing on App Store rankings and in-store visibility. At the same time, he contributes extensively to the iOS community locally and virtually. He holds “Ofice Hours” to invite local developers to chat about work and documents his iOS development and business experience on his blog. Without compromising on market logic, he extends his practices in accord with professional logic, thereby earning positive word of mouth for his contribution to the community and increasing his marketing efectiveness.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

“Don’t build products. Build platforms,” exhorts a news article.<sup>10</sup> Yet another news article featured four large players in the software industry, Facebook, Google, Amazon, and Apple, characterizing them as platforms rather than technology providers.<sup>11</sup> The differentiating element in this generation of software providers is the ability to build platform ecosystems, incorporating an extensible codebase, a marketplace, interested users, and application developers who contribute their own innovations. An increasing number of these developers are independent third-party development firms, or “indie” developers who, despite their importance to these platform ecosystems, have remained understudied. The lens through which the literature has treated such platforms has been economic or technical, while the organizational and social aspects of the developer community have been largely ignored (Gawer 2014). In this paper, we focus on the specific processes that developers use to participate on such platform ecosystems, as they navigate through platform owner’s technological, market, and cultural influence, and the competing imperatives from the profession and the market.

Examining this domain through an institutional logics perspective is especially useful in explaining the efect of societal or field-level cultural beliefs on individual actors’ decisions and behaviors (Thornton 2002). Building on the theories of logics pluralism and embedded agency, we postulate that iOS app developers operate in a pluralistic environment where two field logics, i.e., market and professional, operate. While research has addressed questions of conflict between the market and quality considerations, typically through the use of ISD methodologies for larger organizations, we extend this work to the mobile app context where individual developers face such conflicting forces. While some developers enact processes consistent with the market or the professional logic as they ideate, execute, and market their apps, we also see practices of synthesis, wherein developers compromise on their own logic, revise their processes to accommodate or graft elements of the opposing logic. We identify specific processes across the spectrum of app ideation, execution, and marketing.

A particularly unique aspect of the iOS platform we study is the overarching influence of the platform owner, Apple, representing an additional institutional player that influences individual behavior through its policies, processes, and culture. Drawing on the concept of situationism, we argue that indie developers’ identities and practices depend on the two field-level logics as well as their interactions with the platform owner. Beyond answering the “what do app developers do” question, we provide answers to the “why” question by identifying the role of the platform owner itself. Apple occupies the foreground in terms of control and governance on the marketplace and influences many of the work practices we see on the App Store. In its dual role as technology provider and marketmaker, Apple deeply influences developers through its adherence to aesthetics and design, its technical design of the App Store and the implications for store competitiveness and user-developer interactions, its role as a gatekeeper/curator, and its links to the Mac OS X indie community. These influences, alongside the enacting of logics, jointly influence the work-processes that developers exhibit in their dominant logic as well as through logic synthesis. That said, we recognize that the platform owner’s role in enabling synthesis as well as influencing the dominant logics requires further theoretical development. While we discuss how the platform owner influences developers’ practices, we do not directly theorize about how platform owners may directly evolve competing logics or how diferent ecosystems may lead to varying forms of synthesis. Future research focusing on the platform owner is needed to fully understand their efects on logic pluralism and embedded agency in platform ecosystems.

While the notion of opposing forces, viewed through a lens of institutional logics, is particularly relevant to our setting, there are alternative theories that study sources of conflict in organizational and firm settings in the literature. One such research stream pertained to the conflict between exploration and exploitation in organizations (Gupta et al. 2006). An alternative stream of work addresses firm responses to hypercompetition in terms of whether bureaucratic firms focused on eficiencies outperform flexible firms that are responsive to the market (Volberda 1996); indeed, since the iOS App Store is hypercompetitive, these conflicts are likely to be salient in app development firms. In ISD itself, there are alternative approaches to managing development processes in terms of governance, control portfolios, and social processes underlying development activity (Iivari et al. 1998, Maruping et al. 2009). Though these alternative perspectives are valid (and remain interesting avenues for future work), our inductive coding process indicated very early that logics was appropriate for our setting, especially since we were studying indie developers and not software firms per se.

Because one of the contributions of our work here is the process of logic synthesis observed on the App Store, we explore how our conceptualization of synthesis is diferent from other similar processes discussed in the literature. First is the concept of loose coupling in Berente and Yoo (2012). Beyond diferences in context, Berente and Yoo (2012) study software (ERP, enterprise resource planning) implementation, while we study software product development; the theoretical underpinnings for the two concepts are distinct. Loose coupling distinguishes between the performance of a given practice and the presentation of that practice. In their study, Berente and Yoo (2012) maintain that loose coupling comes from contradictions between the logic of the managerial rationalism, associated with the enterprise system during the implementation, and other logics that individuals draw on to guide their practices, such as the logics of scientific professionalism, project management profession, and organizational persistence. Individuals ceremonially conform to the logic of managerial rationalism to give the appearance of compliance and maintain legitimacy, while adopting various forms of loose coupling strategies. By comparison, logic synthesis as we describe it is based on the powerful influence of the platform owner that forces conformity to its technological and market governance requirements, as well as the logics enacted by the developers based on their own individual agencies. Developers synthesize to enhance their own entrepreneurial performance in a competitive, yet community-oriented environment or to reconcile contradictions in their own working style. Unlike the imperative to engage in loose coupling to satisfy managerial requirements in an organization, indie entrepreneurs see no need to pay ceremonial attention to the platform owners’ rules and regulations or those of their own logics, while actually engaging in another set of practices, unlike employees of larger firms.

Yet another diference between our perspective of logic synthesis and existing observations of logic reconciliation in the literature pertains to the directionality of movement by the agent. Historical perspectives on logic conflict over time show a logic (such as the community banking logic; Marquis and Lounsbury 2007) eventually supplanted by an alternative logic (such as the national banking logic). In the context of market and professional logics, Thornton (2002) shows how elements of professional logic (editorial logic) are compromised for the corresponding market logic. However, in our context, we observe a strong imprint of professional and market logic in the software development, and by extension the app development, field. In addition, through the mechanisms of focal logic compromise and revision and opposite logic grafting, we note instances of logic synthesis operating in both directions, i.e., from professional to market and vice versa. In addition, unlike prior work, we see these processes within entrepreneurs, as opposed to diferent organizational actors. These activities speak to logic pluralism in the software development field, where market and professional logics still hold value.

## 5.1. Implications for Practice

Beyond the contributions of our analysis on logic enactment and synthesis on platform ecosystems, the “so what?” question remains to be answered, i.e., to enunciate the practical implications of our work. We start with the “platformization” of the software industry. There is increasing focus on making platform ecosystems efective and eficient (Tiwana et al. 2010) as well as interest in entrepreneurial activities on such ecosystems (Boudreau 2012) and the value that third-party complementors provide to themselves and to consumers. For these components of the platform ecosystem to work well, an understanding of how indie developers work and interact on such platform ecosystems is necessary, and a direct implication of our work in this paper. Here we adopt a neutral stance on whether it is beneficial for developers to be fully consistent with a dominant logic or to practice logic synthesis. We do not observe synthesis across all of our interviewees. To the extent that developers experience positive capital on the labor market or belong to the Mac OS X indie community, we see limited evidence of practices of logic synthesis. Therefore, we cannot inform the reader (or the developer) on the benefits or drawbacks of synthesis. The literature on logic pluralism, however, suggests considerable value in grafting and in practicing reconciliation, in diferent forms (Reay and Hinings 2005, Townley 2002). In addition, recent work has suggested that open innovation techniques may actually be socially desirable since they allow a form of synthesis between professional and market logics (Lakhani 2015). Further examination of these trade-ofs and strategies in these contexts provides many opportunities for future research.

Our findings also have practical implications for indie entrepreneurs in other professional arenas that combine a significant artistic, creative, and technical component. Increasingly, these professional arenas are associated with platform owners that provide core technology and infrastructure support to third-party complementors. Examples include 3D printing designers who leverage Shapeway’s industrial 3D printing technology and manufacturing capacity to create 3D printing products that they sell on Shapeway’s marketplace; craft beer brewers who do not have resources for running a brewery, provide beer recipes to Two Roads, a brewing company that ofers brewing facilities and economy of scale to small brewers who want to focus on creating and selling craft beer. While the specific instances of professional and market logic would vary for these platform ecosystems, our study suggests that it is necessary for independent creative workers to consider both logics in their entrepreneurial practices, as well as influences from platform owners and other developers.

The impact of societal logics at the level of the field and the continuing influence of the platform owner are common elements and represent potential generalizations of our work here. However, these same features may also limit the extent to which our analysis can seamlessly extend to other contexts. Apple as a software platform owner exhibits a number of characteristics that other mobile platforms do not. First, the firm is well known for its tight control over third-party developers and the applications released on the App Store. Second, it has a strong Mac-based application indie developer community that has internalized Apple’s culture and also serves as the role model for the platform’s new mobile app indie developer community. Third, the firm exerts considerable influence on app developers using elements of professional logic (a preference for design and aesthetics as a primary goal) and market logic (top charts and overt competitiveness). In some cases, developers exert these pressures on each other by imbibing the “Apple philosophy.” Given these unique aspects of the iOS platform, generalizations of synthesis to other platform ecosystems must be carried out with care.

Our work points to several other avenues for future work. For example, there may be other factors that facilitate developers’ logic synthesis. First is the mediating role of product category; games, as a more competitive application category on the App Store, will likely incentivize synthesis compared to categories with lesser competition. Second, it may be interesting to formally study the performance implications of developers’ logic synthesis practices using large sample analyses. Would synthesized developers perform better on the App Store than those who do not? Additionally, would the form of governance used by the platform owner (contrasting the controls applied by Apple to the open model adopted by Google on its

Android platform, for instance) mediate any relationship between synthesis and performance? Finally, we have argued that the app economy is institutionally pluralistic; however, work in institutional logics also suggests that over time certain logics become dominant and drive out competing logics (i.e., Thornton 2002). It is too early to argue that this may occur in the software development field but, as the field grows more mature, such dynamics may occur, with corresponding parallels in the app economy. These represent many interesting questions that can be addressed on the newly emerging technology platforms; it is our hope that our work will spark more interest in the topic of institutional logics in the technology platforms context.
