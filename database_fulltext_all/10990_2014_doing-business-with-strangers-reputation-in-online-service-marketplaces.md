Antonio Moreno

Managerial Economics and Decision Sciences, Kellogg School of Management, Northwestern University, Evanston, Illinois 60208, a-morenogarcia@kellogg.northwestern.edu

Christian Terwiesch

Operations and Information Management, The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania 19104, terwiesch@wharton.upenn.edu

nline service marketplaces allow service buyers to post their project requests and service providers to bid for them. To reduce the transactional risks, marketplaces typically track and publish previous seller performance. By analyzing a detailed transactional data set with more than 1,800,000 bids corresponding to 270,000 projects posted between 2001 and 2010 in a leading online intermediary for software development services, we empirically study the effects of the reputation system on market outcomes. We consider both a structured measure summarized in a numerical reputation score and an unstructured measure based on the verbal praise left by previous buyers, which we encode using text mining techniques. We find that buyers trade off reputation (both structured and unstructured) and price and are willing to accept higher bids posted by more reputable bidders. Sellers also respond to changes in their own reputation through three different channels. They increase their bids with their reputation score (price effect) but primarily use a superior reputation to increase their probability of being selected (volume effect) as opposed to increasing their bid prices. Negative shocks in seller reputation are associated to an increase in the probability of seller exit (exit effect), but this effect is moderated by the investment that the seller has made in the site. We conclude that participants in this market are very responsive to the numerical reputation score and also to the unstructured reputational information, which behaves in a similar way to the structured numerical reputation score but provides complementary information.

Keywords: auctions; online service marketplaces; procurement; reputation

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. Motivation

Online reverse auctions have been used since the 1990s for procurement in large corporations, but recent technological developments have enabled small- and medium-sized enterprises and even individuals to use similar mechanisms to fulfill their service procurement needs. On the supply side, potential workers can use platforms that provide mechanisms that facilitate work transactions to offer their services in short-lived collaborations that, as predicted by Malone and Laubacher (1998), are becoming a viable alternative to traditional employment models. Platforms offering online service marketplaces matching buyers and sellers of services have proliferated (Economist, The 2010, Hipple 2010), with some of the leading examples being http://www.vworker.com, http://www.elance.com, http://www.freelancer.com, and http://www.odesk.com.

In online service marketplaces, buyers (firms or individuals) post tasks they would like to procure (e.g., the development of an iPhone application) and sellers (e.g., iPhone developers) bid for them. Such online service marketplaces present distinctive traits in contrast to their offline counterparts. Traditional markets often involve personal relationships that can generate trust. Online interactions are usually less socially embedded and more anonymous. In online service marketplaces, buyers have little information about bidders, and little control over their work, which leads to increased adverse selection and moral hazard problems and to a higher uncertainty regarding the outcome of the collaboration. This creates an opportunity for reputation to be developed online. Reputation creates a link between past behavior and the expectation of future behavior (Mailath and Samuelson 2006). The intermediary platforms typically offer a reputation system that keeps track of the buyers’ and bidders’ past behavior and facilitates trust (Resnick et al. 2000, Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002, Dellarocas 2003).

The existence of a reputation system that makes sellers’ actions a part of public information in the market presents an important innovation with respect to traditional labor markets, where information about worker performance is usually kept within the firm. Traditional labor markets are based in relatively longlived collaborations but relatively short-lived persistence of information. In contrast, in online service marketplaces the information about previous transactions is long lived, and prospective employers will have access to this information for as long as the worker remains on the site. This reduces transaction risks and has consequences for the viable size and duration of projects, allowing for very small (in cost) or very short (in time) projects to be contracted (e.g., short-lived, “spot” collaborations).

The performance of reputation systems is critically shaped by the behavioral response to them. In equilibrium, these systems must be informative in order for users to respond to them. It can be the case that sellers willingly participate in them (even when a bad reputational score might be realized, which generates useful variation in scores) or that the reputation system enforces a separating equilibrium, whereby “good” sellers are willing to participate while others drop out of the market. The focus of this paper is to understand how participants in online service marketplaces react to the information tracked by the reputation system.

Online service marketplaces are significantly different from online marketplaces for products (e.g., eBay), for which a substantial amount of research has studied the role of reputation (e.g., Bajari and Hortaçsu 2003, Resnick et al. 2006, Cabral and Hortaçsu 2010). Table 1 summarizes some of the main differences between them. Online service marketplaces as studied in this paper take the form of reverse auctions, where the sellers submit bids in response to buyers’ requests for project work. This allows sellers to use their reputation score to increase their pricing power, which is not possible in traditional product markets, where sellers do not have pricing power. Moreover, given the multiple bids with varying prices and reputation scores, the buyer faces a multiattribute auction. Hence, unlike in the commonly studied eBay setting, price alone is not likely to predict the winning bid (in our sample, more than 40% of the projects were not awarded to the lowest bid). As an illustration of the potential differences, consider an eBay seller with a superior reputation extracting a premium from it. This premium will reach the marketplace in the form of a higher transaction price. Consider now a seller with a superior reputation that is bidding for a project. This seller might choose not to charge a price premium but to gain a higher probability of being selected. If this is the case, reputation would result in a different project allocation, but not necessarily on higher transacting prices. Understanding this richer behavioral response to the reputation system in online service marketplaces is the central objective of this paper.

## 1.2. Contributions of This Paper

Despite the growing importance of online service marketplaces in changing the nature of work, and the special importance that reputation plays in the associated changes, there exists little empirical work analyzing the role of different types of reputational information in such settings.

One explanation for this limited empirical evidence in the study of reputation in online service marketplaces relates to the availability of data. Whereas in product auctions such as eBay, transactions are visible to the public, with the product being predictably rewarded to the highest bid, in online service marketplaces the bids are not visible to the public and the multiattribute nature of the auction makes predicting the winning bid difficult, if not impossible. Through collaboration with vWorker (formerly rentacoder.com), one of the leading marketplaces for software development and other business services, we have obtained access to an extensive proprietary transactional data set. Our data set includes more than 1,800,000 bids corresponding to more than 270,000 projects posted between 2001 and 2010 by 122,000 coders. This data set includes participants from more than 80 countries. In fact, more than 85% of the projects have the buyer and the winning seller located in different countries.

Our work considers two sources of reputational information that can be cumulated in the marketplace. The first one is the structured numerical score, which is readily available and summarizes the ratings of the previous transactions. The second one is an unstructured measure that is based on the verbal comments of previous buyers. We have access to the more than 100,000 comments left by buyers reviewing the performance of the seller after the conclusion of a project, and we encode the sentiment of the comments using text mining techniques. Along with its structured/ unstructured nature, the information differs in its accessibility and persistence. The average numerical reputation score is shown to all participants in the marketplace with no need to make any effort. The textual comments are available as well, but they are only visible when participants browse the history of transactions associated to a given user. At the same time, the impact of a bad rating on the average numerical reputation score can be compensated with good ratings, whereas bad textual comments are more persistent in the system, in the sense that they do not disappear and no aggregate average information is displayed. Our work studies how these two sources of reputational information affect market outcomes.

Besides studying the effect of structured and unstructured reputational information, we want to understand how sellers adjust their behavior depending on their own reputation. A unique feature of our data is that we observe all of the transactions in the market and during a long period of time, so that we are also able to follow buyers and sellers over time. This allows us to study how sellers adjust their bid setting strategy as their reputation changes. Although it is reasonable to expect that, all the rest being equal, buyers will prefer sellers with higher reputations, it is an empirical question whether sellers will leverage an increase in their reputation by increasing their prices or by increasing their likelihood of winning bids. In other words, it is not clear if an increase in the reputation of a seller will translate into higher prices in the market or if it will simply manifest by a different project allocation. It is also an empirical question whether seller exit is sensitive to the reputational information cumulated in the site. If sellers are more likely to exit after receiving a negative reputational shock, feedback will have a very important effect on the composition of the market.

To tackle these questions, we use a discrete choice framework to study how buyers choose between competing bids from different service sellers and how they trade off different attributes, focusing primarily on the role of reputation. Our econometric approach, together with the unique data set we assembled, allows us to make the following contributions.

First, we show that in the multiattribute auction context created by the online service marketplace we study, buyers trade off the cost of the service with the reputation of the seller (both structured and unstructured). We find a significant and large reputation premium. One additional point in the reputation score (out of 10) has approximately the same effect on bid choice as a reduction by one standard deviation in the bid amount. This extends the prior research on product auctions to reverse, multiattribute service auctions.

Second, we show that bidders internalize the fact that reputation is valuable to buyers and adjust their bidding strategy as they accumulate reputation. Reputation affects seller behavior and outcomes through three different channels. Bidders increase their bids as their reputation improves (price effect). However, bidders adjust their bid prices to a lesser extent than the buyers adjust choices. One additional point in the reputation score (in a 0–10 range) is associated with an increase in bid equivalent to 2.7% of the standard deviation of the bids received for a project. In other words, the sellers benefit from their increased reputation mostly through higher volume (volume effect) rather than through higher prices. A negative shock in the reputation of a seller significantly increases the probability that the seller leaves the market after that transaction (6.4% higher after a bad rating, more than 40% higher after a bad comment). This effect is less prominent when the seller has “invested” on the site (e.g., has obtained skill certifications or maintains a detailed user profile).

Third, we analyze how different circumstances moderate the effect of reputation on buyer’s choice. Our data present significant variation not only in reputation scores but also in other available information and attributes of the seller. This allows us to study how the effect of the reputation score interacts with other variables. We show that reputation is less important for sellers who have certified their skills by taking an online test, for sellers who are from the same country as the buyer, for sellers with whom the buyer has worked before, and for sellers who have a picture in their profile. We interpret these findings according to existing economic and behavioral theories. Although some similar results have been described in the recent literature on online labor markets, our work examines how the results generalize across different platforms (e.g., oDesk versus vWorker) and types of reputational information (structured versus unstructured).

An important aspect of this paper is that we study the effect of both structured and unstructured reputational information. We show that intangible aspects captured by our unstructured measure play an important role in buyer choice and seller behavior. Although these intangible aspects behave in a similar way to the structured numerical reputation score, we find that they provide complementary information that is not captured by the numeric reputation score.

## 2. Related Literature

Our study of reputation in online service marketplaces has close connections to literature streams in information systems, operations management, labor economics, and economics and management of organizations.

Reputation in Online Auctions. This paper is closely related to the information systems literature that studies reputation in online auctions and its effect on market outcomes. Most of the work in this domain has focused on product auctions, especially in the eBay market (Bajari and Hortaçsu 2003, Resnick et al. 2006, Cabral and Hortaçsu 2010, among others). Bajari and Hortaçsu (2004) review some of the most relevant papers, which overall find some evidence supporting the claim that seller reputation has a positive effect on prices in eBay auctions. Some recent work in information systems has focused on the role of online reputation systems and, more generally, online feedback mechanisms. For example, Bolton et al. (2004) perform an experimental investigation on the effectiveness of electronic reputation mechanisms, and Bakos and Dellarocas (2011) compare litigation and online reputation as quality assurance mechanisms. Dellarocas (2003, 2006) provide comprehensive reviews. Other papers address some of these issues empirically using observational data. For example, Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) study the effect of word of mouth on sales, and Dellarocas and Wood (2008) study the feedback mechanism at eBay and the impact of reciprocity and the informativeness of missing reviews. Our work contributes to this literature by analyzing the impact of reputation and feedback mechanisms in a service context. We contribute to the recent trend of work that uses text mining techniques to study the importance of user feedback and reviews (Ghose et al. 2009, Archak et al. 2011) by using similar techniques to classify the sentiment (positive or negative) of textual reviews about the performance of service providers and its impact on market outcomes.

Procurement Auctions. The operations management community has devoted considerable effort to study procurement auctions. Most of the papers describe analytical models (e.g., Elmaghraby 2000, 2004; Tunca and Zenios 2006). Pinker et al. (2003) provide a review of the literature related to the management of online auctions. Some recent work has also focused on service marketplaces (Allon et al. 2012) and markets for the procurement of innovation (e.g., Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Yang et al. 2011). Despite the abundance of theoretical models, there is very limited work studying online service marketplaces or procurement auctions empirically. Among those, Gefen and Carmel (2008) study offshoring and global trade in online service marketplaces, Snir and Hitt (2003) study the effects of cost bidding in Internet-based procurement of professional services, and Tunca et al. (2014) study online procurement auctions for legal services by General Electric. Our paper complements this literature in procurement auctions by analyzing the role of reputation in markets for service procurement.

Labor Economics. Recent work in labor economics has considered the role of information in online labor markets (Horton 2010), and this is an area of very active research (for a recent perspective, see Agrawal et al. 2013a). For example, Pallais (2014) studies the effect of revealing more information about workers’ abilities by conducting a field experiment in an online marketplace, where she is able to randomly assign different information conditions to workers she previously hired on the site. In a related paper, Stanton and Thomas (2012) analyze the role of intermediary agencies in screening candidates in online labor markets. Some work has focused on the global dimensions of these online labor markets. For example, Mill (2011) shows that employers use a worker’s country as a way to infer expected service quality, Agrawal et al. (2013b) find that the effect of verified experience information is more pronounced for contractors from less developed countries, and Ghani et al. (2012) show that diaspora connections are still important in online marketplaces.

Our contribution to this stream of research is twofold. First, we study some aspects that have not been considered in these studies, such as the role of unstructured information or the effect of information on exit. Second, we examine how some results of this literature generalize across different platforms (e.g., oDesk versus vWorker) and types of reputational information (structured versus unstructured). Most of the research in this stream of literature has used data from oDesk, one of the leading online labor markets. This is a marketplace where the modal contract is for hourly work. In contrast, our work uses data from vWorker.com, where most of the transactions are for entire projects. In that sense, our context is closer to the aforementioned studies in procurement auctions, where request for quotations are posted and potential suppliers bid for them. Our work contributes to bridge the gap between the literature on online labor markets, which has paid close attention to information, and the literature on procurement auctions, where there is limited empirical evidence of the effects of reputation.

## 3. Description of the Empirical Setting

## 3.1. vWorker.com

Our data comes from vWorker.com, an online intermediary that brings together buyers and sellers of software development services and other professional services. The company began operating as rentacoder.com, but later expanded the scope of services beyond software and changed its name.<sup>1</sup> Using the terminology of Table 1, vWorker could be described as a service marketplace implementing reverse multiattribute auctions where sellers bid for projects that buyers award at their discretion.

At vWorker, registered buyers (firms or individuals) submit projects to the intermediary seeking bids from coders. Along with this submission, the buyer provides a project specification, lists the required skills for a successful completion of the project, and sets a targeted deadline. Registered sellers (coders) read the descriptions of the projects. Depending on their relevant expertise and their overall interest in the project, a small fraction of coders then submits bids to the intermediary. Bidders can observe the current number and characteristics of participants in an auction, but they cannot see the competing bids of other coders. At a time predetermined by the buyer, the intermediary ends the auction and presents a catalog of all bids to the buyer. The buyer sees the bids, including prices, coder location, coder reputation (average of previous ratings), and the number of ratings the coder has received. With a click on the coder, the buyer can obtain more detailed information on the coder, including a picture of the coder as well as a history of the coder’s prior work alongside comments and ratings posted by previous buyers.

When choosing from the menu, the buyer thus observes all bids and has access to all information about the submitting coders. Based on this information, the buyer awards the project to one coder. At the moment of choice, the buyer potentially faces both adverse selection (are the coders qualified for the job?) as well as moral hazard (will the coders keep their promises?). The intermediary provides a set of measures to reduce these problems. First, the intermediary keeps a rating (reputation score) for each of the participants in the marketplace based on the feedback from historical transactions. It also provides the history of the textual comments left by previous buyers in the system. Second, the intermediary provides an arbitration system to resolve potential disputes should the service not meet the buyer’s expectations. When the buyer chooses the coder, the buyer submits the funds corresponding to the bid he has chosen to an escrow account managed by the intermediary. The money remains in escrow until the service has been carried out to the satisfaction of the buyer. Since the buyer can withhold payment if the project is not successfully completed, this mitigates some of the moral hazard problems.

Upon completion of the work (or after arbitration), the money (or in the case of arbitration, a fraction of the money) is transferred to the coder. Finally, the buyer has an opportunity to rate the coder, leading to an update in the coder’s reputation score, which is computed as the average of the previous ratings received by the coder. The buyer can also provide verbal comments (positive or negative) for the coder.

Turning our attention to the supply side of this market, coders make several decisions. First, they decide whether to bid for a project or not. If they decide to bid, they decide the amount they want to bid, and if they are awarded the project, they decide the level of effort they exert. Furthermore, coders can take tests on the site to certify their skills (for example, they can take a C++ exam to become a certified C++ coder). These certifications appear in their profiles and can be viewed by buyers. Coders can also be influenced by their own reputation when setting their bids.

Our empirical setting differs from oDesk, which is the marketplace that most of the papers studying online labor markets have used. The most salient difference between oDesk and vWorker.com is their modal type of contract. In oDesk, the usual type of contracts are for hourly work and the employees are compensated for their time, whereas at vWorker employees bid for entire projects and are compensated a fixed amount at the completion of the project. oDesk allows very close monitoring of the time that workers spend working on a project, including recording keystrokes and taking periodic screenshots so that the employer can verify that the employee is actually working for the time that the employer is paying. In contrast, vWorker.com limits moral hazard by only releasing the payment when the employee has successfully delivered the project or after arbitration if there is a disagreement. Both platforms allow contractors to certify their skills by taking tests administered by the platform. oDesk also allows contractors to be affiliated with agencies (see Stanton and Thomas 2012).

Our data set includes the 273,837 projects that were posted on vWorker between May 2001 and November 2010. We observe a total of 1,822,705 bids. This amounts to an average of 6.66 bids per project. Most of our data correspond to the period in which vWorker was called rentacoder.com and the projects are primarily concerned with software development services. The projects are typically small. The mean winning bid is \$126.50 (median winning bid is \$50). Most of the bids (94.5%) are below \$500.

For each project, we compute the number of bids received; the winning bid; and the highest, lowest, mean, and median bid. We also know the date when the bid request was posted and the rating given to the coder. Table 2 shows some summary statistics at the project, bid, and coder level.

## 3.2. Measures of Reputation

Our analysis uses two measures of reputation. One is the numerical reputation score, which is a number between 0 and 10 that is calculated as the average of the ratings that a coder has received to date. This information is shown to the potential buyers in the market. The second measure of reputation is based on unstructured information available on the site, in the form of textual comments left by previous buyers. Since we observe the complete history of coder participation and success on the site and the comments left by previous buyers, we can use text mining techniques to extract the sentiment of those previous comments and construct an alternative measure that captures this unstructured information. We have more than 100,000 comments in the database, which makes manual sentiment classification infeasible. We use the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) Library (see Bird et al. 2009) to automatically code the sentiment of the comments. We start by constructing a training set with 2,000 comments for which we manually code the sentiment (positive or negative). We use Amazon Turk for this, with each comment being rated by at least two individuals. We then use this training set to train two main classifiers of the NLTK library: naive Bayes and maximum entropy.<sup>2</sup> Finally, we choose the sentiment according to a simple voting scheme of the different classification mechanisms. Once we have coded the sentiment of each comment, for every bid we compute the average sentiment of the comments received to date by the coder placing the bid. Note that although both the numerical reputation score and the comments are available to the potential buyers on the site, the average sentiment of the comments received to date is a magnitude that we calculate, and it is not directly available to participants in the market.

## 4. Theoretical Foundations and Hypotheses Development

We analyze three main effects of coder reputation on market outcomes. First, we study how buyers choose among competing bids, focusing on how buyers react to coder reputation. Second, we analyze how the evolution of coder reputation affects their bids, volume, and likelihood of exiting the market. Third, we study how particular informational situations and characteristics of the coders moderate the effect of the coder reputation on buyer choice.

To develop our hypotheses, we rely mainly on results derived from economic models of asymmetric information as they apply to reputation systems. Reputation systems are primarily used to reduce moral hazard and adverse selection problems. In online service marketplaces, moral hazard could occur once a project is awarded if the coder engages in postcontractual opportunism by exerting less effort than the one required to deliver what was promised. The reputation mechanism can reduce moral hazard by offering a sanctioning device (Dellarocas 2006). As noted by Dellarocas (2006, pp. 633–634), “If the community follows a norm that punishes traders with histories of bad behavior (by refusing to buy from them, or by reducing the price they are willing to pay for their products) and if the present value of punishment exceeds the gains from cheating, then the threat of public revelation of a trader’s cheating behavior in the current round provides rational traders with sufficient incentives to cooperate.” Klein and Leffler (1981) develop the notion that repetition can induce trustworthiness and contract performance. In our particular case, focusing on buyers’ actions, if such a sanctioning device is in place, we would expect buyers to be more likely to award projects to coders with histories of good behavior (e.g., to coders with high reputation) or to be willing to accept higher bids from coders with a better reputation. In our setting, funds are only released when the seller successfully delivers the project (or after arbitration favorable to the seller, if there is a disagreement), which mitigates issues of moral hazard.

Buyers do not have information about the real ability or intentions of the sellers, creating an adverse selection problem. In such a setting, reputation mechanisms can act as signaling devices. For example, the fact that a coder has received consistently high ratings in a particular type of project may allow the buyer to update his beliefs with respect to the skills of the coder. Assuming that commitment to high effort is a “type” characteristic (e.g., there are coders who always exert high effort and coders who always exert low effort), the reputation score<sup>3</sup> also gives a signal about the likelihood of the coder exerting high effort. Frameworks dealing with reputation as Bayesian updating on a hidden type are described in Kreps et al. (1982) and Kreps and Wilson (1982).

Dellarocas (2006) describes the distinction between pure moral hazard and pure adverse selection settings in that in the former case, all sellers are assumed to have the same ability level and are capable of the same type of behavior, whereas in the latter case they have different intrinsic abilities. In pure moral hazard settings, the reputation system is used to constrain behavior, whereas in pure adverse selection settings, the reputation system is used to induce learning. Our setting presents both features, although funds are only released upon successful project completion, which mitigates some of the moral hazard issues.

Our first analysis aims to estimate the effect of coder reputation score on buyers’ choice behavior. The two frameworks described above support the hypothesis that higher coder reputation should result in a higher perceived utility for the buyer and thus a higher probability of the bid being chosen. According to a pure moral hazard model, a higher reputation score would induce higher effort by the coder because building reputation is costly for the coders; a coder with a higher reputation is exposed to a higher potential sanction for a deviation of the expected behavior and therefore is more likely to cooperate. According to a pure signaling model, a coder with a higher reputation score is more likely to have better skills or to be committed to exerting high effort in the task. Consequently, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1.</sup> The probability of awarding a project to a coder increases with the reputation of the coder.

Prior work in product auctions has established a price premium of reputation in product auction settings (see Bajari and Hortaçsu 2004). Empirical support for this hypothesis in our setting would complement the existing literature by extending the results to service contexts, which presents different features as summarized in Table 1. Along with confirming that the direction of the effect is the one we hypothesize, we are interested in quantifying the trade-off between price and reputation made by buyers in online service marketplaces. Furthermore, a novel feature of our analysis is that we consider two types of reputational information, the structured information provided in the form of a reputation score that averages the previous seller ratings, which is shown to the potential buyers of the site, and the unstructured information that can be extracted from textual comments provided by previous buyers. To our knowledge, these complementary sources of reputational information have not been studied in service contexts.

The second part of our analysis is concerned with how a coder behaves in this market. Although it is to be expected that, all the rest being equal, buyers will prefer more reputable sellers (Hypothesis 1), it is an empirical question how profit-maximizing coder sellers will react to this buyer preference. Specifically, a coder who achieves a higher reputation can follow one of two strategies. First, the coder can benefit from his reputation by increasing the bid amount and keeping the probability of being chosen constant. Second, the coder can benefit from his reputation by bidding at the same level as before and enjoying a higher probability of winning a given auction. In other words, coders can extract the benefits of higher reputation through price or through volume. It is an empirical question to find how those two (potentially coexisting) mechanisms play out in this market. We thus hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.1.</sup> The bid amounts set by coders increase with their reputation score.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.2.</sup> The coder’s success rate (the probability of a coder winning a project) increases with the reputation score of the coder.

Beyond testing these hypotheses, we are interested in the magnitude of the price adjustment, if such adjustment exists. The comparison between the magnitude of the price effect in coder bidding and the reputation-price trade-off made by the buyer allows us to interpret whether the coder extracts the reputation premium mostly through price or through volume. These hypotheses also allow us to explore a subtle difference between online service marketplaces and product auctions. Consider an eBay auction for a good with at least one bidder and no reserve price. If there is a reputation premium, we would expect this to be materialized through a higher closing price in the auction. Consider now a project in a service marketplace like ours. Higher reputation of one bidder does not necessarily result in a higher expected transaction price. The reputation premium can be manifested just through an alternative allocation of the project, other than the one that would have occurred if the reputation had stayed the same. For example, the higher reputation can just help this coder gain a project that would have gone to another coder with a lower reputation and the same price. If coders do not increase their bids with their reputation score (i.e., if

Hypothesis 2.1 is not supported), the reputation premium will result in a different allocation of projects but not necessarily in higher prices. But if coders increase their bids as they increase their reputation (i.e., if Hypothesis 2.1 is validated), at least some of the reputation premium will reach the market in the form of higher transaction prices.

Along with the effects of bid prices and winning probabilities, we are interested in understanding other sources of coder strategic behavior that can be influenced by changes in coder reputation. In particular, if reputation is an asset in these markets, a negative shock in the reputation of a coder might make the coder more likely to exit the market. These coders may transfer to other systems or reenter the same market with a new identity (see Friedman and Resnick 2001). Leaving the site would be more costly for coders that have invested in assets that are not transferrable to other markets, such as the certifications that are obtained on the site. Consequently, we expect that those coders will be less likely to exit the market as a consequence of a negative shock in their reputation. Similarly, coders that have invested time in creating detailed profiles (e.g., including pictures and other information) will have higher costs of leaving the site, and we hypothesize a smaller effect of a negative reputational shock on their probability of exiting the market. These ideas are summarized with the following hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.3.</sup> The probability of a coder exiting the market increases after receiving a bad rating, but it increases less for coders that have certifications and for coders that have a picture on the site.

To the best of our knowledge, the hypotheses developed above on the relation between coders’ own reputation and their pricing, success, and exit dynamics have no precedent in the empirical literature.

Finally, we revisit the effect of reputation on buyer choice. Although the reputation score is an important element to facilitate online transactions, the effect of reputation score on buyer choice is not equally important in every situation. We develop hypotheses on how the buyer’s utility for reputation score interacts with other available informational situations and coder characteristics. Our purpose is not to establish an exhaustive list of all of the situations that might interact with reputation but to offer some representative examples of moderating variables that affect the utility of the reputation score, according to mechanisms that can be explained from available theories drawn from signaling models, sanctioning models, and behavioral models of trust. Similar situations have been studied in other marketplaces (such as oDesk), and our work studies how some of those results generalize across other formats (e.g., oDesk versus vWorker) and type of reputational information (structured versus unstructured). In particular, we propose hypotheses regarding how the impact of reputation on buyer’s choice is moderated by four situations that exist in our market but that also have a broader applicability.

First, consider the role of coder certification. Because the reputation score has a signaling function, it is informative to study how its impact changes when there are alternative signals available. Our market provides one feature that allows us to explore this question: vWorker allows coders to certify their skills by taking a timed skill assessment test. For example, coders can take a test to certify their C++ coding skills. The certification information is visible to buyers in the platform, and they might take it into account when awarding projects. This certification works as a signaling device (Spence 1973): it is costly to obtain (workers must take a test) and the costs of obtaining it are higher for less able coders.<sup>4</sup> We therefore expect that the buyer’s perceived utility will increase with the availability of coder certification. How should the availability of additional information affect the impact of the reputation score on buyer’s choice? For coders who do not have certification, the reputation score is the only available signal. Coders who have certified their skills have another available signal and therefore in those cases the reputation score is a less informative signal overall, so we expect it to have a lower impact on buyers’ decisions than when there are no additional signals. This is consistent with findings of previous work that has explored the role of intermediary membership in oDesk (Stanton and Thomas 2012), documenting that verified performance is less informative for workers that belong to an intermediary network, which plays a similar role as a certification in vWorker. This notion is formalized in the following hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.1.</sup> The probability of awarding an auction to a coder is higher when a coder is certified, and the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when a coder is certified.

Second, consider the importance of a prior relationship between the buyer and the seller. From a signaling standpoint, having worked with a coder in the past gives firsthand information on the coder’s skills. Gefen and Carmel (2008) find that buyers prefer coders with whom they have worked before. We build on those results and hypothesize that, as we suggested for certification, the presence of an additional available signal can reduce the informativeness of the reputation score or other unstructured reputational signals. There are also other important behavioral considerations derived from the literature on management of organizations. This literature has studied the aspects that influence perceived trustworthiness and that can induce trust in uncertain transactions. Familiarity between organizations and individuals has been observed to breed trust (Gulati 1995), and therefore we would expect that familiarity with the coder will result in higher valuation for the buyer. Furthermore, perceived trustworthiness can also make up for a lower reputation score. This is formalized in the following hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.2.</sup> The probability of awarding an auction to a coder is higher when the buyer has worked with the coder before, and the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when the buyer has worked with the coder before.

Third, consider the effect of location on reputation and trust. It has been noted that similarity between agents simplifies communication and makes trust generation easier (Kossinets and Watts 2009). Therefore, we predict that buyers will prefer coders from the same country, all else being equal. An alternative justification for this hypothesis is that when the buyer and coder are from the same country, there are less frictions, and there are alternative sanctioning devices. For example, it is easier for the buyer to use a court of law if the project is performed poorly and arbitration is not favorable. The availability of alternative sanctioning devices reduces the appeal of using threats to the reputation score as a sanctioning device, and therefore we would expect the impact of the reputation score to be lower when the coder is local. Furthermore, the increased perceived trustworthiness of a local coder can make up for a lower reputation score. Previous work in other contexts has documented how location affects the impact of information. For example, Forman et al. (2008) have studied how shared geographical location affects the relationship between product reviews and sales, and Agrawal et al. (2013b) have documented that information is more important for contractors of less-developed countries, using data from oDesk. More generally, Arora and Forman (2007) have studied the relationship between proximity and information technology outsourcing. We formalize these notions as they apply to our setting with the following hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.3.</sup> The probability of awarding an auction to a coder is higher when the coder is local, and the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when a coder is local.

Fourth, the last moderator of reputation that we explore is the availability of a coder’s picture. Recent research in psychology has demonstrated that people are more willing to punish an agent that they consider mindful (Gray et al. 2007), and it has been suggested that anthropomorphizing agents results in increased trustworthiness (Waytz et al. 2010). Accordingly, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.4.</sup> The probability of awarding an auction to a coder is higher when the coder has a picture, and the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when a coder has a picture.

The rest of the paper presents models to test these hypotheses. Section 5 focuses on Hypothesis 1. Section 6 proposes models to test Hypotheses 3.1–3.3 on the effect of reputation on coder behavior. Section 7 extends the analysis of §5 to test Hypotheses 3.1–3.4 on how the effect of reputation on buyer behavior changes in different situations.

## 5. Reputation and Buyer Behavior

## 5.1. Econometric Model of Buyer Choice

A multiattribute service procurement auction can be modeled using a conditional logit specification (McFadden 1973). We assume that the utility that a generic buyer obtains from accepting a bid b submitted for project i can be expressed as

$$
u _ {i b} ^ {*} = X _ {i b} ^ {\prime} \beta + \epsilon_ {i b},\tag{1}
$$

where $X _ { i b }$ are the attributes of the bids that affect the utility that the buyer receives from choosing that bid. Let C4b5 denote the coder that submits the bid $b ,$ B4i5 denote the buyer that submits the project $i ,$ S4i5 denote the set of bids received for project $i ,$ and D4b5 denote the date when the bid b is submitted. These attributes can be broken down into the subvectors $X _ { i b } = ( X _ { \mathrm { p r o j e c t } } \mid X _ { \mathrm { b i d } } \mid X _ { \mathrm { c o d e r } } \mid X _ { \mathrm { r e l a t i o n } } \mid X _ { \mathrm { b i d - s e t } } )$ using the following simplified notation:

$X _ { \mathrm { p r o j e c t } }$ are attributes that depend only on the project and are constant for all bids received for the project, such as the number of bids received for the project.

$X _ { \mathrm { b i d } }$ are attributes that depend only on the bid, such as the bid amount.

$X _ { \mathrm { c o d e r } }$ are attributes that depend on the coder, such as the coder’s country of origin and potentially on the time D4b5 when the bid is submitted, such as the reputation score of the coder at that time.

$\bar { X } _ { \mathrm { r e l a t i o n } }$ are attributes that depend on the relationship between a given coder and a given buyer, and potentially on the time D4b5 when the bid is submitted. Examples are an indicator of whether the coder and buyer are from the same country or an indicator of whether the buyer has worked previously with the coder at the time of bid submission.

$X _ { \mathrm { b i d - s e t } }$ are attributes that depend on the bid and on the other set of bids S4i5 received for the project.

One example of this type of variable is whether a bid is the smallest one received for a project.

We do not observe the utility that the buyer obtains from choosing a bid, but we do observe which bid $b = 1 , \dots , J$ the buyer chooses, if any. Let $Y _ { i } = b$ if bid b is chosen by buyer for project $i ,$ which implies $u _ { i b } > u _ { i k }$ for all $k \dot { \neq } b$ . If the error $\epsilon _ { i b }$ follows a Gumbel (type 1 extreme value) distribution, the probability of choosing a bid has a closed form

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (Y _ {i} = b) = \frac {e ^ {X _ {i b} ^ {\prime} \beta}}{\sum_ {b = 1} ^ {J} e ^ {X _ {i b} ^ {\prime} \beta}}.\tag{2}
$$

Note that the coefficients that correspond to the variables included in $X _ { \mathrm { p r o j e c t } }$ (i.e., variables that remain constant for all of the bids received for a project) cannot be identified from this model since the conditional logit model requires variation across alternatives. Similarly, projects for which there is no difference in the outcome of all of the bids $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ all of the bids received are awarded, or none of the bids received are awarded) are dropped in the estimation process since all of the variation in those cases can be explained with a project fixed effect, and those cases do not contribute to the identification of the parameters of the model.

## 5.2. Results

We start by analyzing how buyers use the available information in their bid choice decision. In particular, we are interested in the effect of reputation on the probability of a bid being chosen. We start with a parsimonious model that allows us to quantify the effect of coder reputation on buyers’ choices.

In the bid-specific set of variables $X _ { \mathrm { b i d } } ,$ we would like to capture the amount of the bid. Since there is a huge heterogeneity between projects and within bids in a project, we normalize the amount of the bids with respect to the rest of the bids received for the project as

$$
B I D N O R M _ {\mathrm{bid-set}} = \frac {B I D _ {\mathrm{bid}} - \lambda_ {\mathrm{set}}}{\sigma_ {\mathrm{set}}},\tag{3}
$$

where set $= S ( i )$ is the set of bids received for project $i ,$ $\lambda _ { \mathrm { s e t } }$ denotes the median bid received for the bid request $i ,$ and $\sigma _ { \mathrm { { s e t } } }$ denotes the standard deviation of the bids submitted to the bid request. In other words, we express bid amounts as the distance to the median bid received for the project, measured in number of standard deviations of the bids received for the project. Note that projects that receive a unique bid do not contribute to the identification of the parameters, and this normalization is inconsequential in those cases.<sup>5</sup>

<sup>5</sup> We have used alternative normalization schemes, such as dividing by the median bid received $\lambda _ { \mathrm { s e t } }$ instead of the standard deviation of the bids $\sigma _ { \mathrm { { s e t } } } ,$ without obtaining qualitative differences in our results.

Along with the normalized bid, we also include a dummy variable that indicates whether the submitted bid is the smallest so that we can capture any additional premium that the buyer attributes to the cheapest offer. Therefore $X _ { \mathrm { b i d - s e t } }$ contains two variables, $B I D N O R M _ { \mathrm { b i d - s e t } }$ and $S M A L L E S T _ { \mathrm { b i d - s e t } } .$

Among the coder-specific variables $X _ { \mathrm { c o d e r } } ,$ we include the variables that capture the reputational information. As described above, we have two types of reputational variables. The first one is the score-based reputation measure that comes from numerical (0–10) ratings left by previous buyers in the system. The ratings received to date are averaged by vWorker.com and this information is shown to potential buyers. We code this information using three variables: a variable that contains the number of ratings received by the coder up to the bidding time $( N R A \bar { T } I N G S _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) ;$ a variable that contains the average rating received up to the bidding time $( R E P U T S \breve { C O R E } _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) ;$ and a dummy variable that indicates whether a coder has not yet received any ratings $( U N R A T E D _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) ,$ which allows us to give a value of 0 to the reputation score of unrated coders that otherwise would have to be dropped from the estimation. Note that the number of ratings and the average rating are presented to users in the site by vWorker.com.

The second source of reputational information is the text-based reputation measure. This comes from the unstructured information left by previous buyers in the form of verbal feedback about the coder’s performance. As explained above, we have coded the sentiment (positive or negative) of each of those comments, and we have computed the average sentiment up to the bidding time (number between 0 and 1). Note that this information is not shown as a summary to the potential buyers on the site, but buyers can browse previous comments, and we are interested in exploring whether the information that can be extracted from those comments plays an important role in this marketplace. As with the structured, score-based metric, we code this information using three variables: a variable that contains the number of comments received by the coder up to the bidding time $( N C O M M E N T S _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) ;$ a variable that contains the average sentiment of the comments received by the coder up to the bidding time $( T E X T S E N T _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) ;$ and a dummy variable that denotes situations where the coder has not received previous comments $( U N C O M M E N T E D _ { \mathrm { c o d e r - t i m e } } ) _ { i }$ , which allows us to give a value of 0 to the average text-based reputation for those coders that have not received previous comments. We have three alternative specifications of our model: one using the score-based reputation measure, one using the text-based reputation measure, and one using both measures.

We also include region dummies (one for the United States/Canada, one for Eastern Europe, one for the rest of Europe, one for India/Pakistan, one for the rest of Asia, and one for other locations).

The positive and significant coefficient for all types of reputational information allows us to validate Hypothesis 1: the probability of awarding an auction to a coder does increase with the reputation of the coder. These results are consistent with both the signaling and the sanctioning role of reputation mechanisms. A buyer may prefer to work with a coder with a higher reputation because this updates his beliefs on the skills of the worker (adverse selection story) or because he thinks that the coder will have more to lose if he does not perform well because of the higher cost of a sanction for a coder with higher reputation (moral hazard story). Interestingly, we find this effect both with the reputational information that is directly shown to the buyers in the form of an average score and with our coded summary of the sentiment of the previously received comments, which is not shown on the site.

It is well known that in data sets with a very large number of observations, it becomes very easy to obtain statistically significant results (e.g., see Lin et al. 2013). Consequently, along with looking at the signs and significance of the coefficients, it is important to examine the economic significance of the effects. For example, it is interesting to analyze the comparative magnitude of their marginal effects, in particular the one of the reputation score. At the median values, one additional reputation point increases the probability of choosing a bid by 0.00556 (column 2). This is approximately the same effect on success probability as reducing the normalized bid value by one standard deviation. This implies that the reputational effects in this market are economically very important. We can draw a similar conclusion by looking at the marginal effect of the textbased measure (column 4).

Our results suggest that the numerical reputation score and the text-based measure have a complementary effect. A model that includes both measures (column 5) has a higher pseudo R2, and is also preferable by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) criteria, which penalize the additional complexity of the model.

Although our results provide evidence that is consistent with Hypothesis 1, there are some caveats to make regarding our preferred interpretation. A coder’s reputation score could be correlated with multiple other factors that buyers in the market care about, some of which are not captured by the set of controls available to the econometrician. For example, the inclusion of a detailed cover letter or the quality of the proposal description could be (positively or negatively) correlated with the coder’s reputation score. This would generate a bias in our results that is hard to quantify. To check whether this can be a substantial issue, we have obtained additional control variables that proxy for these effects: a dummy variable that indicates whether an attachment was included with the bid and a variable that captures the level of detail included in the proposal with the number of words that were typed. We find that adding these variables barely change our coefficients of interest (e.g., the coefficient of the reputation score in column 1 goes from 0.483 to 0.482). Even if we cannot control for every possible unobserved factor that might be correlated with a worker’s reputation score, the robustness of the estimates of interest to the inclusion of aspects that would seem important gives us some reassurance that potential biases are likely to be small.

Another limitation of our setting is that workers endogenously choose to bid for certain projects. It could be that workers with higher (or lower) reputation may decide to bid for certain types of projects, which could introduce a particular kind of bias in the estimates. In order to assess whether this sorting could generate serious issues to our estimates, we conduct a category-level analysis. If this were a severe issue, we would expect to find categories where the reputation effect is less important attracting a particular type of coder, which would result in different estimates. Our category-level results indicate that the reputation effects persist within a category (the effects are indeed very similar across categories), suggesting that coder sorting does not introduce an important bias in our estimates. Similarly, the results are not qualitatively different if we restrict our attention to highly competitive markets (those that receive six or more bids).

We propose a more detailed model in §7, where we discuss the interactions of the reputation score with other variables, and we observe that the effects that we identify with this parsimonious model do not qualitatively change. Note that even in situations where the estimate of the effect of reputation is biased, the interaction terms can be accurately identified.

## 6. Reputation and Coder Behavior

Having established the effect of reputation on buyer bid choice, we now turn our attention to how coders adjust their bidding behavior over time as their reputation varies. Our data set is unique in that respect because we can follow coders over a 10-year time span. This allows us to observe many coders that see changes in their reputational situation over time, which allows us to disentangle the effects of reputation from other permanent coder fixed effects. We focus on three questions: (1) how coders adjust their bids as their reputation changes (price effects), (2) how the probability of coders winning projects changes with changes in their reputation (volume effect), and (3) how the probability of coders leaving the market changes with changes in their reputation (exit effect).

## 6.1. Price Effect

We start by analyzing how coders adjust their pricing behavior as a consequence of changes in their reputation. If coders are aware that buyers are willing to pay a significant premium for bids coming from coders with a high reputation, they may increase their relative bids as they obtain more reputation. We propose the following specification to estimate the magnitude of the effect:

$$
\begin{array}{c} B I D N O R M _ {b t} = \mu_ {j} + \alpha X _ {j t} + \beta X _ {\text {relation}} \\ + \gamma C O N T R O L S + \epsilon_ {j t}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where j denotes the coder C4b5 and t denotes D4b5, the time at which bid b is placed. We include coder fixed effects $\mu _ { j }$ to control for time-invariant characteristics of the coder. We only include variables that refer to the coder $( X _ { j t } )$ or the relation between coder and buyer $( X _ { \mathrm { r e l a t i o n } } )$ and not the group of variables that refer to the bid set (such as whether the coder’s bid is the smallest bid received) because the coder does not have access to that information. For the coder information, we are especially interested in the reputation variables, and we have specifications that consider the score-based variables (NRATINGS and REPUTSCORE), the textbased variables (NCOMMENTS and TEXTSENT), and the combination of both, as we did in the buyer choice problem.

If the coefficient of the reputation variables (REPUT-SCORE and TEXTSENT) is positive and significant, then our Hypothesis 3.1 (the bid amounts set by coders increase with their reputation) will be supported. The expected effect of NRATINGS and NCOMMENTS on price is ambiguous: on one hand, the more projects a coder has won in the past, the more knowledgeable he or she is expected to be. This is valued by the buyer, and therefore coders with more previous ratings will be able to charge more while maintaining the probability of being chosen. On the other hand, experience can also reduce cost. If coders who have been awarded projects in the past have lower costs, they could charge lower prices while keeping their expected revenue constant.

If coders are not reaping all of the potential benefits of reputation through their pricing behavior, they might be doing so through an increase in the volume of projects that they obtain via the site. We test whether higher reputation is associated with higher success, defined as the probability of winning projects.

We use a logit model with coder fixed effects. Let $Y _ { i b } = 1$ if the bid b submitted by coder $j = C ( b )$ for project i is accepted and 0 otherwise. Let $P =$ $\bar { \mathrm { p r o b } ( Y _ { i b } = 1 ) }$ . Then we propose the following model:

Our results also show some subtle consequences for the market of the increase in a coder’s reputation, contrasting with product auctions such as eBay where the good is awarded to the highest bidder. In markets like eBay, if there is a reputation premium, this materializes through a higher closing price in the auction. In contrast, in vWorker higher seller reputation does not necessarily result in a higher expected transaction price. The reputation premium can be manifested just through an alternative allocation of the project, other than the one that would have occurred if the reputation had stayed the same. For example, the higher reputation can just help a coder gain a project that would have otherwise gone to another coder with a lower reputation and the same price. In other words, instead of simply resulting in higher market prices, reputation helps to achieve better matches between projects and coders.

Overall, these results show support for our Hypothesis 2.2 (coders increase their success rate with their reputation), at least for the structured numerical measure of reputation. In other words, coders are more likely to win projects when their reputation is higher. Note also that these results give only a conservative estimate of the increase in volume that arises from higher reputation since coders also submit more bids as they become more reputable.

## 6.3. Coder Exit Effect

We now analyze whether reputation has an effect on coder exit behavior. Given that buyers in this market value reputation, we have hypothesized (Hypothesis 2.3) that coders will be more likely to exit the market when they receive a negative shock in their reputation. This negative shock can come from a rating that is lower than their current average or from a comment that is more negative than their current average.

To test this hypothesis, we focus on winning bids because only those can receive ratings or comments that will affect a coder’s future reputation. The results are presented in Table 8, grouped by the type of reputational information included (score, text, or both). For each of these, we report the results of a model with just the main effects of the variables and a model that includes the interactions.

We start by describing the results with the numerical reputation score (columns 1 and 2). Column 1 extends the model presented in Table 4 (column 1) by adding the levels of the variables HASCERTS, HASPIC, FAMILIARITY, and LOCAL, and column 2 adds the interactions of these variables with REPUT-SCORE. The coefficient of the variable HASCERTS is positive and significant, which implies that the probability of awarding an auction to a coder is higher when the coder is certified. Furthermore, the interaction between HASCERTS and REPUTSCORE is negative and significant. This implies that the reputation score has a lower impact on a buyer’s choice probability when the coder is certified. In other words, reputation and certification work as substitutes. This supports our Hypothesis 3.1. Stanton and Thomas (2012) have found similar effects between intermediary affiliation and the effect of seller information using data from oDesk. The coefficient of the FAMILIARITY variable is positive and significant and the interaction between FAMILIARITY and REPUT-SCORE is negative and significant. This implies that familiarity increases choice probability but reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when the buyer has worked with the coder before, which validates Hypothesis 3.2. The coefficient of the LOCAL variable is positive and significant, and the interaction between LOCAL and REPUTSCORE is negative and significant. This implies that the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when the coder is local.<sup>8</sup> The coefficient of the variable HASPIC is positive and significant, with the interaction between HASPIC and REPUTSCORE being negative and significant. This implies that the reputation score has a lower impact on buyer’s choice probability when the coder has a picture, which validates Hypothesis 3.4. One interpretation for these results is that anthropomorphism generates trust, and trust can make up for a less than perfect reputation score.<sup>9</sup>

The results that we have described for the numerical reputation score also hold when we use the reputational information derived from previous textual comments (columns 3 and 4), and Hypotheses 3.1–3.4 are also validated for this alternative reputation measure. When we combine the two sources of reputational information (columns 5 and 6), the results are largely consistent, except for the interaction of the textual reputation measure with HASPIC and of the numerical reputation measure with FAMILIARITY, which are not significant in this model.

Ai and Norton (2003) have noted the challenges of interpreting the sign of the interaction coefficients in nonlinear models. To confirm support for our hypotheses regarding the effects of reputation in different contexts (i.e., for different levels of the HASCERTS, LOCAL, FAMILIARITY, and HASPIC variables), we estimate the model with no interactions and we take advantage of the fact that our variables of interest are binary to evaluate the marginal effect of the reputation variables (REPUTSCORE and TEXT-SENT) at the different possible values of HASCERTS, LOCAL, FAMILIARITY, and HASPIC. We find that the marginal effects of the reputation variables are higher when coders do not have certificates, are not local, have not worked together before, and have no picture on the site, consistent with our predictions.<sup>10</sup>

Along with the interactions for which we had developed hypotheses in §4, the models with interactions also include the interaction between the reputational information and an indicator variable that denotes that the coder is from an emerging market.<sup>11</sup> The coefficient is positive and significant, which suggests that reputation scores might be more important for coders of certain geographic areas. This is consistent with the results of Agrawal et al. (2013b) documenting (using data from oDesk) that information is more important for contractors of less-developed countries.

We note that the direction and magnitude of the coefficients of the parsimonious model presented in §5.2 do not qualitatively change when we add more variables, suggesting that the parsimonious model is not subject to significant omitted variable bias. We have also estimated models that incorporate in isolation each of the interactions shown in columns 2 and 4, adding one interaction at a time. Hypotheses 3.1–3.4 continue to be validated and there are no substantial differences in the coefficients. Actually, potential concerns about bias coming from omitted variables are mitigated in the specifications with interactions (even if there was bias in some of the direct effects, the interactions could be precisely identified). From a model selection perspective, our preferred specification is the one shown in column 6. The AIC and BIC criteria indicate that the improvement in the fit is worth the additional complexity of the model.

Overall, these results show that the value of the reputational information depends on other characteristics of the coder and/or the relationship between the coder and the buyer and that both structured reputation (numerical score) and unstructured reputation (previous comments) interact with the rest of the information in a similar way, despite one measure being shared by the reputation mechanism and the other measure being associated with more intangible aspects.

## 8. Conclusion and Discussion

Our analysis has shown how buyers and sellers react to the information provided by reputation systems in online service marketplaces. Buyers react to seller reputation and are willing to pay a significant premium to award a project to a more reputable bidder. On a representative bid, one additional point in the reputation score has the same effect on the probability of being chosen as a reduction of the bid amount of one standard deviation of the bids posted for that project.

Sellers also adjust their behavior in response to the information tracked by the reputation system. Reputation affects seller actions and outcomes through a combination of three effects. First, sellers increase prices with their own reputation (price effect). However, they increase their prices more conservatively than what it seems buyers would be willing to support. This results in the second effect: coders increase their probability of success in the site (volume effect). Our results suggest that sellers reap most of the benefits of their reputation through higher volumes rather than through higher prices, which do not increase enough to keep constant the probability of being chosen. The third channel through which reputation affects coder behavior is by driving coder exit. Coders that receive a negative shock in their reputation, in the form of a bad rating or a bad textual comment, are more likely to leave the system immediately after that transaction. However, site-specific investments, such as coder skill certifications or detailed user profiles, reduce the probability of exit.

The reputation score does not live in a vacuum, and the context of the transaction is important to determining the impact that reputation will have on choices. We have shown that coder certification reduces the value of reputation, and the same happens with situations that prompt interpersonal trustworthiness, such as familiarity between buyer and coder, common origin of buyer and coder, and coder anthropomorphism.

Unstructured information in the form of feedback left by previous buyers plays a very important role on the site. Although these intangible aspects behave in a similar way to the structured numerical reputation score, we find that they provide complementary information that is not captured by the numeric reputation score. The effects of this type of information on coder exit are particularly important (more than 40% increase in the exit probability after a negative comment).

Overall, these findings have important implications for participants in online service marketplaces; for intermediaries operating these marketplaces; and, at a broader societal level, for our understanding of the evolution of labor markets.

At the participant level, our results help inform buyers’ and coders’ strategies in online service marketplaces. For example, our results suggest that coders with low reputation can reduce the impact of their negative ratings by getting certified or working with local buyers.

At the intermediary level, our results provide guidance to the design of reputation systems for online service marketplaces. The reputation system is not the only mechanism in place to control moral hazard and to signal quality in online service marketplaces. Most of the online service marketplaces, including vWorker.com, use an escrow system that only releases the funds after the work has been approved. On the other hand, platforms like vWorker offer coders the possibility of certifying their skills. Our results on the interplay of reputation score, third-party certification, and interpersonal trust are relevant for the design of mechanisms that combine a reputation score with other features to reduce transaction risk.

That negative shocks in reputation have a substantial role in driving coder exit can result in inefficiencies that can be reduced through more sophisticated mechanisms. Since the information in the site is long lived, a very bad comment can have a very longlasting impact, and some coders may be compelled to move to another market (where they may have to start from scratch) or to reenter the market with a fresh identity (with the associated loss of information and digital assets). Some sites in related contexts offer the possibility of responding to these negative comments. For example, Yelp.com allows business owners to provide additional information when they believe that a review is not accurate. Providing similar features in service markets can reduce the extent to which an unusually bad rating or comment results in a loss of efficiency because of coder exit. Service intermediaries can also reduce the likelihood of coder exit by increasing the switching costs. This can be done by developing assets that are valuable within the market but worthless if the coder exits the market. For example, if exiting the market implies losing the certifications, and coders have to retake the tests if they reenter the system with a new identity, this reduces the probability of exit after a bad rating. Another idea that intermediaries could experiment with is to have the information “expire.” If feedback from previous sellers is removed from the site after a certain time, some coders that would otherwise leave after a very bad comment may decide to stay in the system because letting comments expire effectively reduces the costs of continuing on the site.

Future work could address other questions related to the design of reputation systems for online service marketplaces, such as the optimal amount of information to be shown to participants in the marketplace. If buyers only see a reputation score and number of ratings, then coders can quickly obtain reputation points by bidding for and winning cheap projects and then using the accumulated reputation to obtain better prices in more expensive projects. Another related area that could be explored is the extent to which reputation systems of online service marketplaces can be gamed. We have shown that coders that receive negative shocks to their reputation are more likely to leave the market. Some of these coders may reenter the market with a fresh reputation. It would be interesting to quantify the prevalence of this behavior and its consequences for quality and efficiency.

At the societal level, online service marketplaces are likely to have a growing impact on conventional employment relationships. Reputation systems are at the core of the transition from traditional, immersive employment relationships into a new world of online transactions where “spot” labor markets are increasingly becoming a viable alternative. Our analysis of this particular online marketplace is encouraging, and it suggests that the reputation system can be effective at enabling transactions that would probably not occur otherwise because of transaction risks and at generating more efficient project allocation outcomes.

With the increasing economic importance of online marketplaces and their role on the changing nature of work, we believe that these and other related questions will receive more attention in the future.
