# Characteristics and Economic Consequences of Jump Bids in Combinatorial Auctions

Pallab Sanyal School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, Virginia 22030, psanyal@gmu.edu

ump bidding, which refers to bidding above the minimum necessary, is a robust behavior that has been observed in a variety of ascending auctions in the field as well as the laboratory. However, the phenomenon has yet to be studied in combinatorial auctions, which are a type of multiobject auction that allows bidders to bid on a set of objects. Such auctions have been found to be beneficial when objects exhibit synergy, e.g., are complementary. In this paper, we explore jump bidding behavior in combinatorial auctions as a function of design choices of the mechanism. In particular, we examine the effects of price revelation schemes on the nature and extent of jump bidding. Furthermore, we study the effects of jump bidding on the economic performance of the auctions. To conduct our study, first, we develop hypotheses using auction theories and behavioral theories of how people use reference prices as anchors, and second, we conduct a laboratory experiment to test our hypotheses and examine bidder behavior. We find that the nature of the prices that the auctioneer chooses to offer as feedback to the bidders can considerably influence their jump bidding behavior, leading to significant differences in auction outcomes. We demonstrate that in combinatorial auctions, in addition to the theories of jump bidding proposed in the literature, bounded rationality of the bidders plays a part in the nature and extent of jump bidding. Our study reveals that in the cognitively challenging package-bidding environment, bidders often pursue computationally frugal but suboptimal heuristics. Our results have important policy implications for mechanism designers.

## 1. Introduction

The advances in technology coupled with the proliferation of the Internet have made auctions a popular mechanism for exchanging goods and services around the world. However, a fundamental shortcoming of the traditional single-item auction mechanisms is their inability to consider dependencies among items, e.g., the complementarity among various computer accessories that a consumer might desire. Since the existence of such synergies is common in a variety of contexts, interest has emerged in developing advanced mechanisms that allow bids on multiple items as a set. Such auctions, where bidders can bid not just on single objects, but on multiple objects as a set (commonly referred to as a package or a bundle), are called combinatorial auctions. These auctions have been used or proposed to be used in a variety of applications, including the auctioning of the rights to use railroad tracks, airport time slots, bus routes, industrial procurement, procurement of school meals, and most notably, the allocation of spectrum rights. In all of these cases, the primary motivation for the use of combinatorial auctions is the presence of asset synergies, wherein combinatorial auctions can mitigate the so-called exposure problem (Bykowsky et al. 2000) by allowing bidders to bid on an entire package of interest.

Many recent studies (e.g., Bichler et al. 2013, Goetzendorff et al. 2015, Plott et al. 2014) have focused on various aspects of combinatorial auctions; however, given the complexity of these auctions, the behavior of bidders is still not well understood. In this paper, we contribute to this stream of literature by examining the effects of design choices in combinatorial auctions on the characteristics and economic implications of an endemic feature of real-world ascending auctions<sup>1</sup> known as jump bidding, which is the tendency of bidders to place bids that exceed the minimum acceptable bid at any stage of an auction (Easley and Tenorio 2004, Isaac et al. 2007). The phenomenon has been observed in many different forms of ascending auctions both in the field (e.g., Easley and Tenorio 2004, Lucking-Reiley 1999) and in the laboratory (e.g., Isaac et al. 2007, Kwasnica and Katok 2007). Even in the high-stakes spectrum auctions with large bid increments, bidders have been seen to engage in a considerable amount of jump bidding (Banks et al. 2003, Börgers and Dustmann 2005, Cramton 1997, Plott and Salmon 2004).

Although studies exploring the causes and effects of jump bidding exist in the auctions literature, the phenomenon—especially the effects of features of the auction on the nature and extent of jump bids—is still not well understood. As Raviv (2008) notes, the “empirical and experimental literature on jump bidding is quite narrow” (p. 328) and the phenomenon “has not been fully documented in the auction literature” (p. 340). Furthermore, the existing studies on jump bidding are primarily in the context of single-object auctions and, more recently, multiobject simultaneous ascending auctions (SAAs).<sup>2</sup> In such auctions, the asking price of an object—which forms the reference for jump bidding—is quite simple, and also unique. However, as we describe in detail below, the nature of ask prices in ascending combinatorial auctions is far more complex, and therefore the economic implications of jump-bidding behavior in such markets are more intriguing.

The growing body of literature on combinatorial auctions consists of a variety of mechanisms—the most famous being the Vickery–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism, which is the combinatorial generalization of the Vickery auction. Although theoretically appealing, the VCG mechanism is not popular in practice because it is a sealed-bid mechanism where bidders receive no feedback on prices, and furthermore it suffers from serious weaknesses such as generating low seller revenues under certain conditions (Ausubel and Milgrom 2006). More popular in practice are iterative auctions, which allow bidders to revise their bids in response to provisional allocation and pricing information. “This information,” Cramton et al. (2006, p. 5) note, “helps the bidders focus their valuation efforts on options that are most relevant.” Not surprisingly, therefore, the spectrum auctions conducted across the world are almost all iterative auctions with multiple rounds.

The iterative combinatorial auctions considered in the literature can be classified into two broad categories— linear price and nonlinear price—based on the nature of ask prices used. Linear price mechanisms use itemlevel prices, with the prices of bundles being simply the sum of the prices of the items constituting the bundle. Prominent examples include the combinatorial clock (CC) auction designed by Porter et al. (2003), the resource allocation design proposed by Kwasnica et al. (2005), and the approximate linear prices mechanism proposed by Bichler et al. (2009). Although these linear price mechanisms have performed well in the laboratory, Bikhchandani and Ostroy (2002) have shown that, because of the existence of asset complementarities, only mechanisms that use nonlinear personalized prices—i.e., mechanisms where each bundle and each bidder in the worst case has an ask price—can achieve 100% efficiency. Among the notable nonlinear price iterative auctions proposed in the literature are the iBundle (Parkes 1999), the ascending proxy auction (APA; Ausubel and Milgrom 2002), and dVSV (de Vries et al. 2007).

Unlike iterative auctions, which proceed in a series of rounds, continuous auctions are similar to the classic English auction, where bids can be submitted at any time (Kwasnica et al. 2005). Adomavicius et al. (2012) conducted a form of continuous combinatorial auction using generic pricing rules introduced by Adomavicius and Gupta (2005). To recognize the pricing rules, consider a simple two-item combinatorial auction for selling items X and Y. Suppose the first two bids are (1) \$100 on the package {XY} and (2) \$50 for {X}. Also, suppose someone is interested in item {Y} at this stage of the auction. Assuming a minimum bid increment of \$1, a bid of \$51 on {Y} would make it provisionally winning along with bid 2 since \$50 + \$51 > \$100 (where \$100 was the auction revenue following the first two bids). However, even a bid of just \$1 on {Y} would keep the bid live (i.e., nonlosing) because, for example, a new bid of \$100 on {X} can make the bid of \$1 on {Y} winning. Thus, a minimal potentially successful bid can have a range of values (from \$1 to \$51 in this example) in a combinatorial auction. The lower bound price (\$0) at or below which a bid will be dead (i.e., losing) is termed as the deadness level (DL), and the upper bound price (\$50) above which a bid will be provisionally winning is termed as the winning level (WL).<sup>3</sup> The DL and WL offer natural bounds for bids in combinatorial auctions (we describe the theoretical underpinnings in §2) and are independent of allocation rules.

Adomavicius et al. (2012) demonstrated through laboratory experiments that for reasonably sized auctions, a continuous mechanism can achieve high efficiency (around 90%) that is not significantly different from that achieved with the CC auction (a popular linear price mechanism). More recently, Plott et al. (2014) reviewed the prominent features of a continuous combinatorial auction and noted that the continuous auctions progress much faster than round-based iterative auctions. Thus, for this study, we study a continuous combinatorial auction setting similar to the ones considered by Adomavicius et al. (2012) and Plott et al. (2014).

## 1.1. Research Questions and Contributions

In the aforementioned setting, computing the provisional allocation (i.e., the winning set of bids at any stage) is a computationally intractable problem,<sup>4</sup> and, therefore, calculating the DLs and WLs for every package of interest at all stages of the auction is cognitively prohibitive for bidders. Indeed, Adomavicius et al. (2012) found that without the explicit availability of DLs and WLs, almost half of the bids placed by bidders in laboratory auctions were inconsequential, i.e., below the DL. In this paper, we argue that since ask-price feedback is essential for making meaningful bids in a combinatorial auction, the nature of prices, i.e., only DL, only WL, or both, that is made explicitly available to the bidders can considerably influence a bidder’s bidding behavior. In particular, since DL and WL can act as two separate reference prices for bidders, the auctioneer’s choice of price visibility schemes can significantly alter the jump-bidding behavior, leading to differences in the outcome of the auctions. Thus, the research questions that we explore in this study are as follows: (1) How does the nature of price visibility affect jump bidding? (2) What are the economic implications of jump bidding in combinatorial auctions?

To answer the research questions, first, using auction theory and the theory on reference prices (Mazumdar et al. 2005, Putler 1992), we develop a series of hypotheses concerning the effects of price revelation schemes on the extent of jump bidding and the impact of jump bids on the economic outcomes of auctions. Second, we test the hypotheses through a laboratory experiment where we manipulate the ask price visible to the bidders. Finally, we analyze the bidding behaviors exhibited in the auctions to explain the observed aggregate outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the first study that analyzes jump bids in continuous combinatorial auctions.

Our results indicate that the price levels, DL and WL, can serve as anchors for bidders as they formulate their bids, thereby influencing the revenue generated by the sellers, the surplus retained by the bidders, and the overall efficiency of the auctions. One significant finding of this research is that more price information, despite being relevant, is not necessarily better for the profitability of the bidders because certain price information can nudge the bidders toward unprofitable bidding behavior. Another contribution of our study is that we demonstrate that in addition to the theories of jump bidding that have been proposed in the literature on single-item auctions, in the cognitively complex combinatorial auction environment, the bounded rationality (Simon 1956) of the bidders is also responsible for the jump bids. Based on the results of our analysis, we build valuable insights that can be used by mechanism designers to choose the price visibility schemes judiciously.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1. Combinatorial Auctions

Combinatorial auctions are multiobject auctions that allow bidders to bid on each individual object as well as possible combinations of the objects. By allowing package bidding, combinatorial auctions permit bidders to express complex preferences, which often leads to more efficient trades and greater auction revenues when the values of the traded assets exhibit synergies (e.g., they are complementary) that differ across bidders (Cramton et al. 2006). However, for each additional item, the number of biddable packages increases exponentially making the task of determining winners in such auctions computationally complex Furthermore, the bidders face a cognitively daunting task of navigating the large bid space and making a profitable bid.

The VCG mechanism eliminates the strategic complexity facing the bidders by ensuring that truthful reporting of values is a dominant strategy for each bidder. Moreover, when each bidder reports truthfully, the outcome is efficient. Thus, the VCG mechanism is a unique combinatorial auction format that is strategy proof, efficient, and also individually rational. However, the mechanism has several serious weaknesses that limit its usefulness in practice.<sup>5</sup> For instance, because the auction is sealed bid, the bidders face the problem of calculating and expressing an exponentially large number of valuations at once.

More attractive in practice are iterative combinatorial auctions, which allow bidders to update their bids after each round based on feedback about the provisional allocation and prices. As Parkes (2006, p. 42) notes, the information feedback is expected to support “adaptive and focused elicitation” of bidders’ values. The theoretical literature on iterative auctions consists of a variety of mechanisms, of which the iBundle (Parkes 1999), APA (Ausubel and Milgrom 2002), and dVSV (de Vries et al. 2007) are prominent nonlinear price mechanisms that produce efficient solutions when bidders follow the straightforward bidding strategy (i.e., bid only on the surplus-maximizing package(s) in every round).

Unlike these iterative auctions that progress in discrete rounds, the continuous auctions introduced by Adomavicius et al. (2012) are similar to the classic English auction, allowing bids to be submitted any time with continual feedback on provisional allocation and prices. More recently, Plott et al. (2014) described a similar mechanism. They asserted that continuous auctions progress much faster than round-based iterative auctions. Because the context of our study is the continuous auction, we describe the setting in detail below.

2.2. The Continuous Combinatorial Auction Setting Let I denote the set of items in the auction and K the set of bidders. A package P refers to any nonempty subset of $I \ ( \varnothing \subset P \subseteq I )$ , and $| P |$ represents the size of the package in terms of the number of items in the package. A bid b is represented by the tuple $( P , a , j ) .$ , where a denotes the bid amount $( a > 0 )$ and j the bidder who made the bid. Given bid $b , P ( b ) , a ( b )$ , and $j ( b )$ refer to the package, amount, and bidder of the bid, respectively. An auction stage t (where $t = 0 , 1 , 2 , \ldots )$ refers to the state of the auction following the first t bids.<sup>6</sup> The set of bids at an auction stage t is denoted by $B _ { t } ,$ i.e., $B _ { t } = \{ b _ { 1 } , \ldots , b _ { t } \}$ and similarly $B _ { 0 } = \varnothing$ . Obviously, $B _ { t } \subseteq B _ { u } ,$ for any t and u such that $t \leq u .$

Given an arbitrary set of bids B in the auction, a set $F \subseteq B$ is called a feasible allocation if the bids in F are all on disjoint packages, or, more formally, for every $b _ { x } , b _ { y } \in F$ such that $b _ { x } \neq b _ { y } ,$ we have $P ( b _ { x } ) \dot { \cap } P ( b _ { y } ) = \dot { \emptyset }$ Let $\check { F } _ { t }$ denote the feasible allocation at auction stage $t ,$ $\mathrm { i . e . , } F _ { t } = \{ F \subseteq B _ { t } \mid b _ { x } , b _ { y } \in F , b _ { x } \neq b _ { y } \Rightarrow P ( b _ { x } ) \cap P ( b _ { y } ) = \emptyset \}$ Winners of the auction are determined by maximizing the seller’s revenue, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname* { m a x } _ { \mathit { F } \in F _ { t } } \sum _ { b \in F } a ( b ) } \end{array}$ . Finding a solution to this problem, referred to in the literature as the winner determination problem (WDP), is NP-complete, i.e., determining winners in the auction is, in general, computationally intractable.

Let WIN denote the set of provisionally winning bids at auction stage t, i.e., the combination of bids that forms the solution to the WDP presented above. Adomavicius and Gupta (2005) referred to a bid $b \in B _ { t }$ as a winning bid if $b \in \bar { W I N } _ { t }$ and as a dead bid if $b \notin W I N _ { t }$ and $b \notin W \bar { I N } _ { t + n } ,$ where $n = 1 , 2 , \ldots$ . In other words, a dead bid is neither a winning bid at the current stage of the auction nor a winning bid in any future stage of the auction. Whereas in single-item auctions a bid can be in either a winning state (i.e., provisionally winning) or a dead state (i.e., losing), in this combinatorial auction setting, a bid can be in a third state—referred to as the live state—where it is neither winning nor dead (as illustrated in §1). Thus, given the different states that a bid can be in, navigating the bid space in the continuous combinatorial auction is far more complex than in single-item auctions, where a bid is either winning or losing.

Based on the aforementioned characteristics of bids, two natural bounds on ask prices can be defined for any potential bid on a package $P \subseteq I$ at auction stage t. The lower bound, termed the deadness level and denoted as $D L _ { t } ( P )$ , represents the minimal bid on package P required for the bid to be live; that is, any bid $b _ { t + 1 }$ on package P at auction stage t will be live if $a ( b _ { t + 1 } ) > \bar { D L } _ { t } ( P )$ . The upper bound, termed the winning level and denoted as $W L _ { t } ( P )$ , represents the minimal bid on package P required for the bid to be winning; that is, any bid $b _ { t + 1 }$ on package P at auction stage t will be winning if $a ( b _ { t + 1 } ) > W L _ { t } ( \bar { P } )$ . DL and WL form two generic boundaries for prices of packages in combinatorial auctions.<sup>7</sup>

## 2.3. Equilibrium Bidding Strategies

The sealed-bid VCG mechanism is a unique auction mechanism wherein truthful bidding is a dominant strategy for each bidder. However, as noted earlier, despite the existence of dominant strategy equilibrium and the promise of 100% efficiency, the mechanism is not popular in practice for a number of practical concerns (Rothkopf 2007 lists 13 of them). In singleobject ascending auctions, a straightforward bidding strategy typically implies the principle of minimum bid, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ choosing the minimum possible price allowed by the mechanism at every stage of the auction. However, in the case of combinatorial auctions, straightforward bidding also entails the principle of surplus maximization, i.e., placing a bid on the package(s) with the highest profit potential.<sup>8</sup> In the prominent iterative mechanisms mentioned earlier, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the iBundle, APA, and dVSV, straightforward bidding is an ex post Nash equilibrium strategy as long as the buyer submodularity condition holds for all valuations. Note that ex post equilibrium, although weaker than dominant strategy equilibrium, significantly minimizes the strategic complexity of the bidders and can lead to higher auction efficiency. Consequently, the straightforward bidding strategy has been considered as a rational bidding strategy in several studies (Ausubel and Milgrom 2002, Bichler et al. 2009, Brewer and Plott 1996, Scheffel et al. 2011). However, Chen and Ledyard (2008, p. 10) point out that “There is no evidence that actual bidders will actually behave this way”—an observation that has been corroborated in several recent laboratory experiments (e.g., Kagel et al. 2010; Scheffel et al. 2011, 2012). For instance, Scheffel et al. (2011, p. 348) compared several iterative designs in the laboratory and observed that “bidders did not seem to follow pure best-response strategies in any of the auction formats, not even iBundle where there are game-theoretical incentives to do so.”

For the continuous auction model described in the previous section, representing the minimum bid increment by $\delta ,$ we define $D L ^ { + } = D L + \delta$ as the minimum amount required to place a live bid and $W L ^ { + } = W L + \delta$ as the minimum amount required to place a winning bid. Petrakis et al. (2013) showed that straightforward bidding at $D L ^ { + }$ is an ex post equilibrium strategy with buyer submodular valuations. Furthermore, the strategy can lead to full auction efficiency. They also suggest that because any bid below $D L ^ { + }$ will be inconsequential, a surplus-maximizing bidder does not have any rationale for bidding below ${ \bar { D } } L ^ { + }$ , but bidding $W L ^ { + }$ can be rational $\mathrm { i f } \ ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { a }$ bidder is the only one able to outbid a winning coalition of bidders” (Petrakis et al. 2013, p. 769). In other words, although bidding at $D L ^ { + }$ keeps a bid live, suitable complementary bid(s) have to arrive to make it winning. If such bids do not arrive, then even a so-called rational bidder will eventually need to bid $W L ^ { + }$ to win. However, the equilibrium strategy offers a natural baseline for comparing actual bidding behavior.

## 2.4. Jump Bidding in Auctions

According to standard auction theory, in single-item ascending auctions, a suitable strategy for a bidder is to increase each bid by the minimum bid increment $\delta .$ Jump bidding occurs when a new bid exceeds the ask price $( \mathsf { s a y } , p )$ by more than the minimum bid increment, i.e., bid $> p + \delta$ . Although the straightforward bidding behavior, where bidders always bid the minimum amount required, has been assumed for enumerating expected auction outcomes in many nonclock ascending auctions, empirically jump bidding has been found to be widespread in many different forms of auctions, including English auctions (Haile and Tamer 2003, Isaac et al. 2005), multiunit ascending auctions (Easley and Tenorio 2004), and the simultaneous ascending auctions (Isaac et al. 2007, Plott and Salmon 2004). For instance, in the nationwide narrowband personal communication services (PCS) auctions conducted by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) in 1994, 59% of all new high bids were jump bids (Cramton 1995). However, the characteristics and economic implications of jump bidding in package-bidding auctions have not been adequately studied.

In the early literature on jump bidding, the behavior was considered irrational, as is evident, for example, from Cramton’s (1995, p. 327) comment following FCC’s nationwide narrowband PCS auctions: “The behavior seems to fly in the face of common bidding wisdom and perhaps even common sense.” However, Easley and Tenorio (2004) argued that, in online auctions, the cost associated with entering bids can explain jump bidding behavior. Plott and Salmon (2004), studying both field data from the spectrum auctions in the United Kingdom and related experimental auctions also concluded that the primary goal of jump bids appeared to be to speed up the auctions. Indeed, by systematically manipulating the opportunity costs of bidding in laboratory experiments, Kwasnica and Katok (2007) found that bidders impatiently chose larger jump bids in conditions where time was more valuable. The investigation by Isaac et al. (2007) of spectrum auctions in the United States and the United Kingdom also points to bidder impatience as a probable cause of jump bidding. Another common explanation for jump bidding offered in the literature is that bidders use jump bids as a signaling strategy—to intimidate opponents (Avery 1998, Daniel and Hirshleifer 1998) or to deter late entry in online auctions, where there is considerable uncertainty about future entry (Easley and Tenorio 2004).

Aside from the typical jump bids frequently observed in single-object auctions, combinatorial auctions present the possibility of a unique form of jump bidding, called block-jump bidding (Kelly and Steinberg 2000), in which a bid on a large subset of objects could prevent other bidders from formulating a comparable composite bid together. The possibility arises due to a well-known problem in combinatorial auctions called the threshold problem (see, for example, Rothkopf et al. 1998), which refers to the difficulty faced by bidders seeking smaller packages in outbidding a bidder who places a high bid on a large package of which the smaller packages are subsets.

Whereas the findings about the probable causes of jump bidding appear to converge in the literature, at least for noncombinatorial auctions, the impact of jump bidding on auction performance is far less understood. Following laboratory experiments with single-object auctions, Kwasnica and Katok (2007) reported that jump bidding had no effect on aggregate auction performance. Similar conclusions were reached by Plott and Salmon (2004) examining SAAs using a disequilibrium model of bidder behavior. However, Isaac et al. (2005, 2007) found revenues increased when jump bidding was allowed in single-unit auctions, whereas Bulow and Klemperer (2009) suggested that preemptive jump bids would hurt seller revenues but increase bidder profits. Furthermore, in terms of efficiency, Isaac et al. (2007) reported that, depending on the environment used, jump bidding had a neutral or positive effect on auction efficiency. Banks et al. (2003, p. 312), on the other hand, concluded that by engaging in jump bidding, bidders may “sacrifice potential profit” and the overall efficiency of the auctions. Similarly, one of the motivating factors for Porter et al. (2003) to propose the CC auction was to eliminate the ill effects of jump bidding on auction efficiency. Thus, in combinatorial auctions, the economic implications of jump bidding are an open issue that we also examine in this paper.

## 2.5. Price Revelation and Reference Prices

In the context of auctions, information revelation refers to the nature and extent of information revealed to the bidders during an auction and has been shown to have a significant impact on the performance of a variety of auction markets (Adomavicius et al. 2012, Bichler 2000, Haruvy and Katok 2013, Kannan 2012, Mithas and Jones 2007, Strecker 2010). For instance, Adomavicius et al. (2012) found that continuous feedback on the provisional winners significantly increased the efficiency of combinatorial auctions compared to the case where such feedback was not provided. They also concluded that in continuous combinatorial auctions, for bidders to ensure that they do not place inconsequential, losing bids, they must know the DLs for every package of interest at every stage of the auction. Because the computation of DLs even in small-sized auctions is cognitively prohibitive,<sup>9</sup> a strong case can be made for auctioneers to provide the DLs for all packages as feedback. Whether the WLs should also be made available, however, is less clear. Note that since the WDP is NP-complete and the computation of the WLs is at least as hard as that of the WDP, it follows that the computation of the WLs is also an NP-complete problem. Therefore, the question of whether WL should be offered as feedback along with DL is nontrivial even computationally. Furthermore, we argue in §3 that the ready availability of WL may act as a reference price for the bidders nudging them toward jump bids.

It is well known that instead of engaging in exhaustive information processing, people tend to use cognitive heuristics to simplify their decision making (Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996, Kahneman and Tversky 1979). Simon (1956) proposed the notion of bounded rationality to dispense with the unrealistic assumptions about the time, attention, knowledge, and other resources available to humans in real-world situations. The models of bounded rationality proposed in the literature can be classified as (i) those that search for alternatives (e.g., satisficing solutions) and (ii) those that search for cues (e.g., fast and frugal heuristics).<sup>10</sup>

One prominent heuristic employed by economic agents involves the use of reference points to evaluate potential outcomes as gains or losses (Kahneman 1992). The evaluation in turn affects preferences and, hence, behaviors. For instance, consumers’ preferences for products and perceptions of attribute values are determined largely by the reference points they use, rather than by absolute values alone (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Nowlis and Simonson 1997). Consequently, consumers use reference prices to estimate the value of an item and make purchase decisions (Mazumdar et al. 2005, Putler 1992). Reference prices that are spontaneously used by consumers (e.g., from prices encountered previously) are regarded as implicit, whereas those that are suggested, for example, by the seller, are termed explicit. Prior research has investigated the strategic use of explicit reference prices—e.g., by presenting the regular price alongside the sale price—to frame the perception of the product in a way that is favorable to the seller (e.g., Shiv et al. 1997). In the context of online auctions, Ariely and Simonson (2003) have shown that an auctioneer, for example, can manipulate the starting price as an explicit reference point to drive up the winning bid for a product.

For combinatorial auctions, we argue that whether the DL, WL, or both are offered as an explicit feedback to the bidders will influence the extent of jump bids placed in a combinatorial auction because, given the cognitively complex bidding environment, bidders will use the ask prices as references while formulating their bids. Thus, in our laboratory experiment, we propose to use the three possible variations of the two natural bounds of ask prices—DL and WL, only DL, and only WL—to act as treatment conditions.<sup>11</sup> The dependent variables are the extent of jump bidding and the traditional metrics of auction performance, namely, seller’s revenue, bidders’ surplus, and auction efficiency, widely used in the literature on combinatorial auctions. We define jump bids as bids that exceed the DL by more than the minimum increment; i.e., any bid that is greater than $D L ^ { + }$ for the package is considered a jump bid. Note that a bid that exceeds the $W L ^ { + }$ is automatically a jump bid because $W L ^ { + } \geq D L ^ { + }$ for every package. As noted in §2.2, in combinatorial auctions, aside from a bid being characterized as a jump bid based on its magnitude, a bid can also be a jump bid based on its size, i.e., a block-jump bid. We define a bid as block jump if it is on a package that includes at least half the number of objects on sale, i.e., if $| P | \ge | I | / 2$ according to our definitions in §2.1. Efficiency of each auction is computed as the ratio of the total valuation of the assets for the winners and the maximum possible valuation of the set of values in that auction. An efficiency of 100% is achieved when all assets are allocated to bidders who value them the most.<sup>12</sup> Whereas high seller’s revenue benefits the seller and high surplus benefits the bidders, high efficiency maximizes the overall social welfare and is thus a desirable goal.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

In this section we present our hypotheses concerning the effects of price revelation schemes on the extent of jump bidding and the impact of jump bids on the economic outcomes of auctions. First, we develop hypotheses about the expected jump bidding behavior using theories on how people use reference prices as anchors, and second, based on our expectations of jump bidding and auction theories, we build hypotheses on the expected economic implications of jump bidding.

In combinatorial auctions with only the DL available, the bidders will have explicit knowledge of the minimum amount required to place nonlosing bids on packages of their interest but not the minimum required to place winning bids. Although in the early stages of the auction, the WL for many of the packages will be the same as the corresponding DL, as the auction progresses, the WL for most packages will most likely be higher than the corresponding DL. Given the computational complexity of determining WLs of packages, bidders cannot be expected to compute them. Thus, we expect bidders to use DL as the sole reference to primarily place live bids (i.e., nonlosing bids). Based on the auction literature on jump bids discussed above, we can expect many of these live bids to be jump bids.

When both DL and WL are available, bidders will use both DL and WL as reference prices to place bids at or above DL or at or above WL. Therefore, we can expect the percentage of jump bids to be at least as high as in the previous case (i.e., when only DL is available) because even when WL is used as a reference, the bids, jump with respect to WL or otherwise, will be jump bids with respect to DL (since WL ≥ DL). Thus, we expect a greater percentage of jump bids with both DL and WL available than with just DL available.

In the third case, when only WL is available, as before we contend that the bidders will be unable to compute the DL on their own. Therefore, we expect the bidders to use WL as the sole reference price. As stated above, the majority of these bids, jump bids with respect to WL or otherwise, will be jump bids with respect to DL. However, when both DL and WL are available, some of the bids that are placed using the DL as the reference will not be jump bids. Thus, between the two cases where the WL will be available, the percentage of jump bids will be higher when only the WL is available compared to when both the DL and WL are available. This leads to the following set of hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> The percentage of jump bids placed with both the DL and WL available will be more than the percentage of jump bids placed with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> The percentage of jump bids placed with only the WL available will be more than the percentage of jump bids placed with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1C</sup> <sup>(H1C).</sup> The percentage of jump bids placed with only the WL available will be more than the percentage of jump bids placed with both the DL and WL available.

We now switch our attention to the economic implications of the proposed jump bidding behavior outlined above. Our first set of hypotheses focus on the possible effects of jump bidding on auction efficiency. The allocative efficiency of a trade increases when goods are allocated to people who value them more. When bidders make jump bids, they can alter an efficient allocation even in a single-object auction (Smith et al. 1991). In multiobject auctions, the negative effects of jump bidding on auction efficiency can be even more dramatic (Banks et al. 2003, Porter et al. 2003) because of the existence of many possible inefficient allocations.

Although we expect the percentage of jump bids to be influenced by the type of reference prices available to bidders, we have no reason to believe that those prices will have any effect on the magnitude or frequency of the block-jump bids. In other words, we do expect ask prices to have an impact on the value of a bid, but not on the size of the package that the bid is placed on. However, when block-jump bids are made, the threshold problem has to be effectively overcome for combinatorial auctions to be efficient. Let us illustrate how the threshold problem can pose a threat to auction efficiency using a simple example with two items, X and Y, and three bidders, B1, B2, and B3. Suppose B1 and B2 value X and Y respectively at \$90, and B3 values the package {XY} at \$150. In this case, the auction is 100% efficient if X and Y are allocated to B1 and B2, respectively. Suppose B3 places a block-jump bid of \$100 on the package {XY}, making the DL of X and of Y \$0 but their WLs (as well as that of {XY}) \$100. To outbid B3, B1 and B2 will need to piece together bids on X and Y, respectively, such that the total is greater than \$100. However, if the coordination fails and B3 wins both items, then the efficiency will be only 83.33% (\$150/4\$90 + \$9055, i.e., much less than 100%. Note that B1 and B2 may find it difficult to overcome the threshold problem if one or both choose to free ride, e.g., B1 makes a bid of \$20 and hopes that B2 alone will make up the difference needed to outbid B3. However, Goeree and Holt (2010), based on their laboratory experiments, concluded that the threshold problem was more of a coordination problem than a free-rider problem.

Since successful coordination will require piecing together an equivalent combination of bids from multiple bidders, unavailability of the DL will make such coordination the most difficult. The bidders will have little by way of reference to start to incrementally formulate a composite bid to match the block-jump bid, resulting in loss of efficiency. Therefore, we expect the efficiency to be lowest when the DL is unavailable, i.e., in the case when only the WL is available. Between the two cases where the DL is available, we have argued above that the case with both DL and WL available will attract more jump bids. In other words, the WL provides an easy reference for bidders to be impatient. Note that, based on auction theory summarized above, the primary reason behind bidders making jump bids is impatience. However, outbidding block-jump bids requires patience on the part of the coordinating bidders. Therefore, overcoming the threshold problem effectively will be more challenging in the case where both the DL and WL are available because some bidders will impatiently use the WL to place winning bids instead of placing a succession of live bids. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> The efficiency with both the DL and WL available will be lower than the efficiency with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> The efficiency with only the WL available will be lower than the efficiency with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2C</sup> <sup>(H2C).</sup> The efficiency with only the WL available will be lower than the efficiency with both the DL and WL available.

In terms of the effect of jump bids on auction revenue, as discussed above, in the early models of jump bidding (Avery 1998, Daniel and Hirshleifer 1998), the behavior was explained as an intimidation strategy that led to the dampening of seller’s revenue by suppressing higher bids. However, later studies using the impatience hypotheses proposed that revenues should increase as a result of jump bidding. For SAAs, evidence from both the laboratory and the field appear to support the latter hypotheses. For instance, Isaac et al. (2007) found that when bidders are impatient, allowing them to make jump bids leads to an increase in revenue as long as the minimum bid increment is low. In combinatorial auctions too, when the amount of jump bidding significantly increases, the seller’s revenue can be expected to increase. Furthermore, in the two cases where WL is available, if the bidders use the WL as the reference price to predominantly place winning bids (as has been observed by Adomavicius et al. 2012), the seller’s revenue will be higher than the case where WL is not available and bidders predominantly use DL as the reference. Therefore, we have the following set of hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A</sup> <sup>(H3A).</sup> The seller’s revenue with both the DL and WL available will be more than the seller’s revenue with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B</sup> <sup>(H3B).</sup> The seller’s revenue with only the WL available will be more than the seller’s revenue with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3C</sup> <sup>(H3C).</sup> The seller’s revenue with only the WL available will be more than the seller’s revenue with both the DL and WL available.

Our final set of hypotheses, derived from Hypotheses 2A–3C, focus on the possible effects of jump bidding on bidders’ surplus. Note that higher efficiency can potentially raise the revenue of the seller or the surplus of the bidder or both. Between the two cases where DL is available, we hypothesized that the efficiency will be higher but seller revenue will be lower when the WL is unavailable compared to when the WL is available. Therefore, we can hypothesize that bidder surplus will be higher when only the DL is available compared to when both the DL and WL are available. Similarly, the surplus generated by bidders in the case where only the WL is available will be lower than in the other two cases because, based on our hypotheses on efficiency and revenue, the efficiency will be the lowest and seller’s revenue will be the highest with only the WL available. These lead to the final set of hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4A</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> The bidders’ surplus with both the DL and WL available will be less than the bidders surplus with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4B</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> The bidders’ surplus with only the WL available will be less than the bidders’ surplus with only the DL available.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4C</sup> <sup>(H4C).</sup> The bidders’ surplus with only the WL available will be less than the bidders’ surplus with both the DL and WL available.

## 4. Research Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we use a laboratory experiment, which also allows us to closely examine the characteristics of jump bids placed in the auctions. Laboratory experiments have been extensively used to test a variety of combinatorial auction designs (Adomavicius et al. 2012, Bichler et al. 2013, Brunner et al. 2010, Goeree and Holt 2010, Kagel et al. 2010, Kwasnica et al. 2005, Porter et al. 2003, Scheffel et al. 2011). The experiments have proved to be an effective complement to the theoretical studies because bidders often deviate from theoretically expected behavior in these environments (Banks et al. 2003, Scheffel et al. 2011). For instance, describing the bidder strategies in the FCC spectrum auctions for PCS, McAfee and McMillan (1996, p. 168) note that whereas auction theorists expected the bidders to be “cagey” to protect their private information as much as possible, in reality the bidders behaved “much more aggressively,” entering jump bids far above the required minimum. Furthermore, the behaviors exhibited by bidders in the laboratory have been seen to closely correspond to the behaviors exhibited by experts in the field even in the case of spectrum auctions, where the bidders are often experienced economists representing telecommunications companies (Milgrom 2004). Below we describe in detail the design of the experiment, the rules of the auction, and the characteristics of the subjects.

## 4.1. Experiment Design

To run the experiment, we construct a continuous combinatorial auction environment, where k bidders compete to acquire N property lots—labeled A, $\mathrm { \mathrm { B } } , \mathrm { C } ,$ etc.—around a lake. We use two different setups for the experiments.

Setup 1 (Symmetric Bidders). In this setup, we use a valuation scheme similar to the asymmetric, random scheme used by Adomavicius et al. (2012). In this scheme, N values (corresponding to the N objects) are drawn from a uniform distribution $U ( \alpha , \beta ) .$ , and then the values are randomly assigned to the N assets for each bidder separately. Asset complementarity, a feature that contributes to the benefits of combinatorial bidding, is introduced by creating superadditive valuations for adjacent properties as follows: Suppose s denotes the superadditivity rate and d denotes adjacency, i.e., the number of sets of adjacent properties in a package. Then, the valuation of a package whose additive value is v would be ${ \boldsymbol { v } } + { \boldsymbol { v } } \times { \boldsymbol { s } } \times { \boldsymbol { d } } ,$ i.e., $v ( 1 + s \times d )$ . For example, suppose $s = 0 . 1$ and the value of property A is \$40, the value of property B is \$60, and the value of property C is \$80. Then, the assigned value of the package {AB} is $1 0 0 \times 1 . 1 = \$ 1 1 0$ since the package has one set of adjacent properties $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , d = 1 ) ;$ the valuation of $\{ \mathrm { A B C } \}$ is $1 8 0 \times 1 . 2 = \$ 21 6$ since the package has two sets of adjacent properties, {AB} and $\{ \mathrm { B C } \} ;$ but the value of the package {AC} is simply \$120 since A and C are not adjacent. For this study, we chose the number of bidders as three $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , k = 3 )$ , the number of assets as six $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } N = 6 ) , ^ { 1 3 } \alpha = 1 , \beta = 1 0 0 ,$ and $s = 0 . 1 . ^ { 1 4 }$

Setup 2 (Asymmetric Bidders). This setup is motivated by the asymmetric valuation scheme used by Goeree and Holt (2010), Brunner et al. (2010), and Kagel et al. (2010). As with Setup 1, we chose the number of bidders as three $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \ k = 3 )$ and the number of assets as six $( \mathrm { i . e . , } N = 6 )$ . However, in this asymmetric scheme, two of the bidders were defined as small regional bidders and one as a large national bidder. The regional bidders had a positive value only for half of the lots $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ three), whereas the national bidder was interested in all of the lots. The standalone values for the regional bidders were drawn from the interval 611 1007, whereas those for the national bidder were drawn from the interval 651 657. In this scheme, we use two complementarity levels: a low synergy regime with $s = 0 . 1$ and a high synergy regime with $s = 0 . 2$ Values for adjacent lots were computed the same way as in Setup 1 using the standalone values and the superadditivity rate.

In both schemes, the minimum bid increment  was set to one. Consistent with the assumptions of the independent private-values model, the valuation scheme was common knowledge to the bidders, but the individual values of the assets were private knowledge. We created three treatment conditions, which were identical in every respect except in the nature of the ask prices offered to the bidders as feedback. In the first treatment (Treatment 1), $D L ^ { + }$ and $W L ^ { + }$ were revealed for every package throughout the auction; in the second treatment (Treatment 2), only $D L ^ { + }$ was revealed for each package; and in the third (Treatment 3), only $W L ^ { + }$ was revealed. In all three treatments, the winning bids were identified to the bidders at all stages of the auction, and the dead bids $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , losing bids that were inconsequential in all future stages of the auction) were eliminated from the display. In other words, at any stage of the auction, only the live bids were visible to the bidders, with the winning subset labeled.

## 4.2. Experiment Procedure

Several auctions were conducted simultaneously in each session. At the beginning of each session, instructions explaining the rules of the experiment were read out loud so that everyone can hear.<sup>15</sup> The instructions were followed by a quiz to ensure that each subject understood the rules of the auction and were familiar with the auction interface. The subjects were then randomly assigned to each auction. The minimum duration of each auction was 15 minutes, but if a nonlosing bid was placed by any of the bidders in the last 30 seconds, the duration of the auction was extended by 30 seconds.<sup>16</sup> The average duration of the auctions was 19 minutes, 9 seconds. The bidders could bid continuously, placing as many bids as they wanted on as many packages as they wanted.

## 4.3. Subjects and Payments

The research subjects, all undergraduate business students (mean age, 20 years; 51% female) at a U.S. university, responded to solicitation emails sent out to all registered undergraduate students at the school. Each subject was allowed to participate only in one auction to avoid confounding our results with learning effects from one auction to the next. Thus, the comparisons reported in this paper are all between subjects. Consistent with the recommendations of the induced-value theory (Smith 1976), subjects were rewarded for their actions (and those of the other agents) through cash payments based on their generated surplus, which was the difference between their assigned valuation for a package and their winning bid on the package. If they did not win anything, their surplus was zero. The surplus generated by the bidders in experimental currency, i.e., francs, was converted to U.S. dollars using the conversion rate 5 francs = 1 U.S. dollar. In addition to the performance-based payments, the subjects received a show-up fee of \$5 and an initial endowment of 50 francs (i.e., \$10) at the start of the auctions.<sup>17</sup>

Setup 2, even the auctions with both the DL and WL available (H2A supported). Furthermore, our hypothesis that the auctions with the DL and WL available will be more efficient than the auctions with only the WL available (H2C) was supported in Setup 2 but not in Setup 1. Examining the bids placed in Treatments 1 and 3 in Setup 1, we find that the WL served as a consistent anchor for the bidders in Treatment 3, which is not surprising, but even in Treatment 1, bidders used the WL as the reference considerably: 56% of the bids in Treatment 1 were winning bids. Furthermore, the number of jump bids with respect to WL (discussed in detail in §5.2) in the two treatments where the WL was available was not different. As a result, the efficiency impacts of jump bidding were not significantly different in Treatment 3 from those in Treatment 1. Even when the DL was available in Treatment 1, bidders did not make sufficient use of it to overcome block-jump bids.

As hypothesized, with only the WL available, bidders placed a large number of jump bids, thereby raising the seller’s revenue. As a result, the revenue generated in Treatment 3 was significantly higher than that in the other two treatments (H3B and H3C supported). Although the auctions with WL only were not as efficient as the other two conditions, seller’s revenue was not hurt because of that. However, as can be expected, the condition was significantly detrimental to bidders’ interests: The surplus generated by bidders in Treatment 3 was lower than that of their counterparts in the other two treatments (H4B and H4C supported). Treatment 1, where both DL and WL were provided, generated more revenue than Treatment 2, where only the DL was provided (H3A supported). Conversely, bidders were able to garner more surpluses on average with only the DL available than with both the DL and WL available (H4A supported).

The finding that on average the bidders were able to generate more surpluses with just the DL available to them than with both the DL and WL available is significant because less information appears to be more profitable for the bidders. Although the result is counterintuitive, it can be explained using the literature on reference prices. For instance, a familiar marketing ploy involves the presentation of the regular price of a product along with the sale price, where the regular price is offered to serve as an explicit reference point for the consumer to frame the price comparison in a way that benefits the seller (Shiv et al. 1997). From the consumer’s perspective, using the higher regular price as an explicit reference as opposed to using an implicit reference might make a sale price more attractive than it otherwise is, and may tempt her into a less profitable transaction. In other words, the availability of additional information in the form of a regular price, despite being relevant, may hurt the consumer financially. Similarly, intuitively it seems that access to the WL in addition to the DL should be useful to bidders, but in reality the WL tempts the bidders into making jump bids that eventually hurt them.

Overall, we find that the ask prices that the auctioneer chooses to offer as feedback to bidders can have a significant impact on jump bidding, which in turn can significantly influence the economic performance of auctions. We find that WL is an attractive reference that tempts the bidders into making a substantial number of jump bids. Furthermore, whereas the availability of DL mostly resulted in higher efficiency, its absence proved beneficial to the seller. Thus, even though from a feedback point of view, one can argue that the availability of WL but not DL is not beneficial to the bidders, the treatment with WL generates the maximum revenue, and, therefore, private sellers might be tempted to choose that feedback option.

## 5.2. Bidding Behavior

Our primary goal in this section is to investigate the bidder strategies that led to the aggregate outcomes reported above. First, we examine in detail the characteristics of the bids, especially the jump bids placed in each treatment, and second, we perform the necessary manipulation checks by testing our arguments for the hypotheses.

Table 5 displays the descriptive statistics of the number of bids placed in the auctions and the auction durations. On average, bidders placed the most number of bids with just the DL available and the least with only the WL available. Comparing the two setups, we find that fewer bids were placed on average in the auctions with Setup 2, which were also of shorter duration. One possible reason for the fewer bids and shorter duration of the auctions in Setup 2 is that in these auctions two out of the three bidders were only interested in half the number of lots compared to their counterparts in Setup 1.

In both setups, with just the DL available, the bidders placed a large number of live bids using the DL as the reference.<sup>18</sup> In this treatment (Treatment 2), since the bidders could realize whether they were winning only when they were in the provisional allocation, and since live bids may not be winning especially when the WL is high in later stages of the auction, the bidders revised their bids frequently till they found themselves among the provisional winners. When they were outbid, they often attempted to incrementally (i.e., by placing a series of live bids) get themselves back into the winning set. However, in Treatment 1, where both DL and WL were available, if the bidders were not willing to incrementally move toward the winning set, they could directly place winning bids using the WL as the reference. Consequently, fewer bids were placed ( = 0005) when both the DL and WL were available compared to when only the DL was available because at least in some instances bidders used the WL to place winning bids rather than revise their bids in small increments. In Treatment 3, without the DL, bidders could not incrementally raise their bids; rather, they placed winning bids directly since with the ready availability of the WL, they knew exactly how much they needed to bid to be in the winning allocation.

The more aggressive bidding in Treatment 3 served to speed up the auctions (average duration 18 minutes), whereas the incremental bidding in Treatment 2 prolonged the auctions (average duration 19 and a half minutes). Thus, faster auctions, a highly desirable auction characteristic, appear to be an important consequent of jump bids.

In terms of the magnitude and timing of the jump bids, we find a consistent pattern across all three treatments in both setups: the large jump bids were primarily placed early in the auctions with the magnitude of the jumps decreasing with the progression of the auctions. Figure 2 displays the average magnitude of jump bids in each quarter of the auctions. We represent magnitude as the percentage by which the amount of the bid was above DL<sup>+</sup>, i.e., (Bid-DL<sup>+</sup>)/DL<sup>+</sup>. As can be observed from Figure 2, the large jump bids were placed in the early stages of the auction on average. These jump bids could be attributed to an attempt to speed up the auctions or a way of signaling.

Although the gradual decrease of the magnitude of jump with the progress of the auctions was a consistent pattern across the three treatments, the average magnitudes were different among the three treatments especially in the second and third quarters of the auctions. It appears that the nature of price revelation has a bigger impact on the magnitude of jump bids over the second and third quarters of the auction; that is, in the early stages of the auctions, bidders placed large jump bids for the reasons mentioned above, but as the auctions progressed, the reference prices had a strong influence on the magnitude of the jump bids. As a result, in Treatment 3, with WL as a reference, the average magnitude of jump bids in the third quarters of the auctions was more than double that with DL as a reference in Treatment 2.

In developing our hypotheses concerning auction efficiencies, we argued that we do not expect the reference prices to have any effect on the percentage of block-jump bids placed in the auctions. The result, displayed in Table 6, confirms our assertion.

Although our definition of jump bids is with respect to DL, we also present the statistics for the jump bids with respect to WL in Table 6 to check our assertions regarding the use of the ask prices as references.<sup>19</sup> The bids that exceeded the provided WL<sup>+</sup> (i.e., the jump bids with respect to WL) in each treatment were significantly higher ( = 0005) in Treatments 1 and 3, where WL was available, than in Treatment 2, where WL was not available, confirming our claims regarding the use of DL and WL as references by the bidders.

As in single-object auctions, where bidders frequently place jump bids defying the principle of minimum bid, as shown in Table 7, in our multiobject auctions, we find little evidence of bidders pursuing the equilibrium strategy of straightforward bidding: First, less than half of the bids in each treatment were minimum bids, and second, approximately 15% of the bids in each treatment in Setup 1 and approximately 19% in Setup 2 were on surplus-maximizing packages.

The consistent deviation from the equilibrium bidding strategy can have many reasons. First, as we indicated above, based on the magnitude and frequency of the bids at different stages of the auction, it appears that the jump bids were being placed to speed up the auctions as well as possibly to signal aggressiveness. For instance, as we have indicated before, even in Treatment 1, where both DL and WL were available, bidders used WL as a reference more frequently than DL (56% of the bids were winning bids in Treatment 1); that is, instead of incrementally formulating a multitude of live bids, bidders were directly using WL as a reference to speed up auctions in the early stages. This is further emphasized by the fact that when WL was unavailable, bidders placed more live bids than winning bids (at  = 0005).

Second, the dearth of bids on surplus-maximizing bundles could be attributed to cognitive hurdles of evaluating a large number of bundles. As we indicated in §2.3, although the behavior is in violation of the rational bidding strategy, others have observed similar behavior in complex multiobject auctions (e.g., Kagel et al. 2010; Scheffel et al. 2011, 2012). For instance, in their experiments with a variety of iterative combinatorial auction formats, Scheffel et al. (2011) found no evidence of bidders following the best-response strategy. In particular, in iBundle, where, as in the continuous auction, best-response bidding is the rational strategy, they found only 13% of the bids to be best-response bids. Scheffel et al. (2011) concluded that in complex decision environments, bidders cannot be expected to follow the theoretical rational strategies. In particular, Scheffel et al. (2012) attributed the limited number of packages that bidders evaluated in iterative mechanisms to working memory limits of the bidders. Furthermore, they found that bidders preselected packages to bid on and that the preselection resulted in the loss of efficiency of the auctions. Similarly, we find the extent of jump bids to be inversely related to auction efficiency. For instance, the availability of WL resulted in a higher percentage of jump bids and also lower efficiencies.

Although the deviation from the rational strategy has been observed in prior literature, our analysis revealed an interesting pattern: bidders placed a considerable number of bids on the winning packages. In each treatment, approximately 20% of the bids were made on one of the provisionally winning packages, which evidently offered a computationally cheap solution for the bidders to be included in the provisional allocation. Rather than looking for surplus-maximizing packages, the bidders resorted to the simpler solution of bidding on the winning packages. Thus, based on our analyses of bidder behavior, we offer bounded rationality of agents as an additional explanation for the jump bidding phenomenon in combinatorial auctions. In the cognitively challenging combinatorial bidding environment, we find the bidders employing numerous fast and frugal heuristics (Gigerenzer and Goldstein 1996, Goldstein and Gigerenzer 2002) rather than attempting to compute optimal solutions. These heuristics are at least partially responsible for the deviation from straightforward bidding behavior.

To investigate whether the heuristics employed by the bidders were suboptimal, we test a regression model using the percentage of jump bids as the explanatory variable for the surplus retained by each bidder after controlling for the feedback effects and the setup effects. Unlike with the auction level analysis in the previous section, where we computed the percentage of jump bids as the ratio of the total number of jump bids in the auction to the number of bids in the auction, for the bidder level analysis we compute the jump bid percentage as the ratio of the jump bids placed by the bidder to the total number of bids placed by her.

Note that whereas fast and frugal heuristics in general are not necessarily suboptimal relative to their information-greedy counterparts, in the context of bidding in combinatorial auctions, the heuristics employed by the bidders hurt their profitability.

Overall, we find that the reference prices that bidders have at their disposal significantly influence their strategies and their own economic welfare as well as that of the seller. Whereas bidding aggressively in the absence of DL hurt the bidders, it proved beneficial to the sellers. Furthermore, withholding the WL information and letting bidders place a large number of marginal bids resulted in more efficient but slower auctions.

## 6. Conclusions

Auctions have been an immensely successful online mechanism for many years, but the single-object auctions prevalent on the Web lack the ability to consider complex consumer preferences, e.g., the complementarities among different products and services that the consumer desires. Since the existence of such synergies is common in many contexts, Steinberg (2012, p. 707) asserts that the “future of auctions is combinatorial auctions” and “testing and evaluation of combinatorial auction procedures is what is now needed.” Our research is focused toward advancing our understanding of the design implications of combinatorial auction mechanisms so that they can be implemented with a high degree of success. To that end, in this paper we study how different design choices of the mechanism can affect a frequently observed phenomenon in many forms of auctions, namely, the jump-bidding behavior of bidders. We find that the nature of prices that an auctioneer chooses to offer as feedback can have a significant impact on jump-bidding behavior, leading to substantial differences in the economic outcomes of the auctions. Whereas certain information regimes are beneficial to the seller, certain others are beneficial to the bidders. These effects are robust across multiple setups.

Another major contribution of this study is the detailed analysis of characteristics and consequences of jump bids in combinatorial auctions. One significant finding of this study is that more information, despite being relevant to the decision-making process, may sometimes hurt the profitability of an economic agent. As we have explained in detail above, this seemingly counterintuitive outcome is the result of agents pursuing computationally frugal but suboptimal heuristics. Based on our analysis, we extend the theory base of jump bidding by offering bounded rationality as a probable cause for jump bidding in complex auctions. In summary, the contributions of this study include (i) a thorough analysis of the characteristics and economic consequences of jump bidding in combinatorial auctions, (ii) an evaluation of three theory-based price revelation schemes for conducting continuous combinatorial auctions, and (iii) an examination of bidder behavior to extend the theories of jump bidding in complex auctions. Our results are expected to assist auctioneers in making more informed design choices based on their objectives.

We believe that this and similar studies, by advancing our understanding of the mechanism, can assist in realizing its true potentials. An interesting extension to this study would be to explore how much the bidder’s problem is exacerbated by increasing complexity of the system. Since bidders struggled to identify the surplusmaximizing bundles (as shown in Table 7), the potential surplus for each package can be a useful feedback to offer. The present study can also be extended by manipulating other attributes of the mechanism, such as bid increment, which have been shown to influence jump-bidding behavior in single-object auctions.
