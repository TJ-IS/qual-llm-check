# The productivity impact of information technology across competitive regimes: The role of industry concentration and dynamism

Nigel Melville <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Vijay Gurbaxani <sup>b</sup>, Kenneth Kraemer <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Stephen M. Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, 701 Tappan St., Ann Arbor, MI, 48109-1234, United States <sup>b</sup> Paul Merage School of Business, UC Irvine, United States

Received 29 June 2004; received in revised form 30 August 2006; accepted 10 September 2006 Available online 25 October 2006

## Abstract

Empirical research has revealed differences in the economic impact of information technology (IT) across industries. However, the source of these differences is unclear. In this study we analyze the role of the competitive environment in moderating the productive impact of information technology and regular capital. We focus on two important features of an industry's competitive environment: industry concentration and industry dynamism. Industry concentration is the degree to which the output of an entire industry is produced by a few firms and is considered an inverse proxy for industry competitiveness. Industry dynamism denotes change that is difficult to predict, measured as the deviation of industry sales from a trend line. We analyze the moderating impact of concentration and dynamism on the output elasticity of information technology and regular capital by estimating a production function using 5211 firm–year observations spanning the years 1987 to 1994. We find that the marginal product of IT is lower in more concentrated industries, while the opposite is true for regular capital. There is limited evidence that the marginal product of IT is higher in more dynamic industries, and strong evidence that the marginal product of regular capital is lower in more dynamic industries. Taken together, our results suggest that IT provides enhanced productivity impacts to firms in more competitive industries without any productivity loss in dynamic industries, in contrast to regular capital. The findings underscore the salience of inclusion of the competitive environment in studies of the productive impacts of information technology. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Competitiveness; Concentration; Dynamic capabilities; Dynamism; Industry structure; Information technology; IT business value; Productivity

## 1. Introduction

There is an emerging consensus that information technology (IT) investment is associated with enhanced organizational performance. Empirical studies at the firm level have provided strong evidence of positive and significant returns to IT investment in both the manufacturing and service sectors [7,10,13,27,54]. These findings are further confirmed by meta-analysis [50,78]. Investigations of specific IT applications and different industry settings have revealed the benefits of IT in a variety of contexts, including transactional IT in valve manufacturing [81], interorganizational systems in metals [64], automation in insurance [42], and computerized reservation systems in the airline industry [3].

Though prior research indicates that on average IT is associated with enhanced organizational performance, there is considerable heterogeneity in the payoffs across firms and industries. Studies at the firm level show that the magnitude of IT elasticity drops by roughly one-half when controlling for firm effects [12], suggesting that firm differences are important. These differences are due to factors such as complementary investments in organizational capital [10], proper alignment between IT and the mix of employees – clerical, managerial, and professional – to generate productivity gains [35], and close alignment between IS strategy and business strategy, which can be difficult and costly to achieve [17,24].

Likewise, industry heterogeneity is also associated with varying economic impacts. A widely cited study by the McKinsey Global Institute [33] found that despite a boom in IT investment by most industry sectors, productivity growth was concentrated in just six sectors. Early research found that the economic impact of IT is higher in manufacturing than in the services sector, though subsequent studies using broader data samples did not find a statistically significant difference [15]. The focus of prior research has not been on identifying specific industry characteristics that moderate the relationship between information technology and economic performance. Rather, researchers have examined the economic impact of IT application in individual industries, grouped industries in different ways, and considered differences in their input. For example, within the manufacturing sector, recent studies have shown that the durable goods sector on average displays higher returns than the non-durable sector [33,36], while earlier studies found no discernible pattern [63]. Another line of inquiry finds that the economic contribution to output is higher in IT-producing industries than in IT-using industries [48,67,77]. Researchers have studied the association between economic performance and the ratio of high-tech capital to regular capital across industries and found mixed evidence [6]. The underlying source of industry differences in IT investment impact has not been the focus of prior research and remains in question: “The use of industry controls does not address the issue of how industry characteristics constrain or promote the ability of competing firms to apply IT for organizational improvement.” [57, pp. 304].

There are several possible explanations. Firms in different industries may use different types of IT, or the same IT for different purposes, resulting in varying performance outcomes. Some industries may contain a higher proportion of technologically proficient employees with specialized knowledge of how to apply IT to improve organizational productivity. Organizational redesigns such as decentralization and hierarchical flattening may not be adopted uniformly across industries. Variation in IT productivity impacts across industries may also arise due to differences in the competitive environment, which has been shown to moderate the efficiency of regular capital [8,72,76].

The competitive environment in which a firm operates is a major factor shaping its strategies and actions [61,69]. It also affects the value that can be derived from a firm's core assets [8,56,68]. Firms in hypercompetitive and rapidly changing environments face greater information-processing requirements, complexity, and the need to change [31]. Information technology is a key tool in addressing such managerial challenges as it facilitates information processing, which may translate to enhanced decision-making. Moreover, IT is a general purpose technology (GPT), i.e., it is multifunctional, flexible and adaptable, and used pervasively in a wide range of sectors [9]. The widespread deployment of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems that share common functional modules across a variety of industries is a case in point. However, for firms in industries undergoing rapid change and fierce competition such as the PC industry, the ability to respond quickly and adapt to new business conditions is especially critical to success. Thus, given its general purpose nature, we expect IT to provide differential returns across alternative competitive landscapes. Regular capital – property, physical structures, mechanical equipment, etc. – is not a general purpose technology, is significantly less flexible than IT, serves fewer functions, and is less adaptable to new uses.<sup>1</sup> It is therefore reasonable to expect differences in productive impacts across the two forms of capital. In sum, our fundamental proposition is that the competitive environment shapes the value generating capability of information technology, and that it has a different effect relative to regular capital.

In this study, we examine the role of the competitive environment in moderating the productivity impact of information technology. We identify two dimensions of the competitive environment that are likely to shape IT business value, have a strong basis in theory, and have not been the focus of prior research: industry concentration and industry dynamism. Industry concentration is the degree to which the output of an entire industry is produced by a few firms, and is an inverse proxy for industry competitiveness [7,70]. Industry dynamism denotes change that is difficult to predict and is measured as the deviation of industry sales from a trend line [26,49]. Specifically, we analyze the moderating impact of concentration and dynamism on the output elasticity of information technology and regular capital by estimating a production function using 5211 firm–year observations spanning the years 1987 to 1994.

Results reveal that the marginal product of IT is lower in more concentrated industries, while the opposite is true for regular capital. There is limited evidence that the marginal product of IT is higher in more dynamic industries, and strong evidence that the marginal product of regular capital is lower in more dynamic industries. Taken together, our results suggest that IT provides enhanced productivity impacts in firms in less concentrated (more competitive) industries, without any productivity loss in dynamic industries, in contrast to regular capital.

This study offers two primary contributions to the literature. First, identification of specific factors in the competitive environment and their moderating effect on IT productivity provides an explanation for mixed findings in prior research while guiding managers and policy makers in their decision-making regarding IT investment. Second, the study demonstrates the salience of incorporating the industry context into the analysis of IT investment impacts and provides a foundational approach to identifying and analyzing such factors.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section we review theories that provide a conceptual linkage between the competitive environment and the efficiency with which factors of production are employed. We then develop two sets of hypotheses related to industry concentration and industry dynamism. Next, we describe the research method, including the theoretical model, data sources, and summary statistics. Empirical results are then presented, followed by a discussion of findings and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Industrial organization theory

The theory of industrial organization (IO) is concerned with the interplay among firm behavior, market structure, and economic performance and is a useful basis for analyzing the performance implications of IT application across competitive regimes. Its early thinking was rooted in the structure–conduct–performance (SCP) paradigm, in which industry structure is posited to shape the conduct of constituent firms, which in turn determines firm and industry performance [74]. Empirical studies by Bain [1] and others provide some support for this basic thesis [5,29,53]. Similar to the SCP paradigm, the X-efficiency hypothesis posits that the absence of competition allows for slack and other inefficiencies such as opportunistic behavior, leading to higher costs [52]. Modern IO theory incorporates strategic aspects of competition, suggesting alternative interpretations of empirical results. As an example, firms that innovate successfully may grow and gain market share, leading to increased industry concentration [65]. Alternatively, welfare losses arising from the lack of competitive pressure within highly concentrated industries may be outweighed by welfare gains due to enhanced investment opportunities and economies of scale [37]. Summarizing the large empirical literature relating organizational efficiencies to industry characteristics, Scherer and Ross [74] conclude that the body of evidence supports the claim that competitive pressure is associated with more efficient operations.

## 2.2. Management theories

Management scholars have developed additional perspectives that offer insight into how the industry context might shape the productive use of information technology. Institutional theory posits that the organizational environment places social and cultural pressure on organizations to conform to certain structural forms [71]. Institutional theory suggests a complex interplay between industry context and information technology, which can be viewed from the perspective of the material-resource environment (demand-side factors, supply-side factors, technologies, and market structure) and the institutional environment (institutional logics, institutional actors, and governance systems) [22]. According to the contingency theory, organizations are most effective when designed to fit with their external environments [51]. Organizational sub-systems must be designed to fit the competitive environment, while at the same time they must be integrated for overall efficiency. In this vein, Burns and Stalker [19] developed a theory proposing two modes of fit: 1) a mechanistic organizational form and a relatively stable environment; and 2) an organic organizational form and an unstable environment. Empirical studies examine whether, to what extent, and in what contexts such contingencies may exist. As an example, the impact of high-performance work systems on labor productivity is found to be moderated by the degree of industry capital intensity, industry growth, and industry differentiation [25]. This suggests the need for a fit between internal organizational practices and the external competitive environment for the attainment of improved organizational performance. Overall, institutional theory and contingency theory both suggest that factors in the competitive environment may play a role in shaping the productive efficiency of information technology.

Given increasingly competitive and rapidly evolving industries in the post-industrial business environment, there is a growing interest in dynamic capabilities, i.e., responsiveness to markets, rapid product design, and managerial ability to redeploy resources in response to environmental instability [32,79,80]. Dynamic capabilities are a source of competitive advantage that enable firms to change rapidly and effectively in response to changes in the competitive environment. Two factors that enable the formation and maintenance of dynamic capabilities are decentralization and local autonomy. In this regard, information and its management via information technology is a critical enabler. IT underlies fundamental shifts in organizational architecture, including the degree of decentralized authority [45], the flattening of organizations [16], and the shift to modern manufacturing [60]. There is empirical evidence that knowledge management mediates the relationship between information technology and dynamic capabilities [75]. In sum, turbulent industry environments require that firms develop dynamic capabilities – enabled, in part, by information technology – if they are to be successful.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Information technologies enable the storage, communication, and processing of information — a fundamental competency in the information age. Given the pervasiveness and strategic importance of IT, its economic impact is likely to be shaped by the competitive environment. Several factors have been suggested, including competition and regulation [22,33], industry turbulence [31,55,58], inadequate industry standards [23], and negative externalities [2]. However, few empirical analyses exist and we know very little about the observed role of these factors across large samples of firms.

In firm-level studies of IT productivity, one common approach has been to conduct a pooled analysis that controls for industry effects by using industry dummy variables. One could argue that these industry dummies may be interpreted as a proxy for the firm's competitive environment [50,54]. While reducing the possibility of spurious findings, industry-level controls do not identify differences in IT productivity across industries, nor do they enable the identification of why one industry may be more efficient overall than another. Another approach is to group firm-level data by sector and estimate separate regressions for each sector. This approach was used by Brynjolfsson and Hitt [13], who found that the gross marginal product of information technology was positive and significant in two sectors (durable and nondurable manufacturing) and statistically indistinguishable from zero in the remaining four sectors (mining, transportation and utilities, trade, and other services). They conclude that they “cannot confirm that the differences in measured returns are due to fundamental differences, or simply ‘noise’ in the data.” Industry-level studies also reveal differences across industries in the productive use of IT [6,62]. Some evidence indicates that computer-producing sectors might apply IT more effectively than others, but the body of evidence is equivocal [47,77].

In this study we focus on two factors in the competitive environment: industry concentration — the degree to which industry revenues are concentrated within a few large firms – and industry dynamism — industry change that is difficult to predict. We have chosen these two variables for several reasons: 1) each has strong conceptual and theoretical foundations, supporting the development of robust hypotheses; 2) they offer great potential to refine prior empirical results indicating heterogeneity in the economic impact of IT across industries; and 3) they have not been the focus of prior empirical studies applying production theory, enabling us to generate new knowledge about the economic impact of information technology.

## 3.1. Industry concentration

Industry concentration measures the extent to which industry output is produced by a few firms and is a commonly used inverse proxy for industry competitiveness. It is widely used in the industrial organization and strategy literatures to enable analysis of market power and industry performance [74,76]. The essence of the reasoning can be traced to Hayek's [43] notion that in the absence of competitive intensity the sophisticated pricing mechanism enabling the efficient allocation of resources is weakened. According to the X-efficiency hypothesis, the absence of competition allows for slack and other inefficiencies that raise costs [52].

Empirical evidence is consistent with the existence of X-efficiencies. In the case of electric power, costs are estimated to be as high as 8.5% higher on average in firms facing diminished competition [76]. In banking, higher concentration is associated with larger staffs and higher labor expense, controlling for urban size, demand, and branch characteristics [30]. With regard to labor productivity, monopolies over pay employees, thereby creating production inefficiencies [5]. Relating

X-efficiencies to the agency theory, Dobson [28] finds that agency costs – and hence X-inefficiencies – decreased during periods of increased privatization in the leveraged buyout boom of the 1980s.

The lack of competition, in addition to decreasing overall organizational efficiency, also decreases the productive efficiency of individual factor inputs. Though few empirical studies exist, results are consistent with the X-efficiency argument. For example, Börsch-Supan [8] examines productivity across five industrial sectors in Japan, Germany, and the U.S. The findings indicate that the nature of competition facing companies strongly influences the efficiency with which capital is utilized. In the U.S. telecommunications industry, increased competition from 1951 to 1990 is associated with a reduction in the excessive use of capital [68].

The mechanism by which competition shapes overall efficiency and the productivity of regular capital – Xefficiencies – is also likely to affect IT capital. Information technology is a large and growing proportion of overall capital spending and is assuming a greater role in the operations of firms. IT infrastructure, including data processing, networking, and data storage, is the central nervous system of the modern firm. IT infrastructure, together with software applications such as email, enterprise resource planning systems, and customer relationship management systems, enables efficiencies and provides new sources of revenue generation. Given the importance of IT, X-efficiencies associated with IT are likely to emerge, just as they did with regular capital, the foundation of the industrial era. As an example, in the highly competitive financial services industry a key basis on which firms compete is the extent to which they can leverage their IT infrastructure, including efficient data processing, communication, and storage, to deliver costeffective products and services [24]. To the extent that there is such an association, it would be reflected in the marginal product of IT and measurable within a production function framework. Though less competitive industries utilize IT for similar purposes, analogous to regular capital, we conjecture that the absence of competitive pressure leads to less efficient use of IT.

There are other possible interpretations of the interplay between competition, IT, and economic performance. Enhanced competition may lead to greater innovation, especially with respect to IT, which may result in enhanced productivity growth. The McKinsey Global Institute study not only found that the majority of IT returns were concentrated in six sectors, but that these sectors were also ranked the highest in terms of the removal of barriers to competition, such as regulatory constraints [33,34]. The study concludes that competition is associated with productivity-boosting innovations.

Our study refines this idea by analyzing the alternative possibility of a direct connection between competition and IT productivity, innovation notwithstanding. Another possibility is that highly concentrated industries tend to control larger amounts of resources and knowledge in technology.<sup>2</sup> This could lead to more efficient use of IT. However, this effect is mitigated by the activities of IT services firms who provide economies of scale, specialization, and scope to all firms. Though we acknowledge the existence of these and other possibilities, we argue for the positive impact of competition in driving IT efficiencies.<sup>3</sup>

Hypothesis 1A. The greater is the industry competitiveness, the higher is the marginal product of information technology.

Despite growing investment in information technology, regular capital – property, plant, and non-IT equipment – still accounts for the bulk of capital stock within firms. Prior research indicates that regular capital is used more productively within more competitive industries [8,68]. As with information technology, alternative explanations exist, including the possibility that within contestable markets the mere threat of competition is sufficient to invoke efficient use of capital. However, given the weight of evidence, and consistent with the Xefficiency hypothesis, we expect the productivity of regular capital to be shaped by the degree of industry competitiveness in the same way that IT is:

Hypothesis 1B. The greater is the industry competitiveness, the higher is the marginal product of regular capital.

## 3.2. Industry dynamism

Industry dynamism refers to change that is difficult to predict, creating uncertainty in managerial decisionmaking [26,49]. In dynamic environments managers must accelerate the speed of the strategic decision-making process. They may also rely more on exploration and innovation as against structured decision-making. Analysis of the PC industry indicates that improved performance in dynamic environments is achieved when managers rely more heavily on the use of real-time information [31], which is enabled in part by IT infrastructure.

IT infrastructure, including mainframe computers, storage devices, server farms, etc., provides the core information storage and processing capabilities of any firm. More IT infrastructure might hold more information, which may turn out to be useful in unstructured decisionmaking, what-if analyses, etc. Moreover, data is flexible and redeployable. Firms are able to subject existing data to new analysis, quickly collect new data using existing infrastructure, and deploy existing IT assets to support new applications. All of these provide digital options that are highly valuable to firms [73]. Within rapidly changing dynamic environments, digital options provided by IT infrastructure improve agility – “the ability to detect and seize market opportunities with speed and surprise” [73]–

The redeployability of data embodied in IT infrastructure may also be viewed from the perspective of dynamic capabilities. In rapidly changing environments, dynamic capabilities are critical: responsiveness to markets, rapid product design, and managerial ability to redeploy resources in response to environmental instability [32,79,80]. IT enables decentralization and local autonomy, which are both critical to the development of dynamic capabilities [16,45]. In less dynamic environments, the need for information to support quick and rapid responses may not be as pronounced. There is evidence for these arguments in prior research finding that the use of communications technologies increases with increases in industry clockspeed, which is closely related to business environment dynamics [58,59]. In contrast, in a MAN-OVA study of roughly 50 matched pairs of IT capability leaders and laggards, Lu and Ramamurthy [55] find mixed evidence regarding the role of dynamism in moderating the association between IT capability and accounting ratios such as return on sales and return on assets.

Taken together, our arguments can be summarized as follows. Information technology infrastructure enables the storage, processing and communication of data, which is critical to rapid response within highly dynamic environmental conditions. Moreover, data is inherently redeployable, enabling the formation of digital options when new ways are found to use existing data and information. IT thus enables the firm to navigate uncertain environments and enhance organizational performance. Relative to more stable environments, then, we might expect firms to reap enhanced benefits from their IT investments within highly dynamic industries. The counter argument is that firms in more stable industries use IT more productively than those in less stable environments. Reasons may include familiarity with technology, legacy effects (difficult to change technology quickly in response to rapidly changing demands), and the possibility that new applications required to respond to dynamic environments require new IT architecture and infrastructure. On balance, however, we argue that conceptual and empirical considerations favor the view that highly dynamic industries create the business context for firms to use IT more productively relative to those in more stable industries.

Hypothesis 2A. The greater is the industry dynamism, the higher is the marginal product of information technology.

Regular capital such as building facilities and electric generators does not support real-time decision-making. Moreover, regular capital is less flexible in terms of its ability to be redeployed to other uses. Thus, we would not expect our arguments to hold for dynamism and regular capital. On the contrary, in highly changing and uncertain environments, regular capital may become outdated or lose its value quickly [56]. Given the difficulty of adapting such things as turbines and plants to alternative uses to create new products and services, we might expect the productivity of regular capital to be greater in more stable environments. We thus hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2B. The greater is the industry dynamism, the lower is the marginal product of regular capital.

## 4. Data and methods

## 4.1. Data

Hypotheses were tested using a time-series crosssectional dataset of 933 publicly traded firms distributed non-uniformly over the eight-year time period from 1987 to 1994. The complete dataset comprises 5365 firm–year observations, while 5211 observations are available for all variables. Firms were selected for inclusion in the analysis based on the availability of variables required for empirical modeling: value added, IT capital, regular capital, labor, industry concentration, and industry dynamism.

Variable construction followed standard procedures [13,27,54]. Value added was calculated by taking the difference between sales and material input purchases, which were both obtained from the Compustat database. Labor expense was also obtained from Compustat. IT capital data were obtained from the CII installation database. It is a measure of the total IT stock of central processors, personal computers, and peripherals. CII IT capital stock is one of the most comprehensive measures of its kind and has been used in prior IT business value studies [10,18,44]. All variables were deflated to 1990 dollars using deflators specific to each factor input via standard methods [13].

Industry concentration is a widely used inverse proxy for competitiveness [7,70]. Industry concentration data are available from the U.S. Census Bureau in years ending in the numerals two and seven (Economic Census years). Given our sample, there are only two years of overlap – 1987 and 1992 – which would reduce the sample size by roughly 75%. To preserve the information contained in the full dataset, we calculate a four-firm concentration measure for each year using sales data from the Compustat database [7,20]. As a check on reliability, we found that the Census Bureau and computed metric are highly correlated (0.78) in the two overlapping years. Industry dynamism is measured by regressing industry sales on time, and dividing the resulting standard error of the regressor by the average industry sales [26,49]. As such, the measure “capture[s] true discontinuities in the task environment and allow[s] similar scores for task environments that present similar patterns but at different base levels of magnitude” [49, pp. 597].

Descriptive statistics are presented in Table 1. IT stock averages \$32.5 million, roughly one-eightieth that of regular capital (\$2.5 billion). Industry concentration averages 0.4, meaning that in the average industry, the top four firms account for 40% of all revenues. Dynamism averages 0.017, meaning that on average, the dispersion of points around a linear time trend normalized to average industry sales is 1.7%. This figure is comparable to that computed in similar industries for similar time periods in prior research [41]. Regarding correlations, most variables are positively correlated with one another, at varying levels of strength. IT capital is only moderately correlated with other explanatory variables: regular capital (.45) and labor (.55). An exception is dynamism, which is negatively correlated with all variables except industry concentration, with which it displays a weak positive correlation (0.05).

## 4.2. Empirical model

We adopt a production-function framework for empirical modeling. We assume that there exists a differentiable, locally convex and monotonic production function relating value added (V ) to IT capital (IT), regular capital (K ), and labor (L). We write the production function as V = V(IT, K, L).

Several functional forms are available, including the Translog, CES, and Cobb–Douglas. A hybrid functional form, the Translog–CES is a highly flexible function, allowing for non-unitary and non-constant elasticity of substitution among inputs. A drawback is its complexity and relatively large number of parameters to estimate, thereby lowering the degrees of freedom. To test whether this flexibility is required, or, whether a simple functional form is suitable, researchers have examined the same dataset used herein, for similar years, and could not reject the null hypotheses consistent with a reduction to the Cobb–Douglas form [27]. We thus utilize the Cobb– Douglas production function, consistent with prior research in this stream [13,54]. We estimate the production function in log-linear form as this approach does not require the assumptions of constant returns to scale, perfect competition, or profit maximization [39,46]. The logarithmic form of the three-input theoretical Cobb– Douglas model is given by:

$$
\ln V = \alpha + \beta_ {\mathrm{IT}} \ln \mathrm{IT} + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {L} \ln L\tag{1}
$$

where α is the efficiency parameter, and $\beta _ { \mathrm { I T } } , \beta _ { K } ,$ and $\beta _ { L }$ are the elasticity of regular capital, IT capital, and labor, respectively.

Incorporating moderating variables into Eq. (1) follows standard procedures [4,21]. We assume that the impact of regular and IT capital on value added varies linearly with the level of concentration and dynamism (main effects are also included in regressions, but excluded below for brevity).

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \ln V & = \alpha + \beta_ {\mathrm{IT}} \ln \mathrm{IT} + \beta_ {K} \ln K + \beta_ {L} \ln L \\ & + \beta_ {\mathrm{CRIT}} \mathrm{CR} \cdot \ln \mathrm{IT} + \beta_ {\mathrm{CRK}} \mathrm{CR} \cdot \ln K \\ & + \beta_ {\mathrm{DYIT}} \mathrm{DY} \cdot \ln \mathrm{IT} + \beta_ {\mathrm{DYK}} \mathrm{DY} \cdot \ln K \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Based on Eq. (2), the mathematical form of the null hypothesis enabling testing of hypotheses is a test of whether the interactive coefficients are different from zero. As an illustration, in the case of concentration ratio and IT, it is: $H _ { 0 } : \beta _ { \mathrm { C R I T } } { = } 0$

The elasticity and marginal product of IT capital in the presence of industry moderators contain additional terms relative to standard Cobb–Douglas formulae. IT elasticity is a function of both concentration ratio and dynamism and is given by $E _ { \mathrm { I T } } { = } \beta _ { \mathrm { I T } } { + } \beta _ { \mathrm { C R I T } } \mathrm { C R } { + } \beta _ { \mathrm { D Y I T } } \mathrm { D Y } .$ IT marginal product is computed directly as follows: $\mathrm { M P } _ { \mathrm { I T } } { = } E _ { \mathrm { I T } } ( Y / \mathrm { I T } ) { = } ( \beta _ { \mathrm { I T } } + \beta _ { \mathrm { C R I T } } \mathrm { C R } + \beta _ { \mathrm { D Y I T } } \mathrm { D Y } ) ( Y / \mathrm { I T } )$ Both equations reduce to the standard forms when $\mathrm { C R } { = } \mathrm { D Y } { = } 0$ . The empirical specification follows directly from Eqs. (1) and (2) by appending an error term assumed to be independently and identically distributed (i.i.d).

Estimation issues include the potential of multicollinearity, leading to wide confidence intervals, heteroscedasticity, which may lead to bias when it is severe, and the potential of endogenous explanatory variables. We attempt to mitigate these problems using well-known econometric techniques [40].

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Baseline results

A pooled regression that excludes moderating variables was estimated using ordinary least squares (Table 2, Model 1). IT elasticity is positive (0.08) and significant, with a standard error of 0.006, similar to that found in prior studies [14,44]. Regular capital and labor are also positive and significant. Given modest correlations among regressors, multicollinearity is not likely to be a problem (Table 1). The sum of IT, K, and L elasticity is 0.947, consistent with the Cobb–Douglas assumption of unitary and constant elasticity of substitution. Variance explained is 87% and F is 3393, significant at the .01 level.

In Model 2 we introduce concentration and dynamism as interactive terms on IT and K and as main effects. IT elasticity more than doubles from 0.08 to 0.184, while that of regular capital decreases by roughly two-thirds from 0.22 to 0.08. They are both significant at the .01 level. Moving to the interactive terms, the IT-concentration term is negative (−.29) and significant at the .01 level.<sup>5</sup> In other words, the higher the industry concentration (lower the competitiveness), the lower is IT elasticity. Taking concentration as an inverse proxy for competition, these results provide support for the retention of Hypothesis 1A. That is, IT output elasticity is increasing in industry competitiveness. In contrast, the capital-concentration term is positive (.42) and significant at the .01 level, refuting Hypothesis 1B. The dynamism-IT term is positive, consistent with Hypothesis 2A, though it is not significant $( p \mathrm { = } 0 . 6 1 )$ . When dynamism is interacted with regular capital the parameter estimate is negative (−1.3) and significant at the .01 level, providing support for the retention of Hypothesis 2B. That is, the marginal product of capital decreases as industry dynamism increases.

It is interesting to compare our findings with those of earlier studies. While prior research has not examined the direct association between competitiveness and IT productivity, studies have analyzed this issue for the case of regular capital [8,68]. These findings are not consistent with our result, which suggests that firms in less competitive industries use regular capital more efficiently. One reason for this apparent contradiction may be that the comparison is not exact, in that prior studies did not disaggregate capital into IT and regular capital. Another reason is the use of alternative models and time-frames. With respect to dynamism, prior research indicates that higher IT capability enables firms to derive better performance in less dynamic environments [55]. As with concentration, however, given the paucity of prior studies as well as differences in principal dimensions of the current study, comparison is difficult. Finally, the main effects concerning concentration are consistent with a large body of prior research indicating that overall operations are less efficient when industries are more concentrated, i.e., less competitive [30,74,76]. Additionally, operations appear to be more efficient in industries that are more dynamic.

## 5.2. Robustness

Potential deviations from modeling assumptions include unaccounted-for effects across firms and time, heteroscedasticity, and endogenous explanatory variables. There may be unaccounted-for effects due to differences in firms and changes over the sample time-frame. An example of the former is managerial practices. Omitting these from the pooled models may lead to bias, as these effects may be picked up by ITor other regressors, thereby changing their values and incorrectly assessing their impacts. In a prior study using a similar time-frame and econometric specification, IT elasticity dropped by about one-half when accounting for such potential variation [12]. It would thus seem prudent to include such effects to determine the extent of their impact on estimated elasticities relative to pooled regressions.

Two possibilities are the random effects model, which assumes that the omitted effect is drawn from a random distribution, and the fixed effects model, which assumes a deterministic influence. Deciding which model is appropriate is based on the context of data, how they were collected, and the environment from which they came. It has been suggested that “to unify the random effects and fixed effects formulations, it may be assumed from the outset that the effects are random” [46, pp. 150]. A random effects approach is consistent with our objective of drawing inferences about the entire population of large U.S. firms, as against only those in our sample. We therefore estimate a random effects model, which includes both temporal and spatial variation in the error term; for completeness, we also estimate a fixed effects model.

The random effects model is estimated using generalized least squares (GLS) (Table 2, Model 3). Compared with the pooled Model 1, IT elasticity has indeed dropped, from .08 to .04, a factor of roughly one-half and similar to that of prior research [12]. The elasticity of regular capital has decreased slightly while that of labor has increased slightly in comparison with the pooled estimator. When introducing interactive terms into the random effects model, IT elasticity (0.09) is also about one-half that estimated within the analogous pooled model (.18), remaining significant at the .01 confidence level (Model 4). The interactive term on IT and concentration has reduced from −0.29 to −0.149, a factor of about one-half. It remains significant at the .01 confidence level, indicating continued retention of Hypothesis 1A. In the case of regular capital, the interactive term also reduces in magnitude, but remains significant at the .01 level. As with concentration, the results for the random effects model for dynamism as a moderator are consistent with pooled results. The IT term is positive (.248), larger than the pooled model (.120), but not significant, while the regular capital term is negative (−0.847) and significant at the .05 level. Main effects are also retained in terms of sign and significance. The random effects model, in sum, is fully consistent with the pattern of results found for the pooled model. For completeness we also estimate the fixed effects model and the results are displayed in Table 2, Models 5 and 6. Here again, the basic pattern of results is retained, with an exception being that the K-dynamism coefficient loses significance. We emphasize that results from the random effects model are of primary consideration, based on guidelines from the econometric literature [46].

We also examined the validity of our assumptions about the error structure across the panels, including whether heteroscedasticity is an issue and whether results are robust to the potential for endogeneity. Regarding the former, White's test revealed the presence of heteroscedasticity, which we corrected for using robust standard errors. Results remained consistent with pooled results. Finally, using lagged values of independent variables as instruments, we also estimated the production function using two-stage least squares to account for potential simultaneity bias. In some cases, the results were even stronger, with interactive terms being higher in magnitude and significance. Overall, the results of two-stage least squares estimation supported the pattern of sign and significance found previously. In sum, given the results of pooled estimations and their robustness when including unaccounted-for effects across firms and time, corrected standard errors, and the possibility of simultaneity, there is strong statistical evidence supporting the retention of Hypotheses 1A and 2B, the refutation of Hypothesis 1B, and weak evidence concerning Hypothesis 2A.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Prior empirical research has revealed differences in the economic impact of IT across industries, including statistically significant industry dummy variables [13], differential productivity across industrial sectors [33], and differences in the benefit–cost ratio of IT across manufacturing industries [63]. Given the range of methods and econometric approaches all pointing to industry differences, it is clear that something is different across industries with respect to the economic impact of information technology. In this study we posited that industry characteristics might be one factor driving such differences. We used theories from industrial organization and management to identify two potential factors that may affect the degree to which IT is applied productively across competitive environments — industry concentration, which is an inverse proxy for competitiveness, and industry dynamism, which denotes the degree of change in an industry that is difficult to predict.

Our results add to the literature on IT productivity impacts by providing a theoretical basis for why the competitive environment matters together with supporting empirical evidence. Prior research indicates that the economic impact of IT is higher within industries that produce information technology versus those that use it [38,77]. Studies have estimated that roughly one-quarter of the productivity acceleration in the late 1990s was attributable to IT-producing industries [48,66]. These industries, including personal computers, routers, peripherals, etc., are recognized to be particularly competitive and dynamic. This study refines these earlier results that found differences between IT-producing and ITusing industries by providing empirical evidence for an underlying factor that explains such differences: industry competitiveness. Our findings provide insight into why large productivity impacts are seen in only a few sectors. While prior studies have implied that the concentration of large productivity impacts in a handful of industry sectors is surprising, our results show that this is an outcome of industry competitiveness and alters our expectations for the predicted industry-level productivity impacts. Though further analysis is required to extend our results to other time periods and alternative empirical models, results suggest that policies that facilitate competitive markets may be an important factor in raising productivity growth via their impact on the productivity with which IT is employed.

The present study also adds to the knowledge of IT productivity impacts by explicitly comparing the role of industry characteristics in moderating regular capital versus IT capital. Given its significantly different properties in terms of redeployability, digital options, flexibility, price-performance evolution, technological advance, and adaptability, we expect – and find – substantially different effects. Competitiveness is associated with higher IT productivity, but lower productivity of regular capital. Though the former supports our hypothesis, the latter was unexpected. One possibility is that scale economies in more concentrated industries are more important for regular capital. Further research is needed to explore this result further, perhaps by introducing scale economies explicitly within an econometric framework to test alternative hypotheses of competitiveness versus scale economies explicitly. Industry dynamism is also found to have a different impact across the two types of capital. In the case of IT, no effect is found, while for regular capital, industry dynamism negatively moderates its marginal product. The latter result makes intuitive sense. A firm is limited in its options for redeploying old plants and factory equipment when technological change has transformed the basis for value creation in an industry. On the other hand, our result for the former is not consistent with prior research showing that IT capabilities have a positive economic impact in more stable industries but no effect in more dynamic industries [55]. Further research is needed regarding the dynamism construct.

On a broader level, the present study extends thinking around the economic impact of IT with respect to industry differences by demonstrating that industry characteristics may be an important and measurable moderating factor. The study is easily extended using alternative industry variables and econometric formulations and several extensions of the research are possible. First, prior research found that a firm may achieve productivity without profitability via its IT investments [44]. Though it has been posited that competition may erode the appropriability of benefits resulting from IT application, this hypothesis remains untested. Is the increased IT productivity within more competitive industries (found herein) also captured by these firms? $\mathrm { O r } ,$ does competitiveness mitigate a firm's ability to capture benefits, perhaps increasing consumer surplus [11]? These are open empirical questions that can be investigated as direct extensions of the current analysis. Second, the results related to dynamism could be extended to further the theory of dynamic capabilities. Might there be a set of organizational practices and routines as well as IT architecture present to a greater extent in highly dynamic industries? Case and field studies might enable a response to this question. A third extension would be to examine other variables in the competitive environment such as growth, labor friction, and regulation. Fourth, one could use alternative methods, such as growth accounting or market value regressions to refine and extend the results reported herein. Lastly, though we used error-components modeling to allow for firm and time effects unaccounted for by our included variables, it would be interesting to model a setting that includes both internal complements such as the use of teams and process reengineering as well as factors in the competitive environment.

Another set of questions arising from the present analysis concerns the difference in value generation and capture across competitive regimes between the types of IT in widespread use during the period of this study (mainframes, minicomputers, personal computers) and those in widespread use today (e-business, intranets, email, etc.). To answer this question, we need to examine how relevant features of IT have evolved between the generation of technology prevalent in our dataset and those in use today. Since 1995, the last year in our dataset, a global information technology infrastructure built largely around Internet standards has emerged, increasing redeployablility and flexibility. The evolution of information technology to the network and Internet era thus attenuates the differences between ITand regular capital – redeployability, flexibility, and digital options – that we used to derive hypotheses. We therefore argue that our results would continue to hold today, and would possibly be even stronger given more recent data. Ultimately, however, this is an empirical question requiring more recent data and further analysis.

For managers, our results suggest that firms in highly competitive industries apply IT more efficiently than firms in other industries. Moreover, the employment of regular capital is less efficient in dynamic environments, but more efficient in concentrated ones, possibly due to economies of scale. We argue that the mechanism accounting for these differences is the inherent redeployability and flexibility of IT that enables rapid change in response to rapid changes in the competitive environment, i.e., dynamic capabilities. This suggests that IT executives should focus on investing in solutions that are flexible and redeployable as a means of creating valuable digital options and increasing the productivity impacts of IT investment in dynamic and competitive environments. Moreover, executives at firms in less competitive and more stable industries might look to those in highly competitive and dynamic industries, such as IT producing industries, when attempting to identify opportunities for better application of information technology. Though information-processing requirements vary across industries, there would appear to be opportunities for more efficient use of IT in less competitive industries. Such benchmarking may shift thinking toward more creative and efficient uses of existing IT and the adoption of newer information technologies applied in new ways to enhance value creation.

Vijay Gurbaxani is Professor of Information Systems at the Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine. His research interests are in the economics of information systems, focusing on evaluating returns to IT investment, competition in the digital economy, and on the sourcing and management of information services. He is the author of a book, Managing Information Systems Costs, and numerous journal articles. His research has appeared in Management Information Systems Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Communications of the ACM. He received his Ph.D. from the William E. Simon Graduate School of Business Administration, University of Rochester, and a Master's degree in Mathematics and Computer Science from the Indian Institute of Technology, Bombay. His doctoral thesis won the prize for the best dissertation in a worldwide competition sponsored by the International Center for Information Technologies.

Kenneth Kraemer is the Taco Bell Professor of IT for Management at the Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine. He has conducted research on the management of computing in organizations for more than 25 years. He is currently studying the diffusion of computing in Asia-Pacific countries, the dynamics of computing in organizations, the business value of IT, and policies for successful implementation of information systems. He has co-authored nine books, including, Asia's Computer Challenge: Threat or Opportunity for the U.S. and the World? and Managing Information Systems. His articles have been published in Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, Information Systems Research, Public Administration Review, Telecommunications Policy, and Policy Analysis. He was the Shaw Professor in Information Systems and Computer Sciences at the National University of Singapore.
