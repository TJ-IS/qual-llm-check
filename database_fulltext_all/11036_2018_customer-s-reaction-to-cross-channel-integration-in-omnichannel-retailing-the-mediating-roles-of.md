## a b s t r a c t

Although omnichannel retailing has gained significant interest among academics and practitioners, the mechanisms through which customers react to Cross-Channel Integration (CCI) in omnichannel retailing remain unclear. To this end, this study builds on the Push-Pull-Mooring (PPM) framework to unpack the processes through which uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs of omnichannel retailers play pushing, pulling, and mooring roles in shaping customers' reaction to CCI. We further explore the moderating influence of showrooming in these relationships. Survey findings reveal that uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs of omnichannel retailers partially mediate the effect of CCI on customer retention while fully mediating the relationship between CCI and interest in alternatives. We also uncovered that customer showrooming strengthens the negative relationship between CCI and retailer uncertainty. We conclude this paper with theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

## 1. Introduction

Leveraging on cross-channel integration (CCI) to assimilate and coordinate multiple online and offline channels for engaging and retaining customers continues to be an elusive challenge for retailers [1–4]. Despite an abundance of past studies on the operations of CCI [3,5,6], little is known about how CCI affects customers' reaction. Consequently, disentangling the effects of CCI on customers has been acknowledged as a critical but underexplored topic in the context of omnichannel retailing [2,3,7,8]. By centering on operations in multichannel retailing [9–13], prior research does not yield adequate understanding of how CCI drives customers' reaction. To advance this line of research, we attempt to shed light on the distinct mechanisms through which CCI influ ences customers in omnichannel retailing.

Omnichannel retailing is a prevalent channel strategy among contemporary retailers [7]. It is distinct from multichannel retailing in terms of customer involvement and retailer control [14]. Whereas omnichannel retailing derives synergy from full channel integration by streamlining cross-channel interactions with customers, its multichannel counterpart is focused on the intensity with which retailers are present across diverse channels [13,15]. Yet, in spite of the envisioned merits of CCI, an in-depth appreciation of customers' reaction to CCI is lacking [3,6]. This in turn gives impetus to our study.

A focal objective of CCI is customer retention [2,7,16]. Scholars have put forth several theoretical explanations for the effects of CCI on customer retention. Whereas Melis, Campo, Breugelmans and Lamey [6] and Verhoef, Neslin and Vroomen [15] conceived retailers' attractiveness to be a crucial determinant of CCI's influence on retaining customers, Herhausen, Binder, Schoegel and Herrmann [13] as well as Neslin and Shankar [17] posited customer lock-in as the mechanism underlying customers' retention via CCI. Recently, scholars proposed retailer uncertainty as a potential hindrance for omnichannel retailers who depend on CCI for customer retention [18,19]. Though past studies have identified distinct mechanisms which dictate the impact of CCI on customer retention, the current research landscape is rather fragmented. For this reason, we advance a consolidated framework that synthesizes and empirically validates the mediating influence of distinct mechanisms that have been promoted in prior research as drivers shaping customers' reaction to CCI in omnichannel retailing.

Moreover, extant literature has primarily focused on the effects of CCI in increasing customer retention and has not explored its impact on minimizing adverse consequences such as customers' interest in alternatives. Omnichannel retailing exists in a competitive business environment where most retailers serve customers who are free-riding on several providers [20,21]. An omnichannel retailer that seeks to acquire and retain customers should also be vigilant against the possibility of losing customers to competitors [22] because of the latter's attractiveness [23]. Therefore, interest in alternatives is also a vital consideration when implementing CCI [24,25]. By differentiating between customer retention and interest in alternatives, this study hence represents a holistic approach in crystallizing how CCI affects customer reaction in omnichannel retailing.

Apart from direct influences, past studies on multichannel customer behavior have also established that what customers bring (e.g., previous experience) could shape their interaction with a select channel [26]. This view is corroborated by Herhausen, Binder, Schoegel and Herrmann [13], who noted that customers' experience could moderate the effect of channel integration on their reactions. In omnichannel retailing, the proliferation of social media, in-store Wi-Fi, and mobile devices has culminated in showrooming whereby customers exploit a channel (e.g., mobile devices) for product evaluation before making purchases in another channel [27,28]. Arguably, showrooming could potentially drive customers' reactions to retailers' strategy, such as CCI [16], because it could erode their perceived value of CCI in reducing retailer uncertainty [27,29], in providing attractive offers, as well as in conserving time and effort [30]. This aligns with Verhoef, Kannan and Inman [8] call for further research in comprehending whether omnichannel retailers should foster or ‘push back’ customer showrooming.

Grounded in the Push-Pull-Mooring (PPM) framework, this study endeavors to uncover how customers react to CCI [31] by providing answers to the following research questions: (1) What are the mechanisms driving customers' reactions to CCI in omnichannel retailing and; (2) How does customers' usage of showrooming moderate their reactions toward CCI?

## 2. Theoretical foundation

2.1. Cross-channel integration and customer showrooming in omnichannel retailing

Omnichannel retailing is retailing that integrates multiple channels to enable customers to simultaneously harness all available online and offline retail channels when shopping [7]. This form of retailing aims to deliver a seamless customer experience through the provision of a borderless crosschannel service system [8] in which channel activities are coordinated across areas of promotion, transaction information management, product and pricing, information access and order fulfillment, as well as customer service [32]. Consequently, CCI, which exemplifies the integration of cross-channel information and functions, becomes a precondition for retailers keen on embracing omnichannel retailing [32,33].

Distinct from multichannel retailing, CCI in omnichannel retailing embodies its own value creation and delivery mechanisms that could aid retailers in serving customers [13,34,35]. Specifically, past studies on omnichannel retailing suggest that the value of CCI lies in realizing cross-channel benefits, including overall sales growth and optimized customer experience [3,8]. Researchers have asserted that omnichannel retailing would assist retailers in retaining customers by reducing uncertainty [18], providing attractive offers, and engendering switching costs [36]. Apart from business gains, scholars have alleged that omnichannel retailers can also leverage on CCI to project a coherent and reputable image to satisfy customers' psychological need for uncertainty avoidance, convenience, flexibility, and self-control [13,17]. In this sense, CCI could bolster customers' awareness of the brand equity offered by omnichannel retailers [8,37], what Bhattacharya and Sen [38] labeled as identity attractiveness. Moreover, although CCI empowers customers to move freely among separated channels of a retailer, they could be fenced in because of escalating switching costs associated with long-term learning from the retailer [5].

However, the implementation of omnichannel retailing is not without its challenges. Showrooming, defined as customers' ability ${ \mathrm { t o } } ~ ^ { \ast } ( 1 )$

evaluate products/services firsthand and (2) use mobile technology while in-store to compare products for potential purchase via any number of channels” [28 p. 360] threatens the efficiency of CCI that accompanies the growth of omnichannel retailing [7,13,39]. Whereas Gensler, Neslin and Verhoef [30] unveiled the diverse motivations behind customers showrooming (e.g., comparing product quality, price, and costs across channels), other studies pointed to the detrimental effect of showrooming on the self-efficiency and performance of in-store salesperson [28]. Herhausen, Binder, Schoegel and Herrmann [13] further contested that CCI could cater to customers with rich experience of showrooming. Because it is apparent from the abovementioned studies that showrooming mirrors customer experience in cross-channel environments, it should govern customers' reaction to CCI strategy in omnichannel retailing.

## 2.2. Customer retention and interests in alternatives

There is consensus within extant literature that customer retention constitutes a key barometer of information systems success in general [25,40], and business success of CCI in particular [2,41–43]. Customer retention refers to the degree to which a customer exhibits commitment and loyalty in selecting a retailer through repurchase intention. It reflects customers' perception of ‘wanting to’ continue a pre-existing relationship with a retailer [44]. Nevertheless, in reality, even a loyal customer may alter their future repurchase because of exposure to alternatives [23,45]. Therefore, interest in alternatives has been construed as another crucial customer reaction in marketing research [24,44,46] by capturing the appeal of viable alternatives that may compel customers to source for additional information [44,47]. Naturally, a lack of interest in alternatives reinforces customers' perception of ‘having to’ maintain their pre-existing relationships with a retailer [25,46]. We therefore contend that CCI would be pivotal in improving customer retention and eroding customers' interest in alternatives because it can augment uncertainty reduction, identity attractiveness, and lock-in efforts of an omnichannel retailer relative to its rivals.

## 2.3. The Push-Pull-Mooring (PPM) framework

The PPM framework [31l is a predominant theoretical lens for investigating customer reaction in the post-adoption of information systems [48] and multichannel shopping [20,49]. Particularly, customers' switching behavior could be swayed by push factors. Extant literature has equated retailer uncertainty with being a push factor [49]. From a risk perspective, consumers' first reaction to retailers' actions is often to minimize their anticipated risk rather than to maximize their expected benefits. In omnichannel retailing, retailer uncertainty has been treated as a significant risk that may push customers to discontinue pre-existing relationships [18,31]. Customers are often wary of exploitation by omnichannel retailers [44] due to difficulty in assessing retailer uncertainty across multiple channels [50]. Conceivably, customers' switching intention will be affected by their uncertainty on the likelihood of retailers to participate in misrepresentation and opportunism [50,51].

Pull effects reflect the degree of customers' willingness to maintain the relationship due to genuine appreciation of the quality attributes of service providers [31]. Bhattacharya and Sen [38] remarked that such willingness stems from customers' perception of retailers' identity attractiveness, which in turn originates from the fulfillment of customers' self-definitional needs. In omnichannel retailing, identity attractiveness can be depicted as a retailer's enduring attributes (e.g., brand image, competencies, product offerings, reputation, and values) that are appealing and can satisfy the diverse needs of customers [9, 38,42]. Identity attractiveness may assume the form of favorable conditions that facilitate customers' desired activities or support them in the attainment of their immediate goals [52]. Past studies have revealed that identity attractiveness reinforces customers' identification with and loyalty to retailers [38,53].

Mooring effects denote a customer's cost-benefit analysis of a retailer relative to viable alternatives and may oblige them to continue a preexisting relationship with the retailer due to economic, social, or psychological costs [44,46,54]. Switching costs refer to costs that are incurred in the process of switching from one retailer to another [55] and may include costs associated with lost performance, psychological risk, as well as search and evaluation [56]. For this reason, switching costs play a mooring role in inhibiting customers from switching to alternatives [31]. Prior research has uncovered that switching costs, acting as behavioral constraints, may discourage customers from exiting pre-existing relationships with retailers [44].

## 3. Hypotheses formulation

We draw on the PPM framework to advance a research model that addresses how customers react to retailers' CCI. Specifically, we conceive CCI as a form of service investment in purporting that customers' reaction to CCI, as captured through customer retention and interest in alternatives, would be determined by push, pull, and mooring factors such as retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness and switching costs. Fig. 1. depicts our proposed research model.

## 3.1. Customer retention

According to PPM [20,49], retailer uncertainty may play a negative pushing role in customer retention. Past studies have reported that retailer uncertainty reduces customer satisfaction [57] and retention [58]. By inducing low trust and high risk among customers [59], retailer uncertainty could push them to switch from existing retailers [31]. Because omnichannel retailing involves substantive risk and uncertainty [18], retailer uncertainty could negatively affect customer retention due to their risk reduction tendencies.

Hypothesis 1a. Retailer uncertainty is negatively related to customer retention.

In the PPM framework, positive attributes of retailers are considered as pull factors to customers [31]. For omnichannel retailers, identity attractiveness reflects the degree to which customers are enticed by a retailer's enduring attributes (e.g., brand image, competencies, product offerings, reputation, and values). Such identity attractiveness could pull customers to maintain their relationship with retailers that are competent in offering goods and services, which fulfill the former's shopping needs [38,41,60]. In turn, these pull effects in omnichannel retailing are likely to manifest as customers' inclinations toward favorable retail experiences. In other words, since omnichannel retailing is meant to cater to customer preferences in cross-channel movements [42], retailers' identity attractiveness could serve as a bridge across multiple channels to sustain customers' engagement irrespective of the channel with which they transact.

Hypothesis 1b. Retailer identity attractiveness is positively related to customer retention.

Prior research further suggests that switching costs could play a mooring role in customer retention [61]. Indeed, scholars have documented that customers could be retained by a retailer simply because of high switching barriers [62–64]. Conceivably, switching costs, which signify customers' relational investments in a retailer as well as their familiarity with the retailer's transactional routines and procedures, would deter considerations of alternatives [65]. Likewise, we postulate that such mooring effects would also hold in the context of omnichannel retailers.

Hypothesis 1c. Switching costs are positively related to customer retention.

## 3.2. Interest in alternative

Consistent with past studies on perceived risk [21], we argue that retailer uncertainty would positively influence customer interest in alternatives. This is because the push mechanism of retailer uncertainty introduces doubts and perceptions of risk in a retailer [31]. In omnichannel retailing, Chou, Shen, Chiu and Chou [21] have stated that customers' perceived risk of a retailer would increase their likelihood of switching retailers.

Hypothesis 2a. Retailer uncertainty is positively related to customer interest in alternatives.

Scholars have alleged that the pull effects, which stem from positive service evaluation, would dampen customers' motivation to switch [31]. Specifically, we submit that retailer's identity attractiveness could be negatively related to customers' interest in alternatives. Retailers' identity attractiveness reflects customers' preference for enduring attributes of a retailer that are distinguishable from its rivals [38]. When consumers perceive a retailer's identity attractiveness to be high, they would be less inclined to switch. This effect also conforms to cognitive dissonance in customers' evaluation of alternatives during decision making [66]. Perceptions of retailer identity attractiveness implies congruence between customers' self-concept and retailer's brand identity [38]. Under such circumstances, “multiple relationships may cause cognitive dissonance, i.e., feeling a sense of belonging to a specific provider while doing business with others” [22 p.308]. To maintain consonance with existing retailers, customers are inclined to resist the offers of others and feel demotivated to search for alternatives, which in turn grants immunity to the marketing efforts of competitors.

Hypothesis 2b. Retailer identity attractiveness is negatively related to customer interest in alternatives.

Switching costs would also reduce customers' interest in alternatives because of mooring effects. Customers, who are confronted with switching costs, would persist with an existing retailer because they would find the expenditure of additional costs to establish relationship with a new retailer to be unacceptable [25,44]. Indeed, studies have shown that customers, when faced with high switching costs, will deem defecting to other service providers as less appealing [20,25] and regard any extra time and effort spent in seeking alternatives to be unwise.

Hypothesis 2c. Switching costs are negatively related to customer interest in alternatives.

3.3. Relationships among CCI and retailer uncertainty, retailer identity attractiveness, and switching costs

Extant literature on relationship marketing holds that providers' service investment would bring about relational maintenance [22]. Retailers' service investment is helpful in reassuring customers who are wary of retailers' risk [44]. Past studies have found that customers' perceived uncertainty of utilizing a service could be reduced by a retailer's reputation, trust, and previous transactions [50,67]. These studies argue that a retailer's CCI could help reduce its uncertainty because such integration will enable the retailer to institute more touchpoints to formulate a positive brand reputation [10,37]. By integrating information across multiple channels, CCI can enhance customers' trust in a retailer and prevent misunderstandings [42]. Through blended cross-channel access, CCI could reduce retailer uncertainty by increasing customers' confidence and decreasing their risk perceptions during transactions [13,68]. Integrated promotion and servicing across channels thus reduce customers' concern for uncertainty by reinforcing a retailer's reputation as being reliable, prestigious, and high-quality [24].

## Hypothesis 3a. CCI is negatively related to retailer uncertainty.

Scholars indicate that retailer identity attractiveness reflects the degree to which customers are satisfied with a retailer's product and service offerings [69] as well as its communicators and channels [38]. In the same vein, we propose that CCI, as a service investment, could augment retailers' identity attractiveness. Specifically, CCI represents a retailer's efforts in accommodating customers' special shopping needs [70] through integrating information, price, quality, and knowledge across multiple channels and touchpoints [3,8]. Such service investments draw customers' attention to the retailer's offerings that are pertinent to the former's specific needs [71]. Additionally, CCI offers a means for customers to interact with touchpoints provided by the retailer and experience the branding that permeates different channels [72]. Increased touchpoint and awareness of the separate channels familiarize customers with a retailer's product, service, and branding, thereby increasing the overall attractiveness of the retailer's identity [73].

Hypothesis 3b. CCI is positively related to retailer identity attractiveness.

Customers are locked in by retailers' investments in service provision that can generate switching costs [44]. CCI provides customers with integrated channels to access all available information and services, a convenience that is inaccessible from transacting with multichannel retailers given their silo-operated service procedures. In this scenario, CCI serves to increase switching costs for customers. Specifically, CCI could provide customers with an integrated service package [9] that increases service quality and convenience [13,34]. By constructing customer profiles and consumption records across channels, retailers could develop intricate understanding of customers' preferences, which in turn may heighten the latter's switching costs through highly personalized services [56]. CCI could also bolster customers' expectation and evaluation criteria of alternatives [56]. With raised expectations, customers may face a challenge in seeking an alternative with a comparable level of service.

Hypothesis 3c. CCI is positively related to switching costs.

## 3.4. Moderating effects of customer showrooming

The PPM framework holds that customers' variety-seeking inclinations and previous transactional experience would moderate the impact of retailers' initiatives on customers' reactions [31]. Particularly, showrooming, which captures customers' level of experience with cross-channel usage, reflects their emphasis on reducing uncertainty, searching for ideal self-definition need, and shopping in a cost-efficient manner [30]. We propose that customer showrooming may strengthen the negative relationship between CCI and retailer uncertainty. By empowering customers to acquire integrated information about a retailer, CCI enhances the former's understanding of the retailer. By extrapolation, showrooming thus heightens customers' capability to evaluate information from multiple sources and contrast their assessment against their own psychological need. Such experience also increases customers' confidence and expertise in cross-channel usage as well as their familiarity with the retailer. Consequently, we believe that showrooming, which attests to customers' inner desire for CCI, would accentuate the relative importance of CCI on uncertainty reduction.

Hypothesis 4a. Customer showrooming experience could strengthen the negative relationship between CCI and retailer uncertainty.

Customer showrooming may also strengthen the positive relationship between CCI and retailer identity attractiveness. Specifically, CCI permits the retailer to promote its enduring attributes across multiple channels, thereby rendering the entire transaction process to be transparent to avoid confusion. Customers with showrooming experience would derive greater value from such integration by being able to comprehend and evaluate the retailer better, thereby magnifying the attractiveness of retailer identity.

Hypothesis 4b. Customer showrooming experience could strengthen the positive relationship between CCI and retailer identity attractiveness.

Finally, we advocate that customer showrooming could weaken the positive relationship between CCI and switching costs. The objective of CCI is to lock in customers and prevent them from switching to alternative retailers. However, for customers with rich experience in showrooming, switching might not be difficult if they can access information in evaluating different retailers and channels with minimal cognitive effort [74]. Conversely, customers with low experience in showrooming would face a steep learning curve if they intend to switch from a CCI retailer. They are also more likely to appreciate the existing retailer, opting to rely on CCI during evaluations of product and service quality.

Hypothesis 4c. Customer showrooming experience could weaken the positive relationship between CCI and switching costs.

## 4. Methodology

We collected data from an online survey administered on respondents recruited through Sojump [http://www.sojump.com], a popular online survey platform in China. After presenting potential respondents with a definition of omnichannel retailers in the survey questionnaire, we inserted a filtering question inquiring if respondents have “ever made a purchase from an omnichannel retailer”. Only respondents who have prior experience with an omnichannel retailer were permitted to continue with the survey after entering the retailer's name. The process yielded a total of 320 responses. After deleting 61 responses for which respondents had failed to either meet the minimum baseline for time (i.e., questionnaires were completed in b5 min) or answer key questions, we arrived at an eventual sample of 259 data points for analysis.

To test for non-response bias, we abided by Armstrong and Overton [75] advocated procedure and conducted a t-test to compare the responses for focal constructs between the lower (i.e., first 25%) and upper (i.e., final 25%) quartiles of the sample. From the comparison, non-response bias does not appear to be a threat in this study. Table 1 depicts the demographic distribution of the sample.

## 4.1. Measurement development

We developed the survey questionnaire through a series of incremental steps. We began by reviewing extant literature to identify previously validated measures, some of which were revised and adapted to the context of omnichannel retailing. Thereafter, we invited five academic researchers with expertise in e-business and marketing to review the measures. Next, forward (i.e., English to Chinese) and backward (i.e., Chinese to English) translations were performed on draft versions of the questionnaires to guarantee content and face validity. Finally, an initial draft of the survey questionnaire was subjected to pilot testing and refined accordingly.

Nine items for retailer uncertainty were adapted from Dimoka, Hong and Pavlou [50] to measure customers' difficulty in assessing opportunistic behavior on the part of retailers (i.e., adverse selection and moral hazard). ## 4.5. Test of mediating effects

To test the mediating effects of retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs, we adhered to the steps advocated by Baron and Kenny [79]. As depicted in Table 4, the direct relationships between CCI and customer retention (β = 0.486) and that between CCI and interest in alternatives (β = −0.158) are significant, thereby satisfying the first condition for mediating effects. The relationships between CCI and retailer uncertainty (β = −0.271), between CCI and identity attractiveness (β = 0.351), as well as between CCI and switching costs $( \beta =$ 0.318) are also significant. This finding satisfies the second condition for the existence of mediating effects. Lastly, the direct relationship between CCI and customer retention $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 4 )$ is significant when we added retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs as mediators. However, the direct relationship between CCI and interest in alternatives $( \beta = - 0 . 0 2 0 )$ became insignificant when we introduced retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs as mediators. From the preceding results, we can deduce that retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs (1) partially mediate the relationship between CCI and customer retention and (2) fully mediate the relationship between CCI and interest in alternatives.

We employed bootstrapping procedures to further examine the mediating effects of retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs. According to Preacher and Hayes [80], indirect effects would be significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) does not include zero at the 0.05 level. Bootstrapping results revealed that retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs mediated the effects of CCI on customer retention with observed indirect effects of 0.124 (95% CI [0.082, 0.17]), 0.071 (95% CI [0.041, 0.106]), and 0.037 (95% CI [0.015, 0.064]), respectively. Similarly, analytical results show that retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs mediated the effects of CCI on interest in alternatives with observed indirect effects of 0.071 (95% CI [0.025, 0.123]), −0.093 (95% CI [−0.142, −0.050]), and −0.116 (95% CI [−0.174, −0.064]), respectively.

## 5. Discussion

The purpose of this study is to shed light on customers' reaction to CCI in omnichannel retailing. We build on the PPM framework to advance a research model in which we hypothesize that CCI, as a form of service investment, would affect customers' retention and interest in alternatives by causing variations in their perceptions of uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs of omnichannel retailers. Interesting insights could be gleaned from our empirical findings with respect to the effects of CCI on customers in omnichannel retailing.

## 5.1. Implications for theory

Aligned with the PPM framework, this study demonstrates that mechanisms in the form of retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs play instrumental roles in shaping customer reactions' to CCI. Deviating from past studies on multichannel retailing, we pinpointed the key transmission processes or mediating factors of retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs that are exclusive to the context of omnichannel retailing due to the latter's emphasis on integration and channel synergy. Our findings resonate with prior research on relationship marketing that position CCI as service investments that can aid retailers in retaining customers by generating trust as well as cultivating the latter's dedication- and constraintbased motivations [44]. This study also responds to the call of scholars for empirical research to unravel the relationship between service investments and competitive advantage [13]. We demonstrate that the push, pull, and mooring mechanisms can be activated through service investments such as CCI [44].

In line with the push-mooring effect on switching reaction, we illustrate that retailer uncertainty and switching costs exert positive and negative impact on customers' interest in alternatives respectively. These findings correspond to past studies that allude to the driving forces of risk perceptions [31] and switching costs [25,81] in steering customers to seek for alternatives. We also uncovered a paradoxical finding in that the pull effect of identity attractiveness, despite being the strongest driver of customer retention, is the weakest predictor of customer switching behavior. Contrary to our hypothesis, identity attractiveness positively influences customers' interest in alternatives. One plausible explanation is that customers' attraction to an omnichannel retailer's identity could symbolize their knowledge of the retailer's performance and might stimulate the former's interest to search for competitors as a point of reference [47]. Still, the aforementioned findings seem to illuminate the ineffectiveness of the pull mechanism in buffering against consumer switching. These findings further supplement our knowledge of the push, pull, and mooring effects in customers' switching tendency respectively [31,44].

Our findings further elucidate that retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs partially mediate the relationship between CCI and customer retention while fully mediating the relationship between CCI and interest in alternatives. Specifically, we discovered that identity attractiveness (pull) is the foremost factor that positively retains customers, thereby lending credibility to the value of brand identity in omnichannel retailing. Retailer uncertainty (push) and switching costs (mooring), in contrast, account more for customers' switching tendency, reflecting the latter's cost consideration when confronted with alternatives. These findings reinforce the viewpoint that the attractiveness and lock-in measures of omnichannel retailers could mediate the relationship between service investments and customer reactions [13]. In this sense, push, pull, and mooring mechanisms can be deemed as core determinants of customers' reac tions to CCI in omnichannel retailing [31].

Finally, our findings reveal that customer showrooming could strengthen the effect of CCI in reducing retailer uncertainty. This observation proffers novel insights into the effectiveness of CCI under distinct contingency conditions: it suggests that the principal concern of ‘showrooming’ customers may be about the uncertainty of omnichannel retailers [7]. Nevertheless, we also found that customer showrooming does not affect the relationships among CCI, identity attractiveness, and switching costs. A possible explanation could be that customer showrooming is motivated primarily by self-efficacy, convenience orientation, price comparison, or risk reduction instead of an innate attraction to retailer identity [20,21,70]. Conversely, switching costs are normally computed based on the net benefits of omnichannel retailers relative to alternatives so much so that customer showrooming may be limited in depressing switching costs.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our findings yield insights for omnichannel retailers when implementing CCI strategy. First, omnichannel retailers have invested massively in CCI to retain customers. Nonetheless, such investments may not translate into customer retention if retailers are unable to convince customers of the value of CCI. Consequently, omnichannel retailers should channel their CCI investments to ensure that appropriate push (i.e., retailer uncertainty), pull (i.e., identity attractiveness), and mooring (i.e., switching costs) mechanisms have been triggered.

Second, omnichannel retailers must realize that CCI investments cannot be justified based solely on the direct relationship between CCI and customer reaction. Instead, they should devote greater attention to the complex interdependencies among CCI, retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, switching costs, customer retention, and interest in alternatives. Our findings indicate that the direct relationship between CCI and interest in alternatives is fully mediated by retailer uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs. Omnichannel retailers should also be mindful of the partial mediating influence of the preceding factors on the relationship between CCI and customer retention. In other words, CCI investments, when undertaken in a purposeful manner by omnichannel retailers, could induce customer retention either directly or indirectly by affecting the latter's uncertainty, identity attractiveness, and switching costs.

Omnichannel retailers should also pay heed to the positive role of customer showrooming in the enactment of CCI strategy. As reported in our study, such experience of showrooming could strengthen the effects of CCI in decreasing retailer uncertainty. Retailers should thus encourage and promote customer showrooming as part of their CCI strategy. This promotion will inspire customers to obtain additional information from integrated channels, which in turn will reduce their corresponding perceptions of uncertainty [18].

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study comes with certain caveats. First, all constructs were measured perceptually. Although our analytical results dismiss the existence of potential biases, future research could replicate our study by leveraging on objective data. Second, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes any inferences on causal relationships from being drawn. Employing a longitudinal design may assist in deciphering the causality between CCI and customer reaction in omnichannel retailing. Third, self-selection bias may be another limitation because our respondents were recruited from a population of active online consumers. Even though customers in omnichannel retailing are online consumers, recruiting respondents in physical stores would enrich the sample. Fourth, while we have incorporated the main channels (e.g., online, offline and mobile devices) fundamental to CCI, we cannot claim to have captured the panorama of omnichannel retailing. Future studies could extend the channel scope by adding customer touchpoint (e.g., brand advertisement, in-store communication and peer to peer communication) that can enrich consumers' shopping experience and overall satisfaction. Finally, we cannot deny the existence of other factors that may potentially shape customers' reaction to CCI. Future investigations could hence expand the scope of our study by exploring the moderating influence of customers' personality, cognition, and other pertinent factors.
