# UNLOCKING THE POWER OF VOICE FOR FINANCIAL RISK PREDICTION: A THEORY-DRIVEN DEEP LEARNING DESIGN APPROACH<sup>1</sup>

Yi Yang Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Sai Kung, HONG KONG {imyiyang@ust.hk}

Yu Qin Department of Operations and Information Systems, David Eccles School of Business, University of Utah, Salt Lake City, UT, U.S.A. {yu.qin@eccles.utah.edu}

Yangyang Fan School of Accounting and Finance, Faculty of Business Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Kowloon, HONG KONG {yangyang.fan@polyu.edu.hk}

Zhongju Zhang W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {Zhongju.Zhang@asu.edu}

Unstructured multimedia data (text and audio) provides unprecedented opportunities to derive actionable decision-making in the financial industry, in areas such as portfolio and risk management. However, due to formidable methodological challenges, the promise of business value from unstructured multimedia data has not materialized. In this study, we use a design science approach to develop DeepVoice, a novel nonverbal predictive analysis system for financial risk prediction, in the setting of quarterly earnings conference calls. DeepVoice forecasts financial risk by leveraging not only what managers say (verbal linguistic cues) but also how managers say it (vocal cues) during the earnings conference calls. The design of DeepVoice addresses several challenges associated with the analysis of nonverbal communication. We also propose a two-stage deep learning model to effectively integrate managers’ sequential vocal and verbal cues. Using a unique dataset of 6,047 earnings call samples (audio recordings and textual transcripts) of S&P 500 firms across four years, we show that DeepVoice yields remarkably lower risk forecast errors than that achieved by previous efforts. The improvement can also translate into nontrivial economic gains in options trading. The theoretical and practical implications of analyzing vocal cues are discussed.

Keywords: Financial risk, voice, vocal cues, verbal cues, vocal-verbal integrations, deep learning, design science

## Introduction

Financial technology is transforming the world of financial services (Gomber et al., 2018). In this domain, using big data analytics to derive actionable decision-making, such as portfolio and risk management, has become a top priority for many financial institutions. In the big data era, investors are researching public companies using data from various sources beyond financial statements and accounting numbers. Managers are communicating with outside investors both verbally and nonverbally (Mayew & Venkatachalam, 2013). Massive amounts of unstructured multimedia data recording the verbal and vocal signals of managers, such as firm annual reports and the audio recordings of earnings conferences, are publicly available and can be accessed by individual and institutional investors. Anecdotal evidence has suggested that hedge fund companies are hiring ex-CIA agents trained in reading vocal cues to assess managers’ public statements at the earnings conference calls.<sup>2</sup> When managers speak, market participants listen and look for any competitive advantage they can find in the unstructured multimedia data.

In this study, we propose a design framework that utilizes managers’ vocal cues in earnings conference calls to forecast a firm’s financial risk. We empirically measure the financial risk of public firms, also known as stock volatility, as the standard deviation of the firm’s stock returns over a given period. In contrast directly forecasting stock returns, it is not controversial in the field of economics to forecast the volatility of a stock using publicly available information (Dumas et al., 2009). Financial risk plays a key role in many downstream tasks related to financial markets, such as investment portfolio management and risk management (Brooks & Persand, 2003; Brownlees & Gallo, 2010). It is also considered a key component of price evaluation in many well-established optionpricing models (Black & Scholes, 1973), and its forecasts are critical to derivatives trading. The derivatives markets are substantial components of global capital markets, and trading in equity options alone accounted for 7.5 trillion U.S. dollars in the first half of 2021.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, the ability to forecast the financial risk of publicly listed firms should be of great interest to practitioners, academics and regulators.

While the finance and accounting literatures have demonstrated that executives’ verbal linguistic words used in earnings conference calls are associated with firm performance, market reactions, and financial misreporting (Larcker & Zakolyukina, 2012; Li, 2010; Loughran & Mcdonald, 2016; Yan & Zheng, 2017), there is limited work examining the role of nonverbal vocal cues in earnings conference calls. Vocal cues, i.e., the sound of the voice used to send a verbal message, plays a significant role in nonverbal communication (Mehrabian, 1972). The human voice and associated speech patterns can be characterized by a number of vocal cues, such as pitch, volume, and so on. Using vocal emotion analysis software, Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012) found a correlation between managers’ vocal emotions and firms’ future performance. Recently, Mayew et al. (2020) identified a correlation between managers’ voice pitch and stock prices. However, those studies mostly focused on evaluating the explanatory power of vocal cues insample, whether nonverbal vocal cues provide significant predictive power out-of-sample is still largely unknown.

Moreover, existing vocal cue analysis methods and machine learning methods have been unable to provide adequate capability in audio data learning, due to three design challenges: time variation, vocal-verbal integration, and vocal complexity

Challenge 1: Time variation. Managers’ vocal features can vary substantially during conference calls. For example, during the presentation section, when the manager is reading a forward-looking statement, the voice pitch may be quite stable. However, individuals’ vocal cues may change as a result of being nervous, uncertain, or excited (Scherer et al., 1991). As such, in the question-answer section, when the manager is being scrutinized by analysts, the voice pitch may change. Mayew et al. (2020) found that the voice pitch of managers during dialogues with analysts is lower by about 4 Hz, on average, compared to their voice pitch during presentation sessions. Therefore, the time-varying patterns of managers’ vocal cues may contain useful information. However, prior studies have ignored the time-varying pattern of managers’ vocal cues in earnings conference calls (Mayew et al., 2020; Mayew & Venkatachalam, 2012), and it is unclear how a predictive model could effectively summarize time-varying vocal sequences.

Challenge 2: Vocal-verbal integration. According to nonverbal communication theory (Mehrabian, 1972), vocal cues play a key role in nonverbal communication, as they can either affirm or discredit a message. For example, if a manager claims the company has strong future growth but uses a pessimistic tone of voice, market participants listening to the conference call may spot the irregularity and interpret the verbal message differently. This implies that to accurately gauge the information contained in managers’ communication, one should integrate verbal language with the accompanying vocal cues. However, existing computational models (Poria et al., 2017) are limited in terms of integrating these two different communication channels (i.e., vocal and verbal).

Challenge 3: Vocal complexity. Identifying the specific acoustic constructs from voice is a complex process, and the literature is still evolving without any consensus on an appropriate model for measuring affective states, emotions, and trust (Mayew & Venkatachalam, 2013). There is also little agreement about how a speaker’s vocal cues should be combined to identify emotional states (Schuller, 2010)—e.g., which vocal cues to investigate and measure and how much weight to place on each vocal cue. As a result, attempts to develop constructs from vocal cues may suffer from high measurement errors.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop an effective design artifact for predicting firm financial risk using managers’ vocal cues that has better performance than that achieved by previous efforts. To achieve this objective, we adopt the design science paradigm (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Hevner et al., 2004) to guide the development of the IT artifact. In doing so, we select Mehrabian’s communication model (MCM) (Mehrabian, 1972) as the kernel theory (Walls et al., 1992). Guided by the MCM theory, we identify metarequirements for the design and propose metadesigns to address above-mentioned challenges. We also instantiate a system, which we call DeepVoice, that integrates metadesigns for financial risk prediction.

We tested the validity of DeepVoice design using a unique dataset of 6,047 conference calls (text and audio) from S&P 500 firms between 2015 and 2018. We rigorously evaluated the overall effectiveness of DeepVoice and each metadesign in a series of experiments. The experimental results show that the DeepVoice framework is remarkably effective and can significantly improve out-of-sample forecast accuracy. We further demonstrate that the improvement in out-ofsample prediction performance can translate into nontrivial economic gains in options trading.

This study contributes to the literature both methodologically and empirically. First, we develop the DeepVoice framework, which integrates deep learning techniques and nonverbal communication theory via a design science artifact to predict firm financial risk. This new method achieves substantially better performance than that obtained by previous research. Moreover, the design principles summarized from our design can generalize to domains with similar data types or characteristics. Practically, in the era of big data and fintech, financial institutions and investors are investing a lot of resources in financial information systems, hoping that big multimedia data (such as text and audio archives) will generate new actionable insights. However, the promise of business value from unstructured audio data has not materialized. This study responds to the call to study unstructured multimedia data to derive rich insights and tackles the formidable methodological challenges associated with the analysis of unstructured big data (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014).

Second, this study contributes to the empirical literature on financial risk prediction. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to study the prediction of market reactions using nonverbal information. Using a large-scale dataset containing over 6,000 audio recordings from earnings conference calls, we empirically document a significantly higher accuracy of financial risk forecast by integrating nonverbal vocal cues with traditional attributes such as financial fundamentals, historical risks, and verbal information. We also demonstrate that the improvement in risk forecast translates into nontrivial economic gains in options trading. The empirical findings of this study strengthen the understanding of the value of nonverbal communication in financial markets. Our results also have implications for researchers, investors, and regulators.

## Literature Review

We organize prior related literature into three main categories. While reviewing these studies, we also highlight the need to use a design science approach to develop a novel method for analyzing vocal cues and examine its potential value for financial markets.

The role of vocal information in financial markets: While the management literature has empirically demonstrated that managers’ verbal linguistic words are associated with firm performance and future market reactions (Li, 2010; Loughran & Mcdonald, 2016), the role of nonverbal vocal cues in financial markets, especially its predictive value is still underexplored. In a lab study, Elliott et al. (2012) showed that nonverbal communication via online videos influences both investors’ investment decisions and their perceptions of trust in management. This empirical study suggested that nonverbal communication may influence investor perceptions. In a series of related research, Hobson et al. (2012) analyzed the first five minutes of CEOs’ speech during conference calls using LVA, a vocal emotion analysis software, and found that the vocal dissonance identified by the software was positively associated with the likelihood of financial misreporting. Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012) used the same LVA software to analyze the first five minutes of CEOs’ speech during conference calls to derive managers’ affective states during these calls and found that managers’ affect is informative regarding their firms financial future. Similarly, Price et al. (2012) used LVA software to measure managers’ emotions in conference calls and found that managers’ emotion is positively related to investors’ reactions. However, all three studies relied on the black box third-party voice analysis software LVA to derive managers’ affective states and emotions. As Mayew & Venkatachalam (2013) admit, “a significant disadvantage of using LVA, as with any other commercial software, is that the construct validity of the emotion measures produced by the software has been subject to limited scrutiny.” This is consistent with the nonverbal communication literature, which has noted that there is little agreement about how speakers’ vocal cues should be combined to identify emotional states (Schuller, 2010; Schuller et al., 2011).

Since the literature on voice analysis in corporate contexts is still in its infancy, there are tremendous opportunities to advance this literature. While prior studies have used voice software to obtain vocal constructs, which may be far from accurate, and evaluated the explanatory power of vocal cues using in-sample data, this study examines the predictive power of vocal cues in out-of-sample data using a novel nonverbal communication design artifact.

Financial risk forecasting with public information: financial economics theory shows that it is possible to predict a firm’s risk using public data (Dumas et al., 2009). Researchers and practitioners go to great lengths to forecast financial risk using various types of data. For example, Poon and Granger (2005) comprehensively surveyed various timeseries models incorporating historical volatility data and found that ARCH-type models are the most commonly used stochastic volatility models. Sridharan (2015) showed that a firm’s financial fundamentals can predict future volatility incrementally in time-series-based volatility forecasts. Carr et al. (2020) studied the application of machine learning to forecast the 30-day realized variance of the S&P index, and Li and Tang (2021) proposed a machine learning system to forecast volatility for S&P 500 stock using ensemble learning algorithms and time-series features.

Thanks to the availability of text data related to firms and the financial market, various studies have analyzed the problem of predicting financial risk using unstructured text data. Kogan et al. (2009) and Yang et al. (2021) showed that textual features in firms’ annual reports (Form 10-Ks) can be used to predict their annual volatility. Other studies (Rekabsaz et al., 2017; Theil et al., 2019; Tsai & Wang, 2017; Wang & Hua, 2014) also suggested using corporate earnings call transcripts or firm reports to predict stock price volatility.

Even though nonverbal audio recordings from conference calls are available, the literature on financial task prediction has mostly used text data and, indeed, barely goes beyond text data. Recently, Qin and Yang (2019) proposed using vocal cues to predict firm risk. However, their sample only contained about 500 earnings conference calls and the vocal cues used in the study were unclear, limiting the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the void of theoretical direction has limited the effectiveness and rigor of such financial risk prediction systems, thus punctuating the need for the development of a theory-driven design artifact. We believe the design science paradigm in IS research (Gregor & Hevner, 2013; Hevner et al., 2004) can help guide a better design for utilizing nonverbal vocal cues because this paradigm favors kernel theories to advise problem solving with a rigorous grounding and formulates a process to find the optimal solution design. In this paper, we follow design science guidelines to develop and instantiate a vocal analytics framework for financial risk forecasting. Therefore, both the conceptual and computational approaches of vocal cues are bridged together in our framework design.

IS design science for financial tasks: The IS discipline has a rich history of using the design science paradigm to design and implement artifacts for important financial applications. Based on the kernel theory of metalearning, Abbasi et al. (2012) proposed using a set of measures derived from publicly available financial statements, coupled with machine learning algorithms, to enhance financial fraud detection. Similarly, guided by the kernel theory of systemic functional linguistics theory, Dong et al. (2018) advocated the use of social media text data for corporate fraud detection. In the context of peer-to-peer lending, Wang et al. (2021) designed a text analytics system to extract soft information from descriptive loan text and to identify credit risks. Li et al. (2015) developed market surveillance systems using common-sense knowledge based on text understanding theory. Chen et al. (2021) proposed a text mining approach that uses news to predict firm stock returns. Li et al. (2016) designed a text mining system to identify and profile key sellers in underground credit/debit card fraud. In short, IS researchers have contributed to financial information systems by adopting a design science paradigm to develop and instantiate effective artifacts. Our work is along this line of research. However, existing IS design science work has mostly focused on textual data. To the best of our knowledge, our work introduces the first design artifact that leverages both voice and text data, which we believe could provide important implications for broader business applications.

## Kernel Theory-Based Design

Design science research advocates for the application of rigorous methods to solve relevant problems (Hevner et al., 2004). According to information system design theory (Walls et al., 1992), the information system design process includes four components: kernel theories, metarequirements, metadesigns, and testable hypotheses. Kernel theories are derived from the natural and social sciences and are used to govern meta-requirements. The metadesign constructs a class of IT artifacts that are anticipated to meet the meta-requirements rigorously. Testable hypotheses are used to evaluate whether the proposed metadesign satisfies the meta-requirement. We present our design framework in Table 1 and discuss the details in the following sections.

## Kernel Theory: Mehrabian’s Communication Model

One of the most important theories in nonverbal communication is Mehrabian’s communication model (MCM) (Mehrabian, 1972), which explains the importance of vocal signals for effective oral communication. MCM theory has three important aspects, and we posit that MCM theory is especially appropriate for guiding the design of a financial risk prediction framework that can effectively incorporate managers’ nonverbal communication information.

First, the theory states that a significant portion of information in communication is conveyed through nonverbal channels—e.g., the vocal channel. During earnings conference calls, the spontaneous and interactive communications between firm managers and analysts provide market participants with a rich information set that includes both verbal and nonverbal vocal cues. Therefore, we conjecture that a manager’s tone of voice in conference calls can provide incremental information that may complement the manager’s words.

Second, the theory states that the tone of voice includes different dimensions, such as the intensity and speed of the voice, the frequency of pauses, and the stability of the voice. Although MCM theory does not explicitly examine the relationship between each dimension and the information conveyed, subsequent psychology communication studies have extensively examined the correlation between different vocal cues and effective communication. The literature has shown that in the context of crisis communication, spokespeople with high-pitched voices are perceived as less competent than spokespeople with lower-pitched voices (Claeys & Cauberghe, 2014; De Waele et al., 2018). Similarly, a meta-analysis (DePaulo et al., 2003) found that low-pitched voices are perceived as more credible and high-pitched voices are perceived as deceptive. Similarly, one study found that lower voice pitch appears to be associated with decreases in anxiety (Laukka et al., 2008). Also, experimental studies have found that human voice pitch decreases (increases) when speakers engage in conversations with competitors who the speaker perceives to be as less (more) dominant (Cheng et al., 2016; Puts et al., 2006). In addition to voice pitch, prior literature has also found that other vocal cues, such as speech fluency (Apple et al., 1979; De Waele et al., 2019), voice intensity (Jiang &

Pell, 2017), and timing and voice quality (Cahn, 1990) have a strong correlation with perceived confidence, trustworthiness, emotions, and other feelings. Moreover, individuals’ vocal cues may change as a result of being nervous, uncertain, or excited (Scherer et al., 1991). In the earnings conference call setting, where managers are under scrutiny by analysts, vocal cue changes may be indicative of managers’ cognitive status, and analysts may obtain incremental information through the change of vocal cues.

Third, MCM theory suggests that it is necessary for vocal and verbal cues to support each other, i.e., to be congruent, for effective communication. When verbal and vocal cues are consistent, the receiver of the message can better understand the information conveyed. If there is any incongruity between the vocal and verbal cues, the receiver of the message may discredit the verbal message. For example, a prior study found that incongruity between verbal and nonverbal information has an impact on perceived trustworthiness (Morioka et al., 2016). Moreover, the discrepancy between one’s vocal cues and verbal cues often reflects cognitive dissonance, which is a state of psychological arousal and discomfort occurring when an individual’s actions are inconsistent with their beliefs (Mazar et al., 2008; Zahn, 1973). Therefore, if a manager says “we are confident about future growth” in a voice tone that is significantly different from their vocal cues in neighboring sentences, such as higher pitch or more pauses, and if it turns out that the firm’s financial risk increases after the earnings conference call, the predictive machine learning model can be trained to associate the vocal-verbal inconsistency with high risk. If such a pattern persists frequently, exceeding the probabilities of chance, the model will be sufficiently trained and can generalize to out-of-sample prediction. Capturing the information behind managers’ messages is therefore expected to contribute to more accurate firm risk predictions.

## Meta-Requirements

Guided by kernel theory, we now discuss the metarequirements for the design artifact. First, in light of MCM theory, both verbal cues and vocal cues should be considered when extracting useful information from a manager’s communication. Such information may provide incremental value to the quantitative information contained in financial statements. As such, the first meta-requirement is that the model should integrate verbal and vocal cues in the financial risk prediction system, in addition to traditional attributes such as firm fundamental variables and historical risks. Because the fundamental variables and historical risks are numerical data (structured) which are different from vocal and verbal data (unstructured), the system should design a layer of the artifact to process data heterogeneity.

Second, the vocal-verbal predictive model’s design should address the challenges of time variation and vocal-verbal integration. For the time-variation challenge, MCM theory suggests that the change in a speaker’s voice may affect the perceived information. In the case of a conference call, the manager’s vocal cues may stay stable in the presentation section but may fluctuate when confronted by questions about slowing sales or the delay of a new product launch. Therefore, the designed model should analyze the sequential pattern of vocal cues during the entire call, instead of using simple descriptive statistics (such as the feature mean) to summarize the sequence. For the vocal-verbal integration challenge, MCM theory suggests that vocal and verbal cues should be considered together for information summarizing. A standard approach concatenates the heterogeneous features directly and passes the features to a predictive machine learning model (Abbasi et al., 2012; Dong et al., 2018). This largely ignores the contextual information and results in unsatisfactory performance. Therefore, the designed model should explicitly capture the vocal and verbal interaction. To summarize, the second metarequirement is that the predictive model should effectively learn the sequential pattern of managers’ vocal cues and the vocal-verbal interactions during the entire conference call.

Third, in terms of the features used to represent voice, the existing literature has no consensus on how a speaker’s vocal cues can be combined to identify emotional states or uncertainty, i.e., the vocal cue complexity challenge (Schuller, 2010). Therefore, rather than attempting to operationalize high-level vocal constructs from a manager’s voice, the third meta-requirement is that the model should identify and use basic vocal cues, such as pitch, volume, and fluency, to characterize the manager’s voice.

## Metadesigns

While meta-requirements define the design goals, the metadesign introduces a class of artifacts that can fulfill these goals (Walls et al., 1992). In this section, we present our metadesigns for an effective financial risk forecasting system using vocal cues.

Metadesign 1: Use Meta-Learning to Incorporate Firm Fundamentals, Historical Risks, and Managers’ Vocal/Verbal Cues in a Financial Risk Prediction Model

According to the first meta-requirement, firms’ financial fundamentals, historical risks and managers’ vocal/verbal communication could potentially predict future financial risks. However, fundamentals and risks are considered structured data while verbal and vocal cues are unstructured data by nature. To facilitate the learning of different data types, we train separate risk prediction models and use a metalearning framework to combine the predictions. Metalearning provides an effective way to learn about the learning process itself to obtain knowledge about which underlying models can be most effectively applied (Brazdil et al., 2008). Prior work in IS has also used a metalearning framework to integrate different data and models in downstream prediction tasks (Abbasi et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2021).

## Metadesign 2: Design an Effective Deep Learning Model to Learn Sequential Vocal and Verbal Cues Interactions

We design a deep learning method, a two-stage long shortterm memory (LSTM) neural network, to fulfill the second meta-requirement, which states that the vocal-verbal predictive model should effectively learn the sequential pattern of managers’ vocal cues and the vocal-verbal integrations during the entire call. Deep learning is a neural network model that can be used to model multiple levels of representation by composing the corresponding layers in a nonlinear way (LeCun et al., 2015). Instead of manually engineering features, deep learning models can be trained to learn the nonlinearity among features (vocal-verbal integrations) that are associated with prediction targets (financial risk). We describe the proposed deep learning method in the following.

First, the dimensionality of verbal features is much larger than that of vocal features. A commonly used dimensionality of a text embedding model is 300, as in Google’s Word2vec model (Mikolov et al., 2013). However, the vocal cues of a sound only have a few dimensions such as pitch and intensity (more detailed discussion in “Metadesign 3: Vocal Cues”). If we simply concatenate the two types of features, the verbal features will overwhelm the vocal features and their interplay will not be captured effectively. To tackle this problem, we designed a feature transformation component based on the LSTM recurrent neural network, so that both verbal and vocal features are mapped separately for an equal-sized representation. We used the bidirectional LSTM network (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997) since it is a widely used neural network architecture designed to work with sequential data. For each sentence, the vocal features and text embedding were fed into the feature transformation component separately. Then, we mapped them into two vectors of the same size. Specifically, we divided the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ earnings conference call sample into a sequence of n sentences with n corresponding audio clips. In the $i ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ sample, for the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ sentence $t _ { i j }$ and the corresponding audio clip $a _ { i j }$ , the bidirectional LSTM-based feature transformation component produces (we omit subscript i):

## Metadesign 3: Use Basic Vocal Cues as Features for Vocal Data.

In our metadesign, the use of basic vocal cues is another major design novelty. Basic vocal cues refer to a set of fundamental voice qualifiers that characterize the sound of the voice. Basic vocal cues differ from high-level vocal constructs that are operationalized to measure emotions or the trustworthiness of the voice. As noted before, high-level vocal constructs potentially suffer from high measurement errors due to the vocal complexity challenge. Therefore, we used a set of established basic vocal cues to characterize managers’ vocal cues based on the collective vocal communication literature.

The first basic vocal cue is the frequency of the voice, also known as pitch. A large body of communications research finds that the pitch of the voice influences how the audience perceives a verbal message. For example, an increase in anxiety is often accompanied by a higher voice pitch (Laukka et al., 2008). Therefore, in conference calls, when managers are scrutinized by analysts, cognitive changes may be captured by their voice pitch. We collected the maximum pitch, minimum pitch, mean pitch, and standard deviation of the manager’s pitch in an audio clip. A vocal cue relating to voice pitch is “jitter,” which captures frequency instability or small rapid changes in pitch.

The second basic vocal cue is intensity, which measures the volume or amplitude contained in a sound and is largely determined by the energy with which an individual speaks. The standard unit of volume is the decibel level. Jiang and Pell (2017) found that confident voices tend to display increased intensity. We collected maximum intensity, mean intensity, and minimum intensity. A vocal cue relating to voice intensity is “shimmer,” which captures amplitude instability or small rapid changes in vocal amplitude.

The third basic acoustic cues are the pulses, periods, and breaks in a sound, which measure the timing of a sound. These speech consistency measures are good indicators of the fluency of the speaker’s voice. Pon-Barry and Shieber (2010) found that doubtful voices are correlated with more pauses and a lower rate of speech. In the question-answer section of a conference call, how long does a manager allow periods of silence to go on, and how often does the manager interrupt analysts or wait an inappropriately long time before speaking? This timing information may reflect the manager’s confidence in an answer, which may affect the manager’s perceived tone.

The last vocal cues reflect the harmonic-to-noise ratio (HNR), and autocorrelation, which separately measure the voice quality of an audio clip. Although the audio quality does not directly measure the speaker’s voice characteristics, it can be used to reinforce speech signals. For example, if the noise level of an audio clip is very high (i.e., low HNR), it may indicate that the audio quality is poor and thus the impact of other vocal cues may be discounted. ## Testable Hypothesis

Our testable hypotheses are intended to assess whether the metadesign is effective and satisfies the meta-requirements (Walls et al., 1992), and they are important for rigorous design science research (Hevner et al., 2004). We identify three metadesigns; thus, there are three corresponding hypotheses. In light of the first metadesign, the spontaneous and interactive communications during earnings conference calls provide market participants with a rich information set that includes both verbal and nonverbal vocal cues. Therefore, we hypothesize that incorporating vocal cues better gauges managers’ information, thus potentially leading to substantial improvements in forecast accuracy, relative to a forecast model based only on firm fundamentals, historical risks, and managers’ verbal cues.

H1: Incorporating managers’ vocal cues will improve the accuracy of financial risk prediction, compared to existing methods that use firm fundamentals, historical risks, and managers’ verbal cues.

Second, a critical component in our framework is the design of a deep learning method: a two-stage LSTM model. This model is explicitly designed to learn the sequential pattern of managers’ vocal cues and the vocal-verbal integrations during the entire call. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2: A two-stage LSTM model is a better design for the sequential pattern learning of vocal cues and vocal-verbal integrations and will improve the accuracy of financial risk prediction compared to existing machine learning models.

Third, Metadesign 3 suggests that using basic vocal cues as features is more appropriate than developing high-level vocal constructs, e.g., emotion, to summarize a manager’s tone of voice. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3: Using basic vocal cues will improve the accuracy of financial risk prediction compared to using high-level vocal constructs.

## Design Instantiation: The Financial Risk Prediction System—DeepVoice

We have discussed design kernel theory, the metarequirements of the design, and the metadesigns. We now present the design artifact itself: the DeepVoice system. The proposed system includes data collection, data extraction, machine learning prediction, and metalearning components, as illustrated in Figure 2.

Data collection: This step dealt with gathering structured and unstructured financial data from various resources. We developed a crawler to fetch earnings conference call transcripts and audio recordings for targeted firms. Structured data, such as firm fundamentals and historical risks, were queried from financial databases.

Data extraction: This step included input data processing. First, we analyzed managers’ vocal/verbal cues at the sentence level. We implemented a text-audio alignment component to tag the corresponding start and end audio marks of each sentence. Specifically, given the entire audio recording of an earnings call and its transcript, we implemented the iterative forced alignment (IFA) algorithm (Moreno et al., 1998) to match each of the focal manager’s sentences in the transcript with the audio clip containing the spoken text of that sentence. Next, for each sentence audio clip, we used Praat (Boersma & Van Heuven, 2001), an open source software, to extract vocal features. Specifically, for each audio clip, we extracted 27 acoustic features for the basic acoustic cues, as listed in Table 2. For textual transcripts, we used pretrained GloVe-300 word embeddings (Pennington et al., 2014) to represent the text features.<sup>4</sup> A snippet of the processed earnings conference call sample is presented in Appendix A.

One critical step in data extraction is executive selection. Most of the earnings conference calls involve multiple individuals (managers and analysts) speaking in turn. Because voice features vary across individuals, voice analysis requires parsing the audio to make it individual specific. To avoid the interference of multiple participants, following prior literature (Mayew et al., 2020), we identified the manager who spoke most in a conference call and incorporated only that manager’s verbal and vocal cues. We selected the focal manager based on the following criteria. First, the manager spoke in both the introduction and the question-answer sections. This allowed our model to capture the time-varying pattern when the manager read a statement and when the manager was confronted by the analysts.

## Testbed Details

We chose the constituent firms of the S&P 500 Index as our data sample because of its importance and tractability. The conference call transcripts were collected from the Seeking Alpha website.<sup>6</sup> The transcripts were annotated with the names of the speakers (executives and analysts) and content. For each transcript, we obtained the corresponding call audio recording from the EarningsCast website.<sup>7</sup> Our sample included 6,047 earnings conference calls, along with their corresponding transcripts and audio recordings, between 2015 and 2018. The breakdown of our sample by industry is presented in Appendix B.

We obtained corresponding firms’ financial fundamentals from Compustat and daily stock prices (dividend-adjusted) from the CRSP database. For financial fundamentals, following Sridharan (2015), we calculated the standard deviation of earnings yield $( E Y _ { \mathrm { s t d } } )$ , the standard deviation of the change in market-to-book premium $( C M M B _ { \mathrm { s t d } } )$ , and the covariance of earnings yield and change in market-to-book premium cov<sub>(EY,</sub> <sub>CMMB)</sub> over the 12 months prior to the earnings conference call date. For risk variables, we calculated firm historical risk and market systematic risk as the stock return volatility and S&P 500 Index return volatility ?? days prior to the earnings conference call date, respectively.

Volatility can be forecasted over different time horizons. Following prior literature in financial risk forecasting using quarterly data (Frankel et al., 1999; Sridharan, 2015), we forecast volatility at different horizons—in particular, daily to weekly volatility (3-day, 5-day), weekly to monthly volatility (10-day, 30-day) and quarterly volatility (60-day), i.e., $\tau =$ 3, 5, 10, 30, 60. The daily to weekly volatility is usually used for computing risk metrics. The weekly to monthly volatility is often used in risk management—for example, according to Basel II regulatory requirements, a financial institution should track its 10-day value at risk (VaR). Longer horizon volatility, such as quarterly volatility, can be used for options evaluation and in portfolio management (Sridharan, 2015).

To construct the training and testing datasets and avoid the look-ahead bias, we divided the data chronologically: the first 80% of the data were used as training data (including validation) for the two-stage LSTM model, 10% of the data were used as the data for the stacking model, and the remaining 10% were used to test out-of-sample data. We present robustness checks on model performance with different data splits in Appendix C and subsample analysis in Appendix D.

Table 3 provides a summary of the dataset. As discussed above regarding the DeepVoice design, we identified the manager who spoke most in a conference call and incorporated that manager’s verbal and vocal cues only. In 69.8% of samples, the selected manager was the chief financial officer, and in 21.9% of samples, the selected manager was the chief executive officer.

## Is Two-Stage LSTM an Effective Design for Sequential Vocal/Verbal Integration?

A key design in DeepVoice is the two-stage LSTM that integrates sequential verbal and vocal cues. In this experiment, we evaluated the two-stage LSTM design by comparing it with several standard and state-of-the-art machine learning methods. The baselines included in this experiment are as follows. First, the vocal and verbal features are simply concatenated into one feature vector and then fed into a machine learning model for risk prediction. This is the easiest and standard way to incorporate different modality features (voice and text). Prior IS design science work has used this approach to integrate different types of data (such as text and quantitative numbers) (Abbasi et al., 2012; Dong et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020), but not voice and text. We considered using two standard machine learning methods, SVR and gradient boosting, denoting them as the baseline Concat-SVR and Concat-GradientBoosting respectively. Second, we considered a deep learning approach that is designed to take different types of data into account by concatenating their transformed features into one feature vector (Cheng et al., 2019), with the hope that the deep learning algorithm itself can learn the data interactions. Specifically, vocal and verbal features are concatenated and then fed into an LSTM deep learning model. This resembles a one-stage LSTM approach.

We also considered a state-of-the-art deep learning model, contextual LSTM, which is specially designed to incorporate vocal features (Poria et al., 2017). The major difference between Poria et al. (2017) and our work lies in the deep learning architecture. In Poria et al. (2017), the input sentences and voices are short (usually less than one minute) and extracted at the utterance (word) level. Their LSTM component designs a special contextualized gate to control for the feature fusion. However, our proposed two-stage LSTM uses feature transformation (first stage) and feature fusion (second stage) to tackle the sequential vocal-verbal integration challenge. The detailed two-stage LSTM model architecture is shown in Appendix G, and robustness checks against other machine learning and deep learning models are presented in Appendix H and Appendix I. We present hyperparameter tunning results in Appendix J.

To better understand the impact of deep neural network design on the DeepVoice performance, we considered a variant of DeepVoice that incorporates an attention layer in the LSTM, denoted as DeepVoice-Attention. The attention mechanism is a useful neural network design for sequence modeling, allowing for the modeling of dependencies without regard to their distances in input sequences (Vaswani et al., 2017). In the experiment, we implemented the scaled-dot product attention, which is similar to the one that is used in Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017). We present more comprehensive experiments on the impact of attention mechanisms in Appendix I.

The experiment results can be seen in Table 5, which shows that DeepVoice consistently improves forecast accuracy at all horizons compared to the baseline approaches, including the state-of-the-art model contextual LSTM. For example, compared to the standard feature concatenation baseline Concat-SVR, the two-stage LSTM approach improves the $R ^ { 2 } o o s$ from 2.94% to 7.31% at ?? = 3, and from 2.69% to 7.45% at ?? = 60. Also, it is surprising to see that the onestage LSTM model does not improve prediction performance, indicating that the feature concatenation using only one stage of LSTM failed to learn, or even overfit, the training samples. Moreover, experimental results show that while incorporating an attention mechanism in DeepVoice does outperform other machine learning models, its performance is worse than that of DeepVoice, which does not use an attention mechanism. This finding can be explained as follows. Our sequential modeling is at the earnings conference call sentence level. Hence, the intersentence dependency is not as important as withinsentence vocal-verbal interactions for risk prediction. Overall, the results confirm that the proposed two-stage LSTM deep learning model is an effective artifact for learning sequential verbal and vocal information. Metadesign 2 and Hypothesis 2 are supported.

## Does Using Basic Vocal Cues Improve Financial Risk Forecast, in Comparison with Using Traditional Vocal-Related Behavioral Constructs?

In this experiment, we examined whether our Metadesign 3, which represents voice with four groups of basic vocal cues, is more effective than the existing approach that uses highlevel vocal constructs. We set up a baseline model, emotion, that replicates the method used in Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012), a pioneering work examining vocal cues in the financial market. We used a state-of-the-art emotion analysis model (Livingstone & Russo, 2018) to measure the manager’s emotions in an earnings conference call. The model is trained on an emotion speech dataset that includes seven emotions (calm, happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, and disgust). Using this model, we extracted the manager’s emotions in an earnings conference call, combined them with text features, and fed them into a support vector regression model for risk prediction.

As shown in Table 6, the emotion model cannot improve forecast performance at all, as the $R ^ { 2 } o o s$ is close to zero. This means that constructed emotion variables are not accurate enough and are barely predictive. This result echoes the findings of prior literature, suggesting that identifying the specific acoustic constructs from voice is a complex process and there is little consensus on an appropriate model for measuring affective states, emotions, and trust (Schuller, 2010). As a result, attempts to develop constructs from vocal cues may suffer from high measurement errors. In other words, without the kernel theory-guided design, researchers and practitioners may reach the misleading conclusion that audio data has no predictive power. Metadesign 3 and Hypothesis 3 are thus supported.

## Economic Utility

Given DeepVoice’s superior performance in risk forecasting, we examine its economic utility. Risk forecasting is critical in options trading; therefore, we analyze whether risk forecasts based on the DeepVoice model can be used to derive profitable option trading strategies. We follow prior literature to implement a long-short straddle strategy (Dockner & Strobl, 2005; Sridharan, 2015). A straddle position is formed as a combination of one call and one put options with the same underlying asset, strike price and maturity. This generates a payoff that is increasing in the absolute price change of the underlying asset, but is insensitive to the direction of the change. Following Goyal and Saretto (2009), we chose the atthe-money call-and-put option, i.e., the closest option to the opening price of the underlying stock on the day after earnings conference call date, to form the straddle.

The straddle returns were constructed following Goyal and Saretto (2009). We summed up the average of the bid and ask quotes of call and put options, using it as a reference beginning price, and used the terminal payoff of the option as the closing price. Our strategy works as follows: from the forecast model, we obtained information regarding whether volatility in the next horizon (60 days) would increase or decrease. We ranked the volatility change (i.e., forecasted volatility minus current volatility) on out-of-sample data from top to bottom and used this information as the buying or selling signal for a straddle. We took a long position for the top-ranked stocks (i.e., those with the most increases in volatility), and went short for the bottom-ranked stocks (i.e., those with the most decreases in volatility). We calculated the portfolio return as the sum of straddle returns (Sridharan, 2015).

We report this long-short straddle strategy returns in Table 7. The results show that if the long-short strategy only includes the top 10 and bottom 10 firms, DeepVoice generates a return of 8.89%, a 91.2% improvement compared to the market model’s 4.65% return. A strategy that longs the highest quintile (Q5) and shorts the lowest quintile (Q1) produces a return of 5.14% using DeepVoice, a 79.1% improvement compared to 2.87% using the market model. It is expected that the top-10/bottom-10 portfolio would have a higher return than the Q5/Q1 portfolio, because the former includes only the most volatile stocks, and straddle options benefit from high volatility. Similarly, DeepVoice produces a higher return than the DeepVoice w/o vocal model, demonstrating that integrating vocal cues with verbal cues offers greater utility than using verbal cues only. Overall, the results in Table 7 confirm that the improvement achieved by DeepVoice in out-of-sample forecasts translates into profitable economic gains. ## Additional Analysis: Pure Noise Features

To test the robustness of the vocal cues, we replaced the vocal cues with pure noise, drawn from a uniform distribution between 0 and 1. We retrained the DeepVoice system and present the forecasting results in Table 10. The results show that having pure noise features deteriorates forecasting performance, compared to the DeepVoice w/o vocal model. Since the vocal cue values are pure noise, the system cannot learn the interactions between vocal and verbal and their relationship with the target variable. As a result, the model overfits the noise signals and fails to generalize to the out-of-sample data. This additional analysis confirms the effectiveness of the used vocal cues in DeepVoice.

## Case Study on Verbal-Vocal Information

We present a case study to illustrate the role of voice in predicting financial risk. This case study is based on earnings conference calls from Advanced Micro Devices, Inc. (AMD) in the first quarter (Q1) and second quarter (Q2) of 2017. We qualitatively explain why using voice and text features can be more useful than using text features alone to predict financial risk.

The firm’s risks after the Q1 and Q2 earnings conference calls were drastically different. The stock price fell by 16.1% in the postmarket session after the Q1 earnings conference call and was volatile on the following days, exhibiting high risk. In contrast, the stock prices after the Q2 earnings conference call were mostly stable, exhibiting low risk. We analyzed the transcript of both conference calls and a corresponding audio recording of the company’s CEO, Dr. Lisa T. Su. Figure 3 represents two sentences in the Q1 conference call, while Figure 4 represents two sentences in the Q2 conference call. We notice that in the Q1 call, there was a significant increase in the mean pitch of Dr. Su’s voice when she said: “So look, we are very pleased with how the Ryzen launch went.” The mean pitch of Dr. Su’s voice was 26.9% higher than her average mean pitch (203.4 Hz) in the neighboring sentences (Figure 3 left). A similar pattern can be observed for the sentence: “On the GPU size, we actually haven’t seen anything abnormal,” (Figure 3 right), with a 21.5% increase in pitch. However, in the Q2 call, when the CEO said “So we are very pleased with the reception to EPYC”, her voice pitch changed only slightly (by 2.7%).

The literature on nonverbal communication has suggested that a high mean pitch may be correlated with a speaker’s lack of confidence (Apple et al., 1979; De Waele et al., 2019; Klofstad et al., 2012). Mayew et al. (2020) also found that a manager’s voice pitch increases as a condition in which the manager exhibits inferiority vis-à-vis the analyst asking the questions. Although the verbal message in the Q1 call was very positive, after considering the accompanied vocal cues, the positive words in the CEO’s statement were perhaps not as positive as they seemed. More importantly, “So look, we are very pleased with how the Ryzen launch went” in Q1 and “So we are very pleased with the reception to EPYC” in Q2 had very similar linguistic content and positive polarity. If we only used verbal messages for risk forecasting, the machine learning model would be likely to overfit because the two conference calls are associated with vastly different risk levels yet have similar linguistic features. Therefore, the incremental information from the integration of vocal cues could help the machine learning model differentiate between the conference calls and learn the relationship between verbal-vocal and risk levels, which would improve the model’s performance on risk forecast for out-of-sample data.

## Discussion

Guided by a design science paradigm, we tackle three challenges—time variation, vocal-verbal integration, and vocal complexity—of modeling vocal and verbal cues for financial risk prediction, and propose a novel artifact, namely the DeepVoice framework. Beyond its direct application in financial risk prediction, the design process itself can be summarized in three design principles and thereby contributes to the IS knowledge base (Ebrahimi et al. 2022; Rai 2017; Samtani et al. 2021). The design principles can generalize to domains with similar data types or characteristics and help future research identify the design artifacts in other applications. We discuss each of the design principles below.

1. Learning the sequential patterns of vocal data: In our design, kernel theory suggests that the change in a speaker’s voice may affect the perceived information. Therefore, instead of using single descriptive statistics, such as feature mean, to summarize the manager’s voice information during the entire conference call (Mayew et al., 2020), we leverage a deep LSTM model to learn the sequential patterns of vocal cues.

Since the audio recordings of earnings conference calls are largely accessible, future research could leverage this design principle and modify the DeepVoice pipeline accordingly to accommodate other financial outcome predictions. This design principle also resonates with existing IS design work that uses other types of sequential data, such as motion sensor data for healthcare-related prediction tasks (Zhu et al., 2021).

2. Integrating multimodal sequential data to facilitate accurate representation learning: There are two modalities in the context of earnings conference calls: vocal cues and verbal cues. Kernel theory suggests that (in)consistency can affect the perceived information.

Therefore, we explicitly learn the integration of vocal-verbal cues via a novel two-stage LSTM design. This design principle can be adopted in applications where intermodality information is crucial for the prediction task. Recent IS research has explored the applications where multimodal data is available and the consistency between modalities has predictive power, such as text/image in social media analytics (Shin et al., 2020). Therefore, IS researchers should consider using a two-stage LSTM design to integrate different types of sequential data.

3. Leveraging the basic data features rather than highlevel constructs: Our design emphasizes the use of basic vocal cues as input, rather than using high-level vocal constructs that are operationalized to measure emotions or the trustworthiness of the voice. This is because there is little consensus on an appropriate model for measuring emotions and trust from voice (Schuller, 2010). To avoid using highlevel vocal constructs with potentially high measurement errors, we work with basic vocal cues. This design principle has important implications for applications where feature constructs or high-level feature engineering might be inaccurate or unfeasible.

## Conclusion

In this work, we propose DeepVoice, a novel financial risk forecasting framework that leverages managers’ vocal and verbal cues during conference calls. Experiments on 6,047 earnings conference calls demonstrate the effectiveness of DeepVoice, compared to existing state-of-the-art methods. The experimental results show that the DeepVoice framework Is remarkably effective. Specifically, it improves out-ofsample R<sup>2</sup> relative to benchmark model predictions by 7.31%, 8.34%, 4.28%, 2.26%, and 7.45% at 3-day, 5-day, 10-day, 30- day, and 60-day forecast horizons. Our experiments also show that the proposed two-stage LSTM model is effective compared to existing machine learning and deep learning approaches in vocal and verbal cues integration. We further demonstrate that the improvement of out-of-sample prediction performance can translate into nontrivial economic gains in options trading. We also conducted a cross-sectional analysis and found that DeepVoice provides greater predictive utility for firms with higher variance in financial fundamentals. Additional analyses on the importance of different vocal cue features and pure noise features also confirm the effectiveness of the DeepVoice framework.

Given the importance of vocal cues in nonverbal communication, information systems researchers (Grover et al., 2018) and financial accounting researchers (Loughran &

Mcdonald, 2016) have called for the creation of strategic business value using nonverbal analytics. However, tools for systematic measurement in real-life settings are far less advanced for voice than text (Mayew & Venkatachalam, 2013). This work tackles the formidable methodological challenges associated with the analysis of vocal data. Our DeepVoice design is directly relevant and applicable to financial market participants, including investors, analysts, and regulators.

The DeepVoice design could influence the design and development of multimedia information systems. Guided by Mehrabian’s communication model, we identify important metadesigns for nonverbal-based machine learning, including the design of a two-stage LSTM model for verbal and vocal integration and the use of basic vocal cues as input features. Given that “data is the new oil” (Agrawal et al., 2018) and audio data are more and more ubiquitous in social media and customer service, among other areas, we believe that the proposed framework is generalizable to other practical business applications. For example, since customer phone calls generate a large amount of audio data, audio analysis may better summarize customer call information, ultimately improving customer satisfaction (Guo et al., 2017). Additionally, insurance companies often conduct audio interviews with clients and could use audio analysis to detect fraudulent activities (e.g., policyholders exaggerate claims when reporting an accident). Similarly, audio and textual information could further improve managerial accounting and financial reporting practices (Warren et al., 2015).

This study has certain limitations. In this work, our prediction target is a firm’s financial risk days after the firm’s earnings conference call. Researchers and practitioners may be interested in longer prediction horizons, such as annual financial risk. In that case, the usefulness of quarterly earnings conference calls may be limited. Future research could incorporate different sources of multimedia data, such as press conferences and manager interviews, to examine the information role of voice in financial markets over a longer horizon. Also, our sample contains S&P 500 firms. Future research could exploit proprietary databases to obtain a more diverse sample beyond S&P 500 firms.

## Appendix E

## Comparison with Time-Series Models

In this appendix, we compare DeepVoice design with popular time-series-based volatility forecasting models. The financial economics literature has proposed several time-series-based volatility forecast models. In the experiment, we tested two popular time-series forecast models. The first one is the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity model GARCH(1,1) (Bollerslev, 1986). The second one is the heterogeneous autoregressive model HAR (Corsi, 2009). We built the forecast model using the in-sample time-series return data and measured the out-of-sample forecast errors. We used the Python package arch<sup>8</sup> for the model implementation.

The results are presented in Table E1, showing that the time-series forecast model underperforms the market benchmark and the DeepVoice model by a large margin. Note that in the market benchmark, firms’ financial fundamentals are also incorporated, while the time-series forecast model only considers the historical return series. The major difference between the time-series-based forecast and the machine learning-based forecast is that the latter learns a nonlinear mapping between features and volatility using all in-sample training data across firms, while the former only uses the return time series within a firm for its own forecast. ## Appendix F

## 5-Minute Realized Volatility During Conference Call Day

In this appendix, we augment the risk model with high-frequency trading data and examine the incremental predictive utility of vocal cues. In DeepVoice, the risk model is trained with firms’ historical idiosyncratic risk and market systematic risk. Prior literature has shown that return volatility estimated using high-frequency price data is a strong predictor of a firm’s future realized volatility (Liu et al., 2015). Moreover, Frankel et al. (1999) found high levels of return volatility during the conference call periods and found that the high level of return volatility and abnormal returns may carry over for several weeks following the earnings announcement (Ball & Brown, 1968; Bernard & Thomas, 1989; Kimbrough, 2005). Therefore, the above empirical evidence raises a question: Does DeepVoice still improve financial risk forecast when the 5-minute realized volatility is incorporated?

We collected the intraday minute-by-minute price data from the NYSE trade and quote (TAQ) database. We considered a sampling frequency of 5 minutes and calculated the intraday realized variance, measured as the sum of squared returns: $\begin{array} { r } { R V _ { t } = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } r _ { t + i / n } ^ { 2 } , } \end{array}$ , where the price series on day t is assumed to be observed n times (Floros et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2015). Realized volatility is the square root of realized variance. We augmented the risk model with the 5-minute realized volatility variable, stack it with the Fundamental model and denote it as Market<sup>5min</sup> model. We then stack it with the two-stage LSTM model for the final financial risk prediction, denoted as DeepVoice<sup>5min</sup>. We present the results in Table F1.

The results in Table F1 lead to two interesting findings. First, DeepVoice augmented with 5-minute realized volatility can still consistently and significantly outperform its counterpart models Market<sup>5min</sup>, and DeepVoice<sup>5min</sup> w/o vocal. This improvement seems to suggest that the DeepVoice approach may also learn the nonlinear relationship between vocal/verbal cues and 5-minute realized volatility. Second, the results show that the 5-minute variable provides additional predictive utility to the financial risk prediction (Market<sup>5min</sup> vs. Market benchmark). This finding is consistent with Li and Tang (2021) that adding realized-variance-based features into machine learning models can incrementally improve the forecast accuracy.

## Robustness Check: Two-stage LSTM vs. Other Deep Learning Models and Attention Mechanism

In this appendix, we compare the proposed two-stage LSTM against other deep learning approaches, on their capability in modeling sequential verbal and vocal features. In our design, we propose to use bidirectional LSTM layer for feature transformation and feature fusion, due to its capability in modeling sequential inputs. Given that there are different deep learning layers, a natural question arises: How effective is the bidirectional LSTM layer for vocal/verbal cues learning? We chose four different deep learning layers for comparison: convolutional neural network, Gated recurrent unit (GRU), bidirectional GRU, and one-directional LSTM. We also fine-tuned the approaches and report their best-performing results.

The results are presented in Table I1. The results show that bidirectional LSTM outperforms the other layer choices significantly, since the R<sup>2</sup> value is negative for the compared deep learning layers. We argue the bidirectional LSTM is a better layer choice for two reasons. First, the LSTM unit is effective in capturing the long dependency. In the context of earnings conference calls, the input sequences are quite long, therefore, LSTM is more effective than CNN which is more local-dependency oriented. Second, the bidirectional design allows each input unit to “look around” its left and right inputs so that it can better capture the vocal pattern changes during the entire earnings conference call.

## Appendix K

## Bid-Ask Spread

For the paper, we employed a long-short options trading strategy to examine the economic utility achieved by DeepVoice. The results reported in the paper are based on returns computed using the average of the bid and ask quotes (midpoint) of call and put options as a reference. In this appendix, we examine the impact of the liquidity of options, measured by the effective bid-ask spread, on the strategy returns.

Specifically, we considered three effective spread measures equal to 50%, 75%, and 100% of the quoted bid-ask spread, similar to the situations considered in Goyal and Saretto (2009). For example, if the bid price of an option is \$2 and the ask price is \$3, then we conside the following scenarios: buy at \$2.75 and sell at \$2.25, which corresponds to 50% effective to quoted spread; buy at \$2.87 and sell at \$2.12, which corresponds to 75% effective to quoted spread; and buy at \$3 and sell at \$2, which corresponds to 100% effective to quoted spread. We present the results in Table K1. The results show that the long-short straddle strategy based on DeepVoice consistently outperforms that of the market model or the text-only DeepVoice model (DeepVoice w/o Vocal). When the effective bid-ask spread is 100%, DeepVoice can still result in a positive portfolio return while both benchmark models result in a negative portfolio return. This analysis confirms the economic utility of DeepVoice accounting for different levels of liquidity and transaction costs.
