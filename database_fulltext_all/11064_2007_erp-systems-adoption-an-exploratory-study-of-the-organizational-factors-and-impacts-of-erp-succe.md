# ERP systems adoption: An exploratory study of the organizational factors and impacts of ERP success

Chuck C.H. Law <sup>\*</sup>, Eric W.T. Ngai

Department of Management and Marketing, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong, PR China Received 21 September 2004; received in revised form 7 February 2007; accepted 11 March 2007 Available online 29 May 2007

## Abstract

We examined the relationships between the success of ERP system adoption, extent of business process improvement (BPI), and organizational performance and investigated the associations between the outcomes of these initiatives and such organizational factors as strategic intent, senior management support, and the status of the IT function within a company. A correlation analysis of 96 firms was made to test our hypothesis that the strategic intent to use ERP was closely related to the success of BPI, ERP, and organizational performance. The results also demonstrated that CEO-IT distance may have little direct bearing on the outcomes of ERP and BPI initiatives. A closer CEO-IT reporting relationship, however, was found to be associated with higher levels of senior management support for both types of enterprise projects. This empirical evidence reinforced the long-held assumption tha organization-level benefits, BPI, and ERP success were closely related; and that these relationships were subject to the influence of the organizational variables.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise resources planning (ERP) systems allow seamless integration of information flows [6] and business processes [30] across functional areas within a company. They support information sharing along a company value chain and help to achieve operating efficiency. ERP packages offer a workflow engine to generate automated workflows according to business rules and approval matrices so that information and documents can be routed to operational users for transaction handling, and to managers and directors for review and approval [23].

Although ERP systems have been recognized as useful to many businesses while touted by vendors and consultants as systems that incorporate good business practice, ERP systems have often been found to fail to be effective [45]. One of the widely discussed issues is the need for an ERP-process fit involving some need for business and process changes. The relationship between business process change and successful ERP adoption is symbiotic: the benefit of ERP adoption often results in business change [31]. However, its outcomes are likely to be influenced by the organizational and cultural antecedents that facilitate or inhibit effective management of organizational changes [2,25,40]. The literature insists that it is necessary for the CEO and senior leaders to encourage the adoption of the technology and change [44]. Consequently, we decided to concentrate in this study on factors at the strategic management level: strategic intent to adopt ERP, the status of the IT leader, and management support of IT and business process improvement (BPI) initiatives.

While the critical success factors for the implementation and use of ERP systems and their effects on organizational performance have been discussed and analysed, there have been many inconsistent and inconclusive findings [46]. We decided that it would be useful to search for empirical evidence to support the claims. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study were to explore the relationships among the extent of BPI, ERP success, and organizational performance; and also the possible impacts of the organizational variables on these constructs.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. The critical role of process change

Based on interviews with 19 Canadian firms, Kumar et al. [28] compiled a list of critical issues, including project justifications, risks, and ERP-process fit, in initiating the adoption of an ERP system. Hong and Kim [19] found empirical evidence of a relationship between organizational fit of ERP and the success of its implementation, and for moderating effects of ERP and process adaptations on this relationship. The impact of redesigning business processes has been considered in many studies [27], showing that ERP-business process fit is a pre-requisite for ERP success. Gattiker and Goodhue [12] found that the adoption of ERP was positively associated with change in process, although a relationship between change and the organizational impact of ERP was not supported.

According to Grover et al. [17], the empirical evidence supporting theories and principles on the role of business process changes on IT and system adoption is limited. Thus, while business process change has been recognized as an important issue in successful ERP adoption, it still needs to be empirically investigated.

## 2.2. The potential benefits of ERP and difficulties of assessment

Some studies have failed to support their hypothesized relationships between ERP adoption and organizational performance. Such unfavorable findings may be due to a difficulty in identifying intangible ERP benefits which are often unquantifiable. Consequently, many researchers directed their attention to the intangible benefits [36] of ERP systems, rather than limited their focus to the traditional financial-based measures. For instance, Murphy and Simon [34] examined the intangible benefits and demonstrated how they could be incorporated into traditional methods of evaluation: the company they studied extended benefits to include intangibles such as customer satisfaction, measured by relating it to increased sales revenue.

## 2.3. Organizational factors

Ein-Dor and Segev [11] proposed that the success of IT projects was affected by organizational factors: management support and the rank of the IS leaders. In many circumstances, the top management team must mediate between technology and business requirements and resolve the conflict of interest among a large number of stakeholders [15].

The location of the MIS function or the seniority of the IS executive will affect on the success of IT and business process initiatives [16,22]. The CIO, as an influential member of the senior management team, must solicit support from the user community and other managers. Li and Ye [29] found that the direct reporting relationship of the IS executive to the chief executive as opposed to other senior leaders moderated the effects of IT investment on the firm’s performance.

Another important organizational factor in IT and IS adoption is the alignment between IT and organizational objectives [5,13]. The fit between ERP systems and business strategies is often considered critical to achieving gains in organizational performance [21,26]. Thus, the strategic intention of business firms to adopt ERP [43] deserves further investigation.

## 3. Interviews with ERP-using organizations

IT executives of three ERP-using companies were interviewed and project documents were collected from them and reviewed.

With a narrow profit margin, the company stays profitable through a low cost strategy as well as in the running of internal infrastructure. Thus, the intent for its adoption of the Oracle ERP was operational (to reduce production and operational costs). The primary trigger for its initial ERP project was to replace an in-house legacy system. Following its acquisition, it was necessary to align its business systems with corporate IT. It implemented the financial and distribution modules first and intends to deploy the manufacturing modules soon.

Needless to say, compliance with a corporate decision to move towards a global standard was necessary.

## 3.2. Organizational issues for ERP adoption

The interviewees were asked about the critical organizational issues and risks that they needed to address during the pre-implementation and implementation phases. The issues varied but converged on a number of items (see Table 2) including seniority of the IT leader, support of the chief executive and other senior managers, business process change, good customer relationships, user support and involvement in the project, and the size and expertise of the IT organization. Companies 1 and 2 have reportedly experienced similar difficulties in driving business process changes. The IT heads were at a relatively junior level and they found it challenging to drive changes across functional boundaries and mobilise the user departments; intercession by the managing directors helped resolve this problem and steer the projects towards a successful outcome. Of these three companies, a converging view emerged that a senior IT head, a supportive chief executive, and a top management team were critical to minimize the risk of resistance to change and increase the likelihood of ERP success.

## 4. The research model

A conceptual model depicting the hypothesized relationships among the relevant constructs is shown in Fig. 1.

## 4.1. Organizational performance

Much consideration was given to data issues in the process of designing this model and the experimental measures. We decided to use perceptual ratings of organizational performance for two reasons. First, we required data concerning the intangible aspects of organizational performance. As traditional measures alone would be inadequate to identify these effects, the use of perceptual inputs allowed us to assess intangibles. Second, quantitative data was not available for some of the factors, of international divisions. While the parent companies of the multi-nationals were public and thus listed on the stock exchange of their home country with audited financial statements and aggregated data available to the public, the financial statements of the international subsidiaries were not available. Also, we had reviewed the financial data of Hong Kong-listed companies and the annual reports of some to determine whether they could provide quantitative data at the required levels. We found that the financial statements made available performance data only in aggregate form, representing data from multiple revenue sources. Much of this data may also have been inflated by income and profit from a miscellany of economic activities other than their primary business; e.g., Hong Kong-listed companies are often dominated by overseas Chinese families and engaged in real estate investment [3], resulting in significant financial gains above the earnings from manufacturing. Thus, we found it too risky to attempt to determine the performance data of their primary business operations. Our decision to use perceptual inputs would therefore avoid the confounding effects of inflated performance data. Dess and Robinson [8] made similar comments about the availability and accuracy of the financial data of individual divisions of multiindustry conglomerates. They suggested that the use of perceptual inputs was acceptable though reliable quantitative data would be preferred when available.

Organizational performance includes measures of multiple dimensions, such as profitability [35], sales growth, expansion of market share [20], and customer satisfaction (goodwill). The organizational performance construct in our model was intended to capture perceptual ratings not only on the financial aspects but also in other dimensions; thus, it included questions of profitability relative to competitors, sales growth, customer satisfaction with products or services purchased, customer retention rate, and overall organizational performance.

## 4.2. ERP success

IS user satisfaction was used as a surrogate for the success of implementing ERP systems. DeLone and McLean [7] said that user satisfaction was widely used because of its face validity and the availability of validated user satisfaction instruments. In our research model, user satisfaction with ERP systems was measured by the end-user computing satisfaction (EUCS) instrument developed and validated by Doll et al. [10]. Though this was initially developed for the end-user computing environment, it has been widely used or validated in different systems contexts, including data warehousing [4] and ERP systems, and also across different cultural settings [14]. Zviran et al. [47] used this instrument to determine the relationships between user satisfaction and perceived usefulness in an ERP context using a sample of ERP users from both high and low organizational positions.

In this study, we posited that successful deployment of ERP systems would contribute to the performance of a firm in multiple ways:

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 1 }$ . ERP success as measured by user satisfaction, is positively associated with the perceived performance of organizations.

## 4.3. The extent of business process improvement

BPI involves the simplification and improvement of work practices and processes through re-engineering [18]. In our study, the extent of BPI was used to measure the degree of process change leading to enhanced process quality in five ways: (a) prevention of errors and defects, (b) process quality standards, (c) process simplification, (d) intra-firm co-ordination, and (e) inter-firm coordination. Given the importance of business processes, the hypothesized relationship between the extent of BPI and organizational performance is

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } } 2 .$ The perceived extent of BPI is positively associated with the perceived performance of organizations.

## 4.4. The interaction between BPI and ERP success

BPI and IT deployment are inter-related. Automating inefficient processes without redesigning them may reduce the benefits that ensue from the effort. Conversely, weak or inflexible IT infrastructure can adversely affect BPR. Apparently, the fit between ERP and business processes is central to the success of ERP adoption [39]. Redesign or improvement of a process is a critical way of achieving mutual fit [33]. Therefore, we posited that a significant degree of association existed between the extent of BPI and ERP success:

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 3 } .$ The perceived extent of BPI is positively associated with ERP success, as measured by user satisfaction.

## 4.5. Organizational factors

Senior management support is important for the success of both IT and BPI projects, and the support for these two types of initiatives was measured by separate questions in this study. It is believed that management support, and the status of the IT function and the CIO are critical to the performance of ERP and BPI initiatives [37]. Thus we hypothesized that:

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 4 a } .$ . Perceived senior management support of BPI initiatives is positively associated with the perceived extent of BPI.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 4 b } .$ Perceived senior management support of IT initiatives is positively associated with ERP success as measured by user satisfaction.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 5 4 }$ . CEO-IT distance is negatively associated with the perceived extent of BPI.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 5 b } .$ . CEO-IT distance is negatively associated with ERP success as measured by user satisfaction.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 6 a }$ . CEO-IT distance is negatively associated with the perceived support of senior management for BPI initiatives.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } 6 } \mathbf { b }$ . CEO-IT distance is negatively associated with the perceived support of senior management for IT initiatives.

We believed, based on [41], that firms adopting ERP could be divided into four categories: dual focus, strategic focus, operational focus, and unfocused. Firms with an operational focus would primarily use ERP to improve operating efficiency and reduce costs, while those with a strategic focus would change their business practices, improve sales, and expand their market reach.

It seemed reasonable to argue that firms with a dual focus would enjoy more benefits than those with a single focus, and obviously therefore firms that were unfocused would enjoy very limited benefits from the adoption of ERP. The hypotheses were restated as

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } } 7 \mathbf { a } .$ . There is a difference in the perceived level of ERP success, as measured by the mean ERP user satisfaction indices, across firms with different strategic intents for ERP.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } } 7 \mathbf { b } .$ . There is a difference in the extent of BPI, as measured by the mean BPI indices, across firms with different strategic intents for ERP.

$\mathbf { H _ { 0 } } 7 \mathbf { c } .$ . There is a difference in the perceived level of organization performance, as measured by the mean organizational performance indices, across firms with different strategic intents for ERP.

## 5. Research methodology

## 5.1. Data source

Perceptual inputs were collected using a postal survey. Survey packages containing a letter addressed to the managing director, a structured questionnaire, and a self-addressed return envelope with pre-paid postage were mailed to 1000 companies based in Hong Kong. The companies had been randomly selected from a pool of 3200 Hong Kong-listed firms and multinationals extracted from the D&B database. The cover letter explained the objective of the study and the rules of confidentiality and anonymity observed by the researchers. Companies that did not respond within 2 weeks were sent a postcard as reminder, in order to improve the response rate.

## 5.2. Survey instruments and measures

1. User satisfaction was measured using the Doll and Torkzadeh [9] instrument of 12 items under five dimensions: content, accuracy, format, timeliness of information, and ease of use of systems.

2. Because it was consistent with our broad objectives of measuring a firm’s performance in multiple dimensions beyond financial aspects, and it had been empirically tested, the Tracey et al. [42] instrument was also adopted. In our study, it was modified to contain five items to capture perceptions of profitability, sales growth, customer satisfaction with products or services purchased, customer retention rate, and overall organizational performance.

3. The instrument used to measure the extent of BPI was based on that of Bhatt [1]; it consisted of five items to measure perceptions of: the prevention of errors and defects, process standards, the simplification of processes, and intra- and inter-firm co-ordination. The last two items were added in recognition of the importance of co-ordination across departmental and organizational boundaries.

4. The questionnaire contained two items on senior management support: one for the IT initiative (labelled ‘‘MSI’’) and the other for BPI (labelled ‘‘MSB’’).

All of these measures were collected using a fivepoint Likert scale, with ‘‘1’’ indicating ‘‘strongly disagree’’ and ‘‘5’’ indicating ‘‘strongly agree’’.

5. One item was solicited on the reporting relationship of the CIO. It was coded to show the CEO-IT distance variable (labelled CID), with ‘‘0’’ indicating direct reporting and ‘‘1’’ indirect reporting.

6. The strategic intent behind the adoption of ERP was determined from one question listing four options: ‘‘(1) cost reduction’’, ‘‘(2) service quality improvement’’, ‘‘(3) sales growth’’, and ‘‘(4) others, please specify’’. Respondents could enter more reasons in the free format of option 4. The responses were analysed and the sample firms were assigned to one of the four categories: 1, operational focus 2, strategic focus 3, dual focus or 4, undefined focus.

## 5.3. Validity guidelines

To provide face validity, the questionnaire was reviewed and pre-tested by six senior MIS practitioners to make sure that the questions were understandable and presented in a proper manner. Any comments were used to improve the wording and flow of the items in the questionnaire.

The questionnaires returned from the survey were screened for completeness. Data for each multi-item instrument were analysed for internal consistency. A Cronbach’s alpha coefficient equal to or exceeding 0.7 was considered acceptable [24].

## 5.4. Data analysis methods

Indices were computed for ERP user satisfaction (EUSI), the extent of business process improvement (BPII), and organizational performance (OPI) by taking the means of the items within the relevant constructs (see Table 3 for the definitions and descriptions of each of the variables.) A non-parametric correlation analysis was conducted to test the hypothesized relationships as defined in the conceptual framework. A series of Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed to determine whether there were differences in the subgroup means of the ERP user satisfaction indices, BPI indices, and organizational performance indices across the four categories of firms with different strategic intents.

## 6.3. The ERP practices of responding firms

In order to understand the general practices of ERP adoption, the survey included questions to elicit information about ERP selection criteria and measures taken by the responding firms to successfully implement ERP (see Table 5). The responses to these questions showed that there was agreement among the respondents (74%) about the mutual fit between the ERP package and business requirements as an important selection criterion. The respondents also thought it was important to have flexibility when configuring the ERP package (71.9% of respondents), see technical strength in the vendor (66.7% of respondents), and have consulting expertise available in the market (62.5% of respondents). Most of the respondents favored an ERP product that allowed easy initial set-up and the subsequent changes of configuration. Only 54.1% of the respondents considered ease-to-use of user interfaces to be an important selection criterion.

Most of the responding firms (69.8%) had engaged in business process change as a means of achieving ERP-business process fit. However, almost as many (67.7%) had made customizations or added bolt-on logic to their ERP systems. This may be a reflection of the fact that, while many firms believed in and engaged in business process adaptation, many also found that some ERP adaptation was unavoidable. In contrast, less respondents (50%) reported business process changes for in-house system development. Only 55% of the respondents reported the use of chief executivechaired steering committee and 51% the use of champions.

## 7. Discussions and implications

Generally speaking, the results indicated the positive associations of the perceptions of ERP success and BPI with perceived organizational performance. Equally important was the empirical evidence of the mutual influence between ERP success and the perceived extent of BPI. The results also empirically demonstrated that management support was significant.

The relationship between perceived senior management support of IT initiatives and ERP user satisfaction was not statistically supported. However, it must be noted that the association between these variables cannot be totally ignored as it was marginally significant $( p = 0 . 0 8 )$ .

To some extent, the findings on the CEO-IT relationship offer support for the general belief that the status of the CIO and the IT function is important to securing support from senior management in the context of Hong Kong and China. While only one of the four hypothesized relationships related to this variable was statistically supported, nevertheless its association with management support of BPI initiatives was marginally significant $( p = 0 . 0 5 7 )$ . These empirical findings hinted that the CEO-IT reporting distance is directly and negatively associated with the levels of management support for IT and BPI initiatives, but its correlation with the achievements in ERP implementation and BPI is indirect.

Our study empirically supported the relationship between the strategic intent behind the adoption of ERP systems and organizational performance. Though firms with an unclear focus fared poorly in comparison to other firms, only eight firms failed to define their ERP objectives. This implied that most of the firms were aware of their objectives when planning the adoption of ERP.

Firms with only an operational focus had a lower performance than those with a strategic or dual focus, though they obtained better performance than those with an undefined focus. It must be noted that more firms reported a dual focus than only a strategic focus. Their superior performance implied that it is important to combine focuses, rather than relying on one alone, in order to maximize gains in organizational performance.

## 7.1. Theoretical and managerial contribution

This study contributed by producing empirical evidence to support theories on the adoption of ERP and the relationships that exist among ERP success, BPI, and organizational performance. Its findings also highlighted the impact of certain variables at the strategic management level of a firm, as needed for the success of ERP adoption and BPI.

The findings clearly showed that setting clear objectives was vital to both project-level and organizational outcomes. Moreover, they demonstrated that firms adopting ERP would gain more benefits from using the system if their objectives were extended to cover both the operational and strategic aspects of the firm.

Another important result demonstrated the need for a closer reporting relationship between the CEO and the CIO/IT function by raising the organizational position of the CIO and his/her department.

## 7.2. Limitations

Although we took care in designing and conducting our study, it was not immune from potential bias. First, as it relies on perceptual inputs from the same respondents for all of the constructs, it could be subject to common source bias. Second, the use of user satisfaction as a surrogate of IS success must be viewed with caution. Though widely accepted as a surrogate measure, the user satisfaction construct has several weaknesses. It is criticised as theoretically weak by many [38]. Melone [32] pointed out that the user satisfaction construct is not standard within the research community, and therefore, the choice between several definitions and measures was primarily subjective. Moreover, she was sceptical of the validity of the oversimplified assumption that the effectiveness of a system could be reliably assessed by satisfied users alone. In recognition of such limitations, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution. We would like to stress that the findings on user satisfaction are only indicative of the associations between the respondents perceptions. Thus, it is less risky to restrict our interpretation to an understanding that the study has demonstrated that there are positive associations among respondents’ perceptions of the outcome of ERP adoption, BPI, and organizational performance in the research setting of this study. Another limitation of this study results from the relatively small cross-sectional sample (which contains only 96 firms operating in Hong Kong and China). It could support only the correlation rather than a full analysis, and produce findings of lower external validity.

## 8. Concluding remarks

This study confirmed relationships that exist among ERP success, BPI and organization performance, and gathered some empirical evidence on the effect of organizational variables, such as the relationships between senior management support and the extent of business process improvement, and between CEO-IT distance and senior management support for IT initiatives. It also demonstrated that system-level and organizational outcomes were subject to the influence of the firm’s strategic intent and objectives when adopting ERP systems. In summary, it contributed to research by producing empirical support for longespoused theories and principles. The findings imply that adopters of ERP must devote sufficient attention and effort in the planning, deployment, and management of ERP systems.
