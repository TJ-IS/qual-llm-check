# Conflicts between privacy and property: The discourse in personal and organizational knowledge

Alina Dulipovici, Richard Baskerville \*

Georgia State University, Department of Computer Information Systems, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta, GA 30302-4015, United States

Available online 2 July 2007

## Abstract

The paper critically examines knowledge ownership when personal or organizational knowledge is transferred between individuals or between individuals and organizations. Employing a form of discourse analysis, we analyse the data from three complementary perspectives (international treatie and conventions on privacy and intellectual property, employment and merchant account contracts, and verdicts from knowledge-related legal cases) to unveil the conflicts between privacy and property rights. The results show a dialectical view where ownership shifts between the individual and the orga nization/community and therefore, the individual may or may not necessarily own what he knows.

Privacy and property have been central issues in computational ethics for more than two decades. The use of information systems for strategic purposes ofers new challenges for these established ethical concepts. Knowledge is not only a form of property governed by intellectual property law; it is also an individual attribute and, as part of the personality, it may be governed by privacy law. As a result, the dialectical view also shows that the ownership shift between the individual and the organization/community means that the organization may or may not necessarily own what it knows. - 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge transfer; Strategic knowledge; Organizational knowledge; Personal knowledge; Privacy rights; Property rights

## 1. Introduction

Research and practice in the area of knowledge management has struggled with estab lishing many basic concepts about organizational learning, not least the definition of knowledge itself, and its distinction from information. These definitions and distinctions are often contentious and contextual (Baskerville and Dulipovici, 2006). Knowledge is a fluid mix of framed experience, values, contextual information, and expert insight that provides a framework for evaluating and incorporating new experiences and information. It originates and is applied in the minds of ‘‘knowers’’ (Davenport and Prusak, 1998). In organizations, it often becomes embedded not only in documents or repositories but also in organizational routines, processes, practices, and norms. In terms of organizational management of knowledge as a resource (organizational knowledge) the concept is extended to include ‘‘information embedded in routines and processes, which enable action’’. Knowledge is an innately human quality, residing in the living mind because a person must ‘‘identify, interpret and internalize knowledge’’ (Myers, 1996).

Information consists of ‘‘facts and data that are organized to describe a particular situation or condition’’. Knowledge is distinguished from information by the addition of ‘‘truths, beliefs, perspectives and concepts, judgments and expectations, methodologies and know-how’’ (Wiig, 1993, p. xvi). Nevertheless knowledge can also become information once it is codified in symbolic forms such as text, charts, or images, etc. (Alavi and Leidner, 2001).

Both knowledge and its management are fundamental, strategic resources of firms. According to knowledge-based strategy, tangible resources are a function of the firm’s know-how (Eisenhardt and Santos, 2002). Consequently, to build and sustain knowledge-based resources, firms must have both knowledge-creating and knowledge-application processes. In this context information technologies such as intranets, extranets, electronic forums, chat rooms, data warehouses, data mining techniques, or software agents may become strategic knowledge management systems supporting one or both of these two processes (Alavi and Leidner, 2001).

These are not novel ideas in the knowledge management literature. Several researchers have already examined successful knowledge management practices (Davenport and Prusak, 1998; Davenport et al., 1998), but few have looked beyond the success stories at the ethical implications of knowledge management practices (Alter, 2006; Baskerville and Dulipovici, 2006). For example, knowledge is often divided into tacit and articulated knowledge (Hedlund, 1994; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). Tacit knowledge generally exists in an individual’s mind, and converted to articulated knowledge when made explicit through speaking or writing (Polanyi, 1967). Potential problems arise when knowledge is transferred from an individual/organization to another individual/organization as such transfer requires a conversion of tacit knowledge to explicit knowledge or a direct transfer of tacit knowledge (Baskerville and Dulipovici, 2006). As possession of knowledge shifts between the individual and a community (e.g. an organization), clarity falls about the rights of the individual who shared the knowledge. To what degree can individuals be obligated to share their knowledge? Once shared, is it owned by the individual or the organization? Under which legal frameworks is such knowledge protected?

Much of the knowledge management literature operates on the assumption that knowledge is a type of goods, and subject to laws about property. Indeed there is extensive regulation of this kind of property, intellectual property, with international conventions and national laws that define many aspects of ownership and the transfer of this ownership. Intellectual property is usually regarded as trade secrets, copyrights and patents. It is a major source of sustainable competitive advantage, especially where this property is dificult to imitate. To the degree that knowledge embodies this intellectual property, it makes competitive advantage sustainable when it is easily transferable and useable inside the firm, but not easily recreated or transferred outside the firm (Teece, 2000).

Even under this property rights assumption, the legal framework of intellectual property does not completely clarify rights to knowledge. Although a knowledge transfer may take the form of a formal trade in which one party agrees to share his knowledge in exchange for compensation (e.g. job, service, product, etc.), the issues of ownership and legal protection still exist. Does transferred knowledge belong to the knowledge-receiver (e.g. individual or organization)? Does transferred knowledge belong to the knowledge-source (e.g. individ ual or organization)? Is either of these two positions ethically better?

Perhaps this confusion arises because knowledge is not only a type of property to be possessed and transferred. As knowledge is an innately human quality, it is also an attribute that can partly define and distinguish one person from another. People respond in various ways when asked, ‘‘Who are you?’’ Some may respond with their origins. (‘‘I am a Londoner.’’ ‘‘I am a New Yorker.’’) Some may respond with their pastimes. (‘‘I am a coin collector.’’ ‘‘I am a trekkie.’’) Some respond with their vocations. (‘‘I am a professor.’’ ‘‘I am a lawyer.’’) These responses convey not only where people originated or what people do, but also the knowledge of the individual brought by their experience and training. To a degree, knowledge is not only a property that people own, it is an attri bute that helps distinguish who people are.

Realization that a person’s individuality is defined to a significant degree by their knowledge implies that knowledge becomes subject to privacy considerations. Privacy involves individual rights against government interference with their personal decisions, against illegal searches by government agencies or private parties, intrusion on seclusion, unwanted spying, etc. The tort of ‘‘invasion of privacy’’ involves the exposure of damaging information regarding the individual (Volokh, 2000). More fundamentally, privacy regards the rights of individuals against ‘‘the demands of a curious and intrusive society’’ (Post, 1989, p. 958). It arises in settings where an individual is in the presence of a community and is a key right of the individual to exercise autonomy and thereby demonstrate human dignity. Privacy is the right to a certain degree of personal immunity, the right to an individual personality. Individual privacy and community are interdependent rather than oppositions, because the norms of the community make possible for individuals to exercise their autonomy (Post, 1989).

It is easy to understand why commercial organizations might gravitate to regard knowledge as intellectual property rather than a personal attribute. The ethics of property ownership and transfer are more familiar in business. But because corporations are becoming increasingly important in multinational politics, expectations have formed that human rights will be respected by these companies. Commerce has growing responsibility for human rights at least within the boundaries of business (Wah, 1998). Knowledge is both a possession that can be shared and an attribute that defines an individual, leaving business and other organizations with a conflicting set of ethical guidelines: Is knowledge management to be governed by the ethics of property ownership or privacy? If privacy ethics prevent an organization from ‘‘owning’’ what it knows, the strategic value of its intellectual property is seriously diminished. Yet ethics may compel this understanding. Mason’s conclusion from 1986 still applies to the current issues if the word ‘information’ is replaced with ‘knowledge’: ‘‘the clear moral imperative is that information technology, and the information it handles, must be employed to enhance the dignity of human society’ (Mason, 1986, p. 5).

This study seeks to challenge the traditional assumption that knowledge is entirely subject to the ethics of property ownership. We will build on three discourses in the literature: knowledge management, privacy, and intellectual property, in order to examine the ethics and trading practices of the diferent types of knowledge. The practical implications of these discourses help to understand the ethical dialectics that lead to situations where the knowledge possessor does not necessarily own what she or he knows, yet has privacy rights that protect against unethical intrusions into her or his personality. Does the individual possess the right to retain knowledge, to refuse to share knowledge, and is it ethical for organizations to impose sanctions on individuals who choose to retain, rather than share, certain forms of knowledge?

If knowledge is both intellectual property and an individual attribute, the strategic management of this knowledge becomes embroiled in the property rights of the organization and the human rights of the organization’s individuals. It involves negotiating the classic privacy tension between the individual and the community. The organization does not exactly own what it knows. Its strategic intellectual property is more essentially embedded in the interdependence of the organization and its members, rather than distinct from one or both. Organizational governance intended to protect the integrity, confidentiality, and availability of knowledge would have to operate in keeping with the norms established by the social interactions within the organization. In other words, the security of this strategic asset is dependent on both the organization as a community, and the people in the organization as distinct and unique personalities.

We explore the discourse reflected in international frameworks, legal cases, and contracts, using a form of discourse analysis that helps build an understanding of the ways of reasoning that become ordered in the social world (Alvesson and Karreman, 2000). With respect to the contracts and the legal cases selected, the goal is to achieve a better understanding. Consequently, the selection is primarily based on theoretical usefulness rather than selected randomly or for their representativeness (Eisenhardt, 1989).

In this sense, our inquiry will be based on three complementary perspectives: international treaties and conventions on privacy and intellectual property, employment and merchant account contracts, and verdicts from knowledge-related legal cases (Fig. 1). Regarding employment and merchant account contracts, we will only examine knowledge-related clauses from these contracts.

The remainder of this research is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the theoretical background, specifically the two main forms of knowledge (personal and organizational), the privacy rights and the intellectual property rights. Based on this, we develop a framework for analysis in which each form of knowledge is analysed both as property and as an individual’s attribute. The framework is then used to examine employment contracts, merchant account contracts, and verdicts from US legal cases. The last two sections discuss the results of the multi-perspective analysis and present the conclusions and potential avenues for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Forms of knowledge: personal and organizational

The knowledge management literature distinguishes several forms of knowledge based on how knowledge is defined: personalized information, a state of mind, an object, a process, a conditional access to information, or a capability (Alavi and Leidner, 2001). Nevertheless all these definitions can be grouped into two categories: individual-level definitions and organizational-level definitions. At the individual level, knowledge is defined as personalized information, as the individual’s state of knowing and understanding, or as the necessary condition to access and retrieve more information. From this perspective knowledge is a human attribute residing in the mind of a person who must ‘‘identify, interpret and internalize knowledge’’ (Myers, 1996, p. 2).

The personal knowledge is thus a mix of experience, values, and contextual information applied through the minds of ‘‘knowers’’ (Davenport et al., 1998, p. 5) in order to evaluate and incorporate new knowledge.

At the organizational level, knowledge may be an object stored in a knowledge base, a process for creation and dissemination, or a capability for building and sustaining corecompetencies (Alavi and Leidner, 2001). By definition, organizational knowledge is embedded in organizational processes, procedures, technologies (e.g. Artificial Intelligence Systems), routines, and structures (Teece, 2000). Note that we adopt Teece’s definition of organizational knowledge because it encompasses both the tacit and articulated dimensions thus, avoiding the unnecessary limitations imposed by Grifith et al.’s (2003) definition.

In fact, both personal and organizational knowledge may be characterized in terms of tacit or articulated knowledge (Hedlund, 1994; Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). As introduced earlier, tacit knowledge is non-verbalized and intuitive and becomes articulated knowledge when spoken or written (Polanyi, 1967). For example, a professor’s knowl edge on how to get reimbursed for travel expenses to conferences represents tacit organizational knowledge. Sometimes, such knowledge is explicitly written down and hence, it becomes articulated organizational knowledge (Table 1). Knowing how to dress at different professional meetings or how to sustain healthy and productive collaborations is the individual’s mix of intuition, experience, and contextual information. Similarly, when such knowledge is verbalized or written, it becomes articulated personal knowl edge (Table 1).

In practice these definitions are rather fuzzy. Depending on the perspective adopted, the same ‘knowledge’ may be either tacit or articulated and either personal or organizational (Hedlund, 1994). Additionally, distinguishing between articulated knowledge and information is not obvious (Wilson, 2002) because parts of the articulated knowledge may become information once such knowledge is codified in symbolic forms such as text, charts, or images (Alavi and Leidner, 2001). For example, Allen Lee transformed his tacit knowledge on reviewing research manuscripts into articulated knowledge and shared it with fellow researchers by publishing an invited note in the Journal of operations management (e.g. Lee, 1995). While the paper embodies his knowledge on this topic, his 15 suggestions can be seen as ‘information’, which is assimilated and incorporated into other researchers’ knowledge structures. Furthermore, tacit knowledge is incorrectly used sometimes as synonymous with personal knowledge because personal knowledge is often under a tacit form (Hedlund, 1994; Polanyi, 1967).

Given these ambiguities, this study examines how individuals manage organizational knowledge and how organizations manage personal knowledge when the two parties consent to trade their knowledge. Recent government and corporate scandals proved that this less apparent side of knowledge management systems is even more salient as it raises issues related to both privacy and property rights.

## 2.2. Privacy rights

Human rights to privacy are defined in various conventions and laws and they are focused on protecting against government and individual intrusions including unwarranted searches, unwanted spying, etc. (Volokh, 2000). However, these rights frameworks have not yet been developed for application to the privacy of human knowledge. The development of the application of these frameworks would involve a form of ethical reasoning known as consequentialism (Getz, 1991). This development would usually involve the application of the principle of utility, which establishes the ethical preference for an action according to the balance of the outcome in terms of achieving the greatest happiness over least unhappiness. Consequentialism is a form of ethical cost-benefit analysis to determine whether a human action is right or wrong. An alternative form of ethical reasoning is deontology which determines whether human actions are right or wrong according to a system of rights and duties that defines rules and principles to guide actions. It can involve rationally formulated principles by a society to govern the creation of rules, constitutions, etc. (Getz, 1991).

To compare the frameworks for privacy and intellectual property, given that there are well formulated principles for intellectual property (deontology), an ideal comparison framework for knowledge privacy should be equally well developed (also deontology). Our alternative choices would be to approach the development of the privacy framework using consequentialism, but this would provide a less suitable comparative framework for prop erty (a comparison of consequentialist reasoning with deontological reasoning). However, data privacy represents a suficiently similar application of privacy principles to knowledge, in that the frameworks apply privacy to information (rather than knowledge). Adopting the data privacy framework permits us to frame our comparison of knowledge privacy with knowledge property using a deontological framework for both areas.

Possessed by any individual, human rights regard life, freedom, and human dignity. In 1948, the UN Declaration of Human Rights established a list of human rights that are universal in content and application. They are not legally binding, but they constitute the driving force behind several human rights legislations, charters, and conventions world wide, including the US.

Among these rights, privacy and data protection are particularly relevant to our study. ‘‘Privacy is a term with multiple meanings. However, in the context of automated recordkeeping systems, which contain personal data about individuals, ‘privacy’ refers to the rights of individuals regarding the collection, storage, processing, circulation, and use of personal information about themselves’’ (Turn, 1982, p. 86). In short, privacy defines the freedom from the intrusion of others into one’s private life. For example, collecting data from several sources resulting in a profile one’s lifestyle represents an invasion of privacy. Such practices are troubling because the knowledge may be used in intrusive ways. Other issues related to privacy are suppression, distortion, and misappropriation (Alter, 2006).

Given the sensitive nature of privacy issues, there are three main organizations that establish guidelines to be followed by the corresponding authorities in each country. These organizations are the United Nations (UN), the Council of Europe, and the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). The guidelines set by the UN are general and widely-applicable, whereas the two other organizations are more proactive with respect to the issues tackled: the Council of Europe, the pioneer in many of these topics, developed the first instrument to protect electronic personal data, and the OECD developed guidelines on issues such as transborder data flows, and security of information systems and networks. For each of the three organizations, Table 2 summarizes the conventions most relevant to knowledge management.

While some countries have omnibus privacy laws, in others, such as the US, privacy rights are variously defined in case law and in laws specific to certain areas (e.g. education, health, banking, etc.). For example, the US aligns its laws with the main guidelines set by the UN, the Council of Europe, and the OECD although there is a more limited protection for a ‘‘right to privacy’’ (Volokh, 2000) and this protection may vary from state to state. Out of consistency concerns, this study uses the definitions proposed by the three interna tional treaties.

## 3. Forms of knowledge and legal rights

None of the privacy or intellectual property rights explicitly considers the management of personal and organizational knowledge. However, we can analyse these issues with the help of the knowledge management literature.

## 3.1. Organizational knowledge as property

Embedded in processes, procedures, technologies, and structures, organizational knowledge is generally viewed as organizational property. Hence, it falls under the protection of intellectual property rights, which restrict access and use of the property, for example, through patents and copyrights. Transfer of articulated organizational knowledge is thus, unambiguously protected by WIPO. This argument is also supported by KPMG’s definition of intellectual property, which is ‘‘not just patents, trade marks, copyrights, database rights, and other ‘pure’ IP, but other forms of articulated knowledge, such as business processes, methodologies, and know how’’ (KPMG, 2002).

## 3.2. Organizational knowledge as an individual’s attribute

If articulated organizational knowledge belongs to the organization, does it imply that the tacit form is an individual’s attribute? For example, knowledge about organizational routines and procedures, products, customers, suppliers, and competitors is ‘‘trapped inside the minds of key-employees’’ (Teece, 2000, p. 38). However, such knowledge has limited commercial value if not embedded in a specific context where it can become a competitive advantage (Teece, 2000). For this reason, organizations depend on employees behaviour to create and transfer knowledge (Bock et al., 2005) and the line between taci organizational knowledge and tacit personal knowledge becomes rather fuzzy.

Tacit organizational knowledge can still be bought or sold at the organizational level, albeit not always ethically, by transferring groups of individuals (e.g. alliances, joint ventures, mergers, and acquisitions of business units). In this case, employees, as part of a ‘‘collection of people’’, earn protection under the human rights frameworks based on their employment contracts. At the individual level, employment contracts are also an impediment to transfer tacit organizational knowledge by changing jobs under the protection of trade secrets laws.

## 3.3. Personal knowledge as an individual’s attribute

As an attribute of an individual, personal knowledge is similar to personal data. For example, personal knowledge, either tacit or articulated, may be a source of income because individuals are generally employed on the basis of their skills and expertise. Personal knowledge may also bring respect and prestige in communities, especially if this knowledge is unique and relevant to the community. Transfer of personal knowledge diminishes the individual because it may have a negative impact on his or her professional value (Wasko and Faraj, 2005) or on the unique character of that knowledge.

In this sense, disclosure of personal knowledge resembles disclosure of personal data and therefore privacy rights could also protect personal knowledge. To our knowledge, however, there are currently no human rights conventions regarding personal knowledge, such as those concluded for private data. If privacy rights were extended to include personal knowledge, then ethical restrictions would inevitably be imposed on companies wishing to transfer personal knowledge from one individual to another. Furthermore, according to privacy rights, transferring personal knowledge without the permission of the knowledge-source would be forbidden. For now, it is not clear if personal knowledge can be ethically treated as a commodity to be bought, sold, or even owned, unless the individual chooses to trade his personal knowledge in exchange for a service or a product as stipulated, for example, in the merchant account contracts.

## 3.4. Personal knowledge as organizational ‘‘public good’’

Knowledge management systems, such as knowledge bases or electronic forums, allow individuals to disseminate personal knowledge. The essential paradox of knowledge is that its dissemination can be both beneficial and detrimental (Trauth, 1999; Wasko and Faraj, 2005). From the perspective of actor-network theory, when knowledge is shared it becomes a source of knowledge for others who may build on it to create new knowledge (Latour, 1987). Additionally, because personal knowledge is rooted in the individual’s experience and values, sharing such knowledge among individuals produces an interesting situation where each individual possesses unique and discrete forms of knowledge (Foucault, 1971). With each transfer, personal knowledge takes on new forms and meanings.

On the other hand, sharing personal knowledge may be detrimental, because personal knowledge could be used to create intellectual property (for example, to write a book or develop a business case) and sharing this kind of knowledge may trigger a diminishment of personal value. Not surprisingly, a cautious employee might withhold his personal knowledge in the interest of maintaining his job (Trauth, 1999). This behaviour could be motivated by self-interest (Wang, 2004) or by an organizational culture that does not encourage reciprocity in knowledge sharing (Constant et al., 1994).

This ‘‘knowledge paradox’’ is similar to other social dilemmas (Cabrera and Cabrera, 2002), which are ‘‘situations in which individual rationality (i.e. maximize individual pay-of) leads to collective irrationality’’ (Kollock, 1998, p.183). The well-known metaphors of the prisoner’s dilemma or the problem of providing public goods, apply to sharing personal knowledge as well. In the first case, two ‘‘prisoners’’ are separately given the choice to share their knowledge. In the latter, which is an extension to N-persons dilemmas, the individual incurs an immediate cost due to sharing its knowledge, but such behaviour creates a benefit to the community. The individual may avoid the cost by not sharing his or her personal knowledge, but if everyone does so, everyone pays the price (assuming there is no incentive or community-defined strategy for avoiding free-riding).

Particularly interesting for us is this second social dilemma. If personal knowledge is considered a resource from which everyone in the community (e.g. organization, workteam, community of practice, society in general, etc.) benefits, then personal knowledge is a public good (Kollock, 1998). Moreover, personal knowledge could be a ‘pure’ public good because it is both non-excludable (i.e. one may use knowledge objects without contributing to the knowledge base) and non-rival (i.e. using a knowledge base does not diminish its availability to others, albeit one can argue that infrastructure limitations may diminish availability to everybody).

In terms of legal protection, obviously, once the personal knowledge becomes a public good it does not belong to the knowledge-source anymore, but to the community. Hence, neither intellectual rights nor privacy rights protect such knowledge.

## 4. Data analysis

The previous sections showed that intellectual property rights are unambiguously defined by the WIPO and they clearly apply to organizational knowledge treated as an organizational property. Some ambiguity exists if organizational knowledge is interpreted as an individual’s attribute. Thus, human rights apply for the ‘‘collection of people’’ while clauses in the employment contract apply at the individual level. With respect to personal knowledge, the existing guidelines refer to personal data and therefore it is more dificult to identify their applicability. When considered an individual’s attribute, personal knowl edge should be protected by privacy rights and by relevant clauses in merchant account contracts. If treated as a public good, the community owns the knowledge and neither pri vacy nor intellectual property applies (Table 3).

Building on the theoretical foundations summarized in Table 3, this section examines a sample of employment contracts, merchant account contracts, and legal cases. Employ ment contracts are agreements between an employer and an employee describing the nat ure of their business relationship such as the compensation received by the employee and the fringe benefit package. Particular salient to our study are the non-disclosure, non-competition, and the conflict of interest clauses of the employment contract. The goal of such clauses is to protect trade secrets and know-how from being (consciously or uncon sciously) disseminated by the employee.

Regarding privacy rights, they are globally defined by international organizations (e.g. UN, Council of Europe, and OECD) and are generally incorporated in merchant account contracts. Merchant account contracts are agreements between a consumer and a merchant stipulating the terms and conditions of the consumer’s usage under which the merchant agrees to provide the product or service. For example, in order to buy a book on Amazon’s website, the buyer needs to create an account and to accept Amazon’s terms and conditions related to that account. By doing so, the buyer gets the right to buy the book, while Amazon gets access to the buyer’s personal data and reading preferences.

Following the logic in Table 3, we first analyse employment contracts from six compa nies, then merchant account contracts from seven companies, and lastly eleven legal cases that exemplify all four cells from Table 3. The sampling strategy was theory-driven (Miles and Huberman, 1994) and the selection of companies (for employment contracts and merchant contracts) and of legal cases was made on conceptual grounds (i.e. Table 3), not based on representativeness or randomness (Eisenhardt, 1989). This strategy is often applied in qualitative studies with a small sample size (e.g. Cousins and Robey, 2005; Maznevski and Chudoba, 2000; Ross and Chiasson, 2005). ## 4.1. Employment contracts

New employees are almost always required to formally sign an employment contract. Legally, there is no obligation for the employee, but a refusal might cost him the job. Moreover, if the employee starts working under the terms and conditions set out in the contract, it is assumed that he accepted the contract despite the lack of a signature. Employees having responsibilities related to the company’s core-competencies may be asked to sign three clauses that are directly related to the employee’s knowledge and constitute the focus of our enquiry. Non-disclosure agreements prevent the employee from using or disclosing organizational knowledge. In the non-compete agreement the employee agrees to refrain from competing in exchange for a job, a promotion, or a bonus. Thirdly, the conflict of interest defines situations that could create diferent business interests for the employee and that could compromise his loyalty to the employer.

For our sample we targeted engineering positions because, by the nature of their role, engineers possess and share both personal knowledge and organizational knowledge. Hence they are generally asked to formally sign some or all of the clauses previously mentioned. In this sense, one of the authors contacted the Human Resources department from about 20 North American IT companies (small and large companies) asking for blank copies of the agreements. Table 4 briefly describes the companies that agreed to provide such documents. The most common refusal invoked was the confidentiality of these documents, even if they were blank.

In certain industries (e.g. information technology, telecommunication, or R&D), noncompete agreements are extremely common. Nevertheless, there are no legal frameworks and they are generally interpreted according to the ‘‘rule of reason’’. This means that they must be reasonable in terms of what an employee can and cannot do. This characteristic transpired in our sample through the diversity of the requirements (see Appendix A). Not even the period of time during which a former employee cannot exercise a similar role in a diferent organization made unanimity: it varied between 6 months and 5 years! Additionally former employees cannot solicit the company’s clients and employees.

The non-compete agreements are often highly favourable to the employer and impede former employees from using the knowledge acquired during the employment. In case of a disagreement, it is generally a judge who decides what ‘‘reasonable’’ means. Many US states apply the so-called ‘‘Blue Pencil Rule’’, which means that if the court finds the agreement too restrictive or unreasonable it has the right to modify it. For example, 5 years might be struck down by the court as unreasonable, but this will only be determined if the employee sues the employer and brings the matter into a court of law. Therefore there is a clear need for non-compete agreements to be better regulated.

With respect to the non-disclosure agreements, none of the companies mention ‘knowledge’ and they refer to it indirectly as ‘information’ (see Appendix A). For NetCo1 it is ‘‘any specification concerning .products and computer programs either developed or being developed; any system configuration or architecture; any information pertaining to client, marketing strategy or development of the company; any information which is not generally publicly known concerning business, afairs or plans of the company, its suppliers or customers; generally, any trade secret or confidential information’’. NetCo2 adds that such ‘information’ may be disclosed to the employee ‘‘verbally or in written, graphic, tangible or intangible form’’, while CompCo1 states that ‘‘confidential information means any information, technical data, or know-how’’. Clearly, both tacit and articulated organizational knowledge are encompassed by these definitions.

CompCo1 goes even further by emphasizing what ‘confidential information’ does not include: information that ‘‘(i) is in the possession of the [employee] without obligation of confidentiality at the time of disclosure; (ii) prior to or after the time of disclosure becomes part of the: public domain without the act or omission of the [employee]; (iii) is disclosed to the [employee] by a third party under no legal obligation to maintain the confidentiality of such information; or (iv) was independently developed by the [employee]’’. This last case is surprising because, as we discuss later in the Evan Brown case, it is dificult to prove that some parts of the employee’s tacit knowledge are exclusively developed independently. It is clear though that the employer is the sole exclusive owner of the employee’s tacit and articulated, personal and organizational knowledge developed during the employment. Moreover, employees are required to return ‘information’ at the end of their employment and, in CompCo1’s case, to give a 10-day notice prior to disclosure! However, few employees have the time to transfer their tacit knowledge orally or in writing before their departure.

Finally, one of the selected companies required employees to sign a conflict of interest policy as well. HiTechCo1’s conflict of interest policy appears redundant because it mainly restates clauses already included in the non-compete and non-disclosure agreements, such as disclosure of confidential information or performing services for a competitor.

In summary, these agreements ofer the employer protection against potential leaks of confidential knowledge. On the other side, employees obtain jobs or promotions but lose the ownership of any invention or discovery. Interestingly, each company has its own definition of ‘confidential information’ and these definitions allude to both tacit and articulated organizational knowledge. Hence, an important issue arises as tacit organizational knowledge is incorporated into the individual’s experience and becomes personal knowl edge (Berger and Luckmann, 1967; Latour, 1987). It is dificult to imagine how employees can return the tacit organizational knowledge upon termination of employment or how they avoid work in the same area for five years. Such discrepancies reiterate the need for standard frameworks for knowledge-related agreements.

## 4.2. Merchant account contracts

Merchant account contracts ofer a completely diferent perspective of knowledge trading activities. As agreements between consumers and merchants, their focus is on personal knowledge rather than on organizational knowledge. Nonetheless, similarly to employment contracts, there is no standard framework and they vary from state to state and from country to country.

For our sample we targeted large companies operating in diferent sectors (i.e. especially sensitive sectors such as pharmacy and banking) and in diferent US states or countries because, given their multi-market operations, their privacy policies should align with the standards proposed by the UN, the Council of Europe, or the OECD. The data was collected from the company’s website or from the notice of privacy practices distributed to customers. The selection of seven companies (see Table 5) is based on theoretical usefulness (Eisenhardt, 1989) as well as on theoretical saturation (Miles and Huberman, 1994). Although around 20 companies were examined, the same information was repeated again and again.

The analysis focussed on what companies and consumers gain from trading the consumer’s personal knowledge (see Appendix B). What companies look for is the right to collect, to store, and to analyse personal information such as name, address, and purchase behaviour for profiling and direct marketing purposes. In addition, online merchants get access to credit history information and can study online behaviour based on data collected from servers and user cookies. On the other hand, consumers get free products or discounts, personalized ofers, as well as the right to buy the merchant’s products and services. The downside is that personal information is shared with afiliates and subsidiaries and the consumer loses control over his or her information. Few companies ask for their customers’ permission to sell or to share their personal information to third parties (e.g. eBay and T.J.Maxx for California residents). Generally, such an option is checked by default and it’s up to the individual to uncheck it, to send an opt-out email (e.g. Amazon, eBay, and PayPal), or to call the customer service (e.g. BellSouth). Oficially, most of the companies do not share collected data with third parties but they do not clearly define ‘‘third party’’. For example, BellSouth shares information with ‘‘current or future, direct or indirect subsidiaries’’.

## 4.3. Legal cases

While employment contracts and merchant account contracts give a prescriptive view of the knowledge trade, legal cases ofer a pragmatic view as well as an interpretation of the legal guidelines. For the data collection we draw from InternetCases.com, a weblog launched in 2005 and maintained by a Chicago attorney who writes about cases on issues related to Internet and technologies. We preferred this source to other more established sources, such as LexisNexis, because the cases are already filtered and only the most interesting (for the peculiarity of the case or of the verdict) are presented. Among all these cases, eleven tapped issues related to privacy and property rights and hence they all became part of our sample (see Appendix C).

These cases illustrate all forms of knowledge discussed earlier: organizational knowledge as property (case 5, case 9), organizational knowledge as an individual’s attribute (case 6, case 7, case 8, case 10, case 11), personal knowledge as an individual’s attribute (case 2, case 3, case 4), and personal knowledge as a public good (case 1). Two consequences emerge from these cases. First, the defendant and the plaintif do not always have the same interpretation of the form of knowledge. For example, the plaintif (e.g. case 7 – Alcatel) considered the defendant’s ideas to be organizational knowledge while the defendant saw it as personal knowledge. This result is consistent with our earlier observation on the ambiguities of the definition of knowledge. Second, the myriad of treaties and conventions on intellectual property creates confusion. For example, given the extent of the similarities between the plaintif’s website and the defendant’s website (case 6), the plaintif might have had a more successful outcome had it sued for copyright infringement rather than for violation of trade secrets.

It can be noticed that there is no clear pattern ‘‘privacy/property vs. personal/organi zational knowledge’’. The verdicts are case by case, greatly depending on the particular situation of each case. Violations of the non-compete and non-disclosure agreements (case 7, case 10, and case 11) raise two important issues. First, as these agreements seek to restrain employees from potentially transferring organizational knowledge to competitors, the line between tacit organizational knowledge and tacit personal knowledge becomes extremely dim. ## 5. Discussion

Knowledge and its management are fundamental, strategic resources of the firm. When knowledge is transferred between the individual and the community (e.g. an organization), privacy and intellectual property issues emerge. On the one hand, it is dificult to distin guish articulated knowledge from information and personal knowledge from organizational tacit knowledge. On the other hand, the knowledge management literature operates under the assumption that knowledge, similar to an object, is subject to property laws. Using a multi-perspective analysis, this study sheds some light on the discourse on knowledge ownership from both the normative stance and the praxis stance.

Regarding privacy rights, organizations such as the UN, the Council of Europe, and the OECD recommend respect and protection of one’s privacy if the knowledge is not of public interest. The verdicts rendered in legal cases are consistent with these rights (e.g. case 1 and case 4). Furthermore, the three organizations established guidelines for com puterized personal data files and for automatic processing of personal data that require merchants to protect access to personal data, conduct regular checks of accuracy and rel evance of collected data, and restrict collection and processing of personal data to the specified purpose. Consistent with the normative discourse, the merchant account contracts state the purpose of the data collection and the companies who have access to the collected data. However, the terms used in these contracts are sometimes ambiguous, leaving room for broad interpretations. Given that the international guidelines do no explicitly address either ‘‘personal knowledge’’ or ‘‘ownership’’, merchants collect personal knowledge and assume its ownership and control. On the other side, individuals receive some compensation for sharing their data such as getting the right to buy a product or a service. However, the right to privacy is not completely ‘sold’ as merchants’ need to protect against knowledge leaks to third parties not mentioned in the merchant account contract (e.g. case 2 and case 3). Hence, the praxis discourse is generally consis tent with the normative discourse albeit merchants try to capitalize on ambiguous contractual terms and definitions.

With respect to the intellectual property rights, articulated organizational knowledge is clearly protected by the twenty-three treaties administered by WIPO. For protection of ‘information’ that corresponds in fact to tacit organizational knowledge, employees are generally asked to sign non-compete and non-disclosure agreements. Alcatel (case 7), Microsoft (case 10), and Motorola (case 11) invoked these agreements given concern of a potential leak of tacit organizational knowledge to competitors. In Alcatel’s case, Evan Brown asked for a release in order to develop his ideas for a new algorithm (Lehrer Goldman, 2002), whereas in the two other cases, former key-employees wanted to work for competitors. We can infer that employers tend to extend WIPO’s normative discourse to both tacit and articulated knowledge, and personal and organizational knowledge. This is also possible given the lack of clear definitions and specific legal frameworks.

To summarize the lessons learned, we used the framework developed for the data analysis (Table 7). First, organizational knowledge, tacit or explicit, is considered intellectual property and belongs to the organization. WIPO treaties protect the organization’s right to own and to use its intellectual property. However, if the organizational knowledge is in tacit form, it’s the organization’s responsibility to manage and to protect this knowledge appropriately (e.g. non-compete agreements, non-disclosure agreements; conflict of interest policy; copyrights; patents, etc.). Two important consequences result: (a) the individual may acquire ownership if the organization does not possess a formal form of exclusive rights or if it does not maintain the ‘secrecy’; (b) the organizational knowledge is interpreted as personal knowledge.

Personal knowledge is generally protected by privacy rights. The individual has ownership of its personal knowledge and transfer of ownership through merchant account contracts does not apply to third parties. The merchant acquires ‘limited’ ownership of its customers personal knowledge. Lastly, personal knowledge is treated as property and becomes a public good if a higher authority estimates that its disclosure is in the public interest.

Comparing and contrasting the cases from multiple perspectives sheds some light on the issue pertaining to knowledge ownership but an even more interesting result is the conflicting treatment of personal knowledge. Our results showed a dialectical view where the individual loses ownership of his own knowledge raising potential ethical conflict settings (Fig. 2).

This study’s results have important implications regarding the ethical use of strategic information systems. For example, in the absence of specific rules and guidelines for business behaviour on which perceptions of an equitable knowledge transfer can be based (Marshall, 1999), cost-oriented consensus has developed especially among merchants and among employers. Being first to analyse your customers’ personal knowledge or to use your employees’ ideas to develop new products does not guarantee supremacy in the market but it can generate a competitive advantage.

Social consensus takes longer to develop because it needs a critical level to generate controversy and social attention (Marshall, 1999). However, if a sense of mutual trust and equitability is not felt, social consensus could deteriorate the efective sharing of knowledge (Adler, 2001).

Another ethical implication concerns the pervasiveness of information systems. Because knowledge transfer is a strategic use of information systems (Wiseman, 1988), it does not necessarily imply that collecting more knowledge leads to better strategic decisions. This study showed that each case of knowledge transfer has its particularities. What may be fair trade in one case may harm individuals, restrain their rights, restrain trade, or create unfair competitive advantage in other cases (Marshall, 1999).

Furthermore the conflicting treatment of personal knowledge hinders monitoring and control of organizational and individual use of information systems. Individuals and businesses alike need to be able to determine the conditions under which customer or employee knowledge is private, organizational, or a public good. In short, they must determine what falls under corporate responsibility and where the role of the individual begins. Conse quentialist ethical reasoning could imply a solution such as granting all ownership rights to individuals. Deontological ethical reasoning could develop codes of practice that iden tify each possible case and its way to handle knowledge. While objections can be formu lated for any form of ethical reasoning (Walsham, 1996), this study shows that further debate, involving both academia and practice, is needed.

## 6. Conclusion

The purpose of this paper is to critically examine ethics related to strategic information systems, namely the conflicts between privacy and property when transferring personal and organizational knowledge. Well-recognized international organizations developed frameworks for both privacy rights and intellectual property rights but these frameworks do not explicitly address the issues raised by transferring knowledge rather than transferring data or information.

Based on three complementary perspectives (i.e. international treaties and conventions on privacy and intellectual property, employment and merchant account contracts, and verdicts from knowledge-related legal cases), we found a dialectical view, which contradicts the assumption that you own what you know. On the one hand, the individual owns his personal knowledge in accordance with privacy rights and he also owns organizational knowledge that is insuficiently protected by employers through means stipulated by the intellectual property rights. On the other hand, the individual loses ownership of knowledge when he accepts employment contracts (organizational & personal knowledge) and merchant account contracts (personal knowledge). Additionally, the individual loses ownership of knowledge (organizational & personal) when such knowledge becomes a public good.

These situations are morally defined and culturally dependent. In such a context it is impossible to claim which position is better (i.e. knowledge belonging to the individual vs. knowledge belonging to the organization). In practice, these positions may easily derail on either side. Individuals may choose not to share their knowledge to protect their professional status while organizations may implement knowledge management systems with a very limited ‘‘zone of discretion’’ (Barnard, 1938) within which system use is based on coercion rather than motivation. Hence, both individuals and organizations ought to ensure that information systems supporting knowledge management have the necessary features to protect knowledge as property and knowledge as personal attributes. Both kinds of features are compelled by the need ‘‘to enhance the dignity of human society’’ (Mason, 1986, p.5).
