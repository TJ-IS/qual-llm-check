# Generalizability of Information Systems Research Using Student Subjects—A Reflection on Our Practices and Recommendations for Future Research

This debate appears in several articles that have been published on the subject as well as in the review process. In this latter arena, however, the debate has become increasingly like a script—the actors (authors and reviewers) simply read their parts of the script; some avoid the underlying issues whereas others cursorily address generalizability without real consideration of those issues. As a result, despite the extent of debate, we seem no closer to a resolution. Authors who use student subjects rely on their scripted arguments to justify the use of student subjects and do not always consider whether those arguments are valid. But reviewers who oppose the use of student subjects are equally culpable. They, too, rely on scripted arguments to criticize work using student subjects, and do not always consider whether those arguments are salient to the particular study. By presenting and reviewing one version of this script in the context of theoretical discussions of generalizability, we hope to demonstrate its limitations so that we can move beyond these scripted arguments into a more meaningful discussion. To do this, we review empirical studies from the period 1990–2010 to examine the extent to which student subjects are being used in the field and to critically assess the discussions within the field about the use of student samples. We conclude by presenting recommendations for authors and reviewers, for determining whether the use of students is appropriate in a particular context, and for presenting and discussing work that uses student subjects.

Key words: external validity; student samples; review process

The use of students as research subjects has been a source of controversy in the social sciences for decades. In the information systems (IS) literature, we have seen this debate played out in our roles as authors, reviewers, and editors. In this commentary, we argue that the debate has become so mechanical that it has ceased to be meaningful. We try to bring reflection and meaning to the debate by demonstrating the reliance of the IS field on students as research subjects, by highlighting the limits in our current debate against the backdrop of the literature which discusses generalizability more broadly, and by showing limitations in how the published research reflects the principles which the generalizability literature would suggest.

We begin the paper using the rhetorical device of a script, a sample interaction between authors and reviewers over the publication of a manuscript. The script reflects a composite of experiences we have seen, from both sides of the publishing process. It is not a real interaction but we contend that it is both realistic and representative of what plays out in the review process. Although not every encounter looks like this, the themes are sufficiently prevalent that we believe they should be exposed and recast.

Is this a good or a bad discussion? We have all likely seen, or made, similar arguments in papers we have reviewed or submitted. If we are honest, we recognize that we too have “read our lines” as we undertook our personal roles. Nonetheless, we argue that it is a weak discussion because we believe—and will demonstrate—that the arguments presented in this sample script have become rote—almost a reflex, and do not reflect the depth of thought that is required. We believe this is true of both those who advocate the use of student subjects and those who oppose it, and we include ourselves in this critique.

In this commentary, we argue that the time has come to move beyond scripted arguments and treat the topic more critically. Methodological choices are an essential part of the research design process and all choices have consequences for the claims that authors make (Rosenthal and Rosnow 2008). Straub (1989) reviewed the state of the field in terms of measure development and found a lack of attention to sound methodology; his call to action served to focus attention on this choice and to improve the way in which we, as a discipline, think about the nature and importance of validation. Other authors have tackled other methodological issues in IS research. Yet this issue, one which is so controversial in many fields (Burnett and Dunne 1986; Gordon et al. 1986, 1987; Greenberg 1987; Park and Lessig 1977; Sears 1986) remains relatively hidden within IS. It exists in the conversations between reviewers and authors, but has yet to be opened for broader discussion and consideration in the field.

We approach our commentary as follows. We begin by explaining the current state of the use of student subjects in IS research. We provide data on the nature and extent of the use of student subjects in Information Systems Research (ISR) and MIS Quarterly (MISQ) over the last 20 years. We then review the extensive literature on generalizability, establishing the principles and concepts important to making general statements from research using student subjects. We then explore the specific reasons why using student subjects might or might not be problematic in the context of different research areas. We include in our review, papers from psychology, general management, and IS. Building on the literature on generalizability, we return to our initial script and critically review the arguments to establish why we believe depth is lacking. We provide detailed recommendations for how the use of student subjects can and should be discussed, and we evaluate the state of the literature in terms of how well we address these recommendations.

## The Use of Student Subjects in Information Systems Research

Because the goal of our research commentary is to provide analysis and guidance on the use of students as research subjects, we begin with a review of current practices. We examined all of the articles published in ISR and MISQ for the years 1990–2010.<sup>1</sup> Table 1 shows the distribution of studies by journal and year. A statistical test (i.e., a Runs Tests) of the yearly use of student subjects (both as a percentage of articles with samples and as a percentage of total articles) showed there was not any change in the use of student samples over time in either journal.

Of the 971 papers published across the two journals, 421 used samples of individuals in their research (details of our methodology are presented in a later section of the paper). We focused on those studies where subjects provided individual-level data, which were analyzed either at the level of the individual or aggregated to a group level. Thus, we excluded those studies where individual key informants provided data on properties of an organization (e.g., Tiwana and Konsynski 2010). This ensured that the choice to use students or some other group of individuals was not constrained by the phenomenon being studied. We also excluded those studies where a small number (n < 10) of students was used in an instrument pre-test. Of the 421 studies that used samples of individuals, 152 (36%) used samples of students for at least some significant elements of the analysis.

## Conceptual Development—The Importance and Meaning of Generalizability

## Definition and Overview

Generalizability has been discussed at length across many disciplines and for many years. The term external validity was introduced by Campbell (1957) in parallel with the concept of internal validity. It was, according to Campbell (1986), a reaction to the prevailing views in experimental social research that random assignment was the only control worth worrying about. This view, explained by Campbell (1986), was particularly problematic as it ignored the many ways in which findings observed in a specific laboratory setting might be interpreted.

According to Campbell (1957) validity is evaluated in terms of two criteria:

First, and as a basic minimum, is what can be called internal validity: did in fact the experimental stimulus make some significant difference in this specific instance? The second criterion is that of external validity, representativeness, or generalizability: to what populations, settings, and variables can this effect be generalized? (p. 297)

In essence, internal validity refers to those factors that can be addressed via random assignment of treatments to subjects whereas external validity refers to those factors that cannot be so addressed. The notion of internal validity was given prominence over external validity in the definition, because without internal validity (the treatment did indeed produce the outcome) the meaning of that causal statement (or its external validity) was moot.

The notion of external validity has been more problematic than internal validity. We see two key reasons for this. The first is philosophical in nature and reflects different paradigmatic positions on the possibility of generalizing as a goal for research. Generalization is a central goal of research within the positivist and post-positivist research traditions. While positivists and post-positivists would differ as to the likelihood of ever achieving the goal of broad generalizations, both would view this as an important goal of research. Philosophically, however, generalization remains an act of induction, and hence cannot be fully justified logically (see Lee and Baskerville 2003 for a detailed presentation of this argument). This does not mean that the generalization is necessarily wrong, only that a logical proof cannot be created. Yet, following from Hume’s truism as described by Lee and Baskerville (2003), scholars in interpretive and critical philosophical traditions largely reject generalization as a goal, focusing more on elements of transferability and on the role of the reader as the locus of generalization (Lincoln and Guba 1985). Lee and Baskerville (2003) make this argument strongly, concluding:

[A] theory generalized from the empirical descriptions in a particular case study has no generalizability beyond the given case. This particular lack of generalizability is not only a feature of qualitative studies, but also statistical, sampling based studies. (p. 236, emphasis added)

In contrast, from a positivist or post-positivist position, Hume’s truism refers more to a need for skepticism or caution surrounding the generalizations we make. This is the position that we take on the possibility of generalizing.

The second reason for the more problematic status of external validity is its imprecise and changing definition (Campbell 1986). Campbell (1957) originally focused on two categories of validity and thus created relatively imprecise groupings which were subject to misinterpretation. Later, Cook and Campbell (1979) added a category for the construct validity of causes and effects, which had originally been part of the definition of external validity. In doing so the importance of theoretical interpretation (the meaning in theoretical terms of the treatment and effects) was highlighted. A few years after that, Campbell (1986) proposed the term “proximal similarity” as a better term for external validity, particularly in the context of applied social research.

The concept of proximal similarity emerges from Campbell’s (1957, 1986) assessments of internal and external validity in analysis of variance terms. Campbell (1957) describes internal validity as reflecting the “main effects” of variables such as maturation or testing. For example, if subjects’ performance on a particular experimental test is deemed to be a function of learning rather than of the treatment, this is an issue of internal validity. Learning (or maturation) has thus had a main effect on the outcome of interest. This main effect, or other similar effects, can be controlled for by adding an untreated control group and randomly assigning subjects to tasks. Campbell (1957, 1986) considers external validity, in contrast, to be a function of “interaction effects.” More specifically, he argues that they reflect the joint effects of membership in the research sample (including participation in the experiment as well as other characteristics of the sample) and the treatments given. Thus, if a particular type of subject is more likely to learn during the course of the experiment, then this is an issue of external validity or generalizability, because the results (while true for the sample) are theoretically expected not to apply to subjects of different types.

Campbell (1986) discusses this point further in comparing the general nature of research in the physical and social sciences. He argues that unlike research in the physical sciences, which has found many “main effects” (that is, findings that apply universally), the history of social sciences research is one of a predominance of higher-order interactions. If this is correct, then presuming to generalize findings from one study to another context is theoretically problematic because our theories, by definition, assume complex interactions.

## Types of Generalizability

Different types, or forms, of generalizability have been discussed in the research literature. Cronbach (1982) introduced the notation of UTOS (units, treatments, observations, and settings) as elements in the description of the design of a study and across which generalization to other populations must be made. He also differentiated the tasks of generalization into generalization from a sample to the sampled population, generalization across subpopulations, and generalizations to novel populations (see Matt 2003). The addition of more precise description and notation assists in understanding the sorts of factors that might influence whether a particular result generalizes from the study context to the broader context of application. Cronbach’s (1982) view is consistent with the idea of generalizations as being limited by potential interaction effects between study characteristics (UTOS) and the findings to be generalized. Seddon and Scheepers (2006) use the UTOS framing in their examination of “other-settings generalizations” and in the presentation of three recommendations for the discussion of generalizability.

A key theme in the literature has been the distinction between generalizing from the sample to the population (what Lee and Baskerville (2003) referred to as EE generalizability and others have called statistical generalizability) and generalizing from the empirical context of the study to more general theoretical conclusions<sup>2</sup> (type ET generalizations in Lee and Baskerville; generalizing to theory in Yin 2002).

Mook (1983) suggests that in addition to making predictions about what “does” happen in real-life situations, the researcher might want to test generalizations and explore what “can” happen in a more general way (p. 382). The distinction is that one is showing what happens in a specific sample of interest, with no further conclusions or statements being drawn, and not predicting what will happen in a population that is beyond that sample. Specifically,

[A]m I trying to estimate from sample characteristics the characteristics of some population? Or am I trying to draw conclusions not about a population, but about a theory that specifies what these subjects ought to do? Or would it be important if any subject does it or can be made to? (Mook 1983, pp. 386–387)

But generalizing to theory does not mean that the details of the study context are irrelevant. If one seeks to generalize to a theory, then following from Campbell (1957), the theory must account for the interactions between the context of the study and the constructs which make up the theory. Because the nature of theories in social sciences is frequently contingent, interactions which might limit the universality of the theory (such as the use of a specific sample like students) bear close scrutiny.

How, then does one identify the probable interactions that might influence the generalizability of research results, either to other empirical settings or to theoretical conclusions? Logically, there are an infinite number of potential interactions and thus there is no way to be certain that one is right (this is a further implication of Hume’s Truism). Locke (1986) distinguishes between ecological validity, which is probabilistic in orientation, and makes all the features similar, and essential features validity, which distinguishes between incidental or nonessential similarity, and similarity in terms of “essential features” within a study (p. 7). Benbasat (1989) makes a similar point, citing Swieringa and Weick’s (1982) distinction between external realism and mundane realism. Mundane realism should “closely resemble the real world” whereas external realism should convince subjects to “believe in and take the experimental task seriously” (Benbasat 1989, p. 43). Locke (1986) further asserts that the theoretical meaning that is ascribed to an experimental task may be similar to the intended meaning in the population even when the settings and subjects are quite different—if the essential features are aligned.

Thus, establishing parallel conditions is not a function of just counting up the similarities, as Lee and Baskerville (2003) quite correctly note. It must be about identifying theoretically meaningful elements of the situation (UTOS in Cronbach’s 1982 terms) that might influence the research outcomes. Some differences may be irrelevant to the purposes of the study. What is of concern to the researcher is identifying those that might, for theoretical reasons, be expected to influence the intended generalization(s). While as researchers we can never be sure we have identified all of the theoretically meaningful variables—thus generalizing remains the development of “well-founded but, as-yet untested, hypotheses” (p. 224)—we can identify conditions, based on theory, that we believe might alter the situation so as to influence the theoretically predicted results.

Discussions of generalizability, in the IS literature and elsewhere, are frequently bound up with discussions of other methodological issues. Lee and Baskerville (2003), for example, focus extensively on how generalizability arguments have been overapplied in the context of qualitative research. King and He (2005) are focused on issues of sampling in research, specifically how coverage and nonresponse errors influence type EE generalizability. Our paper focuses on generalizability specifically as it relates to the use of students as research subjects, which is most often undertaken in laboratory and survey research. Even though each article discusses elements that are not unique to its particular focus or emphasis, the point of view of each article influences the ways in which generalizability issues are presented. Lee and Baskerville, for example, take a strong position with regard to the challenges in drawing any generalization, which is consistent with a more interpretive stance in research, but perhaps less applicable to postpositivist and positivist researchers. At the other end of the spectrum, King and He’s emphasis is on statistical generalizability, and the paper says little about the philosophical issues that Lee and Baskerville present. Our own focus leads us to attend to particular issues, perhaps most in common with the work of Seddon and Scheepers (2006).

## Generalizability and the Use of Student Subjects

The use of students as research subjects has been debated alongside the broader discussion of generalizability. Smart (1966) commented on the view of social psychology as the “psychology of the college sophomore” (p. 115). He demonstrated the preponderance of students in social psychological research (80%) and outlined key ways in which college students differ from other populations that might influence the ability to make more generalized claims based on research conducted with students. Intellectual ability, age (which interacts with various characteristics, such as intelligence, social behavior, and learning), and socioeconomic status (which relates to various personality factors and reactions to various types of reinforcement) are key differences between college students and the broader populations to which social psychological research seeks to understand.

Sears (1986) continued this line of argument, contending that, “college students are likely to have lesscrystallized attitudes, less-formulated senses of self, stronger cognitive skills, stronger tendencies to comply with authority, and more unstable peer group relationships” (p. 515). He further argued that these differences may lead to erroneous conclusions about the presence and importance of various research concepts, and about their inter-relationships. Schultz (1969) argued that students may be more apt to answer survey questions dishonestly, either in an attempt to deceive the researcher, or in an attempt to provide “good,” or supportive, data. Tolman (1959) provides what is perhaps the most extreme expression of this view: “college sophomores are obviously not real people” (p. 7).

But differences between people (that is, intellectual, attitudinal, and other personal attributes) are not the only sources of difference that are relevant to a discussion of generalizing from student subjects. The setting<sup>3</sup> in which the research takes place can also limit the generalizability of the findings from student subjects. Gordon et al. (1987) note the particularistic nature of management research that is “replete with social content,” and yet shows that we have seen little discussion of how aspects of the setting might influence the theoretical results. If the setting of the research—be it the artificial setting of a laboratory or the unique social setting of the university—is different from the setting to which the results are intended to generalize, then it is important to establish the basis of the differences and their potential implications for the results. The university environment is likely to be different in several key ways from an organizational environment. Students in the university may be more akin to the customers of a company than to its employees. Relationships between students working on group projects may be more temporary than relationships between employees working together. Lastly, the university culture is likely to be more egalitarian and open than the culture in many business organizations. Is it reasonable, then, to conclude that reference group influences and attitudes would have the same effect among students and working professionals? What are the implications of complying or failing to comply with fellow students and professors versus with colleagues and managers? How these differences might affect the generalizability of research results, particularly those that relate to the social context of IS use, is important to assess. Gordon et al. (1986) reported, for example, that in most quantitative studies, “the experimental task was replete with social content and, therefore, likely to be perceived differently by subjects with diverse backgrounds” (p. 203).

The tasks within research also potentially limit generalizability of findings from student subjects. Most studies involve a task of some kind that is being carried out (e.g., a particular decision-making task in an experiment), or referred to in a survey (e.g., using the web for course-related or work-related information gathering). Differences in the task context between the sample and the population to which generalizations are sought must be accounted for in the discussion of the results.

A number of studies within other fields, such as marketing (Burnett and Dunne 1986, Park and Lessig 1977), have examined differences in means and variances of a variety of variables across different samples. Within the IS field, Hughes and Gibson (1991) compared various aspects of the decision-making process between students and managers in the context of DSS research. They found differences in both pretraining and post-training performance. The approach taken by all of these studies, however, is most closely aligned with type EE generalization and might be of limited value in resolving the issue of whether student samples are appropriate from a theoretical generalization perspective. Thus, while they suggest a cause for concern, they do not tell the full story.

Proponents of using student subjects often put forth a similar claim to Mook’s (1983) “generality of theoretical arguments” position, that student subjects are appropriate for theory building (cf., studies cited in Gordon et al. 1986, p. 192; Greenberg 1987; Lee and Baskerville 2003, p. 224, lines 8–11) because the focus is on internal theoretical mechanisms within the theory and not on generalizable statements about the findings. However, as noted earlier, building a theory without recognizing the potential aspects of study design that might alter the relationships within the theory, risks developing something that applies only in narrowly contrived situations. Taken to the extreme, we risk being as unhelpful as the physicist in the old joke, whose elegant solution to the farmer’s problem of ailing chickens would only work for spherical chickens in a vacuum.<sup>4</sup>

Overall the discussion of these two positions (opponents/proponents) suggests that one of two circumstances must exist for student-based research to produce generalizable statements about theory or findings: (a) students are part of population or (b) “similar” conditions are aligned (Gordon et al. 1986 parallel; Locke’s 1986 essential features). This puts primacy on the first adjective in Lee and Baskerville’s (2003) definition of generalizations as “well-founded but as-yet untested hypotheses” (p. 224, emphasis added). But even from this perspective, generalizability is still a difficult process requiring reflection, and determining what makes a generalization statement “well founded” remains challenging but achievable. Seddon and Scheepers (2006) lay out a logical process for doing this with their three elements. But this process relies heavily on judgment, on reflexivity on the part of the researcher, and on open communication regarding the boundaries of the research. As Mook (1983) asserts “we must move beyond a simple count of differences and think through what the effect of each one is likely to be” (p. 386). Moreover, the complexity of research purposes, theories, and designs, and the complex interactions between them mean that each situation must consider these issues.

Based on the theoretical discussions above, we present five key recommendations for authors to include in their discussions of the use of student samples. These recommendations, shown in Table 3, are rooted in the literature on generalizability and the specific sorts of elements that authors and reviewers need to discuss in order to address each recommendation.

In the next section, we review each of these recommendations and assess the extent to which they are being reflected in the IS literature. We start by critically analyzing the dialogue and arguments presented by the actors in our sample script against these recommendations, and show how the scripted discussion fails to address these principles. We then review current practices of generalization from student samples found in the IS literature to see the extent to which the review processes result in published literature that follows the recommendations.

## Reviewing the Script

Going forward in our discussion and analysis of why student subjects are conceivably a challenge in our sample script, we focus on “other-settings generalizations” as outlined by Seddon and Scheepers (2006), or what Lee and Baskerville (2003) call types EE and ET generalizability, and King and He (2005) among others, termed external validity. Generalizing from student subjects to working professionals reflects a clear example of “other-settings generalizations.” We comment on each of the actors in the dialogue and their arguments, and demonstrate the ways in which both the author and reviewer responses lack sufficient depth of thought and inadequate articulation of the issues associated with student subjects. This review of the script demonstrates the “lip service” (the roteness of the arguments) to the topic of student samples and the absence of the necessary reflection to advance our collective thinking around how our theories might be influenced by the contexts in which we are testing them. To move beyond this scripted discussion, Table 3 presents possible points of discussion for considering what is necessary when assessing the appropriateness of a sample of student subjects to the particular research phenomenon.

In point A1, the author fails to move beyond a scripted discussion by failing to discuss how the unique sample relates to the target population. In the broadest sense, the sample is unique, not because it is composed of students, but rather because it is a sample. Gordon et al. (1986) argue that “behavioral sciences are unlike many natural sciences because so few pains are taken to demonstrate the representativeness of samples vis-à-vis target populations” (p. 203). Recommendation 2 suggests identifying the population, and recommendation 3 suggests explaining the representativeness of the sample. Seddon and Scheepers (2006) lay out a detailed approach to assessing the representativeness of a sample, based on Shadish et al. (2002). Thus, any study should include a more thoughtful reflection on the characteristics of its samples and their implications for the conclusions the researcher seeks to draw.

There are several reasons why students—as a particular kind of sample—may be unique on factors which are relevant to IS research (recommendation 3). As discussed previously, authors have shown students to have different cognitive abilities (Sears 1986, Smart 1966) and others have argued that students may be more apt to answer survey questions dishonestly, either in an attempt to deceive the researcher, or in an attempt to provide “good,” or supportive, data (Rosenthal and Rosnow 2008, Schultz 1969). Moreover, the theoretical meanings of the tasks and settings may not be different. For example, using a technology to support work in a course may mean something quite different from using the same technology to support professional work, because a course and a job are likely to play different roles in one’s life. Similarly, the influence of supervisor encouragement as compared to teacher encouragement may be different given that supervisors and instructors likely have different levels of both formal and informal power relative to employees and students, respectively. We do not argue that this will always be so but rather it is incumbent upon authors to justify their samples based on the particulars of the study and the nature of the theory being examined. Doing so requires understanding of the goals of the research (recommendations 1 and 2), the unique characteristics of these elements (recommendation 3 and 4), and the interaction between those unique characteristics and the phenomenon being studied (recommendation 5).

Continuing through our sample script, we see that the reviewer, in point R1, dismisses the work based on its sample, but no specific reasons are given as to why the sample is problematic in this particular context. The reviewer is guilty of the same lack of detailed consideration and dialogue as the author on the previous point. The reviewer has made a blanket statement about the appropriateness of using student subjects across all settings, tasks, theories, and research purposes. This sort of statement is not justified, and is missing explanations based upon issues in recommendations 1–3. There are, in fact, several conditions under which using students in IS research may in fact be quite appropriate. If the students are part of the population of interest, such as Internet consumers of music or Generation Y’s attitudes towards technology and IS, then their inclusion in the sample frame is appropriate because they are part of that population (recommendation 2).<sup>5</sup> Using student subjects is also appropriate when similar meanings can be ascribed to the task/setting for student subjects (Locke 1986) (recommendation 3) and when external validity is not the goal of the research (recommendation 1) (Mook 1983).<sup>6</sup> If any of these conditions apply to the research at hand, then R1’s claim is ill founded. Based upon the arguments we present, to claim that “IS research has moved beyond using student subjects” is too strong, and reflects the scripted discussions that are sometimes played out during the review process.

The next arguments represent the sorts of supporting arguments we often see in this dialogue. The author uses past practice (A2) as the justification for using student subjects (recommendation 3). But, as Gordon et al. (1986) argued, past practice is not a sufficient basis to justify a research decision. If past practice is also characterized by scripted responses, then using it to justify current decisions is simply perpetuating this type of scripted discussion. Moreover, as we have argued, it is necessary to examine the choice of sample within the context of each particular study— what are its research objectives, what is the theory and how might theoretical relationships be expected to be influenced by the unique characteristics of this sample (the individual differences identified earlier, differences in the setting and in the task) (recommendations 4 and 5).

The reviewer presses further for the author to collect more data (R2), but again this claim is made without specific reflection on why or how the results based on student data might be expected to be unrepresentative. To move beyond a scripted discussion, the reviewer’s call for more data collection should be specific as to what characteristics of the data are being questioned (recommendation 4), why those characteristics are important in this specific context, and what sorts of characteristics of the new data would be acceptable (recommendation 3).

Several points are raised in the author’s response regarding additional data collection (A3). But again, notice that neither the reviewer nor the author has discussed why the use of this particular sample of students would or would not be problematic (recommendation 4). So far, the discussion is just about the overall use of students as subjects. The author’s response here rests on the notion that establishing generalizability is more about process than the design of one specific study. This point is correct. However, it does not mean that attention to our sample choices is irrelevant. External validity is established through repeated replications in different subpopulations; it implies a purposeful approach and there is little evidence in the IS literature of a clear strategy of research based on replications (Berthon et al. 2002).

The request of the reviewer to add a discussion of limitations (R3) is, perhaps, appropriate. However, note that the reviewer has not offered a specific concern to which the author can respond; instead, only the vague notion that students are problematic is offered (recommendation 4).

The next set of responses (A4) raises the elements of theory as a critical dimension of the author’s response. The author argues that the theory is broad and expected to apply to all members of the population. There are two problems here. The first is that again, there is no discussion of the dimensions on which the samples are similar or different and how this would affect the potential results in other settings (recommendation 4). This is a general issue, which could apply to many domains of research in which students are used as research subjects.

The second problem is more specific to the case of technology adoption, which is one of the dominant areas where student subjects are used. This point requires further elaboration, as it provides an example of the sorts of considerations that need to be given to the choice of a sample for testing a particular theory. The paper in our sample script relies on the decomposed theory of planned behavior (Taylor and Todd 1995), an adaptation of Ajzen’s (1991) theory of planned behavior (TPB), a broad theory of how human behavior unfolds. The authors argue, then, that because TPB is a broad theory of human behavior, and because students are people too, there is no reason to expect differences. Yet, TPB requires that in each study the researcher begin by eliciting the salient beliefs of potential subjects. Thus, each study begins with a pilot phase in order to identify the salient behavioral, normative, and control beliefs (Ajzen 1991). It is only in this way that researchers can identify the beliefs that are (a) accessible to the members of the target population, and (b) salient to the behavioral decision in question. This belief elicitation was conducted by Davis et al. (1989) in developing technology acceptance model (TAM) but since then, little attempt to elicit relevant beliefs has been made (see Riemenschneider et al. 2003 for an exception). In addition, our motivation in most studies of technology acceptance is to understand, and potentially influence, the behavior of managers and professionals. Thus, our efforts in IS research have been to adjust these general theories of human behavior to become more particularistic theories of the behaviors of working professionals using IS. It is true that these theories still have roots in the basic theory of human behavior, but our interests have moved away from this. As a general theory of human behavior, TPB suggests that behavior is a function of intention, which in turn is a function of attitude, subjective norm, and, in the case of TPB, perceived behavioral control. In IS research, this is not our principal interest. Most studies are, or should be (Benbasat and Barki 2007), more interested in understanding the antecedents of these elements (the beliefs which produce attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control) and the other variables which influence these beliefs (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000, Venkatesh 2000, Venkatesh and Davis 2000). Thus, we have changed the focus of the research and the prescribed methods (not identifying salient beliefs). These changes mean we have adapted the theory, and we contend that our adaptation has been one of changing, at a minimum, the target population under study. We do not mean to suggest that our theories cannot be further adapted to other specific contexts (for example, Venkatesh and Brown (2001) do this for the case of home computing adoption) but we argue that such an adaptation is a significant undertaking, whose purpose is to predict the behaviors of individuals in the new setting rather than to use the new setting to predict behaviors of working professionals in organizations (recommendation 3 and 4).

Returning to our sample script, the students in this example are not members of the population of working professionals. They probably aspire to be so, but they are likely not currently so. This affects, in particular, the generalizability to other organizations and tasks rather than other subjects. Even if students are working outside of school, typically the focus of studies using student subjects is on their roles as students (or subjects in a lab simulation) and the technologies they use in those roles. Thus, within this role they are surrogates for the population rather than members of it.

The second point made by the author in the script is that the purpose of the research is theory building and measure development rather than creating a definitive test of the theory for predicting from that theory. But the paper then goes on to suggest implications for management thus going beyond the domain of generalizing to theory. This is frequently the case in IS research. Despite our unique samples, we make quite broad claims about research findings (Seddon and Scheepers 2006). But, in this case, given the expected differences between students and working professionals and the specificity of the theory as it has developed over time, it is not clear that such generalizations are warranted (recommendation 5).

In the end, each of the parties has acted out their role in the scripted discussion. One could argue that things “worked out” because the paper was published. But is that outcome really the right one? To be sure, the reviewers’ concerns about student subjects were not well articulated. But the actual extent to which the authors’ findings were limited by this methodological choice has largely gone unexplored in the review process and the paper.

## Assessment of the IS Literature—Are We Really Reading from the Script?

Our paper began with a provocative claim, that IS researchers are simply reading parts in a script without fully discussing generalizability and the arguments presented in the sample script have become rote. Based on an extensive review and synthesis of the literature on generalizability, we presented five recommendations for what we believe a more appropriate level of discussion would entail. These recommendations elaborate upon those provided by Seddon and Scheepers (2006) and King and He (2005). They attempt to provide a more concrete set of recommendations by which papers can be crafted and evaluated. Nonetheless, assessing the extent of generalizability remains a matter for judgment and thus our intent is not to provide absolute rules about when students should or should not be used, but a way for authors and reviewers to justify their use of students or their objections to student samples. In the remainder of our paper, we use these five recommendations to review current practices in the IS literature.

To assess the practices associated with using student subjects, we began by identifying all of the articles published in MISQ and ISR from 1990 to 2010. Papers were identified from an electronic search through each year of the journal and all papers were downloaded and saved for further review. Two experienced graduate students assisted with the coding of the articles. Two of the authors verified the coding by examining a subset of the coded articles. Coding began with identification of the methodology and context of the studies (reported earlier) as well as the sample characteristics. Context was coded using the categories established by Vessey et al. (2002) based on information provided in each paper. Regarding sample characteristics, we identified four groups of articles. The majority of papers identified (465) did not use any data collected from people. This set included conceptual papers, those that used secondary data only, or those that used simulation and modeling data. These studies were excluded from further analysis. Eighty-five (85) papers collected primary data from people but these individuals were reporting as key informants on organizational-level processes. Because these studies were focused on units of analysis other than the individual or group and the individual is simply providing data about the unit, we excluded these studies from consideration. There were 421 papers that used samples of individuals. Of these 152 used student subjects for a significant portion of the data analysis.

For the 152 articles that used student subjects, we conducted detailed examinations of how they addressed the various practices identified from the literature. To assess conformance with our recommendations, coders first conducted a word search for the terms generalizability, population, student, and sample to see if explicit statements could be found. In addition, each paper was read by the coders, with particular emphasis on the introduction, methods section, and implications portion of the discussion.

We ended up coding conformance with the first four recommendations (Table 4). We did not assess the fifth recommendation. This recommendation deals with the extent to which the discussion of implications for research and/or practice in the article is consistent with the stated goals and choice of sample. We were unable to make an assessment of this recommendation because, as we will discuss below, there is very little discussion of generalization intent or target population. Without explicit treatment of these issues, any analysis we would make of appropriateness of discussion would require us to infer the authors’ intentions, and we felt that such analysis might do a disservice to the authors if we incorrectly inferred their intent.

## Recommendation 1: Explicit Presentation of Goals

Our first recommendation is that the goal of the research with respect to generalization should be explicitly and clearly presented. Table 3 highlighted the elements that would make up this recommendation. Does the study seek to provide generalizable findings (Lee and Baskerville’s 2003 type EE generalization), or does it only seek to generalize to theory (type ET) or to demonstrate, as Mook (1983) notes, that an effect can occur under some conditions? Unless authors explicitly define and communicate their goals, it is difficult to assess the extent to which the sample is appropriate. If a study does not seek to make an “other-settings generalization,” then the issue of generalization becomes moot. We do need to exercise caution about what it really means to do research that does not intend generalization to other settings as a goal. Most research in the positivist or post-positivist tradition seeks, at least at some level, to speak to broader and more general truths than are explicitly demonstrated by the findings. Thus, claims regarding lack of generalization need to be evaluated carefully to ensure that they reflect the implied goals of the paper (note: this point also relates to recommendation 5 regarding the way in which implications are discussed). In our review of studies in MISQ and ISR, we found only 10 studies (7% in Table 4) that explicitly defined their goals with respect to generalizability.

We found no studies that spoke explicitly of their aims to generalize to other populations (i.e., other people). At the same time we found many studies, such as Fuller and Dennis (2009), which implied a desire for generalizability to other (contexts and tasks). Measure development papers often speak to a desire to create a valid measure for use in future research, which implies a desire to create a generalizable instrument. Other studies seek to develop a theory of a phenomenon, which again implies a desire for generalizability. But these aims are often implied and the reader may not accurately be able to determine the authors’ intentions.

Being explicit about aims is an essential first step in more thoughtfully addressing generalizability in IS research. Authors need to pay more attention to what they hope to achieve with respect to generalizability. Having an aim of generalizable results is appropriate, despite the fact that such generalizations will always be speculative (Lee and Baskerville 2003) because they reflect inductive reasoning. Focusing on internal validity is also appropriate, if the research warrants it. As Mook (1983) points out, there are several important instances in which seeking to provide external validity might actually interfere with the aims of the research. But regardless of the authors’ intentions, the aims need to be clearly communicated so that readers and reviewers can properly assess whether the aims have been sufficiently justified and whether the research design meets the needs of the authors’ aims.

## Recommendation 2: Explicit Population Definition

When other-settings generalization is a goal of the research the population to which generalization is sought must be explicitly defined. As with the discussion of goals above, it is incumbent on the researcher to identify and communicate the intended target for generalization. Defining the population helps readers to understand the scope of generalizability sought for the research and makes it possible to assess the extent to which the design allows for such generalizations. A variety of possible population targets exist across the IS research we reviewed. For studies of technology adoption at work, the intended population is likely to be the population of working professionals. But for technology adoption outside the workplace, the population is less clear; is it adults in general or a more narrowly constrained group? For Internet shopping, the population may be consumers who access the Internet. It may also be a more restrictive group of consumers (for particular types of products) or a broader group (such as consumers in general if the study seeks to include adopters and nonadopters of Internet shopping). In other cases (around training and learning, for example) the population may be post-secondary students (e.g., Piccoli et al. 2001).

We found 14 studies (9% in Table 4) that explicitly defined the population of interest. A further 17 (11%) imply the population of interest. Studies that explicitly defined the population did so in two ways. Most began with the characteristics of the sample and then related that to the population. For example, Kumar and Benbasat (2006) wrote:

The study used university students as participants, and the results generalize to this target population. (p. 437)

Others, such as Gefen et al. (2003) began with the population, and then related the sample to those population characteristics:

The main data collection aimed at the same population, that is, experienced online shoppers who were undergraduate and graduate business students at a leading business school in the mid-atlantic region of the United States. (p. 68)

Regardless of which approach is taken, having clearly defined the population of interest for the study is essential for the next recommendation (justifying the sample) to be considered.

## Recommendation 3: Sample Justification

Every paper should include a clear and specific justification for the use of students as the sample, including an assessment of parallelism among elements of the subjects, setting, task, method, and manipulation. If we are to accept our generalizations as well founded (albeit untested), then understanding how the choice of sample, task, methods, manipulations, and context or setting interact with the generalization goals and target population (recommendations 1 and 2) is essential. Table 3 provides highlights of possible points of discussion in recommendation 3.

In our review of the literature, we found mixed practices on this recommendation. Only 32% of studies included any justification for the sample choice they made (Table 4). This reinforces the arguments of Gordon et al. (1986) that we pay too little attention to this critical research choice. The vast majority of studies simply do not discuss it. Within the 48 studies that did provide a justification for the sample, nine based their justification on past practices in IS research, arguing that because prior studies had used student subjects in this research area it was appropriate for them to do so. This sort of argument is problematic as it ignores the differences in research aims and target populations that each study invokes, and potentially the implications of different theoretical perspectives for the types of variables studied and their generalizability. Nine studies within the 48 base their justification on students being a significant part of the target population of interest. Typically these were in the domains of online shopping, learning, or technology acceptance. This justification is acceptable (as long as the population is clearly defined) and can be seen as strengthening the design if done purposefully. It can be argued that focusing a study on one subset of the population of interest is valuable, because it helps to rule out confounding factors. Thus, internal validity can be increased by this design choice. Adding in a replication to another subsample of the population, as for example was done by Nadkarni and Gupta (2007), provides an excellent balance of internal and external validity.

The majority of studies which provided a justification for the sample used a logical argument to relate the characteristics of the sample to the population. This approach is essential when the subjects are being used as surrogates for the population of interest (e.g., surveying undergraduate students about technology adoption in their coursework with the intent to draw generalizations related to the use of technology by professionals at work). A full justification should be based on the elements of sample (who), task (what) setting (where), methods (how), manipulations (how), and errors (how) as each of these can influence the extent to which generalizations can be drawn (meaning, general statements). Griffith and Northcraft (1996) provide an excellent example of full justification for the student sample:

The context of the study was thus more field than laboratory (Mawhinney 1986). Subjects were members of the organization (the class) that would utilize the product of their work, and this work had to be completed whether or not the study took place. Subjects’ use of the technology was directly related to their real world need to complete presentation materials (overhead transparencies) for their projects. As suggested by Campbell (1986), the constructs tested in this research were comparable to those extant in a field setting. Although subjects’ participation was relatively brief, parallel tasks in an organizational setting would be similarly limited (e.g., a project team creating a presentation to report on its work). Therefore, the constructs examined here are expected to operate as they would with similar tasks and technologies; differences in effects should be in level rather than direction. (p. 102)

But such clear and thoughtful reflection on the sample choice was a notable exception in the literature. It is important that authors discuss how students are parallel on important population characteristics within the setting and task. For example, authors might describe how and why a part-time student is or is not similar to a worker using a corporate wiki training site, if that task similarity is central for their study. In addition, not all students are created equal; various issues, such as nontraditional students, parttime students, executive education students, graduate students, and those with and without work experience need to be carefully presented by authors and reviewers. In the future, students may form different life-roles than they did in the past and authors or reviewers need careful discussion of these similarities or differences. Only a handful of the 48 studies that attempted to justify the sample came close to this level of discussion. Thus, while this recommendation is more closely followed than the first two, we are still a long way from achieving the sort of practice that the literature on generalizability would recommend.

## Recommendation 4: Discussion of Limitations of Sample

All research has limitations. Every methodological choice creates the possibility of both strengths and weaknesses. Choosing to sample broadly maximizes the extent to which responses will represent the population, but focusing narrowly may enhance internal validity by controlling for extraneous variation. Thus, the potential limitations of the sample should be discussed thoughtfully, referencing both the goals of the research and the characteristics of the sample and population as they relate to the goal. Table 3 highlights the possible points of discussion in recommendation 4.

Less than half of the studies (47% in Table 4) reviewed mentioned the use of student subjects as a limitation. Yet our analysis of the example of technology acceptance research shows that there are ways in which using student subjects can be limiting to the goals of creating generalizable results.

As King and He (2005) note, often the discussion of limitations is defensive as highlighted in our sample script. The issue is quickly raised and then dismissed as not critical. We further argue that it does not often reflect a deep level of engagement with the potential issues, following instead the usual scripted arguments. Most discussions are extremely short (a single sentence, which is an example of the roteness of the arguments presented in the sample script) and we did not find any examples of studies that followed the Shadish et al. (2002) recommendation to explicitly discuss the boundaries beyond which their knowledge claims might not apply or the uncertainties or misgivings in the claims of other-settings generalizability.

Wheeler and Valacich (1996) provide ran example of one of the studies that provides more depth in the discussion of limitations:

There are obvious limitations to generalizations from this research. This experiment employed ad hoc groups which were engaged in making a decision with no direct consequence to them. While the students appeared to understand the task and to actively participate, they are not typical of organizational decision makers. Furthermore, these students were novice users of this technology and this particular heuristic. As recognized in AST, additional experience with structures will likely influence the skill with which they are appropriated during a group decision process. By strict interpretation, the implications from this experiment should be limited to decisions made by technology-supported groups using some form of a heuristic. Our experience with many real, organizational groups, however, suggests that the lessons from these results are more applicable than strict interpretation would allow. (p. 444)

Dennis et al. (1999) provide an even more detailed discussion:

This study suffers from the usual limitations of laboratory experiments (see McGrath 1982). The task was comparatively simple in that it required 30 minutes of work. The subjects here were ad hoc groups of undergraduate students with little intrinsic motivation and little prior GSS or business experience. It is possible that this form of task structuring only helps inexperienced users because those experienced with the task may be less likely to overlook parts of the task. However, the earlier study found the same effects whether studying undergraduates inexperienced with the task or managers working on a task with which they had prior experience. Several other issues relating to the use of laboratory experiments in GSS research have been noted (Dennis et al. 1991), but to date there is no empirical evidence to suggest that lab studies with undergraduates produce different conclusions than similar studies with managers (Dennis et al. 1996a).

Without presuming that all issues about the use of undergraduates as subjects have been addressed, it is believed that these results, which are similar to prior studies that have used both students and managers (Dennis et al. 1996b), have a message for managers and practitioners performing GSS-supported brainstorming. If the task offers an opportunity for modest structure, it should be taken. In this study, groups that used structure produced about 40% more ideas than those that did not. An effect of this size can be usefully employed in practical applications while research probes more deeply into the limits of its effectiveness and develops new techniques with even greater potential. (p. 104)

Lee and Baskerville (2003) found both over- and under-statement of limitations regarding sampling choices. We also found this pattern. Most studies, in our judgment, understate the limitations associated with student samples. But a few studies raise the student sample as a limitation even when prior justification has been provided that students are part of the target population and representative of that population. For example, in studies of online shopping, the use of student subjects per se, is not a limitation to generalizability.

Thus, we advocate that authors spend more time exploring the limits of their research, and recognize that their design choices always bound the results in some way. Discussion of the limitations imposed by a sampling choice should be thought of as an opportunity to think about how the characteristics of the sample might have influenced the results and use this as a base for theory development rather than just as a pro forma statement that generalizability is suspect and requires replication.

Recommendation 5. Consistency Between Elements Our final recommendation is that the discussion of implications for research and/or practice must be consistent with the stated goals and choice of sample. If the paper argues that its goal is not to generalize to the population, then the implications section must reflect this choice and should not provide extensive statements of generalizations. Thus, this recommendation closes the loop between the first recommendation (being explicit about goals) and the presentation of results and implications to ensure consistency with that goal.

As noted earlier, we did not assess the literature against this recommendation as it would have required drawing our own inferences about the generalization goals of the research (because none of the studies provided an explicit statement of these goals vis-à-vis population generalizations). But given that so few studies fulfill the first four recommendations (and no single study satisfied all four), we believe that our progress on this recommendation is likely limited. However, we urge authors and reviewers to carefully consider all five recommendations in their discussions.

## Conclusions

The use of student subjects in research has been a long-standing controversy across many disciplines. We have been taught to question their validity, as there are situations in which their use undermines the goals of the research. Many researchers have relied extensively on the use of student subjects in their research and have cogently argued for their appropriateness. We suggest that it is time to be more open to student samples, where their use is clearly articulated and justified beyond the rote arguments. Broad claims about the appropriateness of student samples are not consistent with theory about the nature of generalization or the range of goals for research, and thus like all research choices, the choice of a student sample must be assessed against those goals and that context. Thus, our argument for openness should not be taken as a blanket endorsement of the use of student subjects either. We strongly believe that samples do matter and that justification for the appropriateness of sample choice is important and must be communicated to readers. What is necessary, then, is to think critically about the aims of the study, the context of the research, and the potential impact that the specific sample choice would have on the ability of the authors to meet their aims.

We present five recommendations that we encourage authors and reviewers to use in both the design and assessment of all research in order to move beyond the rote arguments presented in the sample script. For authors, these recommendations provide a foundation for thinking about research choices as they relate to generalizability and specifically for thinking about the implications of the sample choice. For reviewers, these recommendations will hopefully provide a better basis for constructive suggestions to the authors about how their discussions can be improved. There is a danger, in providing specific guidelines as we have done, that these guidelines will become the replacement script through which we can discuss generalizability without really getting to the heart of the issue. Thus, we urge both authors and reviewers to interpret these guidelines not as a checklist to be addressed, in as limited a fashion as possible, but as a set of questions to be asked at all phases of the research to guide deeper thinking on the subject of sampling and generalizability.

We were somewhat surprised by the limited attention to the use of student subjects in the literature. We had expected to find that most studies made a brief mention of the choice, consistent with the scripted approach we outlined in our opening vignette. We found, instead, that most studies simply do not address the issues at all. Less than half provide any justification for the sample and more than 90% provide no clear basis to establish the authors’ aims with regard to generalizability. Similar to Straub’s (1989) study of measurement validation, our review of 20 years of research in our premier journals, finds that as a discipline we are simply not discussing our sampling choices to an appropriate degree.

Our call, then, is for increased attention to this critical methodological choice. For authors, reviewers and editors, we believe the time has come to expand the discussion of the samples we use, the reasons for those choices, and their implications (both positive and negative) for the conclusions of the research. It is time to move our discussions out of the scripted, yet often meaningless, approaches of the past and recognize the complicated, multi-faceted and yet critically important nature of our choices about samples.
