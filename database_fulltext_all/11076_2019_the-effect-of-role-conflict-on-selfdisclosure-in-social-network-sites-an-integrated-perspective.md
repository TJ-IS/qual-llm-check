## Abstract

As people increasingly integrate social network sites (SNSs) into their daily lives, their social connections on SNSs become more diverse, including their friends, co‐workers, and relatives. In such a context, users may receive different role expectations from their various social circles and experience role conflict in their usage of SNSs. Furthermore, previous literature suggests that people may not always make privacy‐related decisions through effortful and deliberate information processing. Drawing on the perspective of boundary regulation and dual process theories, this study clarifies the consequences of role conflict on SNSs. A theoretical model is developed to examine the effect of role conflict regarding privacy risk and perceived control, which, in turn, impact self‐disclosure behaviour, as well as how this process is moderated by high‐ versus low‐effort processing. The results of 4 experiments provide strong support for our model. The implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Social network sites (SNSs) such as Facebook and WeChat have become more and more popular over the last decade. On SNSs, people seek to maintain their existing social connections\* and develop new connections (Boyd & Ellison, 2008; Wang & Li, 2015). For example, the number of US Facebook users is estimated to reach 172 million by 201 (Statista, 2015), and the median number of friends per user is around 200 (Mazin, 2014). In China, WeChat is the most popular mobile SNS (Verot, 2015). According to the report from WeChat (2016), the average number of WeChat users per day is around 768 million, and 78.7% of users have more than 50 connections. As the number of users' socia connections on SNSs increases, their social circles become more diverse, including their friends, classmates, relatives co‐workers, and online friends.

With diverse social circles, users may thus perceive incompatible role expectations and experience role conflict while interacting with their social connections on SNSs. As a result, they may feel it is quite challenging to manage their identity and struggle with their personal and professional images integrated on the same platform (Besmer & Lipford, 2010). In certain scenarios, users' professional images can be damaged, and their careers can be negatively impacted by role conflict on SNSs. For example, users may feel concerned about uploading pictures of themselves with alcohol at parties because they are on sport teams and do not want their coaches to view these pictures (Besme & Lipford, 2010). In such a context, users receive inconsistent role expectations and experience role conflict: While users' friends may expect users to upload those pictures and show their friendship, users' coaches may expect users not to upload pictures with alcohol. Therefore, if users choose to upload party pictures on SNSs, they may lose their positions in sport teams and their professional careers can suffer. To avoid potential role conflict, users may avoid uploading such pictures or even break connections with those with whom they may have conflict. As the market value of SNSs depends heavily on the social interactions of active users (Smith, 2013), such reactions may not only impede social exchanges between users but also negatively impact the value of SNSs. Therefore, understanding the effects o role conflict can help users to better protect their privacy on SNSs and inform SNS providers' operational strategies.

Previous literature on privacy mainly focuses on how users make self‐disclosure decisions by themselves (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Smith, Dinev, & Xu, 2011) and ignores the role of users' social connections in the process of privacy-related decision-making. For example. the “Antecedents-Privacy Concerns-Outcomes” (APCO) mode (Smith et al., 2011) states that antecedents (eg, individual traits and cultural influences) impact users' privacy concerns, which, in turn, lead to behavioural outcomes. Yet, on SNSs, users may have social connections from different social circles, which probably have inconsistent norms, standards, and values of roles. In such a context, when users disclose information on SNSs, the information disclosed may not meet expectations from every social circle. As a result of this, users may experience role conflicts, making them feel more concerned about privacy because they know that their different, “friends” can observe their behaviours (Besmer & Lipford 2010: Brandtzæg, Lüders & Skietne, 2010) In the example above, users receive different role expectations from their friends and coaches and therefore experience role conflict. In such a scenario, users' self‐disclosure decisions are influenced by role conflict from their social connections. Users may thus feel that it is quite challenging to control their disclosed information on SNSs and their privacy is compromised (Zhang, Wang, & Xu, 2011). Hence, our first objective is to examine how users' role conflict with their socia connections influences their self‐disclosure decisions on SNSs. Drawing on the perspective of boundary regulation (Derlega & Chaikin, 1977), this study clarifies the essence of role conflict and examines how role conflict can influence people's self‐disclosure behaviour.

Furthermore, previous research on privacy assumes that users are capable of making privacy-related decisions through effortful and deliberate information processing (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Li, Sarathy, & Xu, 2011; Smith et al., 2011). However, this assumption has been challenged (Dinev, McConnel, & Smith, 2015), and users may not always make privacy‐related decisions with high‐effort processing. For example, Preibusch (2015) examines people's privacy behaviours after Snowden. Edward Snowden is a former Central Intelligence Agency employee wh leaked information about the PRISM program (a surveillance program by the US government designed to collect internet communication). The results show that users' privacy concerns rise immediately after the PRISM revelation but their privacy behaviours fade quickly. The PRISM revelation temporarily increases usage of piracy‐enhancing technologies (eg, Tor and Anonymox) but does not result in any significant effects in the long term. The results from Preibusch (2015) are not consistent with previous literature and indicate that users may not always follow effortfu processing in their privacy decisions

Dinev et al. (2015) thus suggest that privacy research can benefit from examining how heuristic processes involv ing low-effort thinking influence privacy-related decision-making. Eor example, the level of effort can moderate the relationships in the APCO model, and empirical studies investigating the effects of different levels of effort can furthe our understanding of privacy‐related decision‐making (Dinev et al., 2015). Hence, our second objective is to examine how role conflict influences self‐disclosure decisions under high‐effort versus low‐effort processing. Drawing upon dua process theories (Gawronski & Creighton, 2013), our study examines the effect of role conflict under differen information processing strategies. Dual process theories are chosen because previous literature suggests that they can be helpful in understanding high‐effort versus low‐effort information processing (Dinev et al., 2015). We argu that people can engage in different information‐processing strategies in different contexts, which result in different privacy‐related decision‐making processes and self‐disclosure behaviours in response to role conflict

To summarize, this study aims to examine how SNS users respond to role conflict and make their self‐disclosur decisions with high‐ versus low‐effort processing by integrating boundary regulation and dual process theories. Four experiments with Chinese participants are conducted, and WeChat is selected as the context. WeChat is suitable for our study because it is the most popular Chinese SNS operating on smartphones and tablets.

Our study has 2 main contributions. First, our study clarifies how role conflict influences self‐disclosure, which has not been thoroughly examined in previous literature. Specifically, we argue that role conflict on SNSs can influence users' perception of their privacy, which in turn impacts their self-disclosure, Our study thus clarifies the mechanisms through which role conflict influences self‐disclosure. This in turn helps us provide important practica guidelines for SNS design. Second, this study clarifies the effect of role conflict under high‐effort versus low‐effort processing, thus expanding previous literature that mainly focuses on high‐effort processing (Dinev et al., 2015). B showing how SNS users perceive their privacy following different information processing strategies, our research can provide both an important boundary condition for existing privacy literature and important insights for SN providers. Specifically, the results of our study can help SNS providers to develop a more effective framework that supports users' social interactions and reduces their role conflict

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first review previous literature and discuss the theoretica foundation of our study. Next, the research model and hypotheses are presented. The research methodology for 4 experimental studies is then presented, and the results of data analysis are reported. Finally, the paper concludes with implications for theory and practice, limitations, and opportunities for future studies

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

In this section, we first discuss the concept of role conflict and clarify the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure on the basis of the perspective of boundary regulation. We then describe the effects of role conflict in high‐effort versus low‐effort processing, drawing upon dual process theories

## 2.1 | The concept of role conflict

When people interact with a specific social circle, the roles derived can help people decide how to behave in certain contexts (Perlman, 1968). A role is defined as the perception people have about themselves when they interact with a certain group in a specific environment (Hall, 1971). In other words, each role has a specific set of expectations for people to follow (Zhang et al., 2011). Role conflict is thus defined as the degree to which people perceive incompat ibility and incongruity in different roles' expectations (Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1992).

Nowadays, people increasingly use SNSs to communicate with others. Unlike offline communication, communi cation on SNSs is not limited by temporal and spatial constraints. As people use SNSs over time, their connection list may include friends, relatives, co‐workers, and business contacts. Thus, SNSs include connections from different socia circles, and the natural boundaries that separate users' different social circles blur (Zhang et al., 2011). As a result users can have diverse social circles within the same SNS communication platform and be evaluated by those socia circles on the basis of corresponding role expectations. Since the expectations for each role are not fully consistent across different social circles, role conflict arises.

Role conflict has important implications for privacy and self‐disclosure on SNSs. In the next section, we clarify how role conflict can influence users' self‐disclosure decisions on the basis of the perspective of boundary regulation.

## 2.2 | Role conflict and privacy boundary regulation

Privacy can be viewed from different perspectives (Smith et al., 2011), and one relevant perspective for this study is to conceptualize privacy as control. For example, Derlega and Chaikin (1977) view privacy as “a process of boundary regulation, controlling how much (or how little) contact an individual maintains with others” (p. 102). In another study, Margulis (2003) views privacy as “control over or regulation of or, more narrowly, limitations on or exemption from scrutiny, surveillance, or unwanted access” (p. 244). In other words, privacy represents the boundary regulation process through which people control their interactions with others (Altman, 1975).

Following the perspective of privacy as control, self‐disclosure, the transmission of information about oneself, supports the process of boundary regulation. Specifically, a boundary is regulated through the adjustment of self‐disclosure output (Derlega & Chaikin. 1977). In such a process, people's privacy is maintained if they have control over the degree of self‐disclosure. As described below, the perspective of boundary regulation is helpful to understand the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure as it describes the role of social circles in the process of self‐disclosure

Derlega and Chaikin (1977) further argue that self‐disclosure involves 2 boundaries. The first boundary, called th “dyadic boundary,” ensures that the discloser is safe to disclose information to others. Here, others may come from a certain social circle (eg, classmates, relatives, or co‐workers). Such a boundary can help avoid undesired socia interactions (Knowles. 1980), Thus, this boundary is the precondition for self-disclosure: People need to perceive that it is safe to disclose their information to a group of others. The second boundary, the “self‐boundary,” maintains a barrier around the person based on nondisclosure. The barrier is open, and the self‐boundary is adjusted when people disclose their information. To summarize, the dyadic boundary defines to whom people disclose, and the self‐boundary states what people disclose.

While privacy is a popular topic in Information Systems (IS) literature, past research has mainly focused on how users make self‐disclosure decisions by themselves (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Smith et al., 2011), and the role of people's social connections regarding privacy has not been thoroughly examined. In the context of SNSs, the disclosed information is more interpersonal (eg, party pictures with friends), and users' social connections can also influence their privacy decisions. As users may experience role conflict while interacting with connections from different socia circles, our first research objective is to examine how role conflict can influence users' privacy‐related decision‐making.

Further, Dinev et al. (2015) suggest that people may not always follow high‐effort processing when they make privacy‐related decisions. In the context of this study, this argument suggests that role conflict may have different effects in high‐effort versus low‐effort processing. Since previous literature has mainly focused on users' privacy decision‐making with high‐effort processing, our second research objective is to examine how role conflict influences self‐disclosure decisions under high‐effort versus low‐effort processing. To better understand the effect of role conflict in different contexts, we draw on dual process theories.

## 2.3 | Dual process theories

Our study adopts dual process theories of cognition to understand the effect of role conflict in high‐effort versus low‐effort processing. Dual process theories argue that people make their decisions through 2 fundamentally different processes (Gawronski & Creighton, 2013; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986): the controlled process and the automatic process. When following the controlled process, people make their decisions using considerable cognitive resources (ie, higheffort processing). The controlled process is deliberative, slow, analytic, and conscious (Epstein, 1994; Evans, 2008). In the context of this study, SNS users may carefully consider the negative consequences of role conflict before sending messages. On the other hand, under the automatic process, people may make their decisions with fewer cognitive resources (ie, low‐effort processing). The automatic process is intuitive, rapid, automatic, and unconscious (Epstein, 1994; Evans, 2008). In such a scenario, they often rely on automatic heuristics (eg, cues, schemas, or expectations) to reduce the amount of cognitive resources spent. In the context of this study, SNS users may use low‐effort processing without carefully considering the potential impact of role conflict before sending messages. To summarize, dual process theories imply that privacy‐related decisions can be made with high‐effort or low‐effort processing, leading to differential effects of role conflict.

Dual process theories have been widely applied in previous literature. Some studies apply specific types of dua process theories (eg, elaboration likelihood model). For example, Ho and Bodoff (2014) examine how depth processing moderates the effect of personalization on perceived usefulness, which, in turn, influences attitude valence in the context of web personalization. Ma, Khansa, Deng, and Kim (2014) suggest that the characteristics of the reviewers and reviews, regarding determining the approach for information processing, moderate the relationship between the average rating of prior reviews and subsequent ratings. Cheng and Loi (2014) examine the effect of brand trust on intention to purchase under different processes. In previous privacy literature, Kobsa, Cho, and Knijnenburg (2016) examine how general privacy concerns and privacy self‐efficacy moderate the relationship between provide and perceived privacy protection. Other studies apply dual process theories in general following a meta‐theoretica approach. For example, Gwebu, Wang, and Guo (2014) apply dual process theories to understand continued usage intention of social media. These studies show that dual process theories are helpful to understand users' decision making following high‐effort versus low‐effort processing in a variety of contexts including privacy. Therefore, it is appropriate to apply dual process theories to examine the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure in high‐effort versus low‐effort processing.

Because the controlled process requires greater cognitive resources and is initiated intentionally, certain preconditions must be met for the controlled process to be initiated (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Thompson, Roman, Moskowitz, Chaiken, & Bargh, 1994). First, people must be sufficiently motivated. Otherwise, people may not want to initiate the controlled process and expend much effort to make decisions. Second, people must have the ability (eg, the relevant knowledge and cognitive resources) to perform high-effort processing, People have limited cognitive resources. To deal with quite a rich stimulus environment, people reserve their cognitive resources for relevant or important issues (refer to the “Least Effort Principle,” Allport, 1954; James, 1890). Thus, the automatic process is the default method for processing information. In other words, people prefer low‐effort processing not because the are lazy, but because they try to be economically minded and only expend their cognitive resources when trul needed (ie, if sufficiently motivated) (Bohner, Moskowitz, & Chaiken, 1995).

On the other hand, people do not focus solely on minimizing effort. They also desire a certain level of judgement confidence (Bohner et al., 1995). In other words, people seek a balance between expending the least amount of effort and having sufficient confidence during information processing. When the level of confidence drops below a certain point (eg, judgement based on the cues under the automatic process), people may be motivated to put more effort into processing information (ie. process information under the controlled process) until they feel that the thresholo of confidence has been reached or surpassed (Bohner et al., 1995).

Under low‐effort processing, information is evaluated by relatively automatic heuristics and mental shortcuts (Polites & Karahanna, 2012, 2013). Gwebu et al. (2014) summarize that people can make decisions under automatic or partially automatic processes with 2 mechanisms. One is the habit/automaticity mechanism, where people's decisions are guided by a “spontaneous and automatic process characterized by the over‐trained and hardwired habit mechanism” (p. 67). The other is the emotion‐based mechanism (Epstein, 1994), which is “a partially automatic process that involves a mixture of affect heuristic and deliberative evaluation of emotion” (p. 67). Following Gwebu et al. (2014), we argue that habit and emotion can influence people's information‐processing strategies during their self‐disclosure decision‐making.

Habit can influence people's information‐processing strategies (Ouellette & Wood, 1998). In this study, we define habit as the degree to which people tend to use a particular SNS to share information with others. Users' habits can provide mental shortcuts because habit is a learned behaviour that is automatically triggered with little conscious effort by environmental cues in a stable context (Ortiz & Markus, 2009). As users learn to use SNSs routinely, thei confidence regarding using SNSs generally increases (Gwebu et al., 2014). Furthermore, following the Least Effort Principle, people are likely to choose not to go through high‐effort processing when they make the same decisions each time (Aarts, Paulussen, & Schaalma, 1997). As a result of this, people are less motivated to engage in high‐effort processing, and the automatic process is more likely to be used

Emotion is defined as “a mental state of readiness that arises from cognitive appraisals of events or thoughts” (Bagozzi, Gopinath, & Nyer, 1999, p. 184). Compared with mood, emotion is relatively brief but intense, context specific, and intentional (Ekman. 1992: Eriida. 1993). Emotion typically emerges in response to situational events (ie, it is induced by a stimulus) in people's environment (Zhang, 2013). In this study, we adopt Russell's (2003) circumplex model, which describes emotion along 2 dimensions: valence (pleasant versus unpleasant) and arousal (activated versus deactivated). We are especially interested in the effect of emotions' valence on people's information‐processing strategies. Essentially, the valence of emotion can inform people about the nature of the current psychological situation (Schwarz, 1990) and alter people's motivation regarding information processing (Bohner et al., 1995).

## 3 | HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

The research model is developed by drawing upon boundary regulation and dual process theories to explain the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure on SNSs (Figure 3). Specifically, our study examines how role conflict influences privacy risk and perceived control, which, in turn, influence self‐disclosure behaviour. Following Xu, Dinev, Smith, and Hart (2011), we argue that privacy control and privacy risk are 2 key variables users consider when balancing the costs and benefits associated with self‐disclosure. Habit is also hypothesized to have a direct effect on self‐disclosure. Additionally, habit and emotion moderate the relationship between role conflict and privacy risk/perceived control. Thus, our research model states that role conflict influences self‐disclosure through privacy risk and perceived control. Further, users may follow different information processing strategies during self‐disclosure. When habit is relatively high or emotion is positive, users probably follow the automatic process; when habit is relatively low or emotion is negative, users probably follow the controlled process. In other words, we do not mean that there is a interaction effect between the controlled process and the automatic process. Instead, we argue that role conflict can influence self‐disclosure following either the controlled process or the automatic process, depending on the value of habit or emotion. ## 3.3 | The direct and moderating effects of habit

Habit is conceptualized as the learning outcomes of behaviours that automatically respond to certain contexts to achieve certain results or goals (Ronis, Yates, & Kirscht, 1989; Triandis, 1980). Once habits are formed, associated behaviours may be automatically triggered by specific situational cues (Aarts, Verplanken, & van Knippenberg, 1997). In such a context, habitual decisions may be made, and subsequent behaviours may be conducted without much deliberation. In other words, before a habit is formed, people may engage in high‐effort processing under th controlled process to decide their subsequent behaviours. As a habit is formed and increases in strength, peopl may reduce the cognitive effort involved (Least Effort Principle) and rely on relevant situational cues to make deci sions and perform certain behaviours. In such a context, they use low‐effort processing under the automatic process.

Previous literature has shown that habit reduces people's elaborateness of information use in their daily trave mode choices (Aarts, Verplanken, & van Knippenberg, 1997). On SNSs, habit is positively related to users' continued use of SNSs (Gwebu et al., 2014). Our study uses habit to refer to users' tendency to use SNSs and share messages. When the strength of habit is relatively high and people often use SNSs, they are likely to use SNSs with little conscious effort and make automatic decisions to send messages. Thus, in this situation, people are likely to process information with low levels of effort under the automatic process. Furthermore, once a habit is formed, users confidence in using SNSs increases (Gwebu et al., 2014), and they are likely to feel that they can better control their disclosed information. As a result of this, they are less motivated to engage in high‐effort processing. In such a context, role conflict may have a weaker effect on privacy risk and perceived control as people make decisions to send messages automatically, even if these messages may raise role conflicts for users. Conversely, when the strength o habit is relatively low, people may still spend a great deal of effort and carefully deliberate potential scenarios under the controlled process when they decide whether or not to send messages. In such a context, they are more likely t be aware of the consequent role conflict and perceive higher levels of privacy risk and lower levels of perceived control from sending these messages.

For example, some users may be used to sharing details of their lives on SNSs. They form a habit of sharing their thoughts and expressing their opinions at every opportunity. They can post their opinions regarding recent news, what they have done over the weekend, or their emotions at a certain point in time. Sharing messages thus becomes their daily routine and part of their everyday lives and users engage in sharing such messages almost without thinking. In such a context, they are likely to make privacy decisions under the automatic process and pay less attention to potential role conflict, as well as privacy risk and perceived control associated with sharing certain messages.

To summarize, we hypothesize that

H5 Habit will positively influence self‐disclosure.

H6a Habit will moderate the relationship between role conflict and privacy risk, such that role conflict has a stronger, positive effect on privacy risk when habit is relatively low, compared with when habit is relatively high.

H6b Habit will moderate the relationship between role conflict and perceived control, such that role conflict has a stronger, negative effect on perceived control when habit is relatively low, compared with when habit is relatively high

## 3.4 | The moderating effect of emotion

Although less examined in previous privacy literature (Li et al., 2011; Li, Sarathy, & Zhang, 2008), emotion is proposed as an important factor in understanding IS‐related phenomena in general (Zhang, 2013) and regarding privacy in particular (Dinev et al., 2015). For example, Li et al. (2011) find that fear has a negative effect on privacy risk, whil joy has a positive effect. Following previous literature on dual process theories (Bohner et al., 1995), we argue that the valence of emotion can influence people's information‐processing strategies by altering their motivation to process (Schwarz, 1990) when the arousal of emotion is relatively low.<sup>‡</sup> Specifically, emotion can inform people about the current psychological situation (Schwarz, 1990).

Previous literature has demonstrated that positive emotion reduces the amount of effort spent on information processing in comparison with neutral or negative moods (eg, Bless, Mackie, & Schwarz, 1992; Bohner, Crow, Erb, & Schwarz, 1992; Kuykendall & Keating, 1990). In other words, people with positive‐valence emotion are less likel to engage in high‐effort processing (ie, under the controlled process). Previous literature has also shown that people tend to underestimate the risks of information disclosure with positive emotion (Kehr, Kowatsch, Wentzel, & Fleisch, 2015). Positive‐valence emotion indicates a safe situation, in which there is no need for high‐effort processing, thus fostering the automatic process. On SNSs, when emotion is positive, users may feel that the curren situations are safe and dyadic boundaries are secure. As a result of this, users do not feel motivated to expend much effort. They are likely to conduct low‐effort processing under the automatic process and ignore the potential effect of role conflict. Thus, role conflict may have a weaker effect on privacy risk and perceived control.

Conversely, negative‐valence emotion informs a problematic or even threatening situation (ie, lowers people's confidence) (Petty, DeSteno, & Rucker, 2001), thus motivating people to devote more effort to information processing (ie, adopt the controlled process) to deal with the problematic situation. On SNSs, negative emotion ca inform users that the current situations are problematic and dyadic boundaries are unsecure. In such a context, users feel less confident and more motivated to rely on the controlled process and conduct high‐effort processin to regain control over the problematic situation (Bohner et al., 1995). As a result of this, they are more likely to be aware of the role conflict raised by sending messages and feel concerned about their privacy risk and perceive control. In such a context, role conflict is likely to have a positive effect on privacy risk and a negative effect on perceived control.

To summarize, we argue that

H7a Emotion will moderate the relationship between role conflict and privacy risk, such that role conflict has a stronger, positive effect on privacy risk when emotion is negative, compared with when emotion is positive.

H7b Emotion will moderate the relationship between role conflict and perceived control, such that role conflict has a stronger, negative effect on perceived control when emotion is negative, compared with when emotion is positive.

## 4 | METHOD

Our study develops hypotheses examining the effect of role conflict and how 2 mechanisms (ie, emotion and habit) moderate the effect of role conflict. Experimental studies were conducted to test our hypotheses; each experiment focuses on one mechanism (Table 1). Role conflict was manipulated by sending a certain message to everyone (ie, high role conflict) versus to a certain social circle (ie, low role conflict). The experiment method is appropriate for this study for 3 reasons. First, experiments explore the effect of variables that can be manipulated (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002). Our study focuses on the effect of role conflict that can be manipulated. Therefore, experiments can test the effect of role conflict. Second, the unique strength of experiments is in describing the consequences that result from manipulating a treatment (refer to “causal description,” Shadish et al., 2002). Therefore, through manipulating role conflict, our study can show how role conflict can influence privacy risk and perceived control, which in turn impac self‐disclosure. Further, since our study aims to examine people's high‐effort versus low‐effort information processing when dealing with role conflict, experimental methods are appropriate to manipulate the influence of cues (Dinev et al., 2015).

Second, WeChat provides the functionality of specifying the target audience for a certain message. This function can b used to manipulate role conflict.

To make the experimental environment close to the real environment, a professional software company was hire to develop a WeChat experimental system. Hypothetical scenarios have been applied in previous IS research (Okazaki, Navarro‐Bailón, & Molina‐Castillo, 2014; Xu, Teo, Tan, & Agarwal, 2009). This method is valid for this study for 2 main reasons. First, people's perception of role conflict and privacy risk is context dependent (eg, dependent upon the time, place, and the content of the message) and varies as the context changes (Acquisti, Brandimarte, & Loewenstein, 2015). Second, privacy measured by surveys may represent people's genera privacy perceptions, rather than specific privacy‐related behaviours (Smith et al., 2011), and is subject to the “privacy paradox” (Acquisti et al., 2015). To better examine the real psychological process triggered by role conflict, a hypothet ical scenario is used in this study.

## 4.4 | Study 1

A controlled experiment with 2 levels of role conflict (high versus low) was designed to investigate the effect of role conflict, testing H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, and H6. Finally, we assessed the mediating effect of privacy risk and perceived control, and methods from Study 1 were followed. Our results (Table 8) showed that perceived control fully mediated the relationship between role conflict and self‐disclosure.

## 4.5.4 | Study 2: discussion

Study 2 not only provides strong support of our mode but also shows that emotion moderates the relationshi between role conflict and privacy risk/perceived control. Although Study 1 and Study 2 provide strong support for our hypotheses, there are still limitations in those 2 experiments. Specifically, the differences between the low role conflict and high role conflict condition are not very large (although they were significant, with medium to large effect sizes). There are 2 possible reasons for this. First, the manipulation may not be strong enough to generate larger differences. Second, it could be due to the scales used for the manipulation check. Conceptually, people may feel a certain degree of role conflict unless they have only one role<sup>#</sup> on SNSs, which is not realistic. As our scales measure role conflict from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” most subjects select 3 or above, even when they were in the low role conflict condition,\*\* thus limiting the range of values for role conflict. To address these limitations and increase the effect of role conflict, we refined our scales and manipulations and replicated Study 1 and Study 2 with Study 3 and Study 4, respectively.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

This study examines the effect of role conflict, as well as the moderating effects of emotion and habit. The result from the 4 experiments provide strong evidence that role conflict has a positive effect on privacy risk and a negative effect on perceived control, which, in turn, impact self‐disclosure behaviour. Furthermore, habit and emotion moderate the relationships between role conflict and privacy risk / perceived control.

The result of H2 is not consistent between Study 2 and Study 4. Specifically, role conflict has a negative impact on perceived control in Study 2 but not in Study 4. One possibility is that as participants gain more experience of using SNSs (ie, WeChat in this study), they may deal with different social roles on SNSs more often. In such a context, they may still feel concerned about their privacy, but they may also consider that they have better control over thei information. As a result, the effect of role conflict on privacy control becomes insignificant in Study 4. Similarly, the moderation effects of habit (H6b in Study 3)/emotion (H7b in Study 4) on privacy control also become insignificant.

H3 is supported in Study 4 but not in Study 2. In other words, privacy risk has a negative effect on self‐disclosure in Study 4 but not in Study 2. As described above, the inconsistency between Study 2 and Study 4 may be due to different levels of usage experience (ie, habit). Therefore, it is possible that habit and emotion may have a joint interaction effect on privacy risk and perceive control. Future studies can examine how the effect of role conflict i moderated by emotion over time as users gain more usage experience of SNSs.

Habit has no significant effect on self‐disclosure. One possible explanation is that the context of our study is online dating and information disclosed is relatively sensitive. Therefore, participants may feel concerned about information disclosure, and habit is not sufficient to influence their decisions. Future studies could test our model in other contexts (ie, less sensitive contexts) and examine whether the relationship between habit and self‐disclosure behaviour becomes significant.

## 5.1 | Implications for theory

Our study makes several important theoretical contributions. First, while SNS users nowadays interact with different social circles and have different roles on SNSs, few studies have examined the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure behaviour. This study addresses this gap in the privacy literature by clarifying how users' dyadic boundaries can become blurred because of role conflict. We argue that role conflict can influence privacy risk and perceived control which, in turn, impact self‐disclosure behaviour. Our study thus contributes to previous literature by clarifying the mechanisms through which role conflict influences self‐disclosure. Previous literature suggests that role conflict is positively related to interactional and psychological privacy concerns (Zhang et al., 2011). Our study demonstrates that role conflict indeed has a negative effect on privacy risk, by manipulating the level of role conflict in the experiments.

Second, our study shows how habit can moderate the relationship between role conflict and privacy risk. Previous research shows that habit is positively related to SNS usage (Gwebu et al., 2014). Our study clarifies that habit can influence users' information‐processing strategies. As habit increases, users tend to devote less effort and are more likely to make decisions regarding sending messages under the automatic process. In such a context, role conflict has a less effect on privacy risk, as users tend to make decisions automatically and ignore the potential negative effects of role conflict. For example, users with a habit of using SNSs may frequently share what they are doing and consider SNS usage as part of their lives. As a result of this, they may not carefully consider potential negative consequences associated with information sharing. Our study thus supports dual process theories by showing that users can make self‐disclosure decisions following the habit/automaticity process (Gwebu et al., 2014). Our study also contributes to previous literature by clarifying how habit can influence users' information processing strategies during self-disclosure and further understanding of users' self-disclosure decisions under high-effort versus low-effort processing.

Third, this study contributes to IS literature in general and privacy literature in particular by elucidating the effect of emotions on privacy. Affective issues, although important in understanding IS‐related phenomena, are often overlooked within the IS field (Zhang, 2013). Dinev et al. (2015) also suggest that affect is important in further understanding privacy issues. Our study shows that emotional valence can influence people's information‐processing strategies. Specifically, we argue that, when emotion becomes negative, users are likely to perceive the current environment as problematic and feel more motivated to devote a great deal of effort to information processing. In such a context, users are likely to feel concerned about role conflict (ie, role conflict has a positive effect on privacy risk and a negative effect on perceived control), making the decision to send messages under the controlled process. Our study thus supports dual process theories by showing that users can make self‐disclosure decisions following the emotion‐based process (Gwebu et al., 2014). The results of our study also further understanding of users' self‐disclosure decisions under high‐effort versus low‐effort processing.

Our study can thus help explain the results from Preibusch (2015), examining people's privacy behaviour after Snowden. Immediately after the PRISM revelation, people may have felt strong negative emotions and felt greatly concerned about their privacy on the internet. Thus, they were likely to have made privacy‐related decisions with high‐effort processing and started to use various tools to protect their privacy immediately after Snowden. How ever, the negative emotions eventually faded, and people may have then felt less concerned about their privacy. Thus, they may revert to low‐effort processing and stop using various technologies to protect their privacy. As a result of this, the usage of privacy‐enhancing technologies did not increase much because of the PRISM revelation in the long term.

Taken together, our study extends previous privacy literature by investigating the effect of role conflict under both high‐effort and low‐effort processing strategies. Our study thus highlights how users make privacy‐related decisions differently with various information‐processing strategies and responds to the call for more studie examining how low‐effort processes determine privacy‐related behaviours (Dinev et al., 2015).

## 5.2 | Implications for practice

This study also reveals new implications for SNS users and providers. Our study shows that role conflict is indeed an important issue and can have negative effects (ie, can positively influence privacy risk and negatively influence perceived control). Given the significant consequences of role conflict, we recommend that SNS users should carefully consider the content that is to be published, as well as the potential audience of the published messages. In particular, we suggest that users should carefully consider their audience prior to sending messages on SNSs. Our results also show that role conflict tends to be ignored when users' habits are relatively high or their emotions are positive. Therefore, we advocate that experienced users should keep track of their audience and be aware of different social circles on SNSs. Additionally, when feeling positive emotions, users may want to wait a while and calm down before sending messages, to avoid making decisions without carefu deliberation.

We also recommend that SNS providers should provide technical features to help users deal effectively with role conflict, since role conflict can increase privacy risk and decrease perceived control. Karahasanović et al. (2009) show that users desire SNS features that allow them to choose which friends can see particular content within the community itself. To this end, we advocate that SNS providers should allow users to classify their SNS friends into different groups and then decide which groups can view certain messages. When users intend to disclose certain information through broadcasting, SNS providers could pop up warning messages and remind users that updated messages can be viewed by everyone in their contacts. SNS providers may also allow users to view their updates from the perspectives of their different social circles, such that users can better understand how their updates can be viewed and by whom. Such viewing can help reduce users' role conflict and thus enhance users' self‐disclosure. SNS providers could also consider adding certain features (eg, linguistic inquiry and word count) (Pennebaker, Booth, & Francis, 2007) to detect the emotion of messages that users plan to pub lish. If a positive emotion is detected, SNSs may show a warning to provide users with another chance to make the decision to send messages carefully.

## 5.3 | Limitations and opportunities for future studies

This study has several limitations. First, our study examines role conflict in a hypothetical scenario in which a user is considering sending a message about online dating. It is possible that the results may not generalize to other contexts of role conflict on SNSs. In our 4 experiments, student subjects were used. As students are major users of WeChat and SNSs in China (Alibuybuy, 2015), our results may be generalized to other SNS users in China. Nevertheless, future studies are needed to test our model with participants from other backgrounds. Further, our results may only be generalizable to Chinese SNS users. Future studies are needed to test and extend our research model wit participants from other cultural backgrounds.

Our contributions may also be limited by using an experimental system mimicking WeChat. While the system resembled the general layout of real WeChat, the system may not reflect the actual environment entirely. However, the actual environment could not allow us to manipulate the role conflict (ie, decide whether or not to send a message about online dating). Therefore, the use of this experimental system was required, despite the limitations it caused Future studies could verify the impact of role conflict in a more natural setting and examine the impact of role conflict when users are actually using SNSs.

This study focuses on the effect of emotion with low levels of arousal. It is possible that the results could differ with high‐arousal emotions. Dinev et al. (2015) argue that high levels of intense emotions can reduce the processing of detail‐relevant information. In such a context, users probably adopt the automatic process, even if the emotion is negative. Future studies could expand our study and examine emotions with high levels of arousal. Furthermore, thi study only examines 2 moderators (ie, habit and emotion). Future studies could examine how other moderators could influence people's information‐processing strategies and make privacy‐related decisions (Dinev et al., 2015).

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Users often experience role conflict when their social circles on SNSs are quite diverse. This study integrates the perspective of boundary regulation and dual process theories to examine the effect of role conflict on self‐disclosure. Our results show that role conflict positively influences perceived risk and negatively influences perceived control; furthermore, users' habits and emotions moderate these relationships. Our study yields important implications for theory and practice. Future studies are needed to extend our study in other contexts and to examine other possible moderators.
