Abst ract : The provision of services has become an increasingly important component of the economy of industrialized countries and the revenue stream for many traditional product companies. This is especially true for companies that offer information technology (IT) products. This paper examines factors that are associated with the extent to which IT product companies are able to develop service revenue, which we refer to as service expansion of IT product companies. We identify the characteristics of the product portfolio—specifically, the composition and scope of firm offerings among hardware, application software, and infrastructure software—as key to successful service expansion. We also propose that this relationship is moderated by prior performance of the product business and industry characteristics such as concentration and maturity. Data from IT product vendors spanning five years are used to test the proposed relationships. Overall, this research provides a theoretical foundation for understanding service expansion and diversification in the IT industry as well as practical guidance for IT product companies considering expansion to services.

Key words and phras es : IT services, IT stack, IT vendors, service diversification, software product portfolio.

Indust rialized economies are experiencing a dramat ic s hift from the production of products to the production of services, with services now accounting for more than 50 percent of the labor force in Brazil, Russia, Japan, and Germany, as well as 75 percent of the labor force in the United States and the United Kingdom. This unparalleled segment growth is not only due to the growth from the service sector but also from the migration of traditional product companies from manufacturing toward providing services. Services provided by product companies, also known as the “hidden service sector” [47], have increased both in the percentage of revenues generated and their strategic role in firm growth [82]. In 2004, 22 percent of sales in the durable goods industry came from product-related services—reaching \$500 billion—compared with 12 percent in 1995 [4]. Information technology (IT) product companies, in particular, are making strategic choices to move toward the provision of services [26]. Hardware vendors face competitive markets that are characterized by falling prices, and software vendors often have to compete with open source options that are available for free. Moreover, technology buyers are increasingly concerned with total cost of ownership. These factors make the expansion of traditional IT products to services a strategic priority for IT product companies and an important area for research.

Past research on services provided by product companies suggests that services can help product companies increase profitability [37, 38, 88, 103] while reducing the vulnerability and volatility of cash flow [87]. In addition to being a revenue source, services also provide a strategic opportunity for future growth, especially when product markets become saturated or when product technologies become mature [82]. Moreover, the vendors that provide both products and services can enjoy the benefit of being a solution provider—that is, a vendor who provides a customized combination of products and services for customers [96]. Davies [30] argued that product and service businesses complement each other—that is, design feedback obtained from services can provide strategic direction for product development, while the integration of serviceable components into products can enable new services. In summary, these studies have focused on service expansion as a management action (independent variable) and examined the resulting consequences for firms. Despite these arguments identifying the advantages of service expansion for product companies, little research has explored the organizational drivers of successful service expansion, making this an open area for research. An implicit assumption is that IT product vendors provide services to exploit their existing competencies, especially the competencies accumulated through the product business [82]. However, no research has explored the specific competencies that enable or motivate these firms to expand their offerings to include services.

The goal of this research is to identify the factors that enable product firms to successfully expand their service offerings. We consider product companies providing services as an expansion into a new but related line of business, such that knowledge and resources from the existing product business are relevant to understanding the new service business [50, 51, 71, 94].<sup>1</sup> This suggests that the product portfolios of IT vendors are likely to be a key enabler of service expansion, as vendors with different product portfolios will have different knowledge resources [51, 72]. We focus on two kinds of knowledge resources—customer knowledge and architectural knowledge— because they are critical to the provision of services. We further suggest that a stack framework, which includes layers (from bottom to top) of hardware, infrastructure software, and application software, may prove to be an appropriate way of characterizing the IT vendor’s product portfolio (see Figure 1). Past research has suggested that the stack structure can be used to understand the complementarity between IT products and the strategic positioning of IT companies [41, 42]. In this paper, we use the stack characteristics as a way to understand the type and relevance of the knowledge available to the IT vendors for service expansion.

Specifically, we conduct an analysis relating product portfolios of IT vendors— characterized through the IT product stack—to service expansion, with customer and architectural knowledge providing the underlying theoretical motivation for this linkage. In addition, drawing from the notion that firm resource leverage varies under different contexts [69, 101], we expect that prior performance of the product business may also be relevant to understanding the extent to which IT vendors leverage knowledge resources from the product portfolio in service expansion. Lastly, we explore the relationship between industry characteristics (i.e., concentration and maturity) and firm service expansion. We empirically test these theoretical relationships using data from 47 IT product companies between 2001 and 2005.

This research makes several contributions. Drawing on past research that has examined the unique characteristics of services (intangibility, heterogeneity, and inseparability), we find that the product portfolio of the IT vendor—characterized through the IT stack—is associated with effective service expansion. In particular, we find that firms with a stack that focuses more on software than hardware and firms with a more diversified product stack are able to derive higher revenue from services. This suggests that the customer knowledge and architectural knowledge provided by the higher layers of the stack and by operating at multiple stack layers are particularly relevant to service expansion of product firms. In addition, the results of our study indicate that past performance in the product business accentuates the above-mentioned product-related effects. Finally, we incorporate an exploratory analysis on industry factors, finding that industry concentration and maturity are each relevant to service expansion.

## Theoretical Background

## Service Offerings

The role of s ervices in product firms has evolved over t ime. Initially, product companies viewed services as “necessary costs” [24], minimizing resource commitments to services and containing the costs of services as much as possible. This view later evolved to where product companies considered services as a part of the productmarketing strategy and important to influencing satisfaction with product purchases, improving new product adoption, enhancing supplier credibility, and maintaining long-term relationships with customers [18]. Although services were important to both first-time and repetitive sales, they were viewed as supplementary to products and not counted as an important revenue source.

Recently, services have gained a more significant role in helping product companies meet their strategic goals. One key aspect of this change is that services are viewed as independent and important sources of revenue rather than as marketing tools to promote or differentiate products. Hewlett-Packard, for instance, has defined “tomorrow’s sustainable business” as one in which it shifts from “selling disposable products to selling a range of services around fewer products” [79, p. 83]. This enables the services to be more responsive to customer needs, helping to facilitate customer outcomes rather than promoting only product sales. For instance, firms could offer services that use competitors’ products when the firms’ existing products do not meet customer needs. Matheiu [66] has called this kind of service “service as a product.”

In this research, we are specifically interested in the degree to which firms are able to develop revenues using the view of “service as a product.” It should be noted that this view omits general customer services, such as those provided through toll-free telephone lines, in-store sales staff, and Web sites. Customer service addresses the general quality of interactions between a seller and customer [73], and although these services can be sources of competitive advantage, they do not correspond to the view of “service as a product.”

For IT product vendors, demands from their customers create opportunities to expand to services. Based on interviews with chief information officers (CIOs), Bates et al. [11] found that companies began to cut their IT budgets in 2001 by freezing new investments and continued this reduction in 2002 by focusing on the cost of maintaining existing applications and infrastructure. In addition, software-as-a-service (SaaS) and application-hosting services can reduce the initial capital required for new IT projects, making services attractive to CIOs with budget constraints [90]. Under these models, organizations pay a subscription to consume an IT service rather than purchase the specific IT products involved. These trends have the potential to create opportunities for IT product vendors who can use existing knowledge resources to expand to services.

In the next section, we discuss the role of different types of firm knowledge relevant to service expansion. We then discuss IT vendor offerings associated with the IT product stack, which is used as a way of characterizing the knowledge available to firms for expansion to services.

## Firm Knowledge and Service Offerings

As mentioned earlier, for our study we consider product companies providing services as an expansion into a new, but related, line of business. Previous findings in the related diversification literature suggest that firms expand to new markets to exploit their existing experiences, resources, or knowledge [74, 94] and that related offerings improve overall firm performance [46, 92, 93]. Helfat and Lieberman proposed that “the greater the similarity between pre-entry firm resources and the required resources in an industry, the greater the likelihood that a firm will enter that particular industry, and the greater the likelihood that the firm will survive and prosper” [50, p. 727]. For instance, Helfat [49] demonstrated that in response to rising oil prices during the 1970s, petroleum firms expanded to coal conversion to leverage their existing knowledge in oil refining. King and Tucci [56] showed that experiences in the production and sales of large disk drives facilitated firms’ entry into the market for smaller ones. Klepper and Simons [57] found that firms that produced radios expanded to television markets more aggressively and more successfully when they had greater experiences in the home radio market, as the technology associated with the home radio closely resembles that of television. Studies have also found that professional services firms expand internationally to exploit their human and relational capital [53]. Overall, prior research has suggested that when firms expand to new markets, they do so by leveraging existing knowledge and experiences. As customer knowledge and technological knowledge are considered important for new product success and overall competitive advantage, especially in the IT industry [72, 86, 93], we focus on customer knowledge and one particular type of technological knowledge—architectural knowledge—and discuss how they contribute to service expansion.

## Customer Knowledge

Customer knowledge refers to the knowledge of customer needs, preferences, and buying behavior [92, 104]. Services are tailored offerings developed specifically for customers [106], and the level of customization has been found to significantly enhance customers’ overall attitude toward an offering [105]. Firms with more knowledge of customers’ needs and preferences are more likely to develop service offerings that are highly aligned with business processes, thus creating more value [53, 59].

Customer knowledge also includes knowledge of how to coordinate and interact with customers, which is particularly important for services [37, 83]. One of the fundamental characteristics of services is that production involves input and participation from customers [40]. Software customization services, for instance, involve collecting information about system requirements from customers, and software implementations require end users to participate in training and often to change their work practices. Research on IT services suggests that managing coordination with customers is one of the core competencies of IT service companies [62, 65]. Thus, product companies with knowledge of how to coordinate with customers are more likely to attract and retain customers in their service business.

Customer knowledge associated with business processes is particularly important for IT vendors offering services. When IT product vendors provide services, the focus of their offerings is extended from the products themselves to the management of business processes in which the products are deployed [81]. Xerox, for example, has moved away from its core products (printing devices) and instead focuses more on helping customers improve efficiencies in their document-intensive business processes. In such cases, knowledge of business processes can enable venders to provide IT services that are closely aligned with customer needs. Thus, customer knowledge of business processes is expected to be critical for IT product vendors expanding to services.

## Architectural Knowledge

Architectural knowledge is the knowledge about the ways in which components are integrated and linked together into a coherent whole [52] and is important for firms providing services. Service performance specifications often include criteria that pertain to a larger system of products that must work together [12, 90] (e.g., 99.99 percent availability or improvement of efficiency by 30 percent), whereas product performance specifications are typically related to product-specific attributes (e.g., speed of a CPU [central processing unit] or size of RAM [random access memory]). As a result, managing and improving service performance requires product vendors to have not only intimate technological knowledge of the focal products but also knowledge of the interactions between the focal products and other components in a complex system [31]. For example, an industrial company with bearings as its core product had to acquire knowledge of how their bearings interact with other components (i.e., architectural knowledge) in order to effectively offer services associated with prevent ing machine breakdown [77].

The definition of architectural knowledge can be further clarified for IT systems as the knowledge of how different layers in the IT stack interact and work as a whole. Knowledge of how layers of the stack interact is essential to being able to offer IT services [89]. Davies [30] argued that the ability to provide solutions combining products and services rather than stand-alone products is enabled by the systemintegration capabilities of product firms, and architectural knowledge is a critical part of system-integration capabilities [17]. The importance of architectural knowledge is particularly apparent for IT product vendors providing SaaS where, in addition to knowledge about their software solutions, they also need to be aware of how to offer the software across different infrastructure and computing platforms [89].

## Firm Knowledge and IT Product Characteristics

Because firm knowledge is extremely difficult to measure directly, we utilize the characteristics of the product portfolios of IT vendors as a way to characterize this knowledge. This choice is driven by past findings that firms develop knowledge through their experiences [10], accumulating core knowledge by developing and supporting their products. Helfat and Raubitschek noted, “at any given point in time, an organization’s portfolio of products serves as a platform for future product sequences” [51, p. 961]. Nerkar and Roberts [72] argued that firm knowledge of technology and customers develops as a function of their experiences in technology and product-market domains. Following a similar rationale, we use the product portfolio—characterized through the IT stack—to conceptualize the depth and relevance of customer and architectural knowledge of IT vendors.

We characterize the IT product portfolio as a stack containing layers (from bottom to top) of hardware, infrastructure software, and application software. The layers are considered to be components that complement each other and work together as a system. A stack-based framework has been found to be useful in numerous technical contexts, where the stack indicates how related components work together to perform complex operations. A stack framework has also been found to be important in understanding the strategic actions of IT companies as well as abnormal returns resulting from mergers, acquisitions, and alliances of IT firms [41, 42].

The layers of the IT stack differ from one another in a number of ways. Hardware is a material product that must be manufactured and assembled by the vendor. Infrastructure and application software each differ from hardware in that they are information products rather than physical products. Infrastructure software operates hardware and provides interoperability among application software. Application software helps end users perform a specific task or supports a specific business process.

In this research, we focus on two characteristics of the IT product stack: stack composition and stack scope. The product stack composition depicts the significance of each layer in the stack to a firm’s product portfolio and can be assessed by the proportion of product sales at each layer of the stack (i.e., the degree to which firms derive revenues from hardware, infrastructure software, and application software). The product stack scope is defined as the degree to which the existing product business is diversified across the levels of the product stack (i.e., the degree to which firms have offerings across hardware, infrastructure software, and application software). While some firms provide products in only one layer of the product stack, others have offerings across hardware, infrastructure software, and application software.

## Product Stack Composition

The product stack composition—or the proportion of hardware, infrastructure software, and application software in the product portfolio—is expected to be associated with the proportion of revenue derived from services, such that higher levels of the stack are associated with increased service offerings. The rationale is that providing services requires vendors to have customer knowledge, and IT products from different layers of the product stack are expected to differ in the degree to which they contribute to the acquisition of relevant customer knowledge by the vendors. In particular, application software can be expected to be associated with more customer knowledge relevant for service provision than infrastructure software, which can be expected to be associated with more customer knowledge relevant for service provision than hardware. We expect that it is access to this customer knowledge that will both facilitate and incentivize entry into the services business.

During the process of customizing their products to suit the specific needs of their customers, IT product companies often need to invest time and human capital to acquire customer knowledge, with the extent of customer knowledge dependent on the characteristics of the products provided. Hardware products are often ready to be used with limited customization. Software, in contrast, usually needs to be customized to match the business processes of the customers. Thus, software vendors are likely to accumulate more customer knowledge through customization of the software. Comparing application software and infrastructure software, application software is used in specific business functions that vary depending on the firm and industry [32], requiring vendors of application software to acquire knowledge of customers’ business domain and their specific business processes; infrastructure software is used in providing support for hardware and interoperability between application software [32], requiring less domain knowledge than application software.

Interactions with customers are another source of customer knowledge. Employees of hardware vendors are buffered from customers to some extent, whereas employees of software vendors need to interact with customers to assess business needs and customize the software [8, 80]. Thus, software vendors are likely to accumulate more customer knowledge through their interactions with customers. Not all software vendors, however, have the same amount of customer contact. While vendors of infrastructure software mainly interact with IT personnel in the client organization, vendors of application software need to interact with both end users and IT personnel. A broader view of customers’ business through interactions with multiple stakeholders enables vendors to identify customer needs [97], an important aspect of customer knowledge. Furthermore, interactions with end users during the customization and user training help application software vendors develop social capital with their customers [35, 70], facilitating the transfer of valuable information on customer needs [95]. For instance, IBM uses feedback from their product customers to construct their bundles of products and services [60].

Customer-specific knowledge can also lead to customer-specific absorptive capacity for continued learning [53], which will reduce the costs of information acquisition from customers in future relationships. This may accentuate distinctions in the customer knowledge resulting from different layers of the IT product stack. For example, as a result of lower costs of acquiring customer information, vendors of application software will be more able to learn new information about customers by leveraging their existing knowledge [36].

Overall, these arguments suggest that application software is associated with higher levels of customer knowledge relevant for service provision than infrastructure software, which is associated with higher levels of customer knowledge relevant for service provision than hardware. Companies with greater customer knowledge will have more incentives and competences to exploit such knowledge through providing services to customers, given that customer knowledge is a critical component of providing services [68, 77]. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: Stack composition is associated with service expansion in such a way that the positive effect of (a) application software is greater than hardware, (b) infrastructure software is greater than hardware, and (c) application software is greater than infrastructure software (i.e., application software > infrastructure software > hardware).

## Product Stack Scope

The product stack scope—or the degree to which firms diversify their offerings across hardware, infrastructure software, and application software—is expected to be positively associated with the proportion of revenue derived from services. Firms with a wider product scope are more likely to possess architectural knowledge because such knowledge is necessary for them to design, develop, and deploy their products. These companies are likely to more effectively integrate products from different stack layers because of their intimate knowledge about the products in each layer. It is expected that this architectural knowledge will act as a firm resource for the services business, facilitating service expansion.

Firms with a diversified product scope are more likely to acquire customer knowledge from multiple touch-points with the customers, leading to a holistic view of customer business processes and needs. This in turn will help them design and introduce services that better satisfy customers [97]. Furthermore, companies with a diversified product scope are likely to absorb and interpret their experiences with their customers. Absorptive capacity theory suggests that the ability of firms to absorb external knowledge depends on their existing knowledge [23]. This literature provides strong evidence that organizational scope, defined as the number of markets in which a firm operates, represents the firm’s capacity to absorb heterogeneous information. Studies by Haunschild and Sullivan [48] and Ingram and Baum [54] suggest that an increase in the firm’s scope is associated with its improved capacity to process and absorb information and experience received from outside organizational boundaries. In a similar vein, IT vendors with a wider product stack scope are more likely to absorb higher levels of customer knowledge.

Overall, IT vendors with a wider stack scope are likely to have both greater architectural and customer knowledge. Given the importance of architectural and customer knowledge in providing services [17, 30, 68, 77], we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2: Product stack scope is positively associated with service expansion of IT product companies.

## Moderating Effects of Product Business Performance

We expect pressures from product business performance to moderate the relationship between product-market experiences and a firm’s expansion to services, such that poor business performance will make existing resource-based effects stronger. Researchers supporting the concept that “necessity is the mother of invention” have provided compelling theoretical arguments that the gap between performance and aspiration stimulates a search for new solutions and organizational change [29]. Empirical studies have also confirmed that a performance shortfall is a driver for organizational innovation. McKinley [67] demonstrated that sales declines are associated with patent applications and product innovation in the manufacturing industry. Chandler [19] posited that firms responded to deteriorating performance by searching for new forms of organization, such as M-form structures.

Past findings on risk-taking also lend support to the “decline stimulates innovation” point of view. Based on prospect theory, decision makers become more risk taking in the domain of losses [98]. Empirical studies have also demonstrated the positive relationship between low performance and the risk-taking behavior of companies [16]. Corporate turnaround theorists also suggest that firms with declining performance will pursue actions to adjust their strategy [9].

Firms with declining performance will seek new strategic directions by leveraging their existing resources. Bowman and Hurry [14] suggested that resources of firms serve as a bundle of options that provide flexibility in response to environmental changes, and the execution of options is usually triggered by the gap between performance and aspiration. Further, Voss et al. [101] found that the relationship between related resource endowment and explorative strategies of firms is amplified by perceived environmental threat. Similarly, Miles [69] found that tobacco firms diversified strategically into other consumer goods markets to exploit their experiences in distribution channels when demand for cigarettes declined. These findings suggest that lagging performance in a product business will accelerate a product firms transition toward services through use of their existing knowledge resources. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Product business performance negatively moderates the relationship between stack composition and service expansion in such a way that the moderation effect will be greater (a) for application software than for hardware, (b) for infrastructure software than for hardware, and (c) for application software than for infrastructure software (i.e., application software > infrastructure software > hardware).

Hypothesis 4: Product business performance will negatively moderate the relationship between product scope and service expansion.

## Data and Method

We us ed dat a collect ed from t he Compust at dat abas e and firm annual reports to test our research model using public firms in the IT industry. We followed the industry classification scheme developed by Standard & Poor’s and Morgan Stanley Capital International, referred to as GICS (Global Industry Classifications Standard) to identify the subsectors relevant to our research model [13, 64]. In particular, the GICS is used in this paper because it provides a more detailed classification regarding software companies. Software companies in the GICS are grouped into application software companies and system software companies, while the Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) puts both groups under “prepackaged” software. Such a detailed grouping helps us identify the stack layer where the companies mainly operate. Firms in the application software, system software, communications equipment, computer hardware, and computer storage and peripheral industries were identified as fitting our sample frame. We further refined our sampling frame by cross-checking the SIC code that mapped to the GICS groups. This resulted in the elimination of subsectors such as radio and television broadcasting, wireless communications equipment manufacturing, telephone apparatus manufacturing, and power and communication line and related structures construction from our sampling frame, as these subsectors are typically not considered part of the IT industry.

We identified the firms in the relevant subsectors and selected the top 100 firms based on their 2005 revenues. We then collected data for the sample firms from the annual reports available through Mergent Online for the years 2001–5, yielding a sample of 152 firm-year observations for 47 firms. ## Measures

It has been a common practice for technology companies to distinguish product revenues from services revenues in their annual reports. The revenue breakdown between products and services is normally shown in the table “Results of Operations” in Item 7 (“Management’s Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of Operations”) and the table “Consolidated Statements of Operations” in Item 15 (“Exhibits, Financial Statement Schedules and Reports on Form 8-K”). As for revenues from different product lines, companies disclose such information to varied extents. Some companies disclose the information based on a categorization similar to the IT product stack—that is, hardware, infrastructure software, and application software. Other companies list revenue based on the names of their products. For the former situations, we read through the firms’ business description and the description of their product lines in their annual reports to make sure that their categorization is the same as that of our product stack. For the latte, we read through the firms’ annual reports, especially the description of business and product lines, as well as other sources (including Hoover’s Online, company Web sites, and trade journals that evaluate those products) to assign their products to layers in the IT stack. ## Service Expansion

Service expansion (SE) is measured as a ratio of service revenue (SR) [82] to total revenue (TR), as shown in Equation (1). Total revenue is the sum of service revenue and product revenue (PR) [82], as shown in Equation (2). This measure of service revenue ratio captures the extent to which firms rely on services as a source of revenue and has been used in past research [28].

$$
S E = \frac {S R}{T R}\tag{1}
$$

$$
T R = S R + P R.\tag{2}
$$

Our measure of service expansion is similar to that used in studies of internationalization (foreign sales as a proportion of total sales) [43, 45, 91]. While the measure of internationalization captures the relative size and strategic importance of foreign operations [44], our measure of service expansion examines the relative size and strategic importance of the services business.

## Stack Composition

Stack composition is measured as the proportion of product revenue in each stack. The product revenue is the sum of application software revenue (AR), infrastructure software revenue (IR), and hardware revenue (HR). The application software revenue ratio (ARR) is calculated by dividing the application software revenue by the product revenue, as shown in Equation (4). Similarly, the infrastructure software revenue ratio (IRR) is calculated by dividing the infrastructure software revenue by product revenue, as shown in Equation (5). The hardware revenue ratio (HRR) is calculated by dividing the hardware revenue by product revenue, as shown in Equation (6).

$$
P R = I R + A R + H R\tag{3}
$$

$$
A R R = \frac {A R}{P R}\tag{4}
$$

$$
I R R = \frac {I R}{P R}\tag{5}
$$

$$
H R R = \frac {H R}{P R}.\tag{6}
$$

## Stack Scope

We calculated the Herfindahl index to capture firms’ concentration in different IT stack layers and use 1 minus the Herfindahl index as a measure for stack scope (SS), as shown in Equation (7). The Herfindahl index has been used as a common measure for concentration in finance and strategy research [33, 45, 53, 85, 91], with 1 minus the Herfindahl index used as a measure of diversification. For instance, a firm that produces only hardware will have a Herfindahl index of 1 and a stack scope of 0. We consider only a firm’s diversification in the product market, so we use the share of each layer in the product revenue rather than total revenue.

$$
S S = 1 - \left(H R R ^ {2} + I R R ^ {2} + A R R ^ {2}\right).\tag{7}
$$

## Product Business Performance

We used product sales growth (PSG) as a proxy for a firm’s product business performance. Sales growth is an important financial performance measure [93, 100]. More importantly, industry analysts commonly use it as a key metric in valuing IT firms [27]. Product sales growth is the change of product sale (PS) in a year t from the previous year, t – 1, divided by product sales of the previous year:

$$
P S G _ {t} = \frac {P S _ {t} - P S _ {t - 1}}{P S _ {t - 1}}.\tag{8}
$$

## Control Variables

We control for firm size and year effects using firm assets as a proxy for firm size and dummy variables for years. In addition, we control for differences between consumer goods and industrial goods using a dummy variable, as services for consumer goods differ from services for industrial goods. We also control for research and development (R&D) spending because of its potential effects on firm knowledge and service expansion.

## Model and Analysis

We report ordinary least square (OLS) [43] regression with all the firms and years pooled. We lagged the dependent variable by one year to assess the impact of product stack scope and composition from year t – 1 on the service expansion in year t. We used Huber–White robust standard errors in the OLS estimation to obtain a heteroskedasticity-consistent estimation of the standard errors.

We use hierarchical regression to test our hypotheses. Model 1 includes all of the control variables. We test H1 and H 2 in model 2, adding the independent variables, stack composition (ARR, IRR, and HRR), and stack scope (SS). Because ARR, IRR, and HRR add up to 1, HRR is dropped from the regression and the coefficient of ARR and IRR are relative to a base of HRR. We test H3 in model 3, adding the interaction terms between stack composition and product sales growth (ARR\*PSG, IRR\*PSG,

$H R R ^ { * } P S G )$ to model 2. Because ARR, IRR, and HRR add up to 1, the interaction $P S G ^ { * } H R R$ is dropped from the regression and the coefficient of the $A R R ^ { * } P S G ,$ $I R R ^ { * } P S G$ are relative to a base of $H R R ^ { * } P S G$ . In model 4, we added the interaction term between stack scope and product sales growth $( S S ^ { * } P S G )$ to model 2 to test H4. The variables were centered prior to calculation of the interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity using the procedure outlined by Aiken and West [1]. We also checked the variance inflation factor (VIF) of all the models and found that it was less than 6 for each model, indicating no serious concerns with multicollinearity among the variables in the model.

## Robustness Checks and Post Hoc Analyses

## Robustness Checks

To check the robustness of our results and to account for the potential correlation in error terms among observations of a firm in different years, we also ran the analyses with Huber–White standard errors clustered by firms. This produces a robust estimation of standard errors given the presence of arbitrary correlations in error terms within a cluster. This method, also called “Rogers standard errors,” is robust to different specifications of the dependence in error terms within a cluster [75, 78]. The results shown in Table 3 are consistent with those from Table 2, although the significance level of some of the variables decreases.

To check whether our nonsignificant results are due to a lack of statistical power, we conducted post hoc power analysis using G\*Power software [21, 22, 39]. The results indicate that our sample size is adequate at the moderate-to-large effect level.

## Industry Analysis

Research in industrial organization has focused on industry structure as a primary explanation for firm action and performance [5], and there is some evidence that product firms expand to services as a result of industry-related threats. Shrinking product profit margins due to fierce competition is often cited as an important reason for firms to move toward services [82]. Providing services (i.e., consultation, implementation, and training) can help with mitigating such uncertainty and risk, thus leading to better acceptance of new products by customers. In the matured stage of an industry, stagnated demand for products and a large install base (providing service opportunities) are often cited as motivation for product companies to provide services [103]. Thus, the move to services could be associated with both industries that are growing (i.e., to eliminate the uncertainty and risk about the products) as well as industries that have matured (i.e., to create new revenue sources). For example, Ericsson created Ericsson Services to become a complete supplier and system integrator in 1999. Three years before, in Ericsson’s 1996 Annual Report, the firm recognized that “deregulation and more competition in telecom markets are forcing operators to move closer to the final consumer in the value stream.”<sup>2</sup>

While our model identifies the specific firm-level drivers of services expansion, it is also possible that industry-level factors by themselves—and in association with firmlevel factors—are important in explaining firm strategy and outcomes. In particular, we examine an alternate model that incorporates industry concentration (IC) and industry maturity (IM). Industry concentration is measured by the proportion of sales from the largest four firms in an industry, indicating the degree to which large firms dominate the competition. Industry maturity is measured by the sales growth of the industry and indicates whether overall demand for the related products and services is increasing or decreasing.

Because we do not have strong theory to predict the direction or nature (i.e., direct or moderating) of the relationship between industry characteristics and service expansion, in addition to our research model we conduct an associated exploratory analysis involving industry concentration and industry maturity. Exploratory research does not focus on specific causal relationships, but rather creates “descriptive reports of phenomena, their correlates, and the contexts where they manifest” [15, p. 93]. We conducted an exploratory analysis identifying the nature of the relationship between industry characteristics and service expansion. Specifically, we examine the industry characteristics and the interactions between industry characteristics and firm resources. Firm resources included the stack characteristics and stack scope.

## Discussion

There has been a profound s hift in t he role of s ervices in the portfolio of offerings of IT vendors. Innovative models of IT service delivery, including SaaS, cloud-based services, application service provision, and IT solutions, provide new ways of bundling products and services together to provide value to customers. However, these innovative business models commoditize computing power—minimizing the degree to which customers perceive differences among hardware vendors—while facilitating the implementation of open source solutions as alternatives to traditional software products. In addition, many of these innovative models threaten vendors who are reliant on traditional models of IT product sales, as customers prefer the predictable cost structure associated with services. While it has been suggested that product vendors expand to provide IT services to exploit their existing experiences [26, 28], because new models of service delivery continue to cannibalize existing product-based models, service expansion becomes not only an opportunity but a strategic necessity. To our knowledge, this is the first empirical study that formally examines the role of existing product offerings and product business performance of IT vendors in facilitating the transition to IT services.

Our results demonstrate that firms with a stack focusing more on software than hardware are able to derive more revenue from services, suggesting that offerings in software provide more relevant knowledge to enable a transition to services. This is true for both application and infrastructure software, as firms with product offerings in these layers were more likely to derive service revenues compared to firms offering hardware products. This suggests that vendors may seek to focus on developing software capabilities as a way of transitioning to the provision of services.

Our analysis did not find any significant difference between the contribution of infrastructure software and application software to service expansion. This result suggests that infrastructure and application software businesses may be similar in terms of their relatedness to services, and infrastructure software may also help IT vendors accumulate customer knowledge. To customize infrastructure software, vendors need to acquire knowledge of the specific IT assets and IT management processes of their customers. This knowledge may be particularly relevant to providing infrastructurebased services to customers.

Our results further indicate that stack scope—that is, offerings across application software, infrastructure software, and hardware—is positively associated with service expansion. Past research has emphasized the importance of product portfolio diversity, especially for firms whose products have to work with other components as part of a system [17]. The benefits associated with such diversity include greater levels of absorptive capacity and innovativeness [30, 48]. Diverse product offerings also enable economies of scope associated with consumption, as customers gain efficiencies and off-load risk by unifying procurement [55]. This research contributes to the understanding of the importance of product stack scope by showing that it can also facilitate expansion to services, a critical area of growth for product companies.

We also find that poor product business performance strengthens the relationship between stack scope and service expansion. This finding is consistent with past work in organizational renewal [69, 101] that suggest that organizational resources serve as options, and poor performance or environmental pressures trigger strategic moves that leverage these options. Our results suggest that the stack scope serves as a resource that can provide strategic flexibility for IT product vendors when they face revenue pressure from an underperforming product business. Leveraging knowledge derived from a wider IT product stack may require initiatives at the corporate level and the implementation may require collaborations from multiple units, which can be difficult. Only performance pressures might induce the management commitment and support needed to overcome these difficulties and translate the knowledge potential into an effective service business.

We did not find that performance pressures influence how hardware, infrastructure software, or application software contribute to service expansion differently. This nonsignificant result may be because, even without the pressures of lagging performance, IT vendors already take advantage of their customer knowledge gained from the experiences of selling software. Customer knowledge that is mainly associated with one layer of the IT stack may exist in a single business unit or group, making it easier to leverage than architectural knowledge and thus less dependent on performance pressures.

## Industry-Level Factors

Our exploratory analysis of industry structure examined the association between industry characteristics and service expansion. We did not find that industry characteristics are directly associated with service expansion. Rather, it is the interaction between industry characteristics and firm-specific characteristics that is associated with service expansion. These findings are consistent with prior findings that also suggest that the effect of business capabilities (here, the IT product portfolio) is contingent on environmental conditions [2].

We find that firms are more likely to take advantage of existing knowledge resources to expand to services when the identified industry is less concentrated or more mature. This finding is in line with prior research suggesting that in less concentrated—and thus more competitive—industries firms tend to choose different levels of quality to relax the price competition [84]. Encapsulating products with services can be considered as a means to differentiate quality. In particular, our results seem to indicate that firms are likely to deploy customer knowledge gained from providing application software to differentiate themselves from competitors through service offerings. Although there is anecdotal evidence supporting service expansion as a strategy associated with both growing and mature industries, our results demonstrate that only in more mature industries are firms more likely to pursue a service expansion strategy. The reason may be that in a mature industry, services may provide an opportunity to draw upon a large install base to increase revenues. In contrast, in the early stages of an industry, firms may provide services solely to complement product sales. In particular, firms are more likely to exploit the knowledge gained from a diversified product stack to provide services in mature industries.

## Theoretical Implications

Prior research on related diversification in the IT industry has focused on product diversification (i.e., within-industry diversification) to discuss the resource synergies between multiple-product businesses sharing the same operation system platform or customer base [25, 93]. Our research extends this literature by examining the diversification of product vendors to services and examining the resource synergies between IT products and services. Although past research has found extensive differences between products and services [3], more recent research takes a boundary-spanning conceptualization between products and services [99]. Our research advances this boundary-spanning conceptualization by examining the shared knowledge resources (i.e., customer knowledge and architectural knowledge) between IT product and IT service businesses.

Although editorial notes have highlighted the need for research that examines services and IT (e.g., [7, 76]), theoretical and empirical work in this area is still nascent. Our work contributes to this emerging and important research stream in the information systems literature, and we hope the findings here serve as a base for further research that examines the motives and drivers for service expansion by IT product firms. In addition, we contribute to this research stream by identifying the key resources (i.e., the firm product stack) along with the mechanisms (i.e., increased architectural and customer knowledge) through which IT product vendors expand to services.

Our paper contributes to the diversification literature by identifying the synergies between product and service businesses, thus expanding the definition of relatedness. Past research on related diversification has used SIC codes as a measure of relatedness, and using this definition, the service expansion of product companies would be considered unrelated diversification [6, 34]. However, when product companies provide services around their products, they are able to exploit the competencies and knowledge gained through their product business, capturing the essence of arguments related to the benefits from related diversification. Our theorizing and conceptualizing of service expansion here as related diversification thus opens a new line of inquiry into firm growth strategies.

This paper also sheds light on the role of existing knowledge in firm growth, as prior studies have produced mixed results. Some research has found that existing knowledge enables growth, because firms grow to leverage their existing or excessive capabilities [50]. Other research has supported the view that existing knowledge provides the inertia that can lead to competency traps [63] and core rigidities [61] that prevent incumbents from undertaking changes. For instance, the findings of Kraatz and Zajac [58] showed that organizations with greater stocks of historically valuable resources are less likely to engage in strategic change, supporting the role of resources as commitments rather than a facilitator of change. Our study shows that the key to reconciling the mixed findings is to focus on the relevant knowledge for the new market that the company is targeting. It is not the knowledge per se but rather the related knowledge that can be leveraged in the new market. In our case, this is the customer and architectural knowledge relevant to services that enables service expansion.

Our paper contributes to the research on the performance of IT companies and competition in the IT industry. Prior research has focused on product portfolio characteristics such as product diversification on the performance of IT firms [20, 25, 93]. Our research suggests that a firm’s product portfolio also influences its ability to provide services, which could affect firm performance [38]. Moreover, as product companies grow their revenue from services, it is important to take service strategy into account when examining the performance of IT vendors and the competition in the IT industry.

Our research also contributes to the theoretical understanding of IT solution providers by helping to unpack the complementarity between product and service businesses. One of the advantages of solutions is the complementarity between product and service businesses. It is suggested that knowledge acquired from services can also be exploited in providing products. For instance, feedback on products and information on how users use products can be collected during servicing and utilized to improve product design. However, the other direction of the relationship—how product businesses can help services—has received little attention. Our research identified that the knowledge gained through product businesses (architectural and customer knowledge) can be utilized when providing services.

## Practical Implications

Given the increasing importance of services to IT product vendors, our paper provides a practical guide for IT vendors who seek to expand to services. We find that not only the intimate knowledge of the product but also the knowledge of interactions among products in a complex system can contribute to providing services. Product companies may acquire such knowledge through mergers, acquisitions, or new employee recruitment. The importance of service expansion may be one reason why mergers and acquisitions in the IT industry have increased significantly in recent years [102], as IT vendors transition toward service provision. For example, EMC has acquired a series of software companies as part of expanding from a storage hardware vendor to a storage solution provider. While such expansion drives growth through services, IT vendors would have to carefully manage the integration of acquired firms in order to effectively tap into the knowledge resources needed for service expansion. Given the potential problems in postmerger integrations, IT product firms might be better served by adopting a balanced approach of expanding their product offerings through internal research and development efforts and through mergers and acquisitions. In either case, the findings reported here provide the rationale for executives to proactively engage in expanding the architectural and customer knowledge stocks of the firm. This is particularly relevant for IT firms that are facing challenges in growing their revenues. Given the time and path dependencies involved in such knowledge acquisition, IT firms are better served to adopt a long-term perspective and invest in acquiring relevant knowledge on a continuous basis to create the options for future growth.

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

This research has several limitations. First, we were unable to directly measure customer knowledge or architectural knowledge. As a result, we were not able determine the degree to which knowledge mediates the relationship between product portfolio and service expansion or to differentiate between the effect of the two types of knowledge on service expansion. In addition, as we used characteristics of product portfolio as a proxy for firm knowledge, there may exist alternative explanations for the link between the characteristics of product portfolio and service expansion. For instance, the reason that companies with a more diversified scope tend to expand toward services may be that they already bundle their products, and adding services would be required to manage the complexities involved. Future work should attempt to specifically measure the knowledge available to firms and attempt to differentiate the effects of each type of knowledge on expansion to services.

Second, we used a sales-based ratio to measure the extent to which IT companies expand to offer services, but there could be a number of other measures of service expansion. For example, service expansion could be measured by an input-based proxy, such as the proportion of employees who work in the service units. As a result, future work should attempt to determine the robustness of the relationships identified here to different measures of service expansion.

Third, there is an additional possibility that specific types of products will enable specific types of services and that services may differ systematically in profitability by type. As a result, future work should distinguish different types of IT services (i.e., maintenance services versus consulting services versus SaaS) and the profitability of each while identifying the relevant enabling products for each service type.

Fourth, in the past ten years, the growth of SaaS and cloud computing has been pronounced, and our sample does not allow us to capture this phenomenon completely. We expect that our results would be stronger if recent time periods were included. Future research is needed to test the model over a broader time horizon and to examine its robustness in predicting IT product companies’ expansion to SaaS and cloud computing.

Finally, we have a limited number of companies and observations in the sample. The limited statistical power might have played a role in the nonsignificant results and failure to support some of the hypotheses, especially when the effect size is small. A larger sample size in future research would help to detect the relationship between stack characteristics and service expansion even when the effect size is small.

## Conclusion

Hist orically, t he management of s ervice bus ines es has been viewed as fundamentally different from the management of product businesses. Recently, however, product companies have introduced service offerings, relied on services as sources of revenue, and considered services as a direction for future growth. Understanding both the drivers of such service expansion and the performance effects are important issues for scholars and executives in the IT industry. As one of the early projects in this area, this paper identified the key knowledge resources of IT product vendors that can be leveraged in services, using the product portfolio of the IT vendors to assess their knowledge stocks and examining how the characteristics of a firm’s product portfolio influence service expansion. By providing empirical evidence based on a sample of large IT firms, this paper has provided validated results that can serve as a basis to draw guidelines for practice and open up new avenues for research. It is hoped that our study provides a foundation for future work investigating service strategies of firms in the IT industry.

## Appendix: Data Collection Procedure

Dat a from Oracle Corporat ion are provided as our first example of the data collection procedure. In Item 1 (“Business”) of Oracle’s 2004 Annual Report, it is stated that “New software licenses include the licensing of database technology software and applications software,” and it goes on to describe Oracle’s database management software and application software (E-business Suite) in greater detail. Based on their description of these two product lines, database management software belongs to infrastructure software and E-business Suite belongs to application software in our IT stack.

Parametric Technology Corporation is provided as our second example. Item 1 (“Business”) of their 2004 Annual Report starts with “PTC develops, markets and supports product lifecycle management (PLM) software solutions and related services that help manufacturers improve the competitiveness of their products and product development processes. . . . Our software solutions, which include a suite of mechanical computer-aided design tools (our design solutions) and a range of Internet-based collaboration technologies (our collaboration and control solutions), enable manufacturing companies to create virtual computer-based products (digital products), collaborate on designs within the enterprise and throughout the extended supply chain.” We consider both product lines (design solutions and collaboration and control solutions) part of application software.

For some companies, although it is clear that they generate revenues from products of different layers, they do not break down revenues based on these products. We consider these cases as missing data.
