## a b s t r a c t

The software industry faces a fundamental change from delivering software On-premises to delivering software On-demand. Well-established companies have to shift their focus from the (still) profitable On-premises market to an, as yet unprofitable, On-demand market to survive. This requires strategies to handle the transformation process. Based on five case studies and following Christensen’s disruptive innovation theory, we learn the successful strategies that software companies can adopt to handle a change from On-premises to On-demand delivery. These strategies include the general ideas that Christensen proposes and separate extensions for the software industry, suggesting an emerging area of IS research.

## Introduction

Software companies are repeatedly faced with game changing technological innovations. These innovations may be either software (Bonaccorsi et al., 2006) or hardware developments (Campbell-Kelly, 2001), and pose strategic challenges for software companies (Deodhar et al., 2012; Kraemer and Dedrick, 2002; Mowery, 2003; von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007). For example, with the shift from mainframe to personal computers, we saw the emergence and rise of firms, such as Microsoft. Simultaneously, other firms lost their markets. Established firms either remained hugely successful, struggled or even failed to survive (Campbell-Kelly, 2001). Christensen (1997) maintains that dominant firms in the mainframe market remained too close to their customers and consequently lost their market position to the new generation of products (Chandy and Tellis 1998). The change from proprietary software to open source software (Bonaccorsi et al., 2006) is another example, as it offers an interesting opportunity for start-up companies, but threatens established players’ existence. Both cases show that software companies repeatedly find themselves facing potentially disruptive change.

Cloud computing technology may be the next challenge for software companies. because it is causing a fundamental change within the industry (Benlian et al., 2010; Kaltenecker et al., 2013). For a long time, On-premises was the software industry’s dominant delivery model. According to this model, software runs on computers on the premises of the persons or organizations using the software (Buxmann et al., 2008). Currently, by using a Software as a Service (SaaS) model, cloud computing allows users to use a provider’s applications on a cloud infrastructure. A few years ago, no one could see where the development would lead, nor recognize its potential. Now, there is a debate on SaaS’s potential to disrupt software industry structures (Kaltenecker et al., 2013; Sultan and van de Bunt-Kokhuis, 2012).

Disruptive innovations with a potential to create a new market not only disrupt the existing market, but also displace the existing technology (Christensen, 1997). Incumbents are often unable to change their strategies, even though good managers run the company. In order to survive in the changing industry, companies have to reconsider their strategies. Well-established companies have to think about products for the upcoming market. Adhering rigidly to old ways and not evolving can lead to problems, such as losing market share (Bower and Christensen, 1995). Additionally, SaaS products, unlike On-premises products, require distinct capabilities (Boillat and Legner, 2013) and established companies have to shift their focus from the (still) profitable On-premises market to an (as yet) unprofitable and unknown On-demand market. Although this is an urgent challenge, information systems (IS) literature has very little advice on disruptive innovation management in the software industry and, more specifically, on the transformation of software companies from On-premises providers to On-demand providers

Neither studies that focus on software companies (De Marez et al., 2011), nor those that focus on SaaS (Benlian et al., 2010) have responded adequately to these problems. Boillat and Legner, (2013) point out that there is a lack of systematic analysis of cloud computing’s impact on software vendors, with most research focusing on SaaS cloud adoption and migration from a user’s perspective. Prior studies with a vendor perspective focus on revenue and price models (Eurich et al., 2011) or develop cloud offering typologies (Marston et al., 2011); they do not focus on strategic management decisions. Thus, implications of cloud computing for software vendors are still at an early stage of investigation.

Literature from innovation management introduced the disruptive innovation theory (Christensen, 1997) to academia and practice. The theory’s strength lies in the simple way in which a disruptive phenomenon is described. Various studies have used the theory of disruptive innovation to analyze industries and support management decisions (Markides, 2006; Yu and Hang, 2009). However, these studies do not focus on the software industry (Herrmann et al., 2007).

The goal of this paper is to obtain insights into the management of disruptive innovation in the software industry. We draw on the current example of cloud computing technology and address the question: How do software companies successfully manage the transformation process from an On-premises provider to a company focusing on SaaS? Bearing the current state of research in mind, we use a case study approach. In order to learn from software companies and their transformation process a preliminary analysis of two case studies was conducted providing initial insights (Kaltenecker and Hess, 2014). Subsequently, three further cases were analyzed, all five case software companies having completed transformation from an On-premises provider to a company focusing on SaaS. Identifying disruptive innovation is a complex process and we refer our readers to studies by Danneels (2004), Markides (2006), Schmidt and Druehl (2008) and Tellis (2006) where this is discussed.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: first, we give a brief overview of the theoretical background, including disruptive innovation theory. Next, we describe the study methodology – the case study approach. We then present study results, followed by discussion of these results from both practical and theoretical viewpoints. The paper concludes with study limitations and future research options.

## Background

## Cloud computing technology

Cloud computing allows ubiquitous, convenient, and On-demand network access to computing resources (Mell and Grance, 2011). It enables a standard software solution in the form of a service, which can be provided over the Internet. Recently, On-demand software has become an important way of delivering software in the information technology (IT) landscape. Gartner expects the cloud computing market to grow to about USD 210 billion worldwide in 2016 (Statista, 2015).

One can differentiate between four basic organizational cloud forms: private, community, public, and hybrid (Mell and Grance, 2011). While these types are technologically comparable, they differ in terms of ownership, organization, and operations. A private cloud is a customer-owned, self-operated infrastructure. The customer controls the standardized IT operating environment, which allows for local adjustments. Only authorized stakeholders can access this environment. The community cloud is more open and allows access for consumers that have shared concerns. A public cloud is owned and run by an IT service provider, offering a selection of highly standardized business processes, mostly on a pay-per-use basis. This cloud allows for flexible and fast subscription-based use and is accessed over the Internet. Lastly, the cloud infrastructure of a hybrid cloud is a combination of the afore-mentioned.

Cloud services are usually divided into three service levels (Mell and Grance, 2011). At the lowest level, Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), a provider offers basic infrastructure, including computers, storage on virtual servers, and network infrastructure functions. Platform as a Service (PaaS) comprises IT services in a middleware laver. These platforms enable the development and integration of application components. SaaS is the highest cloud computing service level, offering its users business applications as a standardized service, SaaS generally involves business processes completely uncoupled from the technology, for example, customer relationship management (Vaquero et al., 2009). Applications are bought On-demand, are easily expandable, and their payment is usage related. SaaS customers pay a fee for the right to use the software components and services on a monthly or quarterly basis (Buxmann et al., 2008). The provider is fully responsible for the software maintenance. All the user requires is a browser and Internet access. SaaS thus offers formerly excluded customer groups opportunities. Owing to its ready provision of software, its flexibility, and its minimal capital investment, SaaS

The SaaS concept is not new; some scholars regard it as a form of outsourcing, resulting in similar advantages and disadvantages (Benlian et al., 2010). This potential mostly stems from the development and widespread adoption of Internet technologies and standards (Buchegger and Riedl, 2005). Today, a user only needs access to the Internet. This might be the reason why ASP has not revolutionized the market (Desai and Currie, 2003; Karpinski, 2008).

## Disruptive innovation theory

Christensen and Bower (1996) distinguish between sustaining technologies and disruptive technologies. They describe technological changes that have a sustained impact on an established trajectory of performance improvement as ‘sustaining’, while ‘disruptive’ technologies are those that disrupt an established trajectory of performance improvement or redefine what performance means. Christensen (1997) further characterizes disruptive technologies as initially underperforming in terms of mainstream attributes. However, disruptive technologies have other features that niche-market customers value Products and services based on new technologies are often commercialized in emerging and less significant markets, mainly because leading companies’ most profitable customers initially do not want them. Additionally, established companies retain their old product line and try to develop and improve its performance because potentially disruptive technologies are not regarded as a rational financial option. Regardless of these companies’ decisions to invest, or not to invest, the potentially disrupting innovation slowly but steadily improves until it achieves the low end of mainstream performance standards. Start-up vendor companies in particular, promote this kind of development. Ultimately, the new products and services have the power to displace the dominant technology (Christensen and Rosenbloom, 1995) as the new technology becomes fully performance competitive. Subsequently, there is a strong likelihood of the new technological innovation pushing the established players out of their core business.

Before looking at management strategies for how well-established software companies can manage potentially disruptive innovations using the example of cloud computing, we have to show that cloud computing has the potential to disrupt an established performance trajectory, or redefine what performance means. Kaltenecker et al. (2013) focused on cloud computing technology in the form of SaaS and found that SaaS changed the trajectory of performance improvement in terms of price and features, resulting in ideal conditions for disrupting the whole software market. Their analysis was about the potential and probability of disruption and not the final result which is disruptiveness (Kaltenecker et al., 2013). As experts and practitioners agree that cloud computing technology is still at the beginning of its life cycle (Kaltenecker et al., 2013) we characterize it as a potentially disruptive rather than a disruptive innovation.

While Christensen’s theory of disruptive innovation is well established in the management literature (Tellis, 2006) several other views on innovation exist alongside it, including diffusion theory (Rogers, 1995) and the concept of radical innovations (Chandy and Tellis, 1998). In our study context these theories are less suitable, as they focus on the successful introduction of radically new products (Chandy and Tellis, 1998) or innovations in general without regard to incumbents (Rogers, 1995). Christensen’s theory focusses on managing potentially disruptive innovations and offers management strategies for dealing with the change (Mann, 2009; Kaltenecker et al., 2013). Christensen’s theory predicts incumbents will hold onto their old product line for too long although they see a potentially disruptive technology emerging; they risk being uncompetitive in the future market by failing to adopt the new technology. Christensen calls this phenomenon the innovator’s dilemma. Kaltenecker et al. (2013) consider cloud computing a technology with high disruptive potential. Thus, well-established software companies are expected to face the innovators dilemma. However, there are companies that seem able to deal well with this dilemma (Kaltenecker et al., 2013; Keller and Hüsig, 2009). A study of how these incumbents respond to potentially disruptive innovations could yield valuable insights. Consequently companies which successfully transition are the focus of this paper. Christensen offers several generic management strategies on how well-established players could possibly manage potentially disruptive innovations. In this paper we investigate whether these strategies work for the software industry and try to make them tangible and actionable.

## Strategies for managing disruptive innovations

A major strand of research focuses on how companies can manage disruptive innovations (Bower and Christensen, 1995; Christensen and Bower, 1996; Christensen, 1997). The strategies that scholars have suggested are directed at managers of large companies across various industries (Christensen, 1997). The advice can be classified into four basic categories.

## Spin-off strategy

In big companies, consultants recommend which projects should be pursued. Often these consultants are interested in a quick financial return and therefore prefer projects targeting sustaining technologies instead of opting for disruptive technologies. Senior managers are often unaware of the ideas rejected in this first selection stage. Potentially disruptive

innovations therefore have less chance of being allocated resources. A spin-off that exists independently from the established business and that focuses on a potentially disruptive innovation might be a solution to this problem. Spin-off employees are more willing to accept smaller successes.

## Leader strategy

Companies that bring a product, based on disruptive technology, to the market within the first two years of its appearance are six times more likely to establish themselves in the new market than companies choosing a follower strategy (Christensen, 1997).

## Expert opinion strategy

While sustaining technologies are essential for generating short-term profits, in the long run, disruptive innovations can displace these technologies. Christensen (1997) believes that staff from the technology or research and development department, are often the first to recognize emerging technologies. Their expertise should be integrated into the investment decision process, although this will almost certainly lead to investment in less profitable products in the short term.

## Trial and error strategy

In the early phase, disruptive innovations have no chance in mass markets. Therefore, it is imprudent to survey mainstream mass-market consumers regarding their expectations of product innovations. A stepwise approach is key to finding a potential market. This approach consists of test markets or test products. Investments in these test markets should be carefully monitored. The strategy involves a step-by-step market approach with trial and error phases.

These four generic management strategies were derived from Christensen et al.’s early publications. In a detailed literature survey, we found evidence for the validity of these strategies. The spin-off, leader, expert opinion and trial and error strategies were applied in different contexts and their value for organizational success was evidenced by authors such as Bergek et al. (2013), Charitou et al. (2012), Chesbrough (2010), Danneels (2004), Hill and Rothaermel (2003), Lyytinen and Rose (2003), Markides (2006), Paap and Katz (2004), Sandström et al. (2009), Schmidt and Druehl (2008), and Slater and Mohr (2006). In this study, we try to ascertain whether these generic management strategies also apply to incumbents in the software industry, while exploring additional management strategies for managing potentially disruptive innovations.

There is a small amount of research that gives us some first insights into software companies and innovation. Carlo et al. (2012) explain how a software company’s knowledge endowments influence the level of IT innovation. Their results show that innovating companies need to be mindful about developing knowledge-based capabilities. Carlo et al. (2014) provide findings concerning the impact of timing on innovation outcomes for software providers. They found that during disruptive IT innovation, an early innovation activity versus a late innovation activity significantly impacts innovation characteristics. In the context of managing dramatic changes due to a highly dynamic market, Antero et al. (2013) found that the evolution of a new business model in technology-intensive industries is essential to survival. Further, Peyton et al. (2014) found that existing research neglects the importance of innovation when explaining business model changes in the software industry.

Christensen et al. (2004) expanded on the original four strategies in the theory of disruptive innovations. They developed several general responses at a higher level of analysis which describe the directions an incumbent could take when faced with a disruptive innovation. Basically, companies could cede or co-opt. Ceding implies that an incumbent leaves the market to the entrants, refocuses on its core customers, abandons its lower-tier markets, and discontinues its low-end products. Co-opting means that an incumbent fights back, using its internal resources. Co-opting could be either a growth-driven reaction – the incumbent targets the entrant’s customers by targeting its market with a modified version of its core product and announces that the entrant’s market is a strategic priority – or a defensive reaction – the incumbent reacts defensively, building a wall around its existing customers to prevent the entrant’s entry.

These responses are not specific enough to provide companies with management strategies. In this study, we try to address this lack and link the management strategies to Christensen’s response options.

## Method

## Case study introduction

The case study approach is well established in the IS literature (Tsang, 2014) and a preferred way of investigating real-life phenomena over which the researcher has little control (Yin, 2009). Case studies have already provided many answers to management problems (Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007), and are particularly suitable for answering why and how questions, as they enable researchers to gain deep insights into new research areas (Siggelkow, 2007). Accordingly and owing to the lack of prior research, this approach seems to be an appropriate way to investigate our research topic. Gibbert and Ruigrok (2010) suggest that best-practice case studies report the research actions taken to ensure methodological rigor. They call the process ‘‘talk the walk”. This study will talk the reader through the procedures used on its walk including research setting selection, data collection and data analysis (Pratt, 2008).

According to Keil (2002) and Yin (2009), we used instrumental case studies, because we selected companies that would advance our understanding of transformation strategies due to disruptive innovation in the software industry. These case

We used the following criteria to select case studies. (1) The company had to be a well-established player in the software industry. (2) The company had to have initially offered a purely On-premises product, which was successfully established in the market (generating demand and revenues). (3) The company also had to offer stable and robust On-demand software (not a beta-version). Table 1 shows basic facts about our case studies. Further, all companies have international customers and, with the exception of Companies C and E, they have subsidiaries around the world. All the companies started developing their On-demand products some years ago, generating between 1% and 40% of their turnover with their cloud-based products. These percentages should not be underestimated, as license contracts will continue to exist in the B2B On-premises market and other arrangements cannot displace them in the short term.

## Data collection

To ensure our qualitative research’s reliability and validity (Huberman and Miles, 1994), we used multiple data sources to allow data triangulation. We collected data from companies' annual reports, and publically available studies and statistics Several semi-structured interviews with managers and specialists (face-to-face or by telephone) formed the basis of the data collection process, with managers answering the extended version of our interview. This version included questions about the interviewees, their current position in the company, and role within the transformation process. We could thus ensure that the participant’s role and the organizational context were taken into account (Dibbern et al., 2008). In the interview, we then asked several questions on cloud computing technology and on the extent to which the interviewee considered it potentially disruptive. Next the managers were asked to describe the company’s transformation process in detail. Here, we emphasized aspects concerning the beginning of the transformation process, the opportunities and risks of the transformation, and the roles of different interest groups. The concluding part of the interview discussed aspects of the theory of disruptive innovation. We presented several statements and the interviewees evaluated whether they agreed or disagreed with these statements and then explained their stance. These statements focused on subjects such as disagreements between managers and technical staff concerning the decision to invest in cloud computing, the integration of test products and test markets in the development process, the foundation of a separate spin-off. ## Data analysis

According to Huberman and Miles (1994) the researcher should begin with the within-case analysis. Therefore we first concentrated on the companies’ individual transformation processes and tried to learn from them and their transformation strategy. Additionally, we analyzed the extent to which Christensen’s four strategies are applicable to these software companies. First, we transcribed the recorded interviews. Next, we summarized each interview. Therefore we had to go back and forth in the data in order to develop a cohesive story of the transformation process in each company. The generation of summaries included some challenges as we had to take care that each of the summaries had a comparable level of detail. The information in the interview was cross-checked and supplemented with information from additional sources to provide a more complete picture. These case summaries formed the basis for our data analysis. Huberman and Miles (1994) recommend a cross-case analysis as a next step. The objective is to extend external validity and enhance generalizability. However, Huberman and Miles (1994) admit that cross-site analytic work is not simple. As the first aim was to identify whether any of Christensen’s four management strategies could be found within our case studies, we searched our data concerning information on these strategies (spin-off-, leader-, expert opinion-, trial & error strategy). As we explicitly asked for them during the interview, we found evidence within every case Thus, these categories could be described as seed codes before we exe: cuted the open coding process in order to find additional management strategies. When we found a company used a particular strategy (e.g. a recruitment strategy) all other cases were scanned for this strategy. Using this approach, we were able to identify similarities and differences between the companies. Although the case companies differed demographically (e.g. age and size), most strategies were observed in all case companies. If a particular strategy was not observed for a particular company, we investigated the reasons behind the absence (e.g. the direct sales strategy was especially important for company B while the recruitment strategy was prominent in A, B, C and D) by checking secondary data and revisiting recorded interviews. For example, when interviewees thought that a strategy was obvious, they did not explain it in detail. However, listening closely to the recorded interviews and checking secondary data helped clarify this fact. This approach was essential to revealing the big picture. Some researchers follow different coding techniques e.g. principles of counting (Corbett, 2013). However, this runs the risk that aspects that are little mentioned are regarded as less important. We would have overlooked important data by following such an approach.

## Results – individual strategies to a successful transformation

In this section, we present the results of each case separately.

## Case 1: Company A

Company A, first, formed a cloud computing group in 2005, which finally resulted in the establishment of a separate On-demand business unit operating independently from the existing On-premises business. The On-demand division was equipped with decision-making powers and resource competences. The company tried to acquire the most innovative employees internally and sought experienced people externally. Some of the external employees had gained experience with transformation processes in other companies and industries.

On the basis of market research the company expected a large On-demand market to emerge. Instead of trying to transform the entire company, Company A started building the new business area from 2005 to 2007. The Senior Vice President underlined that it had only been possible to transform the company because a strongly established business unit worked with the new business area unit, thus protecting the growing business. In order to keep the established business strong, the company ensured that it did not cannibalize its established products. The idea was to address a completely new customer group with its On-demand products. Thus, Company A provided its big customers with its established On-premises product and targeted small and medium-sized companies for its On-demand solution. The company needed to quickly find a market because, in the words of the Senior Vice President, it is not the big company that eats the little one, but the fast one that eats the slow company.

First, the company tried to gain new customers by simply transferring an unchanged On-premises product to the cloud This strategy proved unsuccessful. Company A then tried to identify the most important cost drivers and learn how to build software that could be offered on a cloud infrastructure. This learning was expensive and lengthy. The company lost a lot of money during the trial and error phase. A lesson learned in this phase was that there are far higher expectations of Ondemand software regarding its attributes, such as its accuracy, whether it was free of errors, and its robustness, than of On-premises software. Although the company knew that it was important to step into the market as a leader, they were also aware that this should not happen at any cost. The company realized that quality was essential for maintaining its brand name. Within the On-premises world, the company’s brand name was associated with high standards, thus an On-demand solution containing bugs would carry the risk of the company losing its status in the new market and in its established market. Nevertheless, the company discovered that a brand name is extremely helpful when entering a new market, as customers already trust it.

The company’s basic message was that cash should be used to grow the business. Company A was in the fortunate situation of needing neither venture capital, nor loan capital. Thus, it did not have to justify its decisions to third-party investors However, this strategy also caused its shareholders to call for a higher dividend payout, instead of agreeing with the investments in the On-demand products. The separate business unit could only be financially supported because the management held firm to its decision

The company was embedded in an ecosystem that included hardware, technology, and implementation partners. To avoid losing important partners, such as subcontractors and suppliers, during the transformation process, the company encouraged these firms to step into the new market as partners. Many of their suppliers adapted their products and joined the company. Co-innovation strategy is the key phrase of the ecosystem approach. The existing and potential new customers, together with the employees and management, were therefore invited to design On-demand product features. Immediately after these meetings, ideas and innovations were developed, implemented, and finally tested in sister companies. The software prototypes were therefore first rolled out to associated partners. When all the bugs had been fixed, the product was introduced to the mass market. Company A ensured that its prices were competitive by comparing them with the prices of competitors’ comparable products.

The company discovered that entering the market with a home-grown product offering a complete cloud-based solution was not the best strategy for penetrating the On-demand market as the cloud market was not ready for such a comprehensive solution. Thus, the company invested in the strategic acquisition of start-up companies with more focused cloud solutions. These acquisitions allowed the company to access knowledge, and strengthened its position in the market. The acquired companies were treated individually, some remaining independent, while others were incorporated.

Today, the company believes it is near the end of its transformation process. The Senior Vice President believes that this business area will further expand in the next few years, adding that the next revolution will focus on the content of software and not on the way we consume it

## Case 2: Company B

This company’s journey to the cloud started in 2007 and followed a carefully thought out approach. The company decided to develop an On-premises software product that was multi-client capable and scalable. Therefore, the basic features of the On-demand product existed even before the company decided to enter the new market. This software architecture influenced the development of the On-demand software positively. Nevertheless, it took a great deal of effort, time, and money to convert the software to a browser-client infrastructure. Transforming internal competences and generating know-how were important aspects of realizing the transformation process. Based on a learning-by-doing approach, expert interviews at fairs, and the analysis of rival firms’ strategy and price policies, the company was able to develop product development and product placement skills. In addition, the company acquired another firm to benefit from its know-how and technology. The founding of a virtual start-up company was another interesting way of generating new ideas. Company B gave three students a SaaS prototype and challenged them to improve it until it was ready for the market. These students were intrinsically highly motivated and produced completely new solutions to existing problems. Furthermore, they were not as expensive as regular employees. This strategy saved the company’s internal resources

The company was not large enough to establish a separate spin-off. However, it was possible to form an online group of employees, who focused exclusively on the development of a SaaS product. The company selected people for this group who showed a strong willingness to participate in the development of new businesses. The management made it very clear that the On-demand business would not cannibalize the established products. The management attitude was key in the transformation process as the core business had to continue working independently alongside the new business area’s development.

The company faced two crucial issues during its transformation process: it overestimated the general demand for SaaS products, and its product distribution mechanisms with resellers needed to be adjusted. The company strongly believed that cloud computing would soon disrupt the software market structures. This overestimation was due to overreliance on market forecasts and customers expressing an interest in On-demand products. Despite their customers’ interest in a SaaS solution, they ultimately opted for the established On-premises product. When customers were questioned about this behavior, they said that they trusted an On-premises solution more, but intended to switch in a few years’ time. Consumers did, however, want to be sure that the supplier they chose could later provide them with an On-demand solution. Furthermore because of the lead time, the company was a step ahead of its rivals. When the market was finally ready for On-demand software, the company offered a solution, while its competitors were still in the middle of their trial and error phase. The second critical issue was the distribution structure. Originally, the company had cooperated with resellers, which worked well as a strategy for the On-premises business, but once the company also offered On-demand software, they could present both solutions to end users. However, the resellers earned more with the On-premises product; in order to prevent resulting problems, Company B adjusted its incentive structure and paid the reseller 30% of the margin upfront to promote the On-demand prod uct. Additionally, the company started direct sales of the On-demand software in order to be independent from resellers.

Company B primarily supplies medium-sized companies with established products. According to the manager, part of their On-demand strategy is targeting smaller companies, because they appreciate On-demand solutions, while medium sized companies are more skeptical. Simultaneously, the SaaS solution was adapted to and grew with their initial customer base consisting of smaller companies. The company therefore sees these company customers as a test market for bigger companies and bigger On-demand solutions. In addition, Company B used its own company as a test market. On-demand software was rolled out in the company and the employees tested the prototype. Owing to the fast internal feedback loops, the company managed to improve the software rapidly. Additionally, the company concentrates on observing the big players’ strategies. These market giants have the power to influence consumer preferences. Company B found that it was essential for smaller companies to orientate themselves to the big players in order to stay in touch with the latest trends and movements in the mass market. However, the company is aware that this strategy is only useful for refining its existing products and not for promoting the development of completely new innovations.

## Case 3: Company C

This small-sized specialist in enterprise content management has focused on the development of On-demand software since 2010. Its breakthrough came in 2011 when the company won the ‘‘Best in Cloud” award in the category ‘‘Software as a Service – Public Cloud.”

The way into the cloud was neither easy nor voluntary. The company suffered distribution problems with its On-premises products. In order to survive, the manager decided to change the company strategy and take a completely new path. He believed SaaS offered a new way to sell products and stay competitive. The company monitored the market, learning that SaaS would indeed give the company the chance to differentiate itself from its competitors. Although the decision to change the company’s strategy was born of necessity, the manager admitted that it also contained a fun factor, because the developers saw SaaS as a new challenge

Once the management was sure about the company’s new direction, they announced the decision officially, informing the employees about technological and organizational changes. The employees reacted quite differently: While the developers were highly motivated, other staff members; the distribution team in particular, were rather skeptical. To ensure everyone was on board, team members were free to decide for which part of the company they wanted to work in the future – either for the traditional On-premise business, or for the new On-demand business.

The company saw itself as a proponent of the leader strategy. The manager mentioned that, although the On-premise product was based on Web browser architecture, the development phase had been a difficult time and the software had many teething problems. When the prototype was finally ready, the company had the chance to adjust the software to the feedback it had received from its lead users.

The transformation process required the development of internal competences. In the On-premises world, companies go to their customers to hear what their exact needs are and subsequently develop software to meet these needs. In the On-demand world, however, this process is exactly the opposite. Company C had to think about a possible solution for the mass market. This meant that the company had to know what its customers’ problems were. Thus, Company C needed more competences than those required to develop software. It had to communicate with the market and keep in touch with its customers to learn about the problems that existed and the solutions that the marked required. To meet these new requirements, Company C went to potential customers and asked them about their company, their internal processes, and any resulting problems. Based on this information, Company C developed ideas for their On-demand software. This strategy was positively influenced by Company C having many opportunities to attend networking events as the company’s manager was a board member of the Software Ecosystems Association.

Becoming visible in the mass market was difficult, and required marketing. However, almost all the employees had a technological background and no feeling for public relations. Company C had to spend a lot of money on marketing, for example, on websites and Google search optimization, in order to be visible in the On-demand market. Company C had to focus on software development, as well as on a specific product line. To understand the economic side of software Company C needed specialists with a finance background. The company understood that only with both types of employees (developers and business experts), could they understand the market and develop software to solve relevant problems. Apart from their core competences (software development), which were performed in-house, the company followed an outsourcing strategy in all other aspects.

In order to develop a deeper understanding of cloud technology, the company emphasized cooperation with different partners. They worked together with research organizations to gain access to the state of the art in user friendliness and Web interface design. The company also cooperated closely with universities concerning the conversion from Flash to HTML5 clients.

Perhaps the most important cooperation partner was T-Systems. T-Systems can be regarded as an incubator for Company C, supporting the successful development of the smaller partner through an array of resources and services, as well as a broad network of contacts. The relationship between the company and T-Systems was a win–win situation. T-Systems received know-how from the smaller partner and introduced Company C to its established customers. T-Systems’s customers, who already used the company’s storage capacity, infrastructure, and servers, were also offered a Company C enterprise content management solution. This piggy back procedure gave the smaller company access to many customers.

## Case 4: Company D

This fast growing company, which specializes in E-commerce software, started its transformation process in 2005. The cofounder admitted that the motivation for the investment in SaaS came from their expectations that revenues would increase dramatically. Apart from the revenue aspect, the company saw new market potential emerging from its growing internationalization efforts. Its worldwide expansion gave it access to the emerging markets in Eastern Europe, Asia, and South America, where consumer budgets were lower and required a different software sales model. Company D found these markets to especially suitable for SaaS products. Additionally, the competitive environment in the home country changed: Not only did start-ups offering cloud-based services enjoy positive customer reactions, but customers’ buying behavior was also changing. IT departments, the traditional buyers of software products, were being replaced with non-experts. Thus, preferences changed from comprehensive On-premises solutions to more flexible and less IT-dependent On-demand solutions. Company D found that fashion stores seemed to be a perfect SaaS consumer. Fashion shops have neither IT departments, nor IT experts, however, they need scalable E-commerce software, as their business experiences peak times at, for example Christmas. These observations led to the decision to invest in SaaS.

The main issue in the transformation process was the fundamental change in the software architecture. To offer welldeveloped On-demand software, the On-premises E-commerce software had to be rewritten. This effort took five years and required a great deal of money. At the beginning of the transformation process, the company invested in servers to have more control over service level agreements, availability, and desired features. A few years later, it cooperated with infrastructure providers. This turned out to be a better strategy, as the peak times were balanced by realizing scalability with an external provider.

Initially, separating the development and sales department into cloud and On-premises business units was not an option. However, with its number of employees growing, the company decided to do just that. Both interviewees agreed that it had been an inevitable step to reduce conflicts over resources. Additionally, the incentive model for salespersons and the management had to be adapted to encourage them to support the On-demand products. The company made upfront payments to incentivize sales staff. This strategic step helped promote On-demand sales, but forced the company to spend a considerable amount of money in advance. According to the co-founder, the most important point was that the CEO and top level management had also been incentivized. He believed that no manager would have supported the transformation if he or she could not profit (e.g. receive bonuses) from the restructuring. Company D financed these measurements from its cash flow.

Company D followed a stepwise transformation process. An abrupt shift from On-premises solutions to a subscription model would have resulted in a sudden drop in turnover. The company knew that its investors and customers were keeping an eye on the company’s performance. Further, Company D believed that its existing On-premises customers should be included in the transformation process. The goal was to ease these customers into accepting the new cloud offerings instead of confronting them with completely new products. Not only was the company’s On-demand software an entry opportunity for new customers but the existing customer base could also add complementary cloud services to their existing On-premises solutions. The company profited greatly from this complementary product strategy.

The company did not explicitly involve its customers in the development process, although they followed a trial and error strategy in respect of the price level. It took some time to evaluate consumers’ willingness to pay for SaaS.

The transformation process required a change in the company’s internal competences. Cloud computing services require companies to be far more consumer-centric, as customers cannot fix the bugs themselves. Managing customer expectations would therefore be totally different. Internally. the company searched for intrinsically motivated people to be trained in the new mindset and with the technology. The company also decided to invest in hiring knowledgeable and experienced people Moreover, a whole team had to be formed to provide 24/7 cloud support. Different organizational structures were tested to implement these human resource changes. The best solution was to separate the development and sales department into cloud and On-premises business units. The support that consultants provided and the strategic acquisition of other companies were other sources of competences. The company bought a Canadian company in 2011. This company specialized in

## Case 5: Company E

At the CeBIT computer expo 2000, this company, which specializes in project management software, was struck by the idea to start providing a new kind of software. The software was ASP and, together with Siemens, Company E started working on this technology. However, the project was abandoned two years later, due to limited customer demand. In 2004, the market seemed to be more open to new technologies and the company saw a second chance, this time in respect to cloud computing technology. Its experiences on the former project allowed the company to benefit greatly in terms of increased development speed. Additionally, its On-premises software was already Web-based and written in Java, providing the On-demand solution with starting advantages.

Since the company was small, it could not form a separate team to concentrate exclusively on the development of On-demand software. Its employees tried to work on both software solutions, which was not an easy task. However, they supported the management’s transformation decision. This was especially true of the software developers and sales staff. At the beginning of the transformation process, the company hosted the On-demand software on its own servers. Growing demand and fears regarding accessibility led the company to outsource the server hosting to specialized partners. The transformation was financed from its cash flow, which allowed the company to decide on strategic steps without investor interference. An interviewee reported that consumers’ trust in SaaS was one of the critical factors in the transformation process. To persuade customers to accept cloud-based products, the company concentrated on its core competencies (software development) which would reduce the risk of critical security issues.

The company adopted an aggressive sales strategy for its On-demand products. Every (potential and existing) customer was actively asked to switch from On-premises to On-demand software. The sales manager admitted that this made them lose their focus, which led to the company losing customers in the process. The company pushed its On-demand services hard, although the cloud-based product was not yet bug-free. In hindsight, the company knew that the On-demand product should have been in a mature stage before it was promoted aggressively. During this process, Company E’s largest customers showed interest in the On-demand software, but ultimately decided on the On-premises version. Additionally, Company E discovered another potential customer group: Companies without IT departments that needed support. This target group led the company to provide its On-demand customers with guidance, consultation, and constant service. The interviewee admitted, however, that Company E had initially left its new cloud customers to their own devices too early. The company learned that guidance was expensive but essential when offering software in the cloud. To control this issue, the company cooperated with partners offering tutorials and telephone assistance. Another strategic decision was the company’s backup strategy: It offered On-demand customers a monthly data backup. This sales argument worked well. Over the last few years, customers’ acceptance of cloud computing technologies has grown. In 2012 and 2013, the company provided 50% of its customer base with On-demand software.

In 2010, Company E offered its On-demand software on the Fujitsu Business Solutions Store. The company’s cooperation with Fujitsu required regular meetings to integrate their technology, infrastructure, and philosophy. This coordination process took two years. The large investment in the partnership paid off as it provided many marketing opportunities, the exchange of knowhow, and consultant support. In 2014, the company built another important partnership with Deutsche Telekom, and is now one of the German company’s 17 partners in the Telekom Business Marketplace. Company E profited greatly from its big partners; its attendance at CeBIT and its membership of a Cloud Ecosystem were key to finding these strategic partners. Although these partnerships enabled the company to be successful, the company had to choose partners very carefully. If a partnership were to fail, Company E’s survival would also be at risk. To minimize this risk, the management decided to only choose big and well-established players as cooperation partners. The interviewee underlined that, besides this important cooperation with big partners, it was the ‘‘Best in Cloud” award that communicated the company’s innovative character, strengthened its public image, and enabled it to reach new customers. In order to manage the transformation process, its employees had to be internally educated. The company also benefited from engaging an external consultant regarding strategic prognoses and cost estimations, with the consultant supporting the management strategically throughout the process.

## Applicability of Christensen’s strategies

## Spin-off strategy

In small to middle-sized firms, a disconnected spin-off was not possible from an organizational and financial point of view. The companies therefore formed online groups (e.g. Company B), or divided a department into On-demand and On-premises units (Company D). However all the cases confirmed that the separation of responsibility and resources eased the start of the transformation. The spin-off strategy is Christensen’s most influential strategy for managing disruptive innovations (Sandström et al., 2009) and our case studies confirm that it is applicable to companies in the software industry However, companies’ size determined how they more specifically handled such segregation of their SaaS business.

## Leader strategy

‘‘It’s not the big one who eats the little one, but the fast one that eats the slow one” (Company A). This mind-set inspired company A to venture into the new market at an early stage. Some companies laid the foundation for their leader strategy by having an On-premises product with some of the features of the On-demand product (e.g. Company B), or by leveraging their experiences in previous projects (Company E). Strategic decisions which maximized the benefits possible from these foundations led to our case study firms transferring their software faster than their rival firms. Additionally, it should be noted that offering both types of software (On-premises and On-demand) was a successful strategy in the early phase and had sig naling effects on customers. When the market was finally ready to absorb On-demand software, customers switched without changing providers. Company D specifically pursued a leader strategy by focusing on international emerging markets. In these emerging markets, consumer budgets were lower and a different distribution model was required. Tellis (2006) notes that market leaders focus on future emerging mass markets and are concerned about competitors getting there first.

## Expert opinion strategy

Expert opinions were collected from multiple sources, and not only from the technology department e.g. Company A received a lot of feedback from its ecosystem, cooperative partners, and customers. Company B took big players into account, but admitted that it would have been advisable to work in closer collaboration with partners and customers. Companies C and E specifically focused on core competences and cooperated with agencies and external consultants to ensure that they made the right decisions. The transformation was only feasible with the help of a broad network (Company C), as well as through constant exchanges with early adopters (Company E). Tellis (2006) points out that both technological and market evolution are important forces that managers must confront and analyze. Well-established companies in the software industry extended the expert opinion strategy to a wider range of sources in order to profit from these

## Trial and error strategy

The fourth category focuses on the use of test markets or test products (Sandström et al., 2009). Initially some companies (e.g. Companies A and B) tried to transfer a nearly unchanged On-premises product to the cloud. When this strategy failed, they realized that they had to transform their internal competences before offering an On-demand product. Christensen et al. (2004) called this phenomenon ‘cramming’. The learning and prototyping process in all the case study companies was expensive and took a great deal of time. Some companies used test markets for their first On-demand product version (e.g. Company A), or used internal feedback loops to improve it (e.g. Company B). Company D followed a trial and error strategy concerning the price level. The trial and error strategy seemed to be particularly applicable to incumbents in the software industry and may be one of the most important strategies.

We conclude that the four strategies derived from the disruptive innovation theory are applicable to companies in the software industry, but should be adapted according to the different characteristics of the company.

## Individual strategies and commonalities

Besides the four management strategies presented above, the companies also developed individual strategies to cope with the transformation process.

## Recruitment strategy

Adner and Snow (2010) state that a company’s transformation may require substantial changes in personnel. Bigger companies emphasized the importance of recruiting internal and external staff who are innovative and experienced. Christensen and Raynor (2003) believe that in order to have the right people companies need to understand that skills are shaped by people’s experiences in previous assignments. Companies A and D used head hunters to entice employees from other big companies and searched for intrinsically motivated people, Company A ensured that it was attractive to potential emplovees by offering financial incentives and independent working structures. In addition, Company A offered secure jobs, and a positive brand name. Owing to its positive image, Company A was easily able to recruit new staff, and promote the newly developed On-demand software. Smaller companies followed other strategies. In order to generate new ideas without acquiring new employees, Companies B and C cooperated with students and universities. These actions also gave them the chance to get to know potential new employees.

## Direct sales strategy

The companies’ distribution strategies were significantly different for their On-demand offerings. For example, Company B depended heavily on resellers for its On-premises product. The core of the new strategy consisted of two parts: the adjustment of the incentive structures and the development of direct On-demand software sales. The other case studies also emphasized the importance of new distribution strategies. During and after the development of their On-demand software, they concentrated on new ways of distributing SaaS. Boillat and Legner (2013) state that a change in favor of direct sales has a major impact on the evolution of software companies’ ecosystems. Value-added resellers, integrators, and software vendors have traditionally played an important role in the distribution process, but companies now have to find other strategies to support On-demand sales.

## Step-by-step strategy

Gilbert and Bower (2002) believe that companies must frame a potentially disruptive innovation as an opportunity instead of a threat. In all our case studies the companies took this opportunity and started the transformation process by focusing on establishing a new business area rather than attempting transformation of the entire company. Companies targeted new business while retaining their existing markets. The companies’ challenge was to balance the benefits of keeping the two business (models) separate while at the same time integrating them enough so as to allow them to exploit synergies with one another (Markides and Charitou, 2004). This process takes time and requires a substantial amount of money. While the companies transformed, the core business continued to work independently alongside the new business area (Chandy and Tellis, 1998). Company A wanted to maintain its market leader position in the On-premises world. Companies B, C, and E wanted to avoid the cannibalization of their established products, while Company D feared an abrupt drop in its turnover that would have drawn negative attention from its investors and customers.

Smaller customers in the B2B market seem to be more comfortable with accepting On-demand software compared to bigger customers, which are still relatively reserved about SaaS (Berendes et al., 2013). Thus, addressing smaller customers rather than established customer firms was the answer to finding new customer groups in the SaaS market. For example, Company A had previously provided big companies with their On-premises software, but developed On-demand software for middle-sized companies. Company B served middle-sized companies in the On-premises world, while focusing on smal companies in the On-demand market. It seems that companies have to take a step back when developing a product based on a new technology. By pursuing a step-by-step strategy the companies did not cannibalize their established markets and could grow with their new customer group.

## Partnership and ecosystem strategy

The literature provides evidence that partnerships and ecosystems are key to transformation (Adner, 2006; Adner and Snow, 2010; Sandström et al., 2009). Through our case studies, we observed the influence and importance of partnerships and ecosystems combined with the acquisition of other companies. Our companies were embedded in an ecosystem (especially Company A due to the company size), or cooperated with institutes to gain access to the state of the art (Company C). Company C profited from big cooperation partners, such as T-Systems, which operated as an incubator for the company. Company E worked in close collaboration with Fujitsu and the Telekom Business Marketplace, profiting from an enormous cloud ecosystem. Part of the acquisition strategy was to look for companies with a good cultural fit. Each acquisition needed a tailor-made integration strategy, which preserved the innovation capability of the acquired company. Companies A, B, and D profited from acquired companies’ innovation capability, know-how, and technology.

## Visionary top-management strategy

Strong management, with determined and ambitious managers supported the vision to transform the case study companies. All our case study interviewees emphasized that their transformation was only possible because of top level guidance. History shows that software companies have managed fundamental changes in their industry, for example, the change from mainframes to personal computers. This innovation required more than just expertise in handling daily business. The difference between a successful and an unsuccessful company was the result of differences in top management. Firms that were well managed and able to handle sustaining innovation were not necessarily able to manage a potentially disruptive innovation. Only those companies that were able to manage a potentially disruptive innovation were able to survive in the long run. Although all managers were aware of a potentially disruptive innovation, only some managers were able to react adequately. Managers who believed that their business environment did not allow them to pursue the potentially disruptive innovation decided not to invest in the new technology because it was initially not profitable enough and would have meant removing scarce resources from sustaining developments, which they felt were necessary to remain competitive. Strong management with the vision to invest in a potentially disruptive innovation was essential for economic survival. This finding is also strongly supported in the literature (Adner and Snow, 2010; Chandy and Tellis, 1998; Tellis, 2006).

Further we detected influences across strategies that were contingent on the company’s age. These dependences occurred between the leader and the visionary top-management strategy. Companies that had been in the software industry for a long time had experienced fundamental changes in the market before (Campbell-Kelly, 2001; Bonaccorsi et al., 2006). Thus, the management of more experienced companies was more sensitive to future trends. Although all the companies in our sample had a visionary manager, it became clear that a manager at the top of a company with a long history was more alert to potentially disruptive changes in the market compared to a manager of a middle-aged company. This sensitiveness had an impact on the time taken to step into the SaaS market. While company A (founded in 1972) stepped into the market in 2005, Company B (founded in 1988) did it in 2007 and Company C (founded in 1990) in 2010.

Interestingly, these dependencies were not seen in Company D and E. We reexamined the data and searched for an explanation. Company E (founded in 1988) stepped into the market in 2004. The reason for the early entry was the company’s former experience in the ASP market. Company E used its previous experiences constructively to develop SaaS. Thus, this company was a model for a pure leader strategy. Company D (founded in 1997) stepped into the market in 2005. In this case, we found evidence that the manager characterized himself as risk seeking. Consequently, the combination of the leader and the visionary top-management strategy might not only be dependent on the company’s age but also on a manager’s persona characteristics.

Other contextual factors, e.g. a company’s global presence, also warrant discussion. Global representation impacted the expert opinion strategy as these companies had access to experts all over the world. The trial and error strategy was also affected because globally represented companies used their emerging markets as test markets before they offered the product in their domestic market. The recruitment strategy too was influenced by a global representation because these companies had access to employees all over the world – not only in terms of quality (high potentials) but also in terms of quantity. This is an interesting finding and it is supported by literature on a software company’s internationalization (Picot et al., 2014).

Organizational structure should also be taken into account when describing interdependencies. Competence allocation seems to play an important role. We learned that top-level support was essential for a successful transformation and for motivating employees. Wong et al. (2011) found that decentralization of decision making increased the topmanagement’s ability to gather information on stakeholder needs, thereby yielding a higher corporate performance; thus, giving employees some power of decision impacts the success of transformation. Organizational structure depends on the company size so this is especially important for big companies. Contextual factors and their influence on the management of potentially disruptive innovations should not be underestimated.

## Conclusion

Over the decades software companies have struggled with the management of disruptive innovations. We seek to contribute to this important area. The objective of this study was to investigate the management of disruptive innovation in the software industry using the example of SaaS. We examined the management strategies of well-established software companies who had successfully entered the SaaS market. We have uncovered nine management strategies that may help managers successfully transform from an On-premises provider to a company focusing on SaaS. These strategies are outlined in Table 5.

Comparing our findings with the four strategies of Christensen et al. we identified some needed modifications. The strategy to establish an independent spin-off seems wise however it is not feasible in the case of smaller companies. Thus, we propose broadening this strategy to the establishment of independent teams in the case of small and middle-sized companies and for companies with fewer resources. The expert opinion strategy canvassed opinions more broadly than expected. Software companies rely on many experts when making strategic decisions. Thus, not only technological staff members had their opinions taken into account when making strategic decisions, but also internal and external experts. Additionally we found out that a recruitment strategy and a direct sales strategy are important to successfully transform a company. Furthermore we saw that companies benefit from visionary top management. We ascertained that this was a key to strategic transformation. We saw that cooperation partners play an important role in the transformation process. Smaller firms greatly benefit from an incubator’s support and the integration in a big player’s market places.

At this point, we would like to point out some research on an innovators’ ecosystem. According to Adner (2012), there is a difference between innovations that succeed and innovations that fail. Adner says that an innovation cannot succeed if its value creation depends on other innovations. He describes the ‘blind spot’ as failing to see how a company’s success depends on partners who would themselves need to innovate. Adner found that for successful innovation two things are important – the company’s execution of promises and the partner’s commitment to the company. Thus, a company needs an ecosystem supporting the innovation. Our partnership and ecosystem strategy is in line with Adner’s (2012) findings. Christensen considers Adner’s work on ecosystems to be a path-breaking perspective on innovation (Christensen, 2014). Adner also emphasizes that great management is needed to pursue potentially disruptive innovations which in turn supports our visionary top-management strategy

We learned that the market for cloud solutions is growing, but that the current market demand still falls short of expectations. Berendes et al. (2013) have established that cloud solutions are as yet only useful for fast-growing start-up companies. Larger companies with special requirements cannot thus far realize cost reductions. Boillat and Legner (2013) also confirm that business users show a relatively slow migration towards cloud solutions and suggest that this is because business users have more complex IT needs compared to private users. B2B software is often used to support core business operations that are critical and linked to security and performance concerns. The result is that well-established and experienced customers are switching more slowly than expected to SaaS solutions compared to smaller IT-inexperienced customers. The latter search for simpler products and need service support. Christensen and Raynor (2003) term this creation of a new value network ‘new market disruption’. It occurs when a product addresses non-consumption, i.e. customers who lack the money or capacity to buy and use the established products or services.

## Contribution

Our findings contribute to both theory and practice. The major contribution of this study is a set of management strategies for managing potentially disruptive innovations (Section ‘Conclusion’). The description of possible dependencies and influences across different strategies (Section ‘Dependencies and influences across different strategies’) leads to a more explanatory theorization and provides advice for practice

Currently, cooperation and acquisition strategies, sales strategies, pricing strategies, and development strategies are important strands of research in IS (Buxmann et al., 2012). The management of software companies is an emerging field of research in IS (Bichler et al., 2014). Boillat and Legner (2013) identified a demand for research on software companies management of disruptive innovation. With our study, we open up the research strand – the management of disruptive innovation – and simultaneously contribute to the topics of managing software companies, cloud computing, and SaaS. We validated the applicability of Christensen’s (1997) strategies for software companies and extended them by drawing on the SaaS example. Expanding an existing theory is considered a theoretical contribution (Whetten, 1989). We also enhanced the body of knowledge on disruptive innovation by linking back the newly identified strategies to other literature on disruptive innovation i.e. research on an innovators’ ecosystem and the visionary top-management strategy (Adner, 2012).

## Limitations and further research

As with any research, this paper has a number of limitations. The results can only be generalized to a certain extent. This study’s findings should only be applied in an appropriate context. Our multiple case study approach which included five organizations operating in different markets, partially addresses this limitation. For external validity it is important to follow replication logic (Eisenhardt, 1989) as the generalization of findings is not automatic. Thus, our results should be tested by replicating the study. Once such replications have been made and our results confirmed, they might be accepted as providing strong support for generalization.

While we generated knowledge concerning the management of potentially disruptive innovations in software companies our case we drew on the example of SaaS and we should point out that two of our nine management strategies, namely the direct sales strategy and the step-by-step strategy, are tailored to this example. The theoretical contribution would be enlarged if all strategies were generalizable to any new and potentially disruptive technology e.g. Metcalfe (2005) discusses different conceptions of making knowledge claims general. The direct sales strategy could be stated more generally to make it applicable to any new technology in software companies. Companies that are faced with a potentially disruptive innovation should think about new ways to distribute their products or services; that is, companies should reconsider their distribution strategy. The step-by-step strategy could also be generalized. Companies should not try to invent a comprehensive new product for large customers right at the beginning of the transformation process. They should cooperate with smaller customers and find out what the market really needs. In the course of time they can enlarge their offering to bigger customers.

This study relies partly on retrospective data; the respondents may therefore have recalled their decisions or events imperfectly. In order to minimize this possible effect, we triangulated the collected data. We tried to shed light on transformation decisions and events from more than one perspective. Conducting interviews with two employees per company was important to reconstruct facts more accurately. One could argue that more interviews per company could have enriched the data; further interviews would have been necessary if there had been contradictions or if parts of the transformation process had been unclear, however, this was not true. Additionally, we used multiple sources to cross-check statements. More research should, however, be done (Bower and Gilbert, 2005) to validate the present study’s findings. Thus, future research should expand the study to other incumbents in the software industry. Additionally, it would be interesting to see if there are specific strategy patterns in respect of company size and the markets in which they operate
