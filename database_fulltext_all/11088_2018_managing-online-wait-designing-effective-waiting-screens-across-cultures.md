# Managing online wait: Designing e<sup>f</sup>ective waiting screens across cultures

Andrew N.K. Chen<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Younghwa Lee<sup>b</sup>, Yujong Hwang<sup>c,d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Accounting and Information Systems, School of Business, University of Kansas, Lawrence, KS 66045, USA

<sup>b</sup> Armstrong Institute for Interactive Media Studies, Department of Information Systems and Analytics, Farmer School of Business, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056 USA

<sup>c</sup> School of Accountancy & MIS, Driehaus College of Business, DePaul University, Chicago, Illinois, USA

<sup>d</sup> College of International Studies, Kyung Hee University, Yongin, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keyword: Online wait Time a<sup>f</sup>ordance Hedonic valence Background color Culture Perceived waiting time

## A B S T R A C T

We investigate how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively manage online wait using a waiting screen with progress cues in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures. A research model is developed based on uncertainty reduction model, resource allocation model, color psychology, and Hofstede’s cultural model. Two controlled lab experiments (n = 269 subjects each) were conducted in U.S. and Taiwan. We found that high hedonic valence and high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance were more effective to induce greater enjoyment and less uncertainty respectively, resulting in shorter perceived waiting time. We also found the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant background color e<sup>f</sup>ect and interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects between culture and progress cue design.

## 1. Introduction

As the Internet becomes the de facto means for people around the globe to search and download contents, purchase goods, and conduct business, many wait situations occur and waiting online can pose a challenge for both web content providers and users because of its negative impact, including stress and dissatisfaction with online stores, resulting in site abandonment and loss of revenue [28]. A recent study of Walmart Labs investigates its websites’ slow page loading issue and <sup>fi</sup>nds that every 100 ms improvement on page loading time of its website leads to a 1% increase in incremental revenue and every 1 second improvement leads to a 2% boost in conversion rates. This study also found that online travel bookings tallied \$129 billion dollars in 2013 but 43% of online travel and lodging shoppers abandoned a site after three seconds of wait.<sup>1</sup> Nah [42] found that the maximum tolerable waiting time online (for information retrieval) is only two seconds. Considering the crucial <sup>fi</sup>nancial impacts of online wait, e-vendors have made considerable investments to technically reduce online waiting time yet the outcomes are still ine<sup>f</sup>ective.

One of the most pervasively adopted alternative solutions is displaying a waiting screen, a webpage shown to users while they wait for responses, such as search results or <sup>fi</sup>les to be downloaded [34]. These waiting screens can contain a variety of interface cues, including progress cues, background color, text, images, and multimedia. For example, a progress cue can provide a graphical provision of waiting duration information (e.g., a <sup>fi</sup>lling bar) or indicate the processing in progress only (e.g., moving dots). These various interface cues are expected to manage online users’ waiting perception (e.g., uncertainty, enjoyment, and waiting time), and ultimately a<sup>f</sup>ect their behaviors favorably towards corresponding websites. However, to date practitioners still tend to design waiting screens based on their own instincts, gut feelings, or educated guesses [34], with limited scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research on online wait management using waiting screens just starting to emerge (e.g., [6,28]). Therefore, there is an imperative need for rig orous research to provide valid and reliable guidance for waiting screen design.

In addition, as online users become more globalized and increasing numbers of organizations operate more than one version of their website across the globe, researchers have emphasized the cultural consideration in web design such as website localization, cultural congruity, and culturability [3,11,12,56]. Barber and Badre [3] recommend incorporating appropriate ‘cultural markers’ in websites, which are “interface design elements and features that are prevalent, and possibly preferred, within a particular cultural group.” Given this background, the primary goal of this research is to propose and test a model of online wait management that explains how di<sup>f</sup>erent interface cues (i.e., progress cues, background color) on a waiting screen in<sup>fl</sup>uence online users’ waiting perception (i.e., perceived uncertainty, perceived enjoyment, perceived waiting time) and behavior (i.e., sa tisfaction, use intention) with consideration of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences (i.e., across two countries with distinct cultures).<sup>2</sup>

We develop our research model based on two models related to time perception: uncertainty reduction model [21,46] and resource allocation model [66], conjecturing that progress cues and background color a<sup>f</sup>ect users’ perception and behavior during a wait. We conducted two controlled experiments by manipulating types of progress cues (low vs. high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance; low vs. high hedonic valence) and background colors (blue vs. red) with a total of 538 subjects from two countries (U.S. and Taiwan) representing di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures. Such a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c study on waiting screen design across cultures can help advance our understanding of the relationships between web interface cues and culture for both researchers and practitioners.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical background

Based on four theoretical references including uncertainty reduction model, resource allocation model, color psychology, and Hofstede’s cultural model, we build our study around the time a<sup>f</sup>ordance and hedonic valence of progress cues, background color of a waiting screen, and culture and website design. We describe each in detail in the fol lowing subsections.

## 2.1. Progress cue design

## 2.1.1. Time afordance and perceived uncertainty

Positing the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of cues on people’s tracking and perception of time passage, researchers [21,66] have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed cues with distinct levels of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance. According to Conn [10], ‘time affordance’ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “a presentation of the properties of a delay in a task or anticipated event that may be used by an actor (e.g., user) to determine the need for an interrupting or facilitating action (p. 188).” Computer and web design in the human-computer interaction (HCI) literature utilize certain interface cues that provide information or feedback to users during a wait can be referred to as ‘time a<sup>f</sup>ordance (e.g., [44]). That is, cues with high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance are those that provide information about the duration of the wait, including the percentage or portion of time remaining, the exact time remaining, and an individual’s position in a waiting queue [41].

Perceived uncertainty (PU) represents ambiguity or lack of con-<sup>fi</sup>dence in the likelihood that an event will occur [60]. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of cues with high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance to reduce perceived waiting time (PWT) has been reported based on the uncertainty reduction model [21,39,41], predicting that the PWT will seem shorter and negative feeling toward the wait will be reduced when individuals receive temporal information about the wait. That is, when perceived uncertainty about the wait is reduced, people tend to worry less about the wait and waiting time, resulting in shorter PWT. Therefore, time affordance is designed to reduce the uncertainty of the wait [45], and can include the di<sup>f</sup>erent components showing progress, working, excep tion, remainder, and completion [10,55]. In HCI <sup>fi</sup>eld, one common time a<sup>f</sup>ordance design, a progress cue, provides the indication of progressing of a computer-based task as waiting situation occurs. The design can take a higher level (e.g., a progress cue with the graphical provision of wait duration information such as a <sup>fi</sup>lling bar) or a lower level (e.g., a progress cue showing the processing in progress such as repeated moving dots or a moving bar).

## 2.1.2. Hedonic valence and perceived enjoyment

Web interfaces contain both functional and aesthetic components [31]. Thus, the aim of web designers should be to come up with a website that is not only useful and usable but also conveys positive emotions and feelings through its aesthetic features [4,65]. Venkatesh [63] suggested that perceived enjoyment (PE) represents the experience of using a system being perceived to be enjoyable regardless of resulting performance from such use. However, previous HCI and IS studies have mostly focused on functional aspects of interface design [34], placing too much emphasis on performance issues and not enough on emotional aspects, such as pleasure, fun, and excitement [31]. This can be attributed to the fact that traditionally the IS discipline is more accustomed to tackling ‘hard’ problems than ‘soft’ issues such as aesthetics, and IS developers are unfamiliar with established visual design practice [44]. Web visitors appear to appreciate hedonic attraction from emotional involvement and a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation of web interfaces. Thus, a traditional functional-oriented design with low hedonic valence can probably induce only partially positive website perception. That is, e<sup>f</sup>ective web interfaces should contain aesthetic value and, therefore, incorporate design with high hedonic valence to induce enjoyable experience for online users. As a result, IS researchers have started to investigate the a<sup>f</sup>ective dimension of systems and web elements used for in<sup>fl</sup>uencing users’ perceived enjoyable experience and response [12,34,67].

The resource allocation model [66] suggests that the use of cues, in particular, visually attractive ones, can distract people from thinking about the wait (i.e., focus their attention to visually appealing cues during wait) as a way to increase enjoyment and reduce PWT. The rationale supporting this perspective is that if more attention is directed toward those cues during a wait, there are fewer resources available for time estimation, resulting in a shorter PWT [38]. In particular, if a cue with high hedonic valence that provides aesthetics and pleasure impression is presented, it e<sup>f</sup>ectively attracts online users’ attention and distract them from waiting situation while invoking positive emotions during wait. Along this line, waiting time literature suggests that affective stimuli can improve customer mood and waiting time perception and, therefore, calls for attention on hedonic component design [8]. For example, Cai and Xu [7] suggest that hedonic components could attract users’ attention and provide them with an enjoyable in teractive experience. We intend to further examine whether interface cues with higher hedonic valence on a waiting screen can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ perceived enjoyment of a waiting experience and subsequent positive response.

## 2.2. Color

In our daily life, color is believed to be perceived on almost every object that people view; it is even present in our dreams [51]. Studies (e.g., [23,64]) show that colors can carry di<sup>f</sup>erent emotional and psychological properties to people and at the same time in<sup>fl</sup>uence both human behavior and human physiology [37]. In the color psychology <sup>fi</sup>elds, many aspects of human’s psychological functioning related to color have been studied such as physiological reactions to color, color e<sup>f</sup>ects on emotions, and color e<sup>f</sup>ects on behavior. For example, Lee et al. [33] pointed out that color is a powerful tool for attracting an individual’s attention, bringing out the desire to consume, and making communication more e<sup>fi</sup>cient. Further, Bonnardel et al. [4] suggested that people are more attracted when they see their favorite colors and look at their favorite color longer. Companies have utilized color to manipulate brand images in customers’ minds [53]. Considering that the Lanham Act in the United States was passed to protect product colors as trademarks, there is a clear indication of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of color for conveying meaning [37].

In the HCI area, color has often been used as a factor to in<sup>fl</sup>uence people’s perception, experiences, and behaviors [1,4]. It is commonly believed that each color is associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent human perception, and the ideal use of color can not only enhance pleasant sensations but also increase usage, recognition, additional value, brand image, and personal identity [61]. In the web environment, Cyr et al. [12] <sup>fi</sup>nd that color appeal, elicited by choice of colors, a<sup>f</sup>ects trust or satisfaction of online shoppers. Gorn et al. [20] suggest that red color can arouse and excite people while blue can relax people; therefore, blue used on a webpage’s background elicits more relaxed feelings, resulting in perceived quickness of processing.

## 2.3. Cultural diferences and website design

Culture represents an integrated system of learned behavioral pat terns that are characteristics of the members of a society [24]. People with similar cultural backgrounds are inclined to learn and share their preferences, beliefs, attitudes, habits, and behaviors. Therefore, to ensure e<sup>f</sup>ective and harmonious interactions between people from different cultural backgrounds, it is necessary to use corresponding cul tural symbols, which can better shape internal interpretive processes and convey shared understanding. In the HCI area, the web is not considered a culturally neutral medium, and as a result, website design preferences should be expected to vary across cultures [11,58]. While standard web interface design principles can still apply, there is a clear need for applying distinct components that can accommodate the needs of individuals and groups of di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures.

The process of designing a website to be coherent with the culture of the target audience is called cultural adaptation [57]. Cultural adaptation can be achieved by the use of ‘cultural markers’ or interface design elements that can signify a cultural a<sup>fi</sup>liation within a particular cultural group, including the use of the native language, national symbols, background color, graphics, and spatial orientation [3]. Cul tural markers are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of local environment and the pre sence, and/or absence of cultural markers in local websites can a<sup>f</sup>ect local users’ attitudes towards the website and web usage. Therefore, a successful and e<sup>f</sup>ective localized website design should apply di<sup>f</sup>erent modes of cultural markers to di<sup>f</sup>erent target audiences. Many studies <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences in the use of symbols, graphics, colors, and other site features (e.g., [11,12]). Along the same vein, di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural markers in a waiting screen signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect online users’ perceptions. A few studies have conducted and found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uences of culture on waiting perceptions and behaviors in online and physical environments [14,52]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there was no proactive study to investigate the relationship between culture, website design and online users’ waiting perceptions, which is worth of further exploration.

One of the best-known framework for comparing cultural di<sup>f</sup>er ences at the country level is Hofstede’s cultural model [25,27]. Hofstede’s cultural dimensions have been extensively validated, widely cited, regarded as in<sup>fl</sup>uential, and replicated and used extensively in the culture research [40,65]. In the context of the web environment, cul ture-related studies have applied and found Hofstede’s framework to be a valid basis for analyzing regional di<sup>f</sup>erences and identifying cultural markers to adapt web interface design to local cultures (e.g., [58]). Taiwan and U.S. are two very di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures in measures from all dimensions from the Hofstede s cultural model. For two constructs used in our research model (i.e., perceived uncertainty and perceived en joyment) and supported by the abovementioned theoretical models (i.e., uncertainty reduction model and resource allocation model), we focus on high versus low uncertainty avoidance and indulgence versus restraint because the two cultural dimensions deems to be related to these constructs as well as context of study (waiting perception and color psychology) along which Taiwan and USA di<sup>f</sup>er.

## 2.3.1. Uncertainty avoidance

This dimension indicates the extent to which “members of a certain culture feel threatened by unknown or uncertain situations [24,p. 161].” It describes a society’s tolerance for uncertainty and ambiguity, and the degree of uncertainty avoidance represents how a society or a country in general deals with uncertainty. In a country with a strong tendency toward uncertainty avoidance (e.g., Taiwan 69)<sup>3</sup> people prefer more certainty and clear guidance of procedures, rules, and structures. On the other hand, people living in a culture with weak uncertainty avoidance $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , U.S. 46) are able to handle uncertainty and ambiguity with relative ease and little discomfort [27]. This cultural di<sup>f</sup>erence has been linked to website design. For example, Cyr [11] <sup>fi</sup>nds that website trust is more important for people from a high uncertainty avoidance culture (e.g., China) than for those from a low uncertainty avoidance culture (e.g., Canada). Singh et al. [58] <sup>fi</sup>nd that better navigation design is needed for high uncertainty avoidance culture.

## 2.3.2. Indulgence vs. restraint

Indulgence represents a culture (e.g., U.S. 68)<sup>4</sup> that “allows relatively free grati<sup>fi</sup>cation of basic and natural human drives related to enjoying life and having fun” [27] while a restraint culture (e.g., Taiwan 49) tends to suppress grati<sup>fi</sup>cation with strict social norms. People in the restraint culture keep their emotions in check and maintain their composure, while those in the indulgence culture likely show more enthusiasm and display their natural emotions. This cultural di<sup>f</sup>erence can a<sup>f</sup>ect online users’ behavior. For example, Li et al. [35] <sup>fi</sup>nd that web users from a high indulgence culture (e.g., U.S.) are more likely to share their reviews (in Amazon.com) with motivation related to fun and enjoyment compared to users from a restraint culture (e.g., China).

With consideration of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences (i.e., Taiwan and U.S. with distinct scores in abovementioned two cultural dimensions), this research investigates whether culture has in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the relationship between interface cues and perceived uncertainty (PU) and perceived enjoyment (PE) in the context of online wait. In addition, color is thought of as part of a given culture since the symbolic meaning of color is often di<sup>f</sup>erent given factors such as geographical areas, religions, ethnic, and tradition [61]. Past studies identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that people of different cultures can have dissimilar preferences for color (e.g., [49,62]). Cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences in color meanings and associations have also been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as Leach [32] suggested that cultures ascribe symbolic meaning to di<sup>f</sup>erent colors. Therefore, carefully chosen colors for products, symbols, and web interfaces in an attempt to better associate cultural preference of users can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence their cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluation and, in turn, induce favorable responses and behaviors. Thus, this research chooses color as one of ‘cultural markers and investigates its e<sup>f</sup>ect on online wait context.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

Based on two models related to time perception (i.e., uncertainty reduction and resource allocation), color psychology and related HCI literature, and Hofstede’s cultural model, this research hypothesizes the e<sup>f</sup>ects of interface cues (i.e., progress cue and background color) with consideration of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences on individuals’ waiting perception (i.e., PU and PE) as well as PWT, resulting in users’ satisfaction and use intention with a website (see Fig. 1). This research investigates how time a<sup>f</sup>ordance and hedonic valence of a progress cue can a<sup>f</sup>ect PU and PE respectively. In addition, by using two colors (i.e., blue and red), we examine how di<sup>f</sup>erent background colors might a<sup>f</sup>ect PU and PE. Further, we investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of culture. Finally, we propose a relationship between PU, PE and PWT, and between PWT and response variables, including satisfaction and use intention to a website.

## 3.1. Progress cues

We carry out a systematic inquiry to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of the time a<sup>f</sup>ordance and hedonic valence of a progress cue on waiting percep tions as described below.

## 3.1.1. Time afordance

Previous studies related to human’s time perception in physica environments con<sup>fi</sup>rm the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of progress information on reducing perceived uncertainty [9,21,38]. A progress cue with low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance usually only provides hints that processing is underway during a wait and a progress cue with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance further acts as a visual cue for how much of the wait has passed and how much remains. For example, twinkling stars and continuous rounding circle bars (in Apple products) on a waiting screen implicitly provide a hint that a task is being processed and, therefore, can be categorized as a progress cue with low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance. Meanwhile, a rectangle cumulative progress bar that explicitly conveys temporal information of progress can be considered as a progress cue with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance. Based on the uncertainty reduction model, we expect that users perceive less certainty when they see a progress cue with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance because they see the progress results or can better estimate when the results will appear. In other words, uncertainty about the wait duration tends to be lower by giving users a cue of the waiting progress with high level of time af fordance. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H1: During an online wait, users will experience LESS perceived uncertainty when viewing a waiting screen with a progress cue with high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance than one with low time a<sup>f</sup>ordance.

## 3.1.2. Hedonic valence

As hedonic attributes of objects (e.g., products, stores, or websites) have been found to a<sup>f</sup>ect individuals’ psychological well-being and oscillate around stimulation, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and evocation, the HCI research has emphasized the importance of using positive and hedonic elements in website design to provide users enjoyment, fun, and excitement [67]. For example, Zhang [67] suggests that understanding a<sup>f</sup>ective evaluations and designing hedonic interfaces accordingly enable e-business companies to attract more users to their websites by rendering greater satisfaction. Similarly, Lavie and Tractinsky [31] show that the aesthetic of visual interfaces, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally web-based interfaces, is a strong determinant of users’ satisfaction and pleasure. A progress cue with high level of hedonic valence might use pictures and images with known characters or celebrities as a progress ticker to induce fun and enjoyment, whereas a progress cue with low level of hedonic valence would only utilize simple rectangular bars. Based on the resource allocation model and given the expected high attractiveness of a high hedonic valence progress cue (e.g., a photographic presentation) but predominant implementation of plain and low hedonic valence progress cues in practice [44], it is valuable to investigate progress cues with high level of hedonic valence and their e<sup>f</sup>ects in an online wait context. We expect that online users will be attracted to a progress cue with high level of hedonic valence so that they tend to perceive greater enjoyment during the wait. It is consistent with the prediction of the resource allocation model that people allocate more resources to cues that are emotionally interesting and imagery-provoking, resulting in the increased enjoyment of the waiting experience. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H2: During an online wait, users will experience MORE perceived enjoyment when viewing a progress cue with high hedonic valence than one with low hedonic valence.

## 3.2. Background color

Based on color psychology, color is commonly believed to attract people s attention and impact their psychological reactions [33]. Some colors arouse and excite while others elicit relaxation. For example, blue is frequently regarded as people’s favorite color, inducing high levels of relaxation and comfort, whereas red arouses people to perceive excitement and pleasantness (e.g., [1,20]). In HCI area, it is found that users’ impressions of webpages might be a<sup>f</sup>ected simply by changing color [12]. Considering that blue and red are the two most frequently adopted background colors in commercial websites, we examine their potentially distinct e<sup>f</sup>ect on PU and PE. Previous studies (e.g., [15]) <sup>fi</sup>nd that cool colors, especially blue, are associated with relaxation, less stress, peacefulness, and calm. In a web content download context, Gorn et al. [20] note that the greater feeling of relaxation that blue color induces is associated with a short PWT than yellow and red color. Based on color psychology arguing di<sup>f</sup>erent colors are associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent emotions, feelings and perception, therefore, we predict that web users who see a waiting screen with blue background color (the color for relaxation) will be in a more relaxed mode and thus tend to perceive less uncertainty while waiting than those who watch the screen with a red background color (the color for anxiety). Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: During an online wait, users experience LESS perceived uncertainty when viewing a waiting screen with a blue background color than one with a red background color.

Researchers in color psychology <sup>fi</sup>eld investigated the e<sup>f</sup>ect of color on human perception and behavior and found that some colors are more e<sup>f</sup>ective at inducing a<sup>f</sup>ective feelings such as excitement, arousal, and pleasure [48]. For instance, Gorn et al. [20] <sup>fi</sup>nd that warm colors, especially red, are physically and emotionally arousing and exciting, triggering a<sup>f</sup>ective feelings. Therefore, we conjecture that web users observing a red background color during the wait would be in more a<sup>f</sup>ective state and feel more excitement (enjoyment) than those who observe a blue background color. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4: During an online wait, users experience MORE perceived enjoyment when viewing a waiting screen with a red background colo than one with a blue background color.

## 3.3. Progress cue, background color, and waiting perception across culture

As suggested by the Hofstede cultural model, the ‘uncertainty avoidance’ cultural dimension which is also subsequently referred to by many culture-related studies indicates that people living in a culture with strong uncertainty avoidance $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , Taiwan) experience greater stress and anxiety when facing uncertainty than those with weak un certainty avoidance $( \mathrm { e . g . , \ U . } S . )$ . In an online wait context, we expect that people from a stronger uncertainty avoidance culture (e.g., Taiwan) prefer seeing clear (explicit) guidance of progress than ambiguous (implicit) guidance when compared to people with weaker uncertainty avoidance (e.g., U.S.). Therefore, we conjecture that people from Taiwan tend to exhibit larger di<sup>f</sup>erences of the amount of PU when seeing between a clear guidance of progress $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance) than an ambiguous guidance (i.e., low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance). That is, Taiwanese people are expected to perceive a greater di<sup>f</sup>erence of PU than U.S. people when they see a progress cue with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance than when they observe one with low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5a: During an online wait, users in U.S. will experience LESS dif ference in perceived uncertainty between viewing a progress cue with high level and low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance than those in Taiwan.

According to the cultural dimension of ‘indulgence vs. restraint’ [25], “Indulgence stands for a tendency to allow relatively free grati-<sup>fi</sup>cation of basic and natural human desires related to enjoying life and having fun (p. 281).” On the other hand, restraint side of this dimension is described as ‘moderation’, ‘keeping oneself disinterested and pure’, and ‘having few desires’ (p. 288).” People in the restraint (neutral) culture (e.g., Taiwan) keep their emotions in check and maintain their composure. On the other hand, people in an indulgent (a<sup>f</sup>ective) culture (e.g., U.S.) will likely show more enthusiasm and display their natural emotions. Hofstede [26] also reports di<sup>f</sup>erences between in dulgence and restraint society that include “more likely to remember positive emotions vs. less likely to remember positive emotions (indulgence vs. restraint) (p. 16).” While waiting online, people may perceive more enjoyment when they see a progress cue with high level of hedonic valence in a waiting screen than when they observe a progress cue with low level of hedonic valence. Further, we conjecture that people from the indulgence culture (e.g., U.S.) tend to indicate an even higher degree of increased perceived enjoyment than people from the restraint culture (e.g., Taiwan). This is because there is freedom to express their emotions (e.g. excitement, fun) for the former, while some reservation can be observed for the latter. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5b: During an online wait, users in U.S. will experience MORE di<sup>f</sup>erence in perceived enjoyment between viewing a progress cue with high level and low level of hedonic valence than those in Taiwan.

Color can convey di<sup>f</sup>erent meanings and induce di<sup>f</sup>erent impressions to people from di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures. Researchers have tried to identify the linkages of meanings of speci<sup>fi</sup>c colors to cultural groups (e.g., [43]). For example, Madden et al. [37] found that certain colors did o<sup>f</sup>er di<sup>f</sup>erent meanings across cultures and recommended further cross-cultural investigation of an association between color and brand image. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Gorn et al. [20] indicated that the color blue is the most preferred color for people in the U.S. because it represents comfort and relaxation (related to low uncertainty). Blue was also recognized as the most popular color for bank logos in the U.S. because it is associated with trust and authority where people have less uncertainty about how they will be treated and how their money will be handled [39]. On the other hand, Ou et al. [47] found that Chinese observers associated blue color with heavy or masculine. Along the same vein, we expect that colors used on web interfaces can convey di<sup>f</sup>erent meaning across cultures. Noiwana and Norcio [43] pointed out the value for crosscultural investigation on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of color in online context noting that “interface designers need to understand color appreciation and color responses of people in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures and regions (pp. 103–104).” In our study, we particularly believe that blue background color used in waiting screens helps Americans perceive lesser amount of uncertainty than Taiwanese because the color is more salient to induce trust, relaxation, and comfort in U.S. than Taiwan. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5c: During an online wait, users in U.S. will experience LESS perceived uncertainty when viewing a waiting screen with a blue background color than those in Taiwan.

People from an indulgence culture (e.g., U.S.) are free to have fun as well as express feelings and emotion and enjoy life by gratifying their natural drives. Therefore, they become happier, more optimistic, and extroverted [24]. On the other hand, people from a restraint culture (e.g., Taiwan) seem to take a more frugal and cynical outlook, favoring moral discipline and believing that grati<sup>fi</sup>cation should be suppressed. Kale and Barnes [30] propose that people from a<sup>f</sup>ective (indulgence) cultures would be relatively more receptive to the use of emotion-invoking colors in web design than people from neutral (restraint) cul ture. Therefore, while the color red in general arouses people to perceive excitement and pleasantness, we conjecture that people from indulgence and restraint cultures would exhibit di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of response to this color stimulation. That is, people from U.S. would show a higher degree of emotional reaction to the arousal nature of red than those from Taiwan. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5d: During an online wait, people in U.S. will experience MORE perceived enjoyment when viewing a waiting screen with a red background color than those in Taiwan.

## 3.4. Antecedents and consequences of PWT

Waiting is stressful, and people are more anxious when the wait condition is uncertain and want to escape from waiting situations promptly. Researchers supporting the uncertainty reduction model (e.g., [21,38]) suggest that uncertain waits are perceived to be longer than certain waits. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that merely informing individuals about the delay that will be experienced can reduce their PWT. For example, Dellaert and Kahn [13] note that if countdown information is provided, uncertainty is reduced and waiting time is perceived short. Considering that progress cues are utilized in a waiting screen to provide wait duration information, we conjecture that less perceived uncertainty from including progress cues would decrease PWT of online users.

H6a: Users will experience SHORTER perceived waiting time when they perceive less uncertainty.

Perceived enjoyment is another factor that can a<sup>f</sup>ect PWT. According to the resource allocation theory [66], people show a general tendency to attend to temporal objects that make them perceive a waiting time longer. If non-temporal objects are introduced and can successfully distract individuals’ attention from the estimate of time, then they perceive a shorter wait. Previous studies suggest enjoyable stimuli as non-temporal objects are e<sup>f</sup>ective to reduce PWT. For instance, Baker and Cameron [2] <sup>fi</sup>nd that experiencing positive a<sup>f</sup>ect during a wait can lead to underestimation of the duration of an event. Given this information, we expect that people experiencing more enjoyment during wait would perceive shorter PWT. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H6b: Users will experience SHORTER perceived waiting time when they perceive more enjoyment.

Further, empirical research supports a negative relationship between PWT and website satisfaction and site use intention. For example, Dellaert and Kahn [13] and Hui and Tse [29] report a negative relationship between long wait and satisfaction and between PWT and use intention as well as between satisfaction and use intention (e.g., [19]). Based on this background, we construe that when individuals perceive a shorter waiting time, they are more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the website and will be more inclined to use it. In addition, when individuals are more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with a website, they are more likely to use or revisit it. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Overview

To investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of progress cue design and background color on PU and PE across cultures and to examine the nomological network between PU, PE, PWT, SAT and UI, a pre-test and two controlled experiment studies in two countries were conducted. Data were analyzed using ANOVA tests for experiments and structural equation model analysis for nomological networks. In order to test our research model and hypotheses, we developed a simulated, online music download website by mimicking commercial music download sites.

For cross-cultural comparison, this research chose Taiwan and U.S. as target countries for several reasons. First, these two countries represent two di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures where we can measure the e<sup>f</sup>ect of culture. With respect to Hofstede’s framework, the Taiwanese culture is characterized as a strong uncertainty avoidance and a restraint culture in contrast to the U.S. culture, which is seen as representing weak uncertainty avoidance and indulgence. Second, both Taiwan and U.S. exhibit a high level of web maturity and Internet usage. Their advanced innovation and utilization of web communications are among the leading group in the information technology front. Finally, both countries have many large, multinational organizations with modern globalization and international connections. To test cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences, we <sup>fi</sup>rst designed a simulated website for the U.S. and then create one for Taiwan by translating content and selecting local songs and pop star images. As like other cross-cultural studies, we followed Brislin’s back translation method [5] to address language di<sup>f</sup>erences in our survey.

## 4.2. Participants

We <sup>fi</sup>rst recruited subjects by distributing <sup>fl</sup>iers and class announcements to college students at universities in U.S. and Taiwan who have had at least a one-time online music purchase experience through a commercial online music download site. After eliminating subjects who provided unusable responses, 269 subjects (U.S.: 107, Taiwan: 162) were included for Study 1 and 269 subjects (U.S.: 116, Taiwan: 153) for Study 2 (Table 2). Participation was voluntary. Subjects could earn class participation points and an opportunity to win a sweepstake (small cash prize) in return for their participation.

## 4.3. Procedure

Each participant arrived at a computer lab at a designated time. Web applications with our manipulations were installed on PCs in computer labs. All computer labs were equipped with up-to-date com puters and connected with high-speed network. All computers were set to system default and monitors were set with the same default bright ness and contrast level. Participants were instructed to complete the input screen (including the selection of songs, quality, format, and payment), wait, and then review the results. For the experiments, we designed a simulated music download website that includes an input screen, a waiting screen with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c progress cue design and a background color, and an output screen (see sample interfaces in Appendix A). An input screen contains three main drop-down boxes for music search, including favorite music (songs), quality (best, regular, etc.), and <sup>fi</sup>le format (mp3, wav, etc.). Fifteen songs listed in a major music chart of each participating country in the week preceding the inception of the experiment are included as favorite music to be selected and purchased. After picking and paying for the song that they like to get, users click the ‘Download Now’ button to start the download process. Users then wait 16 seconds to complete downloading music while seeing a waiting screen.<sup>5</sup> As shown in Appendix $\mathrm { A } ,$ each waiting screen consisted of a company logo, a progress cue, and a background color. We designed di<sup>f</sup>erent progress cues by manipulating their levels of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance and hedonic valence. All waiting screens also include either a blue- or a red-colored background, the two most used colors in waiting screens of commercial websites, which also carry quite di<sup>f</sup>erent cultural values for two countries under our investigation. At the end of the wait, an output screen that is the same with the input screen except it displays the information that the download is successfully completed is presented to the participants. Finally, by clicking “Continue” button, each subject proceeds to an online questionnaire. The total amount of time required to complete the task is less than 30 min.

## 4.4. Pre-Test

## 4.4.1. Method

Before the two main studies, we conducted a pre-test to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of a progress bar in an online wait context. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we seek to demonstrate that showing online users a progress bar while waiting for download results leads them to lower perceived uncertainty (or higher perceived enjoyment) than waiting without seeing a progress bar. Eighty eight participants were recruited at a large U.S. Midwestern university for the pre-test and randomly assigned to either a progress bar condition $( n _ { P R } = 4 3 )$ or a no-progress bar condition $( n _ { N P R } = 4 5 ) .$ Perceived uncertainty and perceived enjoyment were selected as two dependent variables. Based on our literature review, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four perceived uncertainty items from [34] and four perceived enjoyment items from [28] (see the list of instrument items in Table 3).

## 4.4.2. Results

We conducted an exploratory factor analysis and found the items measuring the same construct correlated with one another, and their factor loading scores were all greater than 0.7, in support of convergent validity. All Eigenvalues of the constructs were greater than 1 $( \mathrm { P U } = 3 . 4 2 4 , \mathrm { P E } = 2 . 6 5 7 )$ . Further, all items correlated more closely with other items intended to measure the same factor than with items used to measure a di<sup>f</sup>erent construct, providing evidence of construct validity. Finally, all constructs exhibited Cronbach’s $\alpha > 0 . 9 0 1$ , thus demonstrating adequate reliability. A MANOVA test was conducted to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the presence of a progress bar on PU and PE. We found a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of the presence of a progress bar (Wilks $\lambda = 4 . 9 6 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Subsequent ANOVA tests showed that participants in the progress bar condition experienced signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower levels of perceived uncertainty $( \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { P R } } = 3 . 0 7$ , $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { N P R } } = 3 . 8 3$ $F = 7 . 0 8 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and marginally higher levels of perceived enjoyment $( \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { P R } } = 3 . 7 7 3 , \qquad \mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { N P R } } = 3 . 3 9 4 , \qquad F = 3 . 6 9 0 ,$ $p = 0 . 0 5 4 < 0 . 1 )$ than those in the no progress bar condition. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings were consistent with the postulation of the uncertainty reduction model [21] and <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies concerning animated components on webpages (e.g., [50]). However, our result indicated that a progress bar only marginally induced higher perceived enjoyment, implying that there might be a need for extra design e<sup>f</sup>ort of a progress bar to further increase users’ perceived enjoyment.

4.6. Study 2: impact of level of hedonic valence, background color, and culture on perceived enjoyment

## 4.6.1. Method

The goal of Study 2 is to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of (a) hedonic valence of a progress cue (high vs. low), (b) background color, and (c) culture on PE, which in turn negatively a<sup>f</sup>ects PWT. A total of 269 subjects (U.S.: 116, Taiwan: 153) were recruited for Study 2, as shown in Table 2. The subjects were recruited using the same method as in Study 1 by providing the same incentives. The experiments were conducted using the same simulated online music download site and in the same lab setting following the same procedure used in Study 1, but with di<sup>f</sup>erent waiting screens by manipulating two levels of hedonic valence of a progress cue (high vs. low), background color (blue vs. red) across cultures (U.S. vs. Taiwan) (see Table 7). Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the four conditions of a two (hedonic valence condition: high vs. low) by two (background color condition: blue vs. red) between-subjects design.

The independent variables in Study 2 are hedonic valence of a progress cue (high vs. low) and background color (blue vs. red). For hedonic valence, we used a rectangle cumulative progress bar as a progress cue with low level of hedonic valence and an incremental display of pop star images showing the progress as a progress cue with high level of hedonic valence (see Appendix ${ \mathrm { A } } { } ) . { } ^ { 9 } { \mathrm { ~ A } }$ rectangle cumula tive progress bar is one of the most frequently used progress bars in waiting screens, primarily designed for achieving its functional goal (i.e., providing progress information to users while waiting) but with low hedonic valence. Meanwhile, we chose an incremental display of pop star images showing the progress as a progress cue of high level of hedonic valence that is designed for providing both fun and progress information. For color, as indicated in the review of the literature, red is known to provide users higher excitement, fun, and stimulation than blue. The dependent variable of Study 2 is perceived enjoyment assessed using the four items used in the pre-test (see Table 3). An exploratory factor analysis showed that all items were grouped well into one factor $( { \mathrm { E i g e n v a l u e } } = 3 . 2 0 9 > 1 )$ and high reliabilities (Cronbach’s α of $\mathrm { P E } = 0 . 9 1 7 )$

The two main studies (Study 1 and Study 2) corroborate pre-test’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings and also provide additional evidence for various design on waiting screens and their role in manipulating individuals’ perceived uncertainty and enjoyment during online waits. Our experimental results largely con<sup>fi</sup>rm that a progress cue with high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance is better for inducing less perceived uncertainty than one with low time a<sup>f</sup>ordance, and a progress cue with high hedonic valence is more ef fective to induce more perceived enjoyment than a progress cue with low hedonic valence. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction e<sup>f</sup>ect between time a<sup>f</sup>ordance and culture as well as between hedonic valence and culture, implying that such e<sup>f</sup>ects are contingent upon cul ture. That is, people from Taiwan (i.e., a strong uncertainty avoidance society) experience a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger di<sup>f</sup>erence of perceived uncertainty when they view a progress bar with high time a<sup>f</sup>ordance than a progress indicator with low time a<sup>f</sup>ordance compared to those from U.S. (i.e., a weak uncertainty avoidance society). Although further validation should be warranted using more data from other countries in each cultural zone, this result suggests that people with a strong uncertainty avoidance culture are prone to higher uncertainty about the wait and thus will be more responsive to cues designed to induce less perceived uncertainty about the wait. Meanwhile, our results show that people from U.S. (i.e., an indulgence society) experience much greater perceived enjoyment when viewing a progress cue with high hedonic valence than a progress cue with low hedonic valence than those from Taiwan (i.e., a restraint society). This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that people from an indulgence culture are more sensitive to the enjoyment aspects of a wait situation and are more responsive to cues designed to increase perceived enjoyment during the wait.

In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that designing a waiting screen with a blue background color is likely to induce less perceived uncertainty of users than a red background color. This <sup>fi</sup>nding veri<sup>fi</sup>es that color blue conveys a sense of relaxation [20] and thus can relieve more anxiety from the uncertainty of an online wait than red color. In addition, when considering color as a cultural marker for website localization, we note that people in U.S. (i.e., a weak uncertainty avoidance culture) perceive less uncertainty during an online wait situation while viewing a waiting screen with a blue background than people in Taiwan (i.e., a strong uncertainty avoidance culture). This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports the belief that people from U.S. tend to associate blue with less anxiety, trust, and authority, which can be linked to certainty and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that using a red background color on a waiting screen results in more perceived enjoyment for users during an online wait. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with the study of Gorn et al. [20] that red induces more excitement, linking to higher a<sup>f</sup>ect. Interestingly, our proposed H5d (i.e., During an online wait, people in U.S. will experience MORE perceived enjoyment when viewing a waiting screen with a red background color than those in Taiwan.) is not supported by our experiment results. One possible interpretation is that red was perceived as a stimulus that induces positive a<sup>f</sup>ect in both countries; thus, the perceived enjoyment from viewing a red background color for people in both countries was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent, although people in U.S. (an indulgence culture) perceived slightly higher level of enjoyment than those from Taiwan (a restraint culture). Another plausible explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding is that people from Chinese (Taiwanese) culture interpret and perceive red di<sup>f</sup>erently from other cultures due to the meaning given to the color and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of associating red with good luck, happiness, and pleasantness [12,37] even though Taiwanese cul ture is a restraint culture.

Finally, the analyses of the structural equation model con<sup>fi</sup>rm our hypothesized nomological network between perceived uncertainty, perceived enjoyment, PWT, and website satisfaction along with use intention. Our analyses found that with the lower perceived uncertainty and higher perceived enjoyment, PWT can be reduced (H6a and H6b). Interestingly, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of a multi-group analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rm the sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences in the structural model. For U.S. people, PE is more e<sup>f</sup>ective than PU at reducing PWT, whereas for Taiwanese, PU is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential at reducing PWT than PE. This <sup>fi</sup>nding provides useful insights into how to customize websites using limited web design resources for e<sup>f</sup>ective online wait management across cultures. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that a shorter PWT can trigger more satisfaction and use intention (H7a and H7b). In addition, a higher user satisfaction can increase use intention toward the website (H7c).

## 6. Implications and conclusions

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

To our knowledge, this research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate togethe the increasingly evolving areas of HCI-related (a) online wait management and (b) culturability and website localization and provide empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Our proposed model and controlled experiments across culture make several contributions to the IS literature. First, with combined reference to uncertainty reduction model, resource allocation model, color psychology, and Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, we propose a research model of online wait management to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of di<sup>f</sup>erent types of progress cue design on users’ waiting perception. Thus, our model extends the boundary of abovementioned theoretical models to provide a solid foundation for future studies to further explore the e<sup>f</sup>ects of waiting screens using various design components across di<sup>f</sup>erent online and cultural contexts. Particularly, it is com monly believed that culture is an important determinant of online users perception, attitudes, and behaviors toward localized websites, but to date few studies have investigated the role of culture in an online wait context. By con<sup>fi</sup>rming that progress cue design (i.e., di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of hedonic valence and time a<sup>f</sup>ordance) and background color are not culturally neutral components but a<sup>f</sup>ect users in dissimilar countries di<sup>f</sup>erently, this research renders valuable scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence to the culturability and website localization literature, facilitating future research in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. In addition, by testing and validating the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of color on waiting screens, this research provides valuable empirical evidence that helps extend the theoretical application of color psychology in an online wait context in two countries of distinct cultural background to investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of color on users’ perception.

Second, by referring to the abovementioned theoretical models and conducting a series of carefully controlled experiments, our research provides scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence on how to implement di<sup>f</sup>erent design of progress cues and choices of background color to e<sup>f</sup>ectively manage online waiting perception and reduce PWT with consideration of cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences. Although past uses of waiting screens with a wide variety of design cues might be proli<sup>fi</sup>c in commercial websites, they were designed mainly based on interface designers’ professional instinct and past experience without apparent scienti<sup>fi</sup>c validation or theoretical guidance. In addition, they incline to design a waiting screen primarily with functional design cues without considering factors such as time a<sup>f</sup>ordance, hedonic valence, color, and cultural di<sup>f</sup>erences. With our theoretically supported research model and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>ndings, this research identi<sup>fi</sup>es promising opportunities for future studies to design and investigate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of various factors in addition to functional ones.

## 6.2. Practical implications

While we should cautiously interpret the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research because we investigate a single country from each culture, this research still provides interesting and useful practical implications for e-business managers and website designers to consider. First, our results suggest that di<sup>f</sup>erent design components on a waiting screen should be considered for users from di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures. For users from a country with higher uncertainty avoidance and more restraint nature (e.g., Taiwan), it is more e<sup>f</sup>ective to adopt design cues that can induce less perceived uncertainty rather than increasing perceived enjoyment in order to effectively reduce users’ PWT. Therefore, if design resource is limited and not all options are available, using a progress bar with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance should be a paramount consideration for a country like Taiwan. On the other hand, for users from a country with lower uncertainty avoidance and more indulgence nature (e.g., U.S.), incorporating design components that can induce higher perceived enjoyment is more e<sup>f</sup>ective to induce users’ less PWT. Therefore, designing progress cues with higher hedonic valence should be a priority for this population if design resource is limited and not all options are available.

Second, our results indicate that using a blue background color on a waiting screen is more e<sup>f</sup>ective at inducing less perceived uncertainty than using a red background color. In addition, it is even more e<sup>f</sup>ective to adopt a blue background color for users from an indulgence culture (e.g., U.S.). Therefore, if inducing less perceived uncertainty of the wait is the most important concern of a web designer, using a blue background color is more appropriate, especially for people from indulgence cultures. On the other hand, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that using red background color on a waiting screen is more e<sup>f</sup>ective at increasing perceived enjoyment than using a blue background color while the e<sup>f</sup>ect of a red background color is indi<sup>f</sup>erent across U.S. and Taiwan. This implies that designers should use a red background color to render high enjoyment to users regardless of their cultural background.

Third, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the presence of a progress cue on a waiting screen (either a progress indicator or a progress bar) enhances users perception of wait (i.e., less perceived uncertainty or marginally more perceived enjoyment) compared to no presence of a waiting screen. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm the value of progress cues to manage online wait, helping interface designers to persuade e-business managers to allocate the limited resources to design e<sup>f</sup>ective progress cues on waiting screens.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that including a progress bar with high level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance (compared to a progress indicator with low level of time a<sup>f</sup>ordance) with a design of high hedonic valence (rather than a functional, low hedonic valence design) is the best option for a waiting screen. This <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that interface designers and ebusiness practitioners should utilize this design option to induce users less perceived uncertainty of the wait and stimulate users’ perceived enjoyment during the wait, which in turn can e<sup>f</sup>ectively reduce their PWT. In particular, by con<sup>fi</sup>rming the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of design components with high hedonic valence on wait management, this research encourages managers to redirect their focus to design a waiting screen that balances both functionality and visual aesthetics.

Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that users were more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with and inclined to use a website if their PWT is shortened and showed higher tendency to use the website if they were satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the website. This implies that when pertinent progress cue and background color are implemented on a waiting screen that can successfully reduce PWT, these elements can directly contribute to increasing online users’ satisfaction, triggering website tra<sup>fi</sup>c and even purchases. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is particularly important for e-business managers because it con<sup>fi</sup>rms that they can effectively manage online waits by only using design cues on waiting screens, a relatively inexpensive solution, without investing in expensive and technically complex solutions (e.g., network bandwidth, high-performance databases).

## 7. Limitations and future research

While this research provides valuable contributions to both re searchers and practitioners of online wait management, we acknowledge that certain limitations make for cautious interpretation of our results and derived implications. First, our research may lack externa validity as we recruit student subjects from only two countries: U.S. and Taiwan. While student subjects successfully represent the target users of online music download and these two countries represent two notice ably diverse cultural backgrounds, additional data can be collected with actual customers of online music download sites from these countries and from other cultures to further validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In addition, future studies by collecting data from multiple countries of each culture are recommended to generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research. Second, in this research we refer and discuss two Hofstede cultural dimensions that are relevant to our research contexts. While there are many pre cedents of choosing one, two, or a few dimensions (out of six Hofstede s cultural dimensions) in IS literature related to cultural di<sup>f</sup>erence, fu ture studies can look into all Hofstede’s as well as other cultural dimensions or actually measure national cultural dimensions for empirically examining them.

Third, while there are three dimensions of color (i.e., hue, bright ness, and saturation) based on color theory, in our research, we investigate only hue of color (red vs. blue) but do not manipulate saturation and brightness of color. Future studies that focus on color are recommended to look in-depth the e<sup>f</sup>ects of all three color dimensions

Our results provide strong support for the proposed model and highlight the importance of managing online waiting perception and creating an overall positive experience through progress cue design and background color selection. In addition, this research renders important evidence of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect of cultures on the design factors on waiting perception, justifying the value of localizing waiting screens.
