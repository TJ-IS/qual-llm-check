# Effects of interaction richness on consumer attitudes and behavioral intentions in e-commerce: some experimental results

Jungjoo Jahng<sup>1</sup>, Hemant Jain<sup>2</sup> and Keshavamurthy Ramamurthy<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Graduate School of Business, Seoul National University, Seoul, South Korea; <sup>2</sup>Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI, U.S.A.

## Abstract

A variety of information and communication media have been employed to support the interaction of consumers with products and sales representatives in electronic-commerce (EC). But, the question of what impact those media have on consumers in EC has not been sufficiently addressed in the literature. The objective of this study is to investigate the effect of the richness of consumers’ interaction with products and with sales representatives on changes in their attitude towards and their intention to use business-to-consumer (B2C) EC. Controlled lab experiments were conducted using two types of products and multiple levels of interaction richness. The results show that interaction richness has positive impact on consumers’ attitude towards B2C EC. Additionally, the study finds that the impact is stronger in the case of purchasing a highcomplexity product than a low-complexity product, and that consumers’ attitude change is positively related with their intention to use B2C EC. Differential effects on two facets of attitude, ease of use in accomplishing the tasks and usefulness were observed leading to interesting implications and future research directions.

Keywords: change in attitude; usefulness; ease of use; intention to accept and use; electronic commerce; interaction richness

## Introduction

Despite the widespread adoption of internet and web use (Madden, 2003, p. ii), its predominant use appears to be primarily for communication purposes (e.g., email and messaging) rather than for serious and sustained online commerce. For instance, by the end of 2004, although over 70 million people in the U.S. used the internet on a daily basis, only 4 million used the internet/web to actually buy a product or service (Rainie & Horrigan, 2005; see also Table 1 and Figure 1 in Hoffman et al., 2004). As web-based electronic-commerce (EC) systems represent a new way for consumers to perform their purchasing tasks, the question of how to motivate consumers to use it as a regular transaction medium remain an interesting and open issue.

Attitude toward an object or a behavior has been identified as an important factor that influences the intention to perform the behavior and the actual performance of the behavior (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980). One of the most frequently studied behaviors in the information systems (IS) discipline has been the use of a certain information technology (IT) to perform a certain task (Baroudi et al., 1986; DeLone & McLean, 1992).

The rationale for this interest is that greater use of IT, especially when such use is voluntary, would occur when the user believes that additional value can be derived from its use (Barki & Hartwick, 1994). Also, the use of IT accompanied with this belief will be directed to exploit the potential of technology to the user’s and/or their organization’s benefit (Poon, 2000; Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). The behavior of interest to this study is using business-to-consumer (B2C) e-Commerce (EC) systems/ sites as a regular transaction medium. Previous studies suggest that acceptance of new IT is influenced by users’ attitude (Robey, 1979; Mathieson, 1991). We assert that users’ attitude toward EC systems plays a critical role in influencing their intention to use and actual use of EC systems as a transaction medium.

In a B2C EC environment, the consumer’s shopping experience occurs only through an interaction with the EC system. However, the virtual nature of EC, that is, the lack of a sense of ‘touch and feel of the products’ and ‘lack of face-to-face interaction’ with a sales representative for product consultation impacts users’ attitude and behavior. We argue that this could be one major stumbling block to the formation of positive attitude toward EC systems. We believe that the richness of interaction with the products and the seller’s representatives supported by the EC system can potentially overcome some of these negatives in molding consumers’ attitude towards this medium. However, the impact of these rich interactions on the attitude of consumers has not been studied. This study focuses on exploring the relationship between interaction richness supported by the EC system and a consumer’s attitude towards using EC as their regular transaction medium. Specifically, it investigates how consumer attitude undergoes a change in response to the consumer’s exposure to different levels of interaction richness of EC systems when engaged in purchasing two different products. It also investigates whether changes in consumer attitude can explain the consumer’s intention to accept and use an EC system as a regular transaction medium.

The paper is structured as follows: the next section draws upon extant theory to develop the research hypotheses; the following section discusses the research methodology, evaluates the psychometric properties of the various constructs used in the study and presents the results; and the further section provides a discussion of the results, implications for research and practice and limitations of the study. The last section presents a set of concluding remarks.

## Theory bases and hypothesis development

We briefly discuss the underlying theory bases and develop the study’s three hypotheses in this section.

## Attitude theory and relevant studies

Attitude is one of the critical factors in explaining human behavior. Attitude can be defined as a learned disposition to respond in a consistently favorable or unfavorable manner with respect to a given object or behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). A person’s attitude toward an object X influences his/her intention to perform certain behavior related to the object X, which in turn leads to actual behavior relating to X (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980). It has been proposed that a person’s attitude to an object is determined by the set of salient beliefs he/she holds about the object (Fishbein, 1967). Associating the expected behavior with various characteristics, qualities and attributes may form the set of beliefs. These associations are created through direct observation, experiences, information acquisition from various sources or inference processes (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Salient beliefs may persist over time and they may be strengthened, weakened or replaced by new beliefs.

A number of theories (e.g., theory of reasoned action (TRA) and theory of planned behavior (TPB)) exist to explain the attitude humans hold about objects and their behaviors (Fishbein, 1967; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980). In this study, Fishbein & Ajzen’s (1975) theory of reasoned action is adopted to explain consumers’ attitude toward EC and their intention to use EC systems as a transaction medium. TRA proposes that a person’s behavior is determined by his/her intention to perform the behavior, and that this behavioral intention is viewed as a function of his/her attitude toward the behavior and his/her subjective norm. This theory further suggests that attitude toward the behavior is formed by beliefs about consequences of the behavior.

Fishbein & Ajzen (1975) also deal with attitude change in TRA. They argue that as people’s attitude toward behavior is determined by their salient beliefs about the behavior, their attitude could be changed by influencing their primary beliefs. They suggest two ways of influencing the individual’s beliefs and thus subsequently attitude, behavioral intention and actual behavior: active participation and persuasive communication. The former refers to ways that a person can be placed in a situation where he/she observes that an object or behavior has a given attribute, whereas the latter means that the person may be told by an outside source that the object or behavior has the attribute (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975).

TRA has been applied in the IS context to understand, explain and predict IT usage (Venkatesh et al., 2003). The technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989) can be viewed as a derivative of TRA, tailored to IT contexts, with two kinds of beliefs influencing attitude and no role for subjective norm. According to TAM, IT usage behavior is a function of behavioral intention, behavioral intention in turn is a function of attitude, and attitude is jointly determined by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use.

As the use of EC systems in most cases is voluntary or discretionary, it can be argued that consumers’ attitude is crucially important to their use of EC systems. Thus, TRA is adopted in this study to explain consumers’ attitude toward EC and to examine the relationship between the interaction richness of an EC system, consumers’ attitude change toward EC, and consumers’ intention to use an EC system as a regular transaction medium. In the following sections, we propose that the interaction richness of an EC system is positively related with an attitude change toward EC. We further propose that such attitude change is stronger in the case of purchasing a high-complexity product than a low-complexity product, and that attitude change is positively related with intention to use an EC system.

## Interaction richness of an EC system and attitude change

In a B2C context, the two key objectives are to (1) effectively present product or service information to users and (2) support effective communication to reduce or compensate for the virtual nature of EC environment (Wells et al., 2005). Dennis & Kinney (1998) and Dennis & Valacich (1999) examined the symbol variety, reprocessability and feedback immediacy characteristics of Media Richness Theory (MRT) (Daft & Lengel, 1986) in the context of communication and group support systems. These variables are also appropriate for the EC systems investigated in this study. The representational richness of product information via multimedia capability subscribes to symbol variety and can play an important role by enhancing product image vividness and thus product perceptions (Jahng et al., 2002; Schlosser, 2003; Jiang & Benbasat, 2005). Reprocessability also helps the consumer process information without any pressure. Feedback immediacy (i.e., interactivity) provides the results of processing the request or transaction without delay. These aspects of richer interaction with the product to obtain and better process product information facilitate formation of favorable attitude.

A second form of support provided during the purchase decision process is communication between the consumers and the vendor of the product or service. Some consumers may require richer communication that reflects a need for a high level of social presence (Jahng et al., 2000) to satisfy the need for language variety and cognitive clarifications (in a physical store environment, this function is primarily supported by a sales person). The lack of social presence reduces the number of cues as well as the multiple levels of meaning that can and (depending on the task context) may need to be processed. A properly designed EC system or site has the ability to provide a desired level of social presence through various technology options (e.g., real-time videoconferencing, electronic chat rooms, etc.).

Thus, consumers’ interaction with products and the seller’s representatives supported by the EC system represents an important and perhaps the only window through which a consumer experiences the online purchase environment. Results from studies investigating the influences of product presentations in the form of various advertisements on consumer attitude indicate that effective product presentation leads to positive attitude toward products (Brown & Stayman, 1992). Previous research shows that rich presentations (e.g., 3-D appearance or virtual experience) of product information through the internet/web create a sense of presence and that such virtual presence simulates a direct experience, which may lead to positive attitude (Biocca et al., 2001; Kumar & Benbasat, 2002; Li et al., 2002). Also, it has been suggested that exposure to salespersons’ benevolence, competence, and other attributes and behaviors may lead to a customer’s trust in the salesperson; this trust leads to customer satisfaction, satisfaction to positive attitude, positive attitude to intentions to buy and intentions to actual buying behavior (Swan et al., 1999). In terms of communication modality, consumer behavior literature suggests that conversational information (e.g., face-to-face or phone) is perceived to be more persuasive and useful than written information (e.g., mail or letter), especially when dealing with new things such as technological innovations (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Thomas, 1992; Lee et al., 2002).

Furthermore, it has been suggested by Bhatnagar et al. (2000) that a key risk in EC is the lack of ability to touch and feel the product (a.k.a. product category risk). This risk is closely related to the nature of user interaction supported by the EC system. Therefore, we would argue that a consumer’s interaction with products and with salespersons supported by the EC system are key characteristics that have the potential to shape the consumer’s perceptions and beliefs about B2C EC systems and significantly influence the consumer’s attitude.

During the early stages of interaction with EC systems, when consumers do not have much prior experience or knowledge of an EC system, consumers could be in any of the three affective states (positive due to, say, excitement or joy of interacting with a new system or object; neutral with no specific feelings; or negative due to anxiety or boredom in interacting with a new system or object) in terms of their initial predisposition toward EC. In such a state, they are likely to be malleable and susceptible to reinforcing, maintaining or attenuating influences and associated attitude change as they get more exposed to EC systems (Barki & Hartwick, 1994). Thus, their attitude may change through direct exposure to and experience with EC systems as well as from other information sources such as word-of-mouth and articles. In this study, we focus on change in consumer attitude through direct exposure to and experience with EC systems. We argue that favorable attitude formation takes place during the interaction of the consumers with richer EC sites. A richer interaction could favorably influence a change in consumers’ attitude toward EC systems as regular transaction media. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H1: EC interaction richness is positively related with change in overall attitude toward the EC system as a transaction medium.

## Product complexity and effect of interaction richness on attitude

Greater interaction richness may not be required in all contexts of a product purchase and its impact could vary based on complexity of products. Along the lines of task complexity, product complexity refers to the extent to which a product is perceived to be complex and can be defined as a composite of three key aspects: multiplicity, variability, and interdependence of features or attributes of the product (Campbell, 1988; Jahng et al., 2000). Multiplicity relates to the number or variety of product attributes or features. Variability relates to the range of values each of these product attributes can assume. Interdependence indicates the extent to which one or more product attributes is dependent on other product attributes that may facilitate or constrain flexibility in consumer choice. We would expect that high-complexity products would require a greater level of interaction between consumers and the products and between consumers and the seller’s representative, whereas simple products would not require rich interaction. In the context of purchasing a simple product, while rich interaction may be a nice thing to have, it is not really a critical component to supporting consumers’ decisionmaking. In this context, lean interactions might be sufficient for supporting consumers’ purchase decisions and are not likely to negatively impact consumers’ attitude toward EC systems. A richer interaction in this purchase context could influence their attitude in a positive direction; however, the improvement in attitude is not likely to be as large as the one observed in a complex product context. It may also be possible that too much richness of interaction in the case of simple products may, in fact, overwhelm consumers, resulting in no change in attitude or even a negative attitude change. Thus, richer interaction will be perceived to be useful to the outcome (e.g., satisfaction, confidence, etc.) when purchasing high-complexity products, whereas such richer interaction may not be perceived to be as useful to the outcome when purchasing low-complexity products. Therefore, richer interactions will be expected to lead to differential influences on the extant beliefs, and subsequently attitude, behavioral intention and actual behavior, depending on the degree of complexity of a product a consumer attempts to purchase.

Some support for these expectations can be drawn from the tenets of MRT (Daft et al., 1987), Social Presence Theory (Short et al., 1976) and Task-Technology Fit theory (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). Additionally, the theory underlying elaboration likelihood model or ELM (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) – which embodies the notion of reaction of the recipient to a stimulus and emphasizes persuasion as the key to attitude change – offers some support. ELM explains that attitude change can occur through two modes: (1) central route via message elaboration and (2) peripheral route relying on cues such as reciprocation, consistency, authority, liking, social proof and scarcity (used typically in advertising messages) that seeks to elicit a quick accept/reject decision on the part of the consumer without much deep consideration of the message content. If consumers are provided appropriate information content and given sufficient time to assimilate and evaluate the information prior to making purchase decisions, richer interactions could support consumers being persuaded to follow the central route of message/informational elaboration for purchase of a high-complexity product. More richness than what is needed for the purchase of a simple product may not be very useful to consumers in their information elaboration activities to produce more favorable attitude change toward B2C EC.

H2: The impact of interaction richness on change in overall attitude toward the EC system as a regular transaction medium will be stronger in the context of purchasing a high-complexity product than a low-complexity product.

Change in attitude and intention of using EC systems As noted earlier, TRA (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and TAM (Davis, 1989) suggest that attitude toward a behavior influences the intention to perform the behavior. Different people can be expected to have different attitudes toward EC systems and thus different intentions to use these systems as transaction media. We previously proposed that richer interaction would increase the favorable attitude (or reduce the unfavorable attitude) held by consumers toward EC systems as transaction media. We further propose that this positive attitude change created by EC systems’ interaction richness would lead to increase in consumers’ intention to use EC systems as regular transaction media. This rationale has been found to be true in a number of IT use contexts (Heijden et al., 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Singh et al., 2005).

H3: Consumers’ change in overall attitude toward EC systems as transaction media is positively related with their intention to use these systems as transaction media.

## Experimental study and results

The research method adopted, operationalization of the constructs, assessment of psychometric properties and the results in this study are discussed next.

## Research method

In order to empirically validate the research model shown in Figure 1 and the accompanying hypotheses, a series of controlled laboratory experiments were conducted. While there are multiple methodologies that can be and have been applied to IS research, an experimental design was deemed to be most appropriate in this study as it provides the researcher with the ability to control and manipulate variables of interest, which in this study were the richness of interaction with the (simulated) seller’s representative (IRSP), the richness of interaction with the product (IRP) and the product type the subjects would interact with. Seven hundred and ninety-two subjects from undergraduate courses in a major urban metropolitan university voluntarily participated in the study. As we are interested in B2C end of EC targeting the mass public at large (that includes students as well), we believe students can be considered to be representative consumers. They are likely to be even more representative than lay public for the complex product (digital camera) chosen in this study. Two types of products, computer diskettes and digital cameras, were used. Digital cameras were determined to fall into the category of high-complexity products in light of the fact that there are over 25 product attributes to consider, many of which are not only interdependent but also take on multiple values, while PC diskettes fell into the category of low complexity. For each of the products, four EC sites representing a combination of high and low IRP and high and low IRSP were developed. Prior to conducting actual experiments, pilot sessions were held with a number of focus groups to evaluate the quality of the prototypes as well as their interaction richness. The participants in the pilot sessions did not have any difficulty in using the prototype systems and were able to differentiate their interaction richness. However, after conducting the pilot sessions, minor modifications such as changing font sizes and proper phrasing of text, etc., were made to the prototypes and the instruction sheets.

A short pre-test questionnaire (see Appendix A) was administered to capture certain demographic information as well as participants’ preexisting attitudes toward the EC system as a transactional medium. Each subject was randomly assigned to one of the eight prototype EC systems (two product types - four levels of interaction richness).

In conformance with the guidelines specified by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) for experiments involving human subjects, we declared that participation was voluntary and that the subjects could withdraw at any time prior to or during the experiment; however, there was no case of anyone withdrawing from the study after volunteering to participate. All research study subjects were given a case scenario that described a buying situation, and it asked them to assume that they were ‘in the market’ for the target product (implying that there is a ‘need’) and that adequate money was available to them if they wished to buy the product (this scenario made it clear that purchase was ‘not mandatory’). Such a practice is in line with other recent research studies (Kumar & Benbasat, 2002; Jiang & Benbasat, 2005). While the use of student subjects and simulated real-world context are quite common in IS and other management research, we do acknowledge the use of such a practice and simulated purchase context as limitations of this study later. Once they had read the case scenario, they interacted with the prototype EC system assigned to them and responded to a short post-test questionnaire (see Appendix B).

The experiments were administered in sessions of 50 min each. A maximum of 40 subjects participated in each session. The experiments were conducted in a computer lab equipped with 48 Pentium computers running in a LAN environment. All participants were informed well in advance about the schedule of experiment sessions.

## Operationalization

The IRSP was manipulated through two communication medium choices: (1) email and (2) real-time, two-way audio and one-way video. Considerable care was taken in selecting the person to serve as the seller’s representative. We interviewed a number of people and selected an individual with good interpersonal communication skills; she was provided adequate training on various features of the products to be able to respond when the subjects requested clarifications and product consultation.

The IRP was manipulated through product information presentation (static picture and textual description of product specifications vs a 3-D interactive trial of the product features and a textual description of product specifications). This is similar to the ‘visual control’ and ‘functional control’ features that Jiang & Benbasat (2005) implemented in their study. Prior to the actual administration of the experiments, the difference in the richness of the four interaction modes was assessed with focus groups using multi-indicator scales (see Appendix B) as detailed in the data analysis section. Upon confirmation that the prototypes were significantly different from one another in terms of richness of interaction with seller’s representative and with the products, we went ahead with the administration of the experiments.

Drawing upon previous research in IS and Marketing (Brown & Stayman, 1992; Barki & Hartwick, 1994; Govindarajulu et al., 2000) a set of nine indicators was developed to represent both pre-test and post-test attitude toward the EC system as a regular transactional medium. These indicators representing attitude map fairly well with the indicators of the broad domain of attitude illustrated by Venkatesh et al. (2003). One more indicator variable (to evaluate criterion validity as will be explained later) was included to capture participants’ overall attitude.

Variables were included to measure two aspects of consumers’ behavioral intentions – their intention to accept/use and their expected frequency of use (Hughes, 2003). Intention to accept/use was measured with two indicators capturing their likelihood and probability of using the EC system (similar to the one that they had interacted with) as a regular transactional medium in the future (Davis, 1989; Petty et al., 1995; Laroche et al., 1996). A single indicator was used to capture participants’ expected frequency of use in terms of how often they would use an EC system similar to the one they had used in the study for their next/future 10 purchases of a product similar to the one that they had interacted with. In addition to these multi-indicator scales, we also captured information on some demographic variables such as age, gender, class performance (GPA), web knowledge, web use, product knowledge and brand knowledge. All the indicators used in the study are described in Appendices A and B.

## Evaluation of psychometric properties

Both construct validity and reliability were examined to evaluate and establish psychometric properties.

Validity Construct validity was examined from four perspectives – content validity, convergent validity (unidimensionality), discriminant validity and criterion validity. Significant precautions were taken during the various stages of construct development to ensure a high degree of content validity (Davis & Cosenza, 1985). The indicator items for measuring the various constructs were identified based on an extensive review of the extant literature. The items were subjected to various stages of refinement including Q-sort procedure (Taylor & Todd, 1995) and prototype testing with a number of focus groups. Based on Q-sort, we reexamined some of our construct definitions and refined the measurement items. Thus, we are confident that the indicators measure the complete domain of the constructs and exhibit adequate content validity.

Convergent and discriminant validity were established through factor analysis (Kerlinger, 1978). A joint factor analysis of all 11 indicator variables using the principal components approach accompanied by varimax rotation was performed to extract the factors and simultaneously examine convergent and discriminant validity (Kerlinger, 1978; Nunnally, 1978). The results are displayed in Table 1.

As can be noted from Table 1, a three-factor (instead of a two-factor) solution emerged after applying the commonly accepted decision criteria in social science research to examine factor structures (Hair et al., 1998). Factors 1 and 3 represent attitude and factor 2 represents intention to accept/use. After careful consideration, we decided to make use of the two emergent dimensions of change in attitude rather than forcing all nine indicators to load onto a single factor. As these two factors of attitude appear to approximate two fairly well-known constructs of usefulness and ease of use, we decided to label each factor of attitude as change in ‘attitude on usefulness’ and ‘attitude on ease of use,’ respectively.

Sample characteristics and test for random assignment The experiments were administered in two waves coinciding with the two product types – the first wave had 408 subjects engaged in the simulated purchase of a complex product (digital camera) and the second had 384 subjects engaged in the purchase of a simple product (PC diskette). There was almost equal size of the sub-sample (88 to 95) in each of the eight clusters (four forms of interaction richness - two product types). The key demographic characteristics of the subjects captured in this study are presented in Table 3.

Although significant precautions were taken to randomly assign subjects across the various experimental configurations, we assessed whether there was any bias in the assignment and, thus, threat to the internal validity of the study. The results of various tests $( \chi ^ { 2 }$ for categorical variables, t-test for metric variables across two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for metric variables across more than two groups) on a number of demographics undertaken to establish random assignment indicate that except for a significant difference on general PC skill and a weak difference on GPA $( P { \leqslant } 0 . 1 0 )$ there is no bias or threat to internal validity in the assignment of participants to the four EC systems. The same tests were also performed on the demographic variables across the two waves of test administration. Results show some differences between the two groups (coincidentally, also the two product types) on product knowledge, brand knowledge, web knowledge, web usage and GPA. It is not surprising that the growing awareness, popularity, and use of the internet/web during the 4-month gap between these two sets of experiments appear to be reflected in the data. The bias on product knowledge seems to be due to obvious differences in the nature of the product: a simple regularly used product vs a relatively rarely used and complex product. Overall, the tests show that the assignment of subjects to the various EC system groups was random and unbiased, but that there exists some bias between the two waves of test administration. Therefore, to weed out any confounding effects and to be assured of internal validity, we controlled for these six variables (GPA, PC skill, web knowledge, web usage, product knowledge and brand knowledge) by including them as covariates in all subsequent data analyses.

## Discussion, implications, limitations and future research directions

## Discussion of research findings

The study results emerged mostly as expected and all three hypotheses of this study (H partially) are supported. As seen from Table 5a and b, interaction richness was found to promote formation of favorable change in consumers’ overall attitude toward EC systems. However, while the support for change in attitude on the easeof-use dimension was strong, support for the usefulness dimension was somewhat weak. The results suggest that interaction richness is closely related to consumers’ overall attitude toward EC systems and that a richer interaction is an influential factor to form an overall positive attitude toward EC systems. The consumers (represented by study participants) find the richer interaction easy to use in obtaining product information, visualizing the product and experiencing its features through the interactive 3-D capabilities provided by the EC system. Additionally, being able to seek advice from the (simulated) sales representative can reduce the consumer’s perception of purchase apprehension caused by lack of touch and feel (Lal & Sarvary, 1999; Kumar & Benbasat, 2002). This option promotes greater confidence and more comfort in using EC systems as a regular transactional medium. The result also implies that richer interaction contributes to consumers’ perceived value of using B2C EC systems for their transactions. However, this is not getting strongly translated into higher attitude on the usefulness dimension toward these systems for future transactions. It is possible that consumers do not feel these rich interactions are absolutely useful for all the product types examined in the study and may prove to be a hindrance in some cases.

ANOVA (controlled for covariates) was used to determine whether there is a significant interactive effect between interaction richness and product type. The significance of the interaction term in the model for change in overall attitude provides support for the second hypothesis of this study. Interaction richness does not lead to the same attitudinal influence in all productchoice contexts, as can be seen from the contingency tables (Table 5b). Interaction richness significantly impacts change in consumer attitude toward EC systems in a complex product purchase situation. This is because the lack of touch and feel or lack of face-to-face interaction is more severely felt by customers and is viewed as a key stumbling block. Thus, in the case of complex products, interaction richness seems essential. On the other hand, in a low-complexity product purchase context, the virtual nature of EC does not really bother the customers as there is not much need to interact with the product or a sales representative. Thus, interaction richness is not considered to be critical in this case. This is also in line with ELM theory’s suggestions; significant efforts on the part of consumers in pursuing information elaboration activities are not needed. However, richer interaction may still be a good and enjoyable factor that positively influences their attitude toward EC. The attitudinal influence in this case is not as large as the one observed in a richer interaction dealing with high-complexity products/services. Note that there were weak or no significant interactive influences of interaction richness and product type on the two individual dimensions (ease of use and usefulness) of attitude. This finding appears to indirectly echo that enjoyment triggered by a richer website might enhance the intentions of new web customers to return but that it may not influence their sustained purchase behavior (Koufaris et al., 2001/02), which in our study is driven by a sustained favorable change in attitude. However, the additional analyses (see contingency tables) indicate that the change in attitude on these two aspects was also in the right direction. That is, it appears that the moderating influence is on the form of effect but not the degree of effect in this instance (Sharma et al., 1981; Arnold, 1982).

The results of this study suggest that favorable attitude change could be expected to initially lead to consumers’ intention to use EC systems as transaction media, but over time they would be mutually reinforcing. These results not only reinforce the relevance of past research using TRA, TPB, and other acceptance and use of technology models (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Venkatesh et al., 2003), but go beyond the focus of previous research by examining and establishing the relationship between change in attitude of users and behavioral intention to accept EC as a regular transaction medium. Furthermore, major theories and models, which explain human behavior in general or specifically IT use behavior (e.g., TRA and TAM), consistently argue that there would be a strong relation between behavioral intention and actual behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Sheppard et al., 1988; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Extending these arguments, we would expect that favorable change in attitude that is positively related with consumer’s behavioral intension to use EC systems as transaction media would ultimately result in actual use of EC systems as transaction media. On the other hand, the absence of favorable attitude change might not lead to actual use of EC systems unless the use is mandatory.

## Implications for research and practice

This study provides some interesting insights on interaction richness and consumer attitude toward use of EC systems. One key implication is that attitude (change) is not a unidimensional construct, at least in the context of EC. Change in attitude on the ease-of-use dimension in accomplishing the tasks of information seeking and making purchase decisions as well as on the usefulness dimension (including enjoyment, relevance and effectiveness) of EC systems are both critical. This finding is particularly important because most EC sites are capable of realizing the first aspect of favorable attitude formation, namely the user-friendly aspects in the design of the website. However, it is much more difficult to build interactive website features/functions that are really perceived to be relevant, useful and helpful. These findings imply that the mere existence of an aesthetically appealing website is not adequate. To promote favorable attitude and convert site surfers to actual consumers, the appropriateness of the site’s interaction richness features needs to be considered within the context of an alignment between the features and product types. In this context, the suggestions provided in recent literature (e.g., Aladwani, 2002) need to be considered while designing the usefulness dimension of the site.

Before their exposure to richer interactions, some consumers might have believed that using EC systems as transaction media for certain products (e.g., highcomplexity products) with non-digital traits (Lal & Sarvary, 1999) involved considerable risk and inconvenience, because of its virtual nature. This concern could outweigh the convenience associated with EC (e.g., low search cost and no travel time). The findings of our study indicate that users’ belief systems as reflected in their affective state can be changed in a positive direction by exposing them to effectively and appropriately designed interactions. Ajzen and Fishbein’s TRA explains attitude formation and change, but does not suggest what specific factors can influence and change the attitude. These factors would, of course, vary by context. In the IS context, a number of studies have proposed and empirically tested whether factors such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use influence user attitudes toward the use of IS (Davis et al., 1989; Taylor & Todd, 1995; Venkatesh et al., 2003). But very few studies have investigated interaction richness in relation to formation of and change in user attitude toward use of IS or technologies. The findings of this study emphasize that lack of formation of suitable attitude on the part of consumers (emerging from virtual nature) could be one of the major obstacles to wider acceptance of EC systems as regular transaction media, and demonstrate the importance of interaction richness in influencing their attitude.

Additionally, this study extends beyond the thrust of MRT (Daft et al., 1987) that has existed for quite some time. MRT focused primarily on communication media richness (face-to-face, telephone, telecommunication medium, etc.) and was developed in a time period prior to the advent of the internet/web. This study does not merely focus on the communication medium but on the ‘interaction’ that a consumer has with both the product and a salesperson.

The study results also provide a few important implications to practitioners. In many instances, B2C EC providers choose to offer products or services at lower prices under an assumption that this will attract and generate a larger base of customers and that larger volumes can compensate for low price offerings. However, these assumptions have not necessarily been consistently validated. A number of customers often surf a website to obtain all necessary information but end up going to a physical store to buy (complex) products/ services even if the price advantage is sacrificed in order to meet the needs of sense organ cues (touch and feel the product and talk to sales representatives). However, the opposite case of going to physical stores to try out the product or service, and then to a website to make the actual purchase in order to exploit the advantage of reduced prices has also been observed. A central thesis of this study was that the inability to foster and sustain customers’ favorable attitude to mitigate the negative consequences of not satisfying the needs of sense organ cues is one key reason why many B2C commercial websites may not have succeeded. Thus, it follows that prospective customers’ attitude should be nurtured and managed to be more favorable by providing suitably designed richer interactions so as to generate higher intentions to use and actual use of EC systems. Richer interactions basically improve customers’ attitude for high-complexity products and may appear to be good to have even for low-complexity products. However, if richer interaction features are too expensive and are not economically prudent in all types of product offerings – particularly in the face of limited resource availability – they would be of greater value when used in a high-complexity product group. Of course, an appropriate course of action would be to perform a cost–benefit analysis that compares the cost of increased interaction richness with the benefit of improved attitude followed by increased use.

## Limitations and future research directions

Attitude is generally formed over a fairly long period of time. Thus, we admit that the change of attitude this study generated and measured over a short period of time could be relatively small and that it is hard to make sweeping inferences and implications out of such small changes in attitude. Nonetheless, the small changes were found to be statistically significant and gave an opportunity to validate the hypotheses this study proposed.

The study made use of two product types that were selected from a set of eight product types after extensive pilot tests with focus groups. While these two products enabled us to classify them as simple vs complex, the risk of financial exposure from an incorrect decision is not comparable. Future studies should consider a wider range of product types with comparable risk in terms of monetary loss. Also, further research should be undertaken to examine other product characterizations and their relations to interaction richness.

Notwithstanding the care we took in designing and administering the research experiments, as is the bane of all laboratory experimental designs, this study may also suffer from a lack of realism compared to a real-world B2C EC environment. The participants in this study were students, not real customers shopping around for a digital camera or a pack of PC diskettes, implying that they had a need for these products. However, students are also drawn from the general population and can be considered to be representative consumers. The study subjects were further asked to assume that they had adequate money available to them if they wished to buy the product although the case scenario made it clear that purchase was not mandatory. While such practices are in line with other recent research studies in IS and other management research (Kumar & Benbasat, 2002; Jiang & Benbasat, 2005), the use of simulated purchase context and associated assumptions are weaknesses of the study. Future research should attempt to use real consumers as subjects. Furthermore, the study could not specifically consider or build-in consumer goals while evaluating change in attitude and behavioral intentions. There is some recent research (Schlosser, 2003) that suggests that there is a need to consider congruence between consumers’ goals and delivery of product information. Future research may creatively introduce this additional factor into the research design.

It is possible that the novelty of technologies used for the richer interactions may have sensitized the subjects so that they ascribed more favorable attitudes to richer interactions. Thus, it would be desirable to conduct a series of longitudinal studies to examine whether the favorable attitude attributed to richer interactions in this study is sustained over time. Such studies may also help identify the nature and form of attitude improvement – linear, non-linear, existence of thresholds – and when thresholds are reached.

In this study, the influence of interaction richness on the usefulness dimension of the change in attitude was not strong or unambiguous. However, we believe that sustained and favorable change on the usefulness aspect of attitude is likely to be more salient and desirable for both EC providers and consumers. Future research needs to determine the key factors of EC interaction that would foster a favorable change in the usefulness aspect of attitude and how to reinforce them. In addition, future study may want to consider how individual-specific traits affect the formation of favorable attitude, and also the impact of other features of EC systems such as the trustworthiness of the vendor behind the site (Heijden et al., 2003) and the level of personalization.

## Conclusions

This study set out to examine if interaction richness of EC systems can compensate for the constraints imposed in a virtual shopping environment such as lack of sense cues (touch, feel, see, hear and smell), and yet foster and maintain a positive change in attitude toward an EC system as a regular transaction medium. Two types of interaction richness were proposed and subjected to empirical testing: one involved a richer communication basis to the interaction between consumers and a simulated sales representative; the other invoked the ability to provide a 3-D interaction between the consumers and the actual product. This study investigated the effect of IRSP and IRP on changes in consumers’ attitude towards and their intention to use B2C EC within the context of two different types of products: a complex product (digital camera) and a simple product (PC diskette). Controlled laboratory experiments were conducted that affirmed significant support for the central thesis of the study: that richness of EC interaction can indeed overcome many of the constraints of the virtual shopping context.

The study results showed that interaction richness has positive impact on consumers’ attitude towards B2C EC. The results also found that the impact is stronger in the case of purchasing a high-complexity product than a lowcomplexity product, and that consumers’ attitude change is positively related with their intention to use B2C EC.
