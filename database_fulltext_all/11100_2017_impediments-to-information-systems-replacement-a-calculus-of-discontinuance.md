# Impediments to Information Systems Replacement: A Calculus of Discontinuance

BRENT FURNEAUX AND MICHAEL WADE

BRENT FURNEAUX (brentf@uwindsor.ca; corresponding author) is an assistant professor at the University of Windsor’s Odette School of Business, Canada. He received his Ph.D. from York University’s Schulich School of Business and is pursuing research that seeks to understand better the obsolescence and discontinuance of information systems (IS), and the sustainability of technological innovations. His research interests also include the nature, role, and significance of theory within IS research and the mechanisms by which information systems deliver value to organizations and individual users. His research has been published in a wide range of journals including MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, DATABASE, and Communications of the AIS as well as numerous book chapters and international conference proceedings. Prior to his academic career, he worked and consulted extensively within the pharmaceutical and hospitality industries.

MICHAEL WADE (michael.wade@imd.org) is a professor of innovation and strategy at the International Institute for Management Development, Lausanne, Switzerland, where he holds the Cisco Chair in Digital Business Transformation. He has published on digital business transformation, innovation, social media marketing, information systems strategy, e-commerce, and small and medium-size enterprise performance. His work has appeared in Strategic Management Journal, MIS Quarterly, the Communications of the ACM, and others. He has also published 8 books and more than 20 case studies, and he appears frequently in the media. His latest book, published in 2016, is Digital Vortex: How Today’s Market Leaders Can Beat Disruptive Competitors at Their Own Game.

ABSTRACT: Relatively little attention has been directed toward examining information system (IS) obsolescence and replacement despite the significant organizational resources invested in such systems. Current understanding is, as a result, both incomplete and relatively preliminary in nature. In particular, there is an important need for research that identifies impediments to system replacement. We therefore draw on protection motivation theory to formulate a model of IS discontinuance that explains how the replacement of obsolete systems is constrained by the risks associated with replacing these systems, the resources that have been invested in them, their complexity, and institutionalized norms that reflect dominant views concerning preferred IS solutions. Partial least squares (PLS) and covariance based structural equation modeling (SEM) of survey data obtained from 112 senior-level IS managers indicates that replacement risks contribute to decreased replacement intentions while system investments and complexity are linked to increased replacement risks. Institutional norms were also found to indirectly contribute to replacement risks through their positive influence on system investments. The proposed model of discontinuance calculus thus demonstrates the importance of managing risks, system complexity, and system investments to ensure that obsolete systems do not become a significant impediment to organizational effectiveness.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: discontinuance calculus, information system discontinuance, institutional norms, IS obsolescence, IS replacement, system complexity.

Management scholars have shown increased interest in understanding the aging and obsolescence of products, services, and technological innovations [10]. This interest reflects a growing need to identify and manage obsolescence in a world that is increasingly dominated by the routine emergence of innovations in products, services, and practices. As a consequence of such innovations, organizational managers face an ongoing need to make decisions concerning the obsolescence and replacement of both tangible and intangible assets as well as product offerings, managerial practices, and business processes [82]. Historically, research has focused primarily on managing the obsolescence of physical assets such as production facilities, vehicle fleets, and other equipment. These types of replace or repair decisions are quite common and notable attention has, therefore, been directed toward ensuring that they are optimal (e.g., [40]). Far less attention has, however, been directed toward understanding the obsolescence of processes, practices, service offerings, and much less tangible assets such as information systems (IS).

Research and practice both suggest that replacing an aging information system has the potential to yield considerable business value [62, 78]. Significant resources are consumed by efforts to operate and maintain existing systems while these efforts deliver fewer benefits and consume more resources as systems become increasingly obsolete [7, 78, 90]. Continued use of obsolete systems thus has the potential to impose needless burdens on scarce organizational resources. Replacement initiatives can, however, be extremely costly, challenging, and difficult to justify [62, 78, 94, 96]. Information systems also differ from physical assets in that they are not generally subject to physical wear [24]. Rather, the obsolescence of an information system is typically rooted in the technical obsolescence that emerges when technically superior offerings are released to the market and the functional obsolescence that arises when organizational needs change such that the capabilities of a system no longer reflect organization needs. Existing systems can, nonetheless, be used well past the point at which they have become technically and functionally obsolete. As a result, practitioners continue to struggle with the challenges of determining whether, when, and how information systems should be replaced [42, 78, 94].

Although prior research has established the importance of technical and functional obsolescence as drivers of intentions to replace existing information systems [25], it has offered relatively little insight concerning the impediments to organizational IS replacement decisions. Some of the theoretical work that has been put forth has remained untested or has not been supported by empirical work [24, 25]. In particular, assertions that higher levels of investment in existing information systems can impede replacement decisions have failed to receive empirical support [25]. Moreover, prior work has not considered how the risks associated with replacing an existing information system can impede replacement decisions despite considerable research suggesting the importance of such risks (e.g., [12, 14]). Research also suggests that organizations will be reluctant to undertake replacement initiatives that are contrary to institutionalized norms concerning the IS solutions that are most appropriate [35, 84]. This assertion does not, however, appear to have been tested empirically. Finally, prior work has typically assumed that impediments to IS replacement will exhibit only direct influences on replacement intentions. Theoretical and empirical work in the domain of IS discontinuance is therefore both preliminary and incomplete. This is constraining research progress and limiting the guidance that can be offered to practitioners seeking to improve their ability to manage obsolescence. We thus formulated and empirically tested a model that addresses the questions of what factors impede IS replacement decisions and how these factors operate to impede such decisions.

Our model of discontinuance highlights how replacement risks, system investments, system complexity, and institutional norms undermine the emergence of plans to replace information systems as well as how these impediments to IS replacement decisions relate to each other and to the forces of technical and functional obsolescence. The model of discontinuance calculus that we propose also provides a far more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of organizational-level IS discontinuance than past models. It thus offers important theoretical and empirical contributions for managerial decision making and for research seeking to examine discontinuance processes—for example, work that has begun to examine the search processes that take place once IS replacement intentions have emerged (e.g., [79]). Our model is also of value to researchers and practitioners interested in IS life-cycle management and related phenomena including organizational capacity for innovation, the success of IS vendors, and the demand for IS skills. We therefore elaborate on our work in the following discussion.

## Theoretical Foundations

Information systems replacement decisions involve trade-offs between costs and benefits that arise at different points in time. The intertemporal nature of such choices forces decision makers to compare the benefits, costs, and risks of continuance against the future benefits, costs, and risks associated with a potentially large number of alternatives. Although intertemporal choices are often evaluated using discounted utility models [23], these models require substantial knowledge of available alternatives and a depth of analysis that is not generally undertaken until after replacement intentions have emerged. Hence, given our focus on impediments to the formation of replacement intentions, we sought to develop and test a model of IS discontinuance that reflects the nature of the tensions evident during the formation of these intentions rather than cost–benefit considerations that would typically only be salient after considerable analysis and evaluation of alternative systems have been undertaken.

## Discontinuance Calculus

Changes such as those associated with an IS replacement decision present decision makers with a fundamental tension between a need for change and the apparent safety of persisting with the status quo [99]. IS replacement decisions can thus be characterized as a form of discontinuance “calculus” in which the costs of replacing a system must be weighed against the expected benefits of possible alternative systems. There is, however, reason to believe that persistence with current practices dominates organizational behavior [36]. Since continued use of existing systems demands relatively little active thought or effort, ongoing use of these systems will tend to persist in the absence of any intervening force [44, 57]. It can thus be expected that intentions to replace an existing system will be highly dependent upon the emergence of salient threats to the status quo, rather than on speculative benefits of potential alternatives.

Given the inertial tendencies that drive continued use of existing information systems, it was necessary to draw on theoretical foundations capable of accounting for what motivates and impedes action when a status quo situation is threatened. In this regard, protection motivation theory (PMT) has been used in numerous contexts to explain how people are motivated to act in response to a wide range of threats [22, 74]. In particular, PMT identifies two key processes as underlying the motivation to act in response to threats such as those posed by IS obsolescence [22, 64]. The first is a threat appraisal process in which the significance of a threat is assessed. The second is a coping appraisal process in which an assessment is made of the extent to which an effective response is possible. PMT then posits that motivation to act is undermined when threats are not seen as significant and when capacity for effective response is deemed to be low.

In accordance with PMT, prior research reports that organizations are motivated to update or replace existing information systems when the threats of technical and functional obsolescence are significant [25, 43]. However, PMT also argues that motivation to act in response to such threats will be impeded by beliefs that an effective response is not possible [22, 64]. Hence, while managers might recognize that the threat of an obsolete system is significant, their willingness to act can be impeded if, for example, they believe that the risks associated with a replacement initiative are such that their organization is not in a position to effectively address this threat. We therefore characterize the tension between threat significance and capacity for effective response as the core calculus at the center of the model of discontinuance calculus that we present (Figure 1). We elaborate on the elements of this model in the following discussion.

## Replacement Intentions

Organizational IS replacement initiatives are typically preceded by the expenditure of some measure of time and other resources to identify suitable alternatives, select a preferred alternative, and plan for the implementation of this alternative. The emergence of replacement intentions among IS decision makers is, therefore, of considerable significance. In effect, the formation of such intentions indicates that decision makers have recognized that the current situation is problematic and action may be necessary. Since it is this recognition that initiates the processes needed to replace an existing system [26], the emergence of replacement intentions indicates that an organization is moving from passive persistence with the status quo to active engagement in a search for alternatives [80]. Given this pivotal role of replacement intentions in triggering organizational-level IS discontinuance, these intentions serve as our key outcome.

## Threat Significance

Provided that continued use of an existing system does not present a salient threat to the operation of an organization or its long-term success, it is unlikely that decision makers will be particularly motivated to launch an IS replacement initiative [37]. Processes of obsolescence can, however, present decision makers with two potentially severe threats. The first of these is the functional obsolescence that emerges as capabilities of an existing system begin to diverge from current organizational needs. This manifests itself as system capability shortcomings. The second threat stems from the emergence of new and better technologies that result in the technological obsolescence of existing technologies. This form of obsolescence is manifest through increased difficulty in obtaining support for older technologies. Growing salience of one or both of these threats is expected to encourage decision makers to at least contemplate the need to discontinue the use of an existing IS [22, 64].

## Functional Obsolescence

Over time the functional utility of an information system begins to erode in a process of functional obsolescence. As the functionality that a system provides begins to diverge from the functionality that is needed, capability shortcomings become evident to organizational decision makers [25, 43, 62]. The functionality of a system designed to support interorganizational data exchange might, for example, be rendered obsolete by the release of a new industry standard that redefines how these exchanges should take place. Similarly, changing regulations can render a wide range of system capabilities obsolete [101]. While it is possible to manage such changes in isolation, over time their cumulative effects tend to create significant discrepancies between the capabilities of a system and what is deemed necessary [65].

An IS that lacks capabilities being demanded by users, managers, customers, and other important stakeholders can present numerous difficulties for an organization. Such a system can, for example, impose the use of ineffective or irrelevant processes, limit strategic innovation, and negatively impact costs, productivity, and customer service [33, 42]. Capability shortcomings can also impose constraints on growth and flexibility that undermine long term organizational success. Hence, the effectiveness of an IS can be expected to diminish as changing internal and external circumstances undermine the suitability of its capabilities [75, 85]. In essence, such functional obsolescence serves, over time, to diminish the fit between what an organization requires and what a system offers. Given the well-documented importance of this fit to system use and organizational performance [30, 104], it is expected that limits on the extent to which an information system is capable of meeting organizational needs will foster replacement intentions that lead to a search for alternatives [79]. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: Greater system capability shortcomings lead to increased replacement intentions.

## Technical Obsolescence

Technical obsolescence arises as a consequence of innovation and new product releases that result in existing systems no longer reflecting the current state of technological development [81]. The pace of change encountered within the field of information systems is such that organizations can readily find themselves operating systems that are technically obsolete though functionally adequate [83]. While organizations may exert greater control over the technical obsolescence of internally developed systems, even these system are subject to technical obsolescence as a consequence of innovations related to their underlying hardware and software platforms [90]. Hence, over time, the challenges associated with growing technological obsolescence of existing systems can begin to pose significant threats to their continued use.

A key indicator that a system is becoming technically obsolete is a growing lack of system support. Vendors tend to withdraw support for technically obsolete systems while labor markets gradually adjust to reduce the number of individuals possessing the skills needed to support such systems [25, 59]. Declining availability of support for information systems is further reinforced by the often severe pressures that developers face to regularly release new products [95]. New product releases provide important sources of organizational revenue and key marketing opportunities. Developers are thus encouraged to focus on developing new products rather than ensuring continued support for existing products. Therefore, the difficulties encountered by organizations using technically obsolete systems and the resources demanded to operate and maintain these systems both tend to increase as the availability of system support declines [42, 90]. In addition, since developers exert reduced efforts to maintain the functionality of unsupported systems, it can become increasingly difficult for organizations to ensure that such systems provide needed capabilities. As a result, a lack of system support can have direct implications for the emergence of replacement intentions and indirect implications through its impact on system capability shortcomings. This suggests:

Hypothesis 2a: Decreased support availability leads to increased replacement intentions.

Hypothesis 2b: Decreased support availability leads to increased system capability shortcomings.

## Capacity for Effective Response

Although replacing an existing system might offer a number of benefits including reduced support challenges and improved functionality, doing so is not without costs and difficulties [24]. As a consequence, decision makers may not be motivated to act if they believe that the capacity of their organization to overcome such difficulties is somehow constrained, particularly given that overcoming these difficulties often involves the sacrifice of near-term utility for longer-term, more speculative benefits [22, 23]. Beliefs that organizational capacity to replace an existing system is somehow limited can thus undermine the emergence of replacement intentions even when continued use of this system presents significant threats to ongoing success [20]. Hence, in the following discussion we identify replacement risk and system complexity as two particularly important constraints on decision makers’ beliefs that their organization is able to respond effectively to the threats posed by technical and functional obsolescence [18, 89].

## Replacement Risk

Persistence with the status quo often represents a relatively low-risk situation. The issues and challenges presented by such a situation are typically known, understood, and being actively managed to minimize the threats that they pose. In contrast, implementing a replacement IS can introduce notable risks [16, 96]. These include the potential for disruption to critical processes, unexpected user resistance, waning management support, technical hurdles, unexpected resource constraints, difficulties developing new competencies, and problems creating and enforcing new organizational routines [71, 89, 102]. In addition, the benefits that are ultimately derived from a replacement effort can be both uncertain and difficult to quantify, particularly when the need for replacement is first contemplated [1]. As a consequence, the risks associated with IS investments typically exceed those of other capital investments [16]. The presence of significant replacement risk, defined here as the extent to which a replacement initiative holds the potential for negative outcomes [2, 20], would thus seem to encourage continued use of an existing IS irrespective of the threats that this might pose and the potential benefits that a replacement system might yield.

IS replacement requires an organization to incur immediate costs for purported future benefits. Managers tend, however, to underweight the opportunity costs associated with forgoing a replacement while significantly overweighting the potential for losses that might arise as a consequence of this replacement [80]. Managers also tend to be particularly concerned with and constrained by the immediate losses that a replacement initiative represents [72]. IS replacement involves relatively certain nearterm losses while the status quo represents potentially larger though somewhat more speculative losses at some point in the seemingly distant future. As a result, the tendency to overweight the potential for loss during a replacement effort is likely to be heightened during the time when replacement intentions are forming [39]. Hence, it is expected that decision makers will feel particularly constrained by replacement risk when they first begin to contemplate the possibility of replacement. This leads to:

Hypothesis 3: Increased replacement risk leads to decreased replacement intentions.

## System Complexity

System complexity encompasses any aspect of an IS that increases the quantity and sophistication of its constituent components [55]. Hence, although one element of complexity is the extent to which a system is integrated with other systems [87], system complexity includes a wide range of other elements such as the scale of a system, the diversity of its user base, the nature of its development platform, and the sophistication and diversity of the organizational processes that it supports [53]. Since all these elements and their interdependencies must be suitably addressed during an IS replacement initiative, system complexity can pose a potentially significant threat to the success of IS replacement initiatives.

While complex information systems are capable of providing organizations with numerous efficiencies in the collection, storage, and dissemination of information, once such systems have been successfully implemented they also create a form of “lock-in” that makes them difficult to replace without significant disruption [42, 87]. Efforts to replace an inventory management system are, for example, likely to be impeded if the system has been extended to permit it to send and receive data from financial and warehouse management systems. Replacing such a system in isolation creates the risk of unexpected, potentially severe failures [87]. Efforts to decouple the system and then reintegrate its replacement are also likely to increase the costs of replacement. Although replacing all elements of a complex system in a single initiative can help to overcome some of these issues, this approach can readily extend the scope of a replacement project well beyond what is desired or even financially feasible. In addition, system complexity typically impedes efforts to fully understand organizational and end user needs as these relate to the system being targeted for replacement. This serves to further increase the risk that replacing a complex system will result in costly, unexpected challenges [75]. There is, therefore, notable potential for disruption when complex systems are replaced [34, 75] and it can thus be expected that complexity will be a direct impediment to the emergence of replacement intentions while simultaneously contributing to increased replacement risk. We therefore propose:

Hypothesis 4a: Greater system complexity leads to decreased replacement intentions.

Hypothesis 4b: Greater system complexity leads to increased replacement risk.

## Antecedents of Threats and Response Capacity

Thus far we have presented a model of IS discontinuance that identifies four direct antecedents of IS replacement intentions. Rooted in protection motivation theory, this model posits a core tension between the threats posed by obsolescence and the capacity of organizations to respond to these threats. It is, however, important that this core be developed and extended to elicit insights into underlying antecedents. Such efforts serve to deepen our understanding of IS discontinuance and provide a richer basis for managerial action [97]. We thus take the first steps in this direction by identifying institutional norms and system investments as having significant consequences for obsolescence and organizational capacity to respond to this obsolescence.

## Institutional Norms

Organizational decisions can be notably influenced by the environmental context in which an organization is operating [59]. Among the more significant of these influences are institutional pressures that lead managers to make decisions and take actions that are similar to those of reference organizations such as partners and competitors [25, 79]. Institutional theorists have directed considerable attention toward examining and understanding the pressures that foster similarity and stability in organizational practices [18]. This work suggests that routine interaction among organizations operating within a given environment and the emergence of rules and procedures to guide these interactions can lead to widely accepted norms that are often difficult to ignore [49, 68]. Efforts to maintain legitimacy within an industry environment and ensure access to needed resources can thus lead organizations to conform to the norms that prevail in their environment [18]. Such conformance also fosters a sense of permanence and stability surrounding these norms that can severely undermine the ability of industry participants to recognize when circumstances have changed or even to contemplate a need for change [46, 63].

Information systems that are used by a large number of industry participants tend to be regarded as representing industry standards and best practices. As a consequence, they are relatively unlikely to be viewed as being obsolete irrespective of changing circumstances. The developers of widely used systems also tend to have access to strong and consistent revenue streams from product licensing and the provision of product support services [67]. As a result, they often have the resources needed to ensure that their systems are updated to reflect changing needs. In addition, developers of industry standard systems have access to a broad customer base that they can draw upon to gain insights into changing industry needs. Such developers can thus be expected to have greater access to the market insights needed to maintain the currency of their systems as well as the incentives and power necessary to pressure customers to routinely update their systems to supported versions [11, 47]. Finally, evidence suggests that organizations direct greater attention toward maintaining systems that represent institutional norms [76]. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 5a: Systems that more strongly reflect institutionalized norms exhibit reduced system capability shortcomings.

Hypothesis 5b: Systems that more strongly reflect institutionalized norms have access to greater levels of system support.

## System Investments

Ongoing investments in an IS are of fundamental importance to the continued evolution of the system including its scope, scale, and integration with other systems [43]. These investments can lead to growing reliance on a system and heightened system complexity [48]. Increased reliance on a system can also expose an organization to more severe consequences in the event of significant difficulties with the system while greater system complexity can increase the chances that such difficulties will actually be encountered. As a result, business and audit risks have been found to increase with the level of investment in a system [34]. Although such risks can typically be managed during routine system operation, replacement efforts disrupt routine operation and thereby heighten the potential for difficulties [9]. Moreover, it can be difficult to understand or even be aware of the challenges that might be faced when replacing the types of largescale systems that often result from extensive, ongoing investments [34]. Therefore, notable risks are likely to be associated with replacing such systems.

Beyond the many system-related risks associated with replacing high-investment systems, replacement of such systems typically involves greater resource expenditures and tends to impact a broader group of stakeholders, which increases the potential for requirements volatility and user resistance [51, 71]. In addition, achieving success with large-scale replacement initiatives often requires a greater degree of managerial support and attention that must also be extended over a longer period of time [61]. This increases the risk that management attention will wane over the course of a large-scale replacement initiative as other organizational challenges arise. Finally, replacing a high investment system can have potentially broad implications for established work processes, standard operating procedures, and task distributions such that the initiative might encounter numerous unexpected impediments [71, 103]. As a result, notable time lags often exist between the introduction of a new IS and acquisition of the expertise needed to fully assimilate and leverage value from this system [6, 102]. Hence, given the heightened potential for system and organizational difficulties when replacing a high investment system, we propose:

## Hypothesis 6a: Increased system investment leads to increased replacement risk.

Organizations face significant pressure to streamline and integrate their processes in order to reduce costs and improve the quality and timeliness of the information provided by their information systems [33]. In this regard, system integration eliminates redundant data, minimizes duplicate data entry, provides better data access, facilitates improved data analysis, and improves organizational decision making [93]. As a consequence, once an organization has successfully implemented an IS, attention is often turned toward investing in the system to further integrate it with other systems and expand its scope to incorporate tasks that were previously performed by isolated systems [66, 69]. These efforts are frequently seen as being important to maximizing the benefits derived from IS investments and improving organizational performance [15, 66, 70]. Nonetheless, such investments can significantly increase system complexity.

Reinforcing the pressures that organizations face to direct IS investment funds toward increasing the scope, scale, and integration of existing systems is a tendency to further integrate an IS once it has received significant investments [50]. Integration with other systems can be seen as preferable to replacing a system and thus “wasting” the investments that it has already received. A positive feedback loop is thus created whereby investments in a system encourage further investments aimed at integrating it into other systems and thereby contributing to further increases in its complexity. Finally, as systems become more complex, maintenance investments foster disproportionate increases in complexity [90]. Hence, given the pressures to invest in improving existing systems and the reinforcing loops this creates, we expect:

## Hypothesis 6b: Increased system investment leads to increased system complexity.

Although system investments can impact the nature of an IS, these investments can, in turn, be driven to a notable extent by institutional norms [79]. The power of institutional norms to influence IS investment decisions is rooted in multiple considerations. To begin, organizations operating in similar environmental contexts will confront similar regulatory regimes [84]. This tends to encourage investments in systems that are regarded as appropriate to the regimes being faced. In addition, managers experience significant pressures from referent organizations that encourage them to invest in institutionally accepted systems [88, 92]. While investing in such systems represents a relatively safe decision, it can also provide costs savings, facilitate interorganizational transactions, and support mutually beneficial intraindustry cooperation. Given that the first organization to implement a particular IS can encounter numerous unexpected issues, investing in an industry standard system might even provide follower organizations with superior performance [54]. Finally, investing in systems that are well-regarded within an industry can provide managers with valuable opportunities to signal their competence [56]. Hence:

Hypothesis 7: Systems that more strongly reflect institutionalized norms experience increased system investment.

## Methodology

A survey study of senior IS managers was undertaken to test our research model. The sample frame used for the study was the alumni database of a large European business school that offers degree and executive education programs to managers throughout the world. A sample of senior-level IS managers was randomly drawn from this database. Electronic and paper-based versions of the survey were developed in accordance with established practices to ensure maximum response rates [17]. Survey measures were based on established measures to the extent that such measures were available (Appendix Table A1). Adapted and newly created measures were developed based on construct definitions and refined through a card-sorting exercise before being pilot tested to assess their reliability. Items exhibiting poor inter-rater agreement during the cardsorting exercise and items exhibiting poor reliability during the pilot test were eliminated. All measurement items were randomly ordered on the survey instrument and buffer items that were not of direct relevance to the research model were added to reduce common-method effects [73]. Responses were provided using seven-point Likert agreement scales with points clearly labeled as “strongly disagree,” “disagree,” “slightly disagree,” “neither agree nor disagree,” “slightly agree,” “agree,” and “strongly agree,” in order to minimize response ambiguity.

An initial draft of the survey was distributed electronically to 25 IS managers drawn from the study sample frame. Responses to the pilot study indicated that the survey and distribution process were appropriate and that survey measures were psychometrically sound. A final version of the survey was then distributed electronically to 798 senior IS managers. Approximately one week after the initial distribution a reminder e-mail was sent to individuals who had not yet provided a response. Two weeks following the reminder e-mail, a paper version of the survey was mailed to nonrespondents with a personalized request for assistance and a postage-paid return envelope. A final e-mail reminder was sent approximately 14 weeks after the paper copy of the survey was mailed. This process yielded 160 completed responses, which, after eliminating undeliverable surveys from the sample, represents a response rate of 25.2 percent.

To ensure that the survey was directed to suitable individuals it included a prescreen question that asked respondents whether they were responsible for decisions to replace at least one of their organization’s information systems. Respondents holding this responsibility were asked to identify one such system in use at their organization and complete the balance of the survey with this system in mind. All others were asked to return their survey without answering any further questions. To avoid range restriction on our dependent variable, respondents were free to choose the system they reported on rather than being asked to report on either a recently replaced system or a system being considered for replacement. Eliminating responses from those individuals who indicated they were not responsible for IS replacement decisions resulted in a final sample of 112 responses from IS managers representing 90 organizations. While each respondent provided only one response, multiple responses were received from organizations that were operating multiple business units. Therefore, all 112 responses report on distinct systems that range from production control systems, product life-cycle management systems, and document management systems to web servers and regulatory compliance systems. Although only one or two responses were received for many system types, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems exhibited some degree of salience (Table 1).

Survey responses were received from organizations operating within a broad range of industries (Table 2) and headquartered in 18 countries (Table 3). Respondents served in a wide range of IS management capacities (Table 4). ## Data Analysis and Results

Data analysis was performed in two stages beginning with an assessment of the measurement model using exploratory and confirmatory techniques [32]. This was followed by an assessment of the structural model using partial least squares (PLS) as implemented in SmartPLS (Version 2.0.M3) and covariance-based structural equation modeling (SEM) as this technique is implemented in AMOS Version 18.0.0 (Build 992) [4, 77]. Both techniques are appropriate given our research model because they both permit the construction of latent variables from observed indicator variables, simultaneous evaluation of multiple path relationships, and assessments of the convergent and discriminant validity of construct measures [32]. However, given persistent dialogue concerning the suitability and relative advantages of each technique, we opted to conduct our analysis using PLS and then verify this analysis using covariance-based SEM [29]. Use of SEM also provided an opportunity to assess the unidimensionality of our construct measures [3, 27]. Bootstrapping was used to test the statistical significance of path relationships in our PLS model while SEM analysis was run using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation, which is considered appropriate when sample sizes exceed 100 to 150 [32]. Although our sample is on the lower end of this range, the comparative fit index (CFI) and the incremental fit index (IFI) have been identified as fit measures that are appropriate to our sample size [91].

System age, organization size, and a binary variable indicating whether the system being reported on was a commercial or custom-developed system were included as control variables in our analysis. Survey respondents reported system age as the number of years that a system had been in use in their organization. Organization size was operationalized as the natural logarithm of the number of employees working at the organization [25]. Employee count data were obtained from publicly available sources including annual reports and organizational websites. Finally, the binary variable used to indicate whether a system was a commercial system was constructed based on system descriptions provided by survey respondents.

## Discussion

Drawing on protection motivation theory, we developed and empirically validated a model of IS discontinuance that identifies threat appraisal and coping appraisal as two key processes underlying IS discontinuance decisions. Our model thus argues that the intentions of IS managers to act in response to the threats posed by IS obsolescence are constrained by the extent to which they perceive the presence of significant impediments that undermine their capacity to respond to these threats. This assertion was validated using survey data collected from 112 senior-level IS managers working within a broad range of industries and organizations. Analysis of these data indicates that our model of discontinuance offers a powerful, parsimonious, generalizable account of IS discontinuance. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of the threat appraisal and coping appraisal processes posited by PMT to understanding IS discontinuance.

Replacement risks, system complexity, system investments, and institutional norms were all identified as significant impediments to the emergence of organizational plans to replace existing information systems. These findings represent notable extensions to prior work, which has thus far only identified technical integration as a relatively weak constraint on the emergence of replacement intentions [25]. In contrast to this prior work, our research model recognizes that organizations are routinely confronted with risks that must be managed to minimize threats to their long-term survival [5]. Hence, in accordance with anecdotal evidence suggesting that replacement risks can threaten the continued viability of an organization and the careers of those who lead a replacement initiative [98], we demonstrate that replacement risks have a significant negative impact on the intentions of decision makers to replace an existing IS. Thus, while it might be expected that decision makers would prefer to promptly replace obsolete systems rather than deferring the associated losses to some future point in time [23], our work indicates that replacement risks foster a notable degree of reluctance to act. Thus, there appear to be a number of interesting opportunities to further explore how replacement risks are understood by decision makers and how they impact IS discontinuance.

Although we found strong support for work that highlights the role of system capability shortcomings as drivers of IS discontinuance, our results suggest a need for refinement of our understanding of the implications of system support availability for discontinuance. In particular, PLS and covariance-based SEM analysis indicate that the negative impact of support availability on IS replacement intentions is at least partially mediated through the role that declining support availability plays in fostering capability shortcomings. This finding suggests that while a lack of support may trouble IS managers, they tend to remain relatively unwilling to pursue replacement until this lack of support results in a system that suffers from notable capability shortcomings.

Moving beyond the core elements of discontinuance calculus, we find that institutional norms constrain IS discontinuance via multiple mechanisms and thus serve as an especially powerful stabilizing force. Specifically, institutionalized norms foster continued use of existing information systems by impeding the processes of obsolescence while simultaneously fostering continued investments in existing systems that ultimately serve to increase the complexity and risks associated with replacing these systems. Thus while it might be the case that IS managers invest in widely used systems because they are better able to meet organizational needs, it can also be the case that managers are pressured to direct greater attention toward ensuring that institutionally legitimized systems are maintained and supported. More generally, the results that we report demonstrate that multiple forces operate to entrench existing systems and make IS replacement both difficult and relatively unlikely.

Although it was expected that system complexity would undermine the emergence of replacement intentions, it appears that complexity influences these intentions only indirectly through its influence on replacement risks. While complexity increases system maintenance costs and difficulties [90], it also fosters stability in organizational relationships and greatly facilitates the execution of organizational processes [52]. As a result, replacing a complex system is likely to represent a significant risk to the stability of these processes and relationships and thereby diminish the willingness of managers to contemplate a replacement initiative. Nonetheless, there would be notable value in future research aimed at unpacking the notion of system complexity in order to more fully understand how it influences replacement risk and how it can be overcome.

Investments in an existing system seem to constrain discontinuance by increasing replacement risk and the level of complexity exhibited by a system. This result explains why prior research has failed to identify system investment as a significant driver of replacement intentions [25]. Rather than having a direct influence on replacement intentions, our work indicates that system investments constrain the formation of replacement intentions through their positive influence on system complexity and the risks associated with replacement. Given that increased system complexity can indirectly impede potentially important replacement efforts, some caution is warranted in relation to ongoing investments in existing systems. While we do not believe that organizations should avoid making such investments, it is important that they be routinely and thoroughly evaluated to ensure that they do not introduce needless complexity.

## Implications for Practice

Among the implications for practice that can be derived from our work are those suggested by the significant impact that replacement risk has on the formation of replacement intentions. In particular, IS vendors seeking to offer new solutions are advised to be cognizant of the importance of replacement risks to prospective customers and to ensure that proposed solutions mitigate or avoid these risks. In addition, vendors should be aware that there is greater reluctance to replace commercial systems and they should target their efforts accordingly. In contrast, IS managers should be aware of how replacement risks can impede effective discontinuance decision making within their organizations. In this regard, it seems important for organizations to develop their capacity to understand and monitor the risks associated with continued use of existing systems as well as the risks associated with replacing these systems. More proactively, there is value in giving greater consideration to the risks of obsolescence and replacement during earlier stages of the IS life cycle. By doing so, managers can steer system design, implementation, and ongoing operation and maintenance in ways that minimize obsolescence and replacement risks.

While there is notable benefit to be had from efforts to better manage the risks of obsolescence and replacement, our work also suggests that organizational reliance on a highly institutionalized system fosters a sense that the system is functionally and technically current. This does not necessarily mean that organizations should preferentially adopt industry standard systems. It does, however, highlight a need to identify and implement systems that will receive the requisite levels of ongoing support, attention, and resources from internal stakeholders and system providers. In some cases it may be that an industry standard system represents the best option while in other cases our findings may steer organizations toward the implementation of a system with either the potential to become institutionalized in the future or a system having the salience of an industry standard without the purported drawbacks of a highly institutionalized system [31]. Finally, organizations should endeavor to monitor the evolution of technology trends in their industries to identify changing norms and respond to these in a timely manner. In this regard, it may be useful to actively engage with system user groups, standards bodies, and other similar entities to help shape norms in desired directions and thereby ensure the continued functional and technical currency of key organizational systems [60].

In broader terms, our work highlights how organizations can be more proactive in addressing obsolescence instead of responding reactively once significant issues arise. It is, for example, important for managers to monitor the external environment for early indicators that an IS might become technically obsolete given our finding that a lack of system support ultimately leads to system capability shortcomings that may need to be addressed with some measure of urgency. Alternatively, an organization may opt to simply accept that technical obsolescence is inevitable and then plan to minimize its impact by, for example, developing internal system support capabilities and creating the incentives needed to retain key support personnel. While this latter approach certainly presents its own set of risks, organizations that fail to proactively manage the functional and technical obsolescence of their systems can expect to face heightened risk and greater pressures to replace their systems at more frequent intervals.

In addition to monitoring the external environment and proactively responding to institutional changes, managers might benefit from efforts to better modularize implementations to reduce replacement risks, foster maintainability, and ensure that excessive complexity does not needlessly impede necessary replacement initiatives. Managers should also recognize the power of IS investments to increase replacement risks and system complexity. These investments should be managed proactively to minimize the introduction of risks and complexity while simultaneously ensuring that maximum business value is achieved and system maintenance costs remain appropriate. In general, explicitly monitoring internal and external pressures that foster obsolescence can help organizations to ensure that IS discontinuance is actively driven by internal needs rather than passively driven by external forces.

## Limitations

An obvious limitation of the present initiative is the constrained sample size that stems from the challenges associated with obtaining survey responses from seniorlevel managers. While a larger sample would be ideal, the insights that we offer are nonetheless based on input obtained from over 110 senior IS managers working within a wide range of organizational and national contexts. We therefore believe that the size and character of our sample is such that we are able to offer important insights and provide valuable guidance to managers and future researchers. A second limitation of the present work is its reliance on survey responses from managers working in organizations that are largely rooted in “western” managerial traditions. Although this is representative of many organizations, there would be notable value in examining discontinuance within organizations operating under radically different cultures. Prior research suggests, for example, that perceptions of replacement risk might vary significantly across cultures [19]. A third limitation is the cross-sectional nature of our work. While cross-sectional inquiry seems appropriate to our interest in understanding the initial drivers of discontinuance, there would be considerable value in pursuing more process-oriented inquiry to better understand subsequent phases of IS discontinuance. A fourth limitation relates to our reliance on perceptual measures and our reliance on a single informant to provide the responses that underlie all of our measures except our measure of organizational size. Given suggestions that not all methods for assessing common-method bias are entirely effective [13], we introduced unrelated measurement items into our survey design to counteract this threat. We also performed a series of post hoc tests as recommended by recent literature to help ensure that common-method bias is not unduly impacting our results [100]. These efforts indicate that our findings are robust.

## Opportunities for Future Research

In addition to efforts to address some of the limitations that we have identified, there are significant opportunities for future research related to individual, organizational, and societal IS discontinuance. Among the opportunities are efforts to identify, characterize, and improve key IS discontinuance activities and processes. Although these activities and processes are likely to become more closely bound to IS implementation efforts once specific replacement systems are identified, there is still notable value in understanding how they emerge, evolve, and interact with each other over time. These efforts could, for example, elaborate on various aspects of the threat and coping appraisal processes that underlie our model. Future opportunities are also available to those interested in better understanding the nature of technical and functional obsolescence, how both forms of obsolescence emerge in relation to organizational assets that are decidedly less physical in character, such as information systems, and how perceptions of IS obsolescence evolve over time among system users and managers. Work might also be undertaken to explore whether the impact of replacement risk, capability shortcomings, support availability, and system complexity on replacement intentions is altered when the system in question is a core business system, a legacy system, or another specific class of system.

The significant roles of replacement risk and system complexity that are highlighted in the present work suggest a number of opportunities to more fully explore system complexity and replacement risk. Work in this area might, for example, seek to better understand the nature of the risks associated with increased IS obsolescence as well as how managerial understanding of replacement risk is formed and revised. This line of inquiry would provide a strong foundation for managerial efforts to better balance the tension between replacement risk and the risks associated with continued use of an obsolete system. Finally, there would seem to be significant value in assessing the level of agreement among key stakeholders concerning core elements of discontinuance calculus including replacement risk, system complexity, capability shortcomings, and support availability. This line of inquiry would help to improve our understanding of how stakeholder agreement or its lack might impact IS discontinuance decisions and processes.

## Conclusion

Drawing on the processes of threat appraisal and coping appraisal posited by protection motivation theory, we formulated and empirically tested a model of IS discontinuance. In so doing we demonstrated the importance of these two processes to understanding IS replacement decisions. We have also highlighted a need to monitor and manage the emergence of system complexity, ongoing system investments, possible replacement risks, and changing institutional norms to ensure that obsolete systems do not become significant impediments to organizational effectiveness. Given the breadth of organizations and system types included in our analysis, our work provides a generalizable, parsimonious view of discontinuance that opens up and supports many possibilities for future research aimed at understanding IS obsolescence and obsolescence in a wide range of other domains including business practices, standards setting, and product and service offerings.
