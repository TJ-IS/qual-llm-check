# Enterprise reputation threats on social media: A case of data breach framing

Romilla Syed

Department of Management Science and Information Systems, College of Management, University of Massachusetts, Boston, 100 Morrissey Blvd. Boston, MA 02125, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Enterprise reputation Reputation threat Crisis Data breach Social media Framing

## A B S T R A C T

Reputation threats on social media in the aftermath of a data breach is a critical concern to enterprises. We argue that any e<sup>f</sup>ort to minimize reputation threats will require an orderly assessment of how reputation threat manifests on social media. Drawing on crisis communication and social media literature, we analyze Twitter postings related to the 2014 Home Depot data breach. We identify a taxonomy of data breach frames and sub-frames and the related reputation threats as manifested by data breach responsibility-attributions and negative emotional re sponses. Results indicate that reputation threats vary for intentional, accidental, and victim data breach frames. Based on crisis stage theory, we also analyze the dynamics of evolving reputation threats as data breach situation unfolds on social media. Results suggest that the data breach frames and associated reputation threats vary across the crisis stages. Further, intentional and accidental frames increase subsequent responsibility-attributions and negative emotions. Tweets with responsibility-attributions further increase the subsequent generation of reputation-threatening tweets. Negative emotions, particularly anger and disgust, also increase subsequent reputation threats. Our study has implications for enterprise reputation management and word-ofmouth literature. The results yield valuable insights that can guide enterprise strategy for social media reputation management and post data breach intervention.

## Introduction

Reputation is one of the most valuable assets of enterprises. A loss of reputation can have a devastating impact on firm competitiveness, stakeholder trust and loyalty, the general legitimacy of the operations, and business survival (Brown et al., 2006; Rayner, 2004). There are various reasons an enterprise may lose its reputation. Of particular concern to us, and as noted in the literature, is the loss of reputation following a data breach (Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Gwebu et al., 2018; Kannan et al., 2007; Syed and Dhillon, 2015). A data breach not only puts millions of customers at risk (ITRC, 2016) and poses considerable costs to businesses (Ponemon-Institute, 2016) but also abruptly threatens enterprise reputation, especially if the public perceives that their self-interest is hurt (Cavusoglu et al., 2004). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne enterprise reputation as an aggregate evaluation or perception of an enterprise’s ability to meet the publics’ expectations of securing customer information (Wartick, 1992).

Although reputation threats can perpetuate from di<sup>f</sup>erent sources, social media expands the spectrum of threats for businesses (Aula, 2010). Research suggests that a crisis often breaks on social media which then leads to the crisis reporting on traditional media (Sung and Hwang, 2014). Further, unlike traditional media, social media allows immediate public reactions to a crisis (Schultz et al., 2011). Often such reactions contain intense indignation against an enterprise that faced the crisis. For instance, after the data breach at Target Corporation, which exposed records of millions of customers, the company su<sup>f</sup>ered substantial public wrath on socia media.<sup>1</sup> Recent Cambridge Analytica scandal also indicates that news related to data leaks trend instantly on social media, providing an opportunity for the public to evaluate the competence of the enterprise to prevent the crisis or the harm caused by the crisis.<sup>2</sup> Increased reputation concern on social media is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed as research suggest that 28% of crisis information spreads globally within the <sup>fi</sup>rst hour and failure to respond within the <sup>fi</sup>rst 21 h leaves an organization open to “trial on Twitter.”<sup>3</sup>

Additionally, social media users can create, receive, and forward their own negative perspective of a crisis situation, often bypassing mainstream media (Oh et al., 2013; van der Meer and Verhoeven, 2013). Hence, social media users might even create and spread un<sup>fi</sup>ltered negative perspective of a data breach. Referred to as the negative word-of-mouth (nWOM), social media postings containing intense indignation not only signal reputation threat in the view of the enterprise who faced a data breach but also allows further dissemination of the reputation-threatening content within the social network (Coombs, 2007; Pfe<sup>f</sup>er et al., 2014). We term such perils as enterprise reputation threat de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the discharge of a large volume of social media postings containing intense indignation, criticism, or complaints against an enterprise that faced the data breach (Pfe<sup>f</sup>er et al., 2014).

Prior information systems (IS) research has studied the implications of data breaches, albeit from the <sup>fi</sup>nancial perspective (Acquisti et al., 2006; Spanos and Angelis, 2016). Recent research explores the impact of data breach announcements on corporate reputation ranks such as Fortune's Americas Most Admired Companies (AMAC) (Gwebu et al., 2018). The nWOM on social media questioning the ability of an enterprise in the wake of a data breach has also received attention (Syed and Dhillon, 2015). Further, the impact of media framing on enterprise reputation has been noted in the extant literature (Coombs, 2007; Romanosky et al., 2011). However, social media framing of a data breach and the framing characteristics that signal threats to enterprise reputation have received little attention. Additionally, recent research shows that social media has emerged as a new communication tool during crisis and emergency situations (Leong et al., 2015: Oh et al., 2013) that provides situational awareness (Vieweg et al., 2010) and supports orderly crisis management process and decision-making (Nan and Lu, 2014; Yin et al., 2012). However, the propagation of reputation-threatening content on social media has not received much attention. When businesses know the type of information and the dynamics that unfold on social media in the aftermath of a data breach, they can make informed decisions regarding post-crisis reputation management (Coombs, 2007: Coombs and Holladay. 2005: Spence et al., 2015: Sturges. 1994)

To that end, this study has two research objectives. The <sup>fi</sup>rst objective is to explore how enterprise reputation threat is framed on social media in the aftermath of a data breach. Informed by crisis communication and social media literature, we argue that enterprises perceive reputation threat depending on how social media users frame the role or ability of the enterprise to prevent or handle the data breach. Further, reputation threat is intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed if social media users increasingly attribute the data breach responsibility to the enterprise or relay strong negative emotional responses against the enterprise (Coombs, 2007). We use the 2014 Home Depot (HD) data breach case that exposed personal information of millions of customers.<sup>4</sup> We conducted an exploratory analysis of approximately 15 K Twitter postings (tweets) to identify a taxonomy of data breach frames and the associated reputation threats as manifested b responsibility-attributions and negative emotional responses.

The second objective is to explore how enterprise reputation threat evolves as a data breach crisis unfolds on social media. Crisis communication scholars note that a crisis progresses through a series of stages, and each stage has its unique characteristics (Sturges, 1994). Hence, e<sup>f</sup>ective crisis response will require an understanding of how reputation threats evolve as a data breach situation unfolds on social media. Informed by Fink’s (1986) crisis stage theory, we conducted the temporal analysis of data breach frames and reputation-threatening characteristics. To gain further insights into the di<sup>f</sup>usion of reputation threats, we conducted a time series analysis of the immediate impact of reputation-threatening tweets on subsequent crisis responsibility-attributions and negative emotions. This analysis allowed us to not only identify the crisis stages surfacing from our research context but also assess the impact of reputation-threatening content on the subsequent generation of reputation-threatening content.

The rest of paper is organized as follows: In the next section, we discuss the concept of reputation threats in crisis situations. We also review the extant literature related to social media in the organizational context. Subsequently, we present the theoretical rationale for the two research objectives. Next, we discuss the research design and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. This is followed by a discussion on theoretical and practical implications. Finally, the paper concludes with limitations and directions for future research.

## Background literature

There are two bodies of literature that inform this research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst relates to online enterprise reputation in the event of a crisis and the second pertains to the pervasiveness of social media in organizations.

## Online enterprise reputation and crisis

The concept of enterprise reputation is increasingly studied in the management literature (Lange et al., 2011). However, the increased interest of scholars has led to multiple and inconsistent de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, conceptualizations, and operationalizations. Although a de<sup>fi</sup>nitive de<sup>fi</sup>nition is yet to emerge, there have been some attempts to o<sup>f</sup>er guiding frameworks. For example, Brown et al. (2006) di<sup>f</sup>erentiate reputation from corporate identity and image. While corporate identity is the perception or mental association about an organization held by the organizational members, corporate image is the mental association an organization wants others to hold or that organizational members believe outsiders hold about the organization. In contrast, corporate reputation is the mental association about an organization held by outsiders. In a related study, Lange et al. (2011) synthesize three conceptualizations of reputation: (1) Being known refers to generalized awareness or visibility, prominence of an organization in the collective perception. (2) Being known for something refers to the perceived predictability of the organizational outcomes and behaviors relevant to speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of interest. (3) Generalized favorability refers to the perceptions or judgments of the overall organization as good, attractive, and appropriate.

In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne enterprise reputation as an aggregate evaluation or perception of an enterprise’s ability to meet the publics’ expectations of securing customer information (Wartick, 1992). Our de<sup>fi</sup>nition conceptualizes enterprise reputation as being known for something where public look to an enterprise to meet their idiosyncratic interests. We adopt this perspective as our research focuses on the judgments held by the social media users about a particular enterprise attribute, that is the ability of an enterprise to prevent or manage a data breach (see Lange et al., 2011). Our choice to analyze online reputation is motivated by the recent research that notes the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of social media to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions about businesses (Benthaus et al., 2016; Berger et al., 2014). Further, our conceptualization is consistent with Brown et al. (2006) viewpoint about reputation, that is, what do stakeholders actually think of the organization?

Moving our focus to online reputation is instrumental in recognizing that the internal mental associations are not the only elements de<sup>fi</sup>ning an enterprise’s identity and worth. Consumers and other stakeholders rely on external evaluations or validations of the claims made by enterprises – for example, to determine the worth of products (Forman et al., 2008), or to mitigate uncertainty about product quality and <sup>fi</sup>t (Kwark et al., 2014), or to assess the legitimacy of medical practitioner (Waguespack and Sorenson, 2011). Proponents of this perspective emphasize that people often trust unbiased opinions more than those claimed by enterprises about themselves (Duan et al., 2008). Further, positive external evaluations attract customers, retain talent, earn a competitive advantage, and improve <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance (Deephouse, 2000; Lange et al., 2011). However, with the popularity and outreach of online content, enterprises are becoming increasingly concerned about their online reputation. Particularly, managing negative online reviews has been challenging as enterprises do not have control over the reviews or an option to be “not reviewed” (Proserpio and Zervas, 2017). Although negative reviews could be fake and mechanisms to reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ect of such questionable practices have been proposed (Dellarocas, 2000), there is also some evidence that <sup>fi</sup>rms engage in review frauds, such as posting fake positive reviews for reputation management. However, such practices can have serious <sup>fi</sup>nancial and legal implications for the <sup>fi</sup>rms (Luca and Zervas, 2016).

This study focuses on negative public reviews or evaluations of an enterprise on social media following a crisis, Scholars note that “reputation is rooted in the historical behavior and associations of organizations but can be abruptly changed if new information about the past behavior comes to light, or if the latest behaviors or associations are jarring to observers” (Lange et al., 2011, p. 154). Particularly, major events such as natural disasters, hostile takeovers, technological breakdowns, product recalls, customer assault, mergers, and acquisitions among others change the perceptions of the core traits of an enterprise (Elsbach and Kramer, 1996; Kovoor-Misra, 2009). In crisis communication research, such disturbing events are referred to as crisis and is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as any unusual event that evokes a sense of threat to the high priority goals, image, legitimacy, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, or survival of an enterprise (Reynolds and Seeger, 2005).

In this study, we analyze the HD data breach situation from a crisis perspective. A data breach not only puts customer information at risk but also shakes the customer con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the organization’s ability to protect their information. Prior research also notes the impact of data breaches on business reputation and customer trust and calls for more research to examine the intangible e<sup>f</sup>ects of data breaches (Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Kannan et al., 2007). Recently, Gwebu et al. (2018) found that data breach announcement can have a negative impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation is a valuable asset to protect a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s market value. The crisis response strategies of high and low reputed <sup>fi</sup>rms are noted to have a mixed e<sup>f</sup>ect on <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation. As discussed in the followings sections, researchers have also begun to analyze the role of traditional media and social media to in<sup>fl</sup>uence enterprise reputation in the aftermath of a data breach (Romanosky et al., 2011; Syed and Dhillon, 2015). The need to protect enterprise reputation in the wake of a data breach is a cybersecurity issue. As Von Solms and Van Niekerk (2013) argue, cybersecurity is more than just protecting personal or organizational information; it also applies to the protection of intangible assets relating to the wellbeing of individuals, organization, and society at large.

## Social media and organizations

Social media is a pervasive mechanism to distill interactions among people (Asur and Huberman, 2010). IS scholars have increasingly begun researching various facets of social media in the organizational context (Berger et al., 2014; Leonardi and Vaast, 2017). In this section, we review three streams of social media literature that are relevant to this study. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream relates to the <sup>di</sup>f<sup>usion of WOM on social media</sup>. In the extant literature, WOM is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an interpersonal communication about an organization or its products or services that is independent of the organization’s marketing activities (Bone, 1995). While the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of WOM in general has been well researched (Cheung and Thadani. 2012: East et al. 2008), the electronic WOM generated on social media sites has gained signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in recent years. Also labeled as “information di<sup>f</sup>usion” research, this body of literature has examined the impact of both network structure and content characteristics on sharing information on social media (Shi et al., 2014; Sutton et al., 2015).

Research notes that social media a<sup>f</sup>ordances allow information communication, collaboration, and knowledge sharing within organizations (see Leonardi and Vaast, 2017). Particularly, businesses are noted to leverage electronic WOM at several levels of value chain spanning across research and development, marketing and sales, customer service and support, human resources, and internal communication (Heidemann et al., 2012). Although the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of social media based WOM has been well researched, the negative e<sup>f</sup>ects on businesses have not received much attention (Benthaus et al., 2016; Berger et al., 2014). Few studies question the accuracy of social media content (Vieweg et al., 2010) and refer to social media as rumor mills (Oh et al., 2013). Social media allows interested parties to manipulate information such as praising own products or bad-mouthing competitors for the strategic purpose (Dellarocas, 2006). Also referred to as “online <sup>fi</sup>restorms,” intense nWOM containing indignation, criticism, or complaints could be detrimental to businesses and presents an opportune research avenue (Berger et al., 2014; Drasch et al., 2015; Pfe<sup>f</sup>er et al., 2014). Further, while both rumor and nWOM represent a belief that is passed from person to person, nWOM have three di<sup>f</sup>erentiating characteristics (see Pfe<sup>f</sup>er et al., 2014): (1) rumor represents uncon<sup>fi</sup>rmed information whereas nWOM can be related to uncon<sup>fi</sup>rmed rumors or con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed events; (2) nWOM are intended to be o<sup>f</sup>ensive; (3) nWOM are predominantly a<sup>f</sup>ective opinions.

The second research stream relates to <sup>enterprise reputation on social media</sup>. Social media is increasingly used by the public to produce and disseminate their narratives about enterprises (Floreddu et al., 2014; Vermeer et al., 2017). Prior research notes that information shared about enterprises can have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on consumer’s brand evaluation and purchasing decisions (Lee et al., 2008). However, the impact is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant when the information contains less favorable evaluations about the enterprise, products or services (Baumeister et al., 2001; Duan et al., 2008). Particularly, the negative opinions created on social media can cause risk of reputation damage to enterprises as users can actively participate to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions and evaluations about the enterprise (Aula, 2010). Consequently, scholars call for more research on strategies to engage on social media for enterprise reputation management (Benthaus et al., 2016; Floreddu et al., 2014; Vermeer et al., 2017).

Recently, Syed and Dhillon (2015) conducted the content analysis of social media postings to explore enterprise reputation threats in the aftermath of a data breach. The authors identify information security dimensions that social media users put to question. Prior research also explores the use of social media management tools to in<sup>fl</sup>uence public perception of an enterprise (Benthaus et al., 2016). The tools can provide additional insights about social media users, which can improve an employee’s ability to engage with the users for impression management purpose. Another study proposes a simulation model for managing public con<sup>fl</sup>ict and <sup>fi</sup>restorms in social media communities (Hauser et al., 2017). The authors note that e<sup>f</sup>ective con<sup>fl</sup>ict management will require consideration of both individual-level and community-level characteristics.

Finally, the third research stream relates to <sup>crisis communication on social media</sup>. In recent years, social media has emerged as a popular medium for o<sup>f</sup>ering situational awareness during crisis situations. Especially in the context of real-world disasters or emergencies, social media has played a critical role in disseminating real-time information and supporting decision-making. For instance, one study analyzed the use of online communication technologies by a group of musicians in the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina (Shklovski et al., 2010). This study notes that musicians creatively adapted the technology by creating online spaces to <sup>fi</sup>nd and disseminate the information within their community. The online communities were a primary source of information and emotional support. Another study analyzes the role of social media in collective problem-solving (Vieweg et al., 2008). The authors found that the decentralized civic participation on social media played an important role in disaster response in the aftermath of the Virginia Tech shootings. In another study, authors analyze information di<sup>f</sup>usion during the 2011 Egyptian protests (Starbird et al., 2012). This study found that Twitter facilitated the interaction between people who were active on the ground and those who were not, which eventually led the crowd to express solidarity and process information in a politically sensitive context. Finally, Sutton et al. (2015) explored information di<sup>f</sup>usion during the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing. The authors found that message transmission on Twitter was jointly a<sup>f</sup>ected by the message content, messaging style, and senders characteristics.

Recently IS scholars have also begun to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of social media during crisis situations. Each of these studies shows the importance of social media as a new communication platform for crisis and emergency communication. By levering publics collective intelligence, emergency response teams can better understand the situation and make best recovery decisions (Yin et al., 2012). For instance, Oh et al. (2013) explore rumor propagation on Twitter during social crises situations. The authors found that source ambiguity, personal involvement, and anxiety as three important factors for generating rumors during the crisis. Nan and Lu (2014) studied organizational crisis management process informed by the decentralized and spontaneous discourse in an online community. The authors note that organizational order can arise unintentionally from spontaneous and seemingly disorganized actions and interactions of ordinary members. This new possibility, namely self-organization, can be particularly powerful in an online community where input from a large number of participants can be quickly harnessed and channeled toward an orderly crisis management process” (p. 1152). In another study, Leong et al. (2015) adopted an empowerment perspective to explore how socia media empowers communities during crisis situations. Using the 2011 Thailand <sup>fl</sup>ooding case, this study notes that social media empowers communities from structural, psychological, and resource perspectives. The empowerment dimensions allow a community to achieve shared identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, collective participation, and collaborative control. In another study, Pan et al. (2012) analyze the crisis response from information <sup>fl</sup>ow perspective. Based on the intensity of information <sup>fl</sup>ow and the density of the social network, the authors propose di<sup>f</sup>erent crisis information response networks. Finally, the challenge of extracting relevant information from social media in times of crisis has also been noted (Cheng et al., 2011). Cheng et al. propose a recommendation framework to receive information from a set of quality “news-reporters.”

In conclusion, the existing research has enhanced our understanding of reputation challenges faced by enterprises on socia media. However, there are two notable gaps.

(1) There is a limited understanding of how enterprise reputation threats in the aftermath of a data breach crisis manifest on socia media. Prior research notes the reputation damage faced by enterprises due to data breaches (Gwebu et al., 2018), especially if the public perceives that their self-interest is hurt (Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Kannan et al., 2007). Recent research has also explored the reputation threats perceived by businesses due to nWOM on social media (Aula, 2010; Benthaus et al., 2016), particularly in the wake of a data breach (Syed and Dhillon, 2015). The e<sup>f</sup>ect on enterprise reputation due to crisis framing on traditional media has also received some attention (Coombs, 2007; Romanosky et al., 2011). However, little research has focused on data breach framing on social media and the framing characteristics that signal threats to enterprise reputation.

(2) Second, the propagation of reputation-threatening content on social media has not been explored. Scholars note that crisis is a process that evolves over time (Pearson and Clair, 1998; Pearson et al., 2007; Sturges, 1994). Recent IS research has also explored the process-perspective of organizational crisis management on social media (Nan and Lu, 2014). However, the dynamics of evolving reputation threats on social media in the event of a data breach has not received attention.

We address the noted gaps by analyzing how the HD data breach is framed on social media. In doing so, this study provides a taxonomy of data breach frames and the associated framing characteristics that are perceived to threaten enterprise reputation. We then explore the crisis stages surfacing from our research context and the propagation of reputation threats through the crisis stages as the data breach unfolds on social media. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings could help crisis managers adequately prepare the data breach response for enterprise reputation management.

## Informing theories

In this section, we discuss the theoretical rationale underlying the two research objectives.

Situational crisis communication theory (SCCT)

Over the last several years, a large body of research in crisis communication has emerged. However, the most notable among these e<sup>f</sup>orts is the development of the situational crisis communication theory (SCCT) (Coombs, 2007). The SCCT extends attribution theory (Weiner. 1986) to examine how a crisis situation shapes the perceptions and behavior of individuals toward an organization (Coombs, 2007). The SCCT posits that unexpected and negative crisis lead people to search for its causes. If people perceive that their self-interest is hurt, they attribute the crisis-responsibility to the organization. Crisis responsibility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which stakeholders attribute damage caused by a crisis to the organization. An increased level of responsibility-attributions decreases the reputation score of organizations (Coombs, 2007).

Additionally, the SCCT posits that stronger attributions of crisis responsibility trigger stronger emotional responses from stakeholders such as increased anger and reduced feelings of sympathy towards the organization (Coombs, 2006, 2007; Coombs and Holladay, 2005). The stronger the negative emotions, the more likely people generate nWOM or take actions that damage organizational reputation. Further, while the original theory does not consider di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotions, other crisis scholars note that public experience di<sup>f</sup>erent types of emotions in response to a crisis (Jin et al., 2010; Kim and Cameron, 2011). For instance, in extending the SCCT, Jin et al. (2007) note four types of negative emotions anger, sadness, fright, and anxiety would be elicited by di<sup>f</sup>erent types of crises. Other empirical studies have also explored di<sup>f</sup>erent emotions on social media in response to a corporate crisis (Utz et al., 2013). Utz et al. note that users relay anger when an organization is regarded to expose stakeholders to the crisis intentionally. Further, anger increases secondary communication on Twitter retweeting of crisis messages. In another study, Jin et al. (2014) explore attribution-dependent (i.e., crisis initiated from internal organizational issue) and attribution-independent (i.e., crisis initiated from an issue external to the organization) emotional responses. The authors analyzed whether crisis origin triggers attribution-dependent emotions (such as anger, contempt, and disgust) or attribution-independent emotions (such as anxiety, apprehension, and fear). However, the authors report mixed results in testing the e<sup>f</sup>ect of crisis origin on crisis emotions and call for further research to explore attribution-(in)dependent emotions.

The SCCT also notes the role of media in framing a crisis. Media framing is based on the assumption that how an issue is characterized in news reports can have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on how it is understood by audiences” (Scheufele and Tewksbury, 2006, p. 11). Framing is achieved through the use of frames, that is, the organizing principles that are socially shared and persistent over time symbolically structuring the social world (Reese et al., 2001). Media communication involves two types of frames: communication frame words used to communicate the information, and cognitive frame an interpretation that an individual draws from information (Druckman, 2001). Media framing devices such as visual images, headlines, quotes, and catchphrases direct people to think about an issue in a particular way (Scheufele and Tewksbury, 2006; Zoch and Molleda, 2006). Prior research also notes that socia media framing shapes the de<sup>fi</sup>nition, causes, attributions, and solutions to the crisis (see Cooper, 2002). There is also some evidence about the e<sup>f</sup>ect of news framing on public emotions. For instance, research suggests that di<sup>f</sup>erent news frames related to a potentia threat elicit fear and anger among the audience (Nabi, 2003). Likewise, in the context of political communication, research suggests that emotional responses are contingent on how policy-related messages are framed (Gross and D’ambrosio, 2004).

While existing research has focused on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of news framing on individual’s cognitive and emotional processing (e.g., Kepplinger et al., 2012), crisis scholars call attention to the e<sup>f</sup>ect of crisis framing on enterprise reputation. Coombs (2007) notes that the framing e<sup>f</sup>ect occurs when a crisis communicator emphasizes speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors. The message recipients will then focus on those factors when forming opinions and judgments about the crisis. Crisis types are a form of frames, and each crisis type emphasizes on speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of the crisis and provide cues for how stakeholders should interpret the crisis. Coombs (2007) argues framing determines di<sup>f</sup>erent crisis types to which stakeholders attribute varying levels of responsibility. Based on responsibility-attributions,

Coombs and Holladay (2002) empirically analyzed 13 crisis types which were reduced to three frames or clusters: intentional or preventable, accidental, and victim.

The intentional or preventable frame has the strongest attributions, and the enterprise is regarded to expose people to risk knowingly, took inappropriate actions, or violated the law. This includes crises such as organizational misdeeds (stakeholders are deceived, placed at risk of injury, or laws and regulations are violated) and human error (employee negligence cause an accident or a product recall). The accidental frame has minimal attributions of crisis responsibility, and the enterprise actions leading to the crisis are considered unintentional or uncontrollable. This includes crises such as challenges (a disgruntled stakeholder claims that the organization is operating inappropriately or unethically), technical breakdowns (a technological failure causes damage), and product failure or malfunction (a technological failure cause a product to be recalled). The victim frame has very weak attributions of crisis responsibility, and the enterprise is considered a victim of the crisis. This includes crises such as natural disasters (act of nature such as earthquake damages an organization), rumors (false and damaging information about an organization is disseminated), workplace violence (current or former employee physically attacks another employee), and product tampering or malevolence (external entity damages or tampers product or services of an organization).

Informed by the aforementioned literature, the <sup>fi</sup>rst objective of this study is to analyze how enterprise reputation threat is framed on social media in the aftermath of a data breach. Using the HD data breach case, we argue that social media postings could provide cues whether the data breach was an intentional lapse of the enterprise or merely an accident or the enterprise was a victim of the data breach. While crisis of any type may manifest intentional, accidental, and victim frames, prior research suggests that information shared on social media varies from one crisis to another (Olteanu et al., 2015). Hence, it is prudent to assume that the sub-frames characterizing intentional, accidental, and victim data breach frames emerging from our research context would di<sup>f</sup>er from other types of crises such as product recalls or natural disasters. Further, informed by the SCCT, reputation threat is shaped by the amount of crisis responsibility attributed to an organization. The stronger attributions of crisis responsibility also trigger stronger negative emotional reactions. We argue that a higher volume of social media postings attributing data breach responsibility to an enterprise or relaying strong negative emotions pose a threat to enterprise reputation. However, as di<sup>f</sup>erent crisis frames evoke di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of reputation threat, we expect the varying levels of responsibility-attributions for each data breach frame. Further, we expect di<sup>f</sup>erent data breach frames would elicit di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotions. Hence we identify the taxonomy of data breach frames and subframes and the associated reputation threats as manifested by responsibility-attributions and negative emotional responses. ## Crisis stage theory

Enterprise crisis is a low-probability high impact event characterized by uncertainty and ambiguity and threatens the viability of the enterprise (Pearson and Clair, 1998). However, crisis is not a discrete event but rather a process (Bundy et al., 2016). Crisis communication scholars note that a crisis progresses through a series of stages, and each stage has its unique dynamics and dimensions (Sturges, 1994). We argue that post-crisis response for enterprise reputation management will require an orderly assessment of how responsibility-attributions and negative emotions progress on social media. Prior IS research also suggests an orderly and rational crisis management approach to recover and readjust after the crisis (Nan and Lu, 2014). This view is consistent with the SCCT that posits negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on organization reputation is reduced if the crisis response frames match the public attribution frames and emotional response (Coombs, 2007). Hence, the second objective of this study is to analyze how enterprise reputation threat evolves as a data breach crisis unfolds on social media. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we analyze whether the stages described by crisis management literature surface from our research context and how does data breach frames and the associated reputation threats evolve as the crisis stages unfold.

In this study, we explore one of the popular crisis stage theory forwarded by Fink (1986). Fink de<sup>fi</sup>nes crisis “as an unstable time or state of a<sup>f</sup>airs in which a decisive change is impending” (p. 15). A crisis “life cycle” comprises of four distinct stages. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage referred to as crisis build up or prodromal period is the period during which crisis signs start to emerge. These signs or precursors if recognized may help an enterprise avert the crisis or prevent negative outcomes of the crisis. Enterprises that remain vigilant to their environment, stakeholders, and important trends are better positioned to recognize the crisis long before the triggering event. The second stage is crisis breakout or acute crisis. During this period a triggering event erupts the crisis, and the damage may be in<sup>fl</sup>icted on the organization and its stakeholders. This is followed by the third stage referred to as chronic stage or abatement. The e<sup>f</sup>ect of this stage may be experienced for a more extended period depending on the severity of the crisis. In the case of a negative crisis, the continued coverage by media, charges and counter-charges, demonstrations, inquiries, and even legal actions may prolong the e<sup>f</sup>ect. Finally, the last stage referred to as termination concludes the crisis. During this stage, the <sup>fi</sup>nal resolution is sought, and the crisis is no longer a concern to the organization or its stakeholders.

In the extant literature, other crisis lifecycle models have also been proposed (Mitroff, 1994: Seeger. 2006). However. Fink's stage theory is one of the earliest, and there is considerable overlap between Fink s crisis stages and other crisis lifecycle models (Stewart and Wilson, 2016). Additionally, several recent studies have applied Fink’s stage theory to study the progression of crisis information on social media. For instance, Paraskevas (2006) applied Fink s theory to formulate an e<sup>f</sup>ective crisis management plan. This stud proposes a crisis response framework based on narrative analysis of how a particular crisis was manifested and how the organization responded. In another study, Sung and Hwang (2014) conducted a content analysis of a crisis event throughout each phase. This study compares crisis progression on social media and traditional media (newspapers and TV broadcasting) as well as other online newspapers and blogs. The authors found four distinct stages of crisis di<sup>f</sup>usion; however, the prodromal stage began with Twitter postings which instigated the crisis reporting on other media. Spence et al. (2015) also studied the variability of tweets across the crisis stages in case of a natural disaster. The authors found that the tweets were less informative and more a<sup>f</sup>ective during the time leading to the acute stage of the crisis, which makes <sup>fi</sup>nding relevant relief information di<sup>fi</sup>cult. Another study proposes a crisis management model based on the social media activity that transcended during the four crisis stages (Stewart and Wilson, 2016). However, this model is limited to response and recovery during natural disasters.

## Research design

## Case description and data collection

Home Depot Inc. (HD) is one of the largest US-based retailing company that deals with home improvement supplies including tools, construction products, and services. On Sept. 8, 2014, HD released a statement that their payment card systems were breached.<sup>5</sup> HD explained that the investigations started on Sept. 2 and the investigators were trying to assess the scope and impact of the breach. The company also explained that they would be o<sup>f</sup>ering free credit monitoring services to the a<sup>f</sup>ected customers. The HD breach is an interesting case to analyze reputation threats for two reasons. First, HD breach topped the Target data breach that was considered the largest retail chain breach in the US history. While Target breach exposed 40 million payment card information, the HD breach exposed 56 million payment cards. Second, as some security analysts argue, HD and other retailing companies who got breached after Target breach should have used the Target data breach as a learning opportunity and applied the knowledge to protect their payment card systems (Hawkins, 2015).

To evaluate the public opinions about the HD breach and the emergent reputation threats, we focused on Twitter, a microblogging site. The e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of Twitter in disseminating information in times of disasters has led to its ubiquitous use in crisis management (Syed and Dhillon, 2015; Vieweg et al., 2010). It is increasingly being used both as a communication tool during emergency situations and as a medium to collect information about crises. Twitter has several features that make it indispensable for electronic WOM communication (Jansen et al., 2009). Users create a social network by “following” other users, or by allowing other users to “follow” them. The “follower-followee” network allows information on social networks to spread beyond geographic borders. Further, the short length of tweets makes them easier to consume and re(produce). Messages can be asynchronously noninvasive as individuals can decide whom to receive tweets from and when to check their tweets. Tweets are also archived and are searchable by web engines.

We collected the data using Twitter API immediately after the news broke. The data was collected from Sept. 8, 2014, to Sept. 30, 2014. A total of 39,416 tweets published on Twitter s public message board were collected using the twitteR package available in the R library (Gentry. 2012). We searched for relevant tweets using hashtags, e.g.. #HDBreach. #HDdatabreach. We subiected the raw data corpus to data cleansing process to assure reasonable quality. The cleansing process allowed us to rule out duplicate and irrelevant tweets, which reduced the <sup>fi</sup>nal number of tweets to 15,550. We also performed basic transformation operations for tweet text such as converting text to lower or upper case, deleting numbers, stripping whitespaces, removing stopwords, and stemming.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research objective is to identify the data breach frames and the related reputation threats as manifested by responsibilityattributions and negative emotions. Hence, we undertook data coding of tweets to characterize: (1) data breach frames and subframes; (2) whether a tweet attributes data breach responsibility to HD; (3) the emotional valence of tweets. We discuss our approach for each coding tasks in the following subsections. Fig. 1 illustrates our overall research approach.

## Identifying data breach frames and sub-frames

To overcome the challenges due to the conversational nature of tweets and the lack of context-sensitive linguistic tools, we employed a semi-automated approach for coding data breach frames (Gimpel et al., 2011). We applied a text-mining technique known as probabilistic topic modeling to identify the thematic structure of the entire set of tweets. The probabilistic modeling techniques are statistical approaches to discover and annotate words around a thematic in text documents (Blei, 2012). Topic modeling does not require analysts to pre-annotate the text. This technique identi<sup>fi</sup>es the themes, referred to as topics that emerge from the text. Topic modeling generates semantically coherent and interpretable topics by enumerating the most likely words corresponding to each topic. Topic modeling techniques are also well designed to account for synonymy (i.e., words with a similar meaning) and polysemy (i.e., words with multiple meanings) (Mimno and McCallum, 2007).

In this study, we applied the Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling technique to identify sub-frames that complement the intentional, accidental, and victim frames (Blei, 2012). The LDA has many advantages over other topic modeling techniques and is widely used to leverage the value from unstructured information (Uys et al., 2008). Moreover, the LDA is known to IS researchers and has also been applied to analyze tweets (Debortoli et al., 2016: Sved and Dhillon. 2015). We used the R package topicmodels. which is available in the R library, for <sup>fi</sup>tting topics (Hornik and Grün, 2011). The LDA treats each tweet as a single document (Zhao et al., 2011) and assumes that the number of topics to be <sup>fi</sup>xed based on concrete inference of data (Blei, 2012).

We ran the algorithm multiple times by varying the number of topics to be generated (e.g., 15, 25, 50, 75, and 100 topics). However, we selected the model with 18 topics because of higher discriminant power and easier interpretability (Uys et al., 2008). Next, we systematically analyzed the words under each topic and assigned the topic label to reflect our interpretation of their meaning. We then grouped the topics by common higher-level theme, referred to as a topic category. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal synthesis seven topic categories or sub-frames emerged, which were then interpretively mapped to the victim, accidental, and intentional data breach

## Annotation of data breach responsibility-attributions

Next, we were interested in coding whether a tweet attributes data breach responsibility to HD. We used the topic modeling results to identify the keywords implying data breach responsibility-attributions. The threshold for the least frequent terms was set to 50, i.e., a word occurs at least 50 times in the tweet corpus. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 308 attribution terms. We also examined the remaining 465 infrequent terms that appear below the threshold in our dataset. Upon close examination of the infrequent terms, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 13 terms that attribute the data breach responsibility to HD. We then annotated the tweet posting as ‘1’ if a tweet contains the attribution word(s) otherwise the tweet is annotated as '0' We recruited two research assistants who manually annotated 1200 tweets to evaluate the accuracy of the annotation scheme. We had a strong agreement of κ = 0.87 between manual and keyword-based annotations.

## Annotation of emotional valence

In the literature several dimensional models for emotional analysis have been proposed (Plutchik, 1980; Russell, 2003). Dimensional models suggest that emotions are best understood, most commonly, in a two-dimensional space consisting of arousal and valence. Emotional arousal refers to the extent to which an emotion is exciting or calming. Emotional valence refers to the extent to which an emotion is positive or negative (see Bradley et al., 1992; Russell, 1980). While most models assume arousal and valence as distinct dimensions, research suggests that valence a<sup>f</sup>ects arousal. For instance, Bradley and Lang (1999) note that the highly positive and highly negative stimuli tend to be more arousing. In this study, we analyze the emotional valence of tweets.

In recent years, several algorithmic approaches referred to as sentiment analysis have been used to detect emotional valence of tweets (Giachanou and Crestani, 2016). One of the approaches is lexicon based, in which tweet sentiment is calculated by counting the number of positive and negative words. For instance, Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan (2013) used a lexicon approach to measure overall sentiment in politically oriented messages. Few studies also consider hashtags for emotion detection in tweets (Mohammad and Kiritchenko, 2015). Another approach is machine-learning based which classi<sup>fi</sup>es the tweets based on di<sup>f</sup>erent features including terms, emoticons, and smileys (Pang et al., 2002; Sintsova and Pu, 2016); however, the accuracy of the classi<sup>fi</sup>ers is very low (Giachanou and Crestani, 2016).

In this study, we follow a two-stage process for annotating emotional valence. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we applied Je<sup>f</sup>rey Breen s (2012) lexicon-based approach for sentiment analysis. This approach is suitable to overcome the challenges of understanding domainspeci<sup>fi</sup>c sentiment (Liu, 2012), particularly in tweets (Chung and Liu, 2011). Following Breen, we enhanced the English opinion lexicon<sup>6</sup> with the domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c words that were previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed using topic modeling results. The words were added to the positive and negative word lists. We calculated the sentiment score for a tweet as the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the number of positive words and the number of negative words. If the sentiment score is > 0, then the tweet expresses an overall positive emotion; if the sentiment score is < 0, then the tweet expresses negative emotion; if the sentiment score = 0, then the tweet has neutral emotion. To validate the accuracy of our approach, two research assistants independently coded 1500 tweets using Breen’s approach. The intercoder agreement between the manual and algorithmic coding was quite high (Cohen’s κ = 0.92).

In the second stage, we further analyzed the negative valence tweets to detect the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotional a<sup>f</sup>ect. The use of a<sup>f</sup>ect analysis in other security domains such as extremist web forums and blogs (Abbasi, 2007) as well as crisis-related tweets has been proposed (Brynielsson et al., 2013). Particularly, Brynielsson et al. (2013) applied a machine learning approach for classifying crisis-related tweets into four types of emotions: positive, fear, anger and other. However, this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is limited to natural disasters, and the authors call for more generic crisis-related classi<sup>fi</sup>ers. Further, the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation results cannot be trusted at individual postings but represent more of aggregated citizen level a<sup>f</sup>ect. Given the lack of emotional lexicons speci<sup>fi</sup>c to data breach crisis, we adopted a manual annotation approach to maximize the annotation accuracy. Prior research also recommends manua annotation as online forums are context driven which can be difficult to interpret without manual intervention (Abbasi. 2007). We adopted Roberts et al. (2012) emotion ontology, which is suitable to analyze emotions in tweets. The emotion ontology consists of Ekman six basic emotions (joy, anger, fear, sadness, surprise, disgust) (Ekman, 1992) and an additional emotion love speci<sup>fi</sup>cally found in tweets. Given the focus of this study, our annotation task focused on four types of negative emotions: anger, disgust, sadness, and fear.

Following Roberts et al. (2012), we recruited two research assistants who were provided the guidelines to increase agreement. Annotators were asked to categorize a tweet into one of the four negative emotions. The annotation was completed in three phases. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase, the two assistants annotated 50 random tweets to arrive at a general agreement on the annotation standard. In the second phase, each assistant coded the same set of 100 tweets. The disagreement was measured (=0.69) and resolved. Next, the assistants coded another set of 50 tweets and a more reasonable agreement was achieved (=0.87). In the <sup>fi</sup>nal phase, the two assistants independently annotated the remaining set of negative tweets with an overlap of 2% of tweets. The agreement for the overlapping set of tweets was 0.91.

## Empirical analysis

To empirically test the association of data breach frames with responsibility-attributions and emotional valence, we performed a chi-square test of independence to determine the “goodness to <sup>fi</sup>t” between observed and expected data. The chi-square test of independence is used to compare whether the proportions of one nominal variable varies for the different values of another nominal variable. The chi-square test is appropriate for large sample sizes. We computed the “goodness to <sup>fi</sup>t” between observed and expected values both for responsibility-attributions and negative emotions. Further, we computed the goodness to <sup>fi</sup>t for four types of negative emotions. Although larger chi-square values provide a reliable indicator of the strength of the relationship between the variables, caution should be exercised when comparing chi-square statistics and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for tables with varying sample sizes. The sample size for the crisis frames vary in our dataset (see Table D1 in the appendix). Hence, we calculated the phi (ф) to adjust the chisquare statistic by the sample size for intentional, accidental, and victim frames. Further, the varying number of sub-frames makes the direct comparison of chi-square less reliable. Hence, we calculated Cramer’s V to account for the varying number of rows and columns between the sub-frames. Cramer's V equals 0 when there is no relationship between two variables and has a maximum value of 1.

Our second objective is to determine whether the crisis stages emerge from our research context and how responsibility-attributions and negative emotions progress as the crisis stages evolve. We employed temporal bracketing strategy to develop qualitative interpretations of the crisis stages emerging from our data (Langley, 1999). Consistent with prior research (Nan and Lu, 2014), we relied on the tweets for temporal bracketing as the salient frames embedded in the content indicate the dominant opinion. As Langley (1999) notes “the decomposition of data into successive adjacent periods enables the explicit examination of how actions of oneperiod lead to changes in the context that will a<sup>f</sup>ect the action is subsequent periods (p. 703). Further, we conducted a time series analysis to supplement our qualitative analysis. Following prior research, we performed several commonly used statistical tests to ensure adequacy of time series modeling of Twitter data as described in the appendix C (see Bollen et al., 2011; Rao and Srivastava, 2012).

We applied Chow test (1960) to verify the crisis stages identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by temporal bracketing. Chow test also allowed us to determine whether data breach frames and the associated responsibility-attributions and negative emotions signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>er between the two sets of data associated with different crisis stages. Chow test relies on E-test to determine whether the effect of independent variables is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent between two slices of time series data. Our slices of time series data correspond to the crisis stages as manifested by tweets related to the HD data breach. We logged the time series data hourly. Following Nan and Lu (2014), the hour when the data breach was o<sup>fi</sup>cially announced was chosen as a breakpoint between the pre-data breach and post-data breach periods. For the remaining stages, we <sup>fi</sup>rst bracketed the data according to the qualitative content dynamics. Next, we used a trialand-error approach to identify the break hours between the buildup and breakout stages (6 AM on Sept. 11), between the breakout and chronic stages (3 PM Sept. 17), and between the chronic and termination stages (11 AM on Sept. 24).

The dependent variable, responsibility-attributions, is the number of tweets attributing data breach responsibility among all posts. The independent variables include the number of posts associated with di<sup>f</sup>erent data breach frames in the previous hour as well as the percentage of responsibility-attribution in the previous hour. We also included the number of tweets for four types of negative emotions in the previous hour as independent variables to assess the impact on generating reputation-threatening content. We created <sup>fi</sup>ve dummy variables corresponding to <sup>fi</sup>ve stages: pre-data breach, buildup, breakout, chronic, and termination. Finally, we generated the interaction terms of each dummy stage variable and the independent and dependent variables. We conducted four regression models, and each model used data from two adjacent stages. For each regression model, we conducted F-test on the coe<sup>fi</sup>cients of interaction terms and the phase dummy variables to validate the breaks between the crisis stages.

To gain further insights, we conducted a time-series analysis of the immediate impact of reputation-threatening content on subsequent responsibility-attributions and negative emotions. We used only the tweets collected immediately after the announcement of the data breach. We eliminated the retweets from this analysis which reduced the number of tweets to 7643. The data is lagged hourly, leading to 278 observations. We conducted two regression analysis. We <sup>fi</sup>rst regressed the number of tweets attributing data breach responsibility on the number of tweets attributing responsibility in the previous hour. We included four types of negative emotions as independent variables. Next, we regressed the number of tweets with negative emotions on the number of negative emotional tweets in the previous hour. We excluded four types of negative emotions from this analysis due to the correlation with negative emotion variable. Finally, for both analyses, we controlled for data breach frames and stage dummies.

## Data breach framing and reputation threats

The topic model discriminates among 18 topics. As part of our analysis, 18 topics were clustered into 7 categories or sub-frames which were then interpretively mapped to the intentional, accidental, and victim frames (see Table B1 in the appendix). The <sup>intentional</sup> frames indicate that HD intentionally exposed the sensitive customer information due to the enterprise’s misdeeds or inappropriate actions. While 49% of tweets relate to intentional frame, 56% of tweets attribute data breach responsibility to HD, and 52% of tweets express strong negative emotions. The most common negative emotional response was anger (65%) followed by disgust (45%), sadness (28%), and fear (16%). The <sup>accidental</sup> frames portray that the data breach was unintentional and that HD had no motive or intent to act inappropriately or harm its customers. The <sup>victim</sup> frames portray HD as a victim of the data breach. In comparison to accidental and intentional frames, a smaller number of tweets (11%) relate to victim frames. Further, 8% of tweets attribute data breach responsibility to HD, and 14% of tweets express strong negative emotions. The most common negative emotional response was disgust (39%) followed by anger (27%), sadness (11%), and fear (9%). Our content analysis identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two sub-frames corresponding to victim frames: (1) External threats: Most of the tweets in victim category note that the data breach is caused by external entities to gain competitive advantage: “Don't be ridiculous. There are people out there who do nothing but dream up how to hack into accounts. HD and Target are victims just as the customer is.”; “Unfortunately, we have some very intelligent individuals out there that are very scrupulous and don't care whom they hurt.” (2) Insider threats: The postings indicate that the HD breach originated from insiders such as employees with malicious intent and operating from a position of trust and authorized access: “Makes me think another level of security defense is to keep your employees happy!”; Unfortunately it only takes one and there s always someone who s disgruntled.

## Discussion

We now discuss the implications of this study on theory and practice. We also discuss the limitations of this study and future research opportunities.

## Theoretical contributions

This study makes several contributions to theory. First, this study o<sup>f</sup>ers a conceptual clarity about enterprise reputation and reputation threats on social media in the context of a data breach. Building on crisis communication and social media literature, we conceptualized enterprise reputation is manifested in how social media users evaluate the ability of an enterprise to prevent or manage a data breach (Wartick. 1992). Further, we conceptualized enterprise reputation threats as the discharge of a large volume of social media postings containing intense indignation, criticism, or complaints against an enterprise that faced the data breach (Pfe<sup>f</sup>er et al., 2014). We operationalized reputation threat as tweets attributing data breach responsibility to the enterprise or relaying strong negative emotions (Coombs. 2007). In doing so, this study extends prior research on data breach and enterprise reputation that limits reputation conceptualization to the publicized corporate reputation rankings such as Fortune’s AMAC (Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Gwebu et al., 2018; Kannan et al., 2007). We believe that our conceptualizations will be useful in guiding future research on enterprise reputation management. For instance, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of enterprise reputation threats due to social media framing of a crisis on the publicized reputation ranks o<sup>f</sup>ers an interesting research opportunity. Further, in line with recent research that explores the impact of crisis response strategies on publicized corporate reputation ranks (Gwebu et al., 2018), our conceptualization sets the ground for analyzing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of crisis response strategy on social media reputation as well as the impact on publicized corporate reputation ranks.

Second, this study contributes by providing a taxonomy of data breach frames and sub-frames. In the extant literature, crisis scholars have examined the role of media framing and public evaluations on enterprise reputation, but social media framing of enterprise reputation in the aftermath of a crisis has received little attention (Bundy et al., 2016; Coombs, 2007; Romanosky et al., 2011). Our analysis suggests that social media users framed HD breach as intentional, accidental, as well as victim crisis, Our findings extend the original crisis framing theory that maps 13 types of crises to intentional, accidental, and victim frames (Coombs and Holladay, 2002). For instance, the authors classify organizational misdeeds as intentional crisis, technological failure as accidenta crisis, and earthquake as victim crisis. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation suggests that a crisis can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into one of three frames, which could be perhaps the case with traditional media framing. However, traditional media frames are challenged by public evaluations of the crisis on social media (van der Meer and Verhoeven, 2013). Social media users can create, receive, and forward their multiple perspectives of a crisis situations, often bypassing mainstream media (Oh et al., 2013). The framing taxonomy has implications for enterprise crisis and reputation management. For instance, unlike traditional crisis response that adopts selective strategies to address a speci<sup>fi</sup>c crisis framing situation (Coombs and Holladay, 2002; Gwebu et al., 2018), enterprises might have to apply a mix of response strategies on social media to address di<sup>f</sup>erent types of framings for the same crisis.

Additionally, using topic modeling technique, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the sub-frames to understand the characterization of social media framing further. Intentional frames suggest that the data breach resulted due to an ethical misdemeanor, poor controls, and weak governance structure. Accidental frames characterize that the data breach resulted due to system vulnerability and human manipulation. Finally, victim frames indicate that the data breach resulted due to internal and external threats. Contrary to Coombs and Holladay (2002) who classi<sup>fi</sup>ed technological error as accidental crisis, our analysis suggests that technical error can be both intentional and accidental as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the sub-frames poor controls and system vulnerability. For instance, an outdated security patch that leads to system exploit is an intentional crisis whereas an unknown software vulnerability exploited by a hacker can be framed as an accidental crisis. Further. Coombs and Holladay (2002) classify human error as intentional crisis: however, our analysis suggests humans can be responsible for both accidental and intentional data breaches. For instance, weak governance and ethica misdemeanor of employees could be the reasons for intentional data breach, whereas human manipulation, for instance using socia engineering or phishing attacks, suggests that a data breach could be accidental. In this way, this study enriches extant literature on enterprise reputation by drawing attention to the multi-dimensional framing of a data breach crisis that goes beyond just the technological failure.

Third, this study extends the SCCT by analyzing di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotional responses. The SCCT in its original form does not consider the speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of negative emotions that public experience in the aftermath of a crisis (Coombs, 2006, 2007). Other crisis scholars also note that public experience a range of negative emotions depending on how a crisis is framed (Jin et al., 2007). Further research suggests that crisis-origin, that is whether an enterprise is responsible for the crisis, triggers di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotions on social media (Brynielsson et al., 2013; Jin et al., 2014). In this study, we adopted emotion ontology proposed by Roberts et al. (2012), which is suitable for the emotional analysis of tweets. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we analyzed four types of negative emotions anger, disgust, sadness, and fear. In doing so, we also contribute to data breach literature. While emotional a<sup>f</sup>ect has been analyzed in other security domains such as extremist web forums and blogs (Abbasi, 2007), the emotional a<sup>f</sup>ect in case of a data breach has not been explored.

We found anger, disgust, and sadness signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for all three crisis frames, whereas fear was a not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant emotion (see Table 1). Prior research notes that anger and disgust are attribution-dependent emotions (Jin et al., 2014), which indicates that the public perceives the HD data breach to be initiated from internal issues. Further, accidental data breach frames had more anger, disgust, and sadness than intentional and victim frames (see ф values in Table 1). Anger was the dominant emotion in intentional and victim frames whereas disgust was the dominant emotion for accidental frames. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with prior research that notes that users relay anger when an organization is considered to intentionally expose stakeholders to the crisis (Utz et al., 2013). This further suggests that public perceives more harm in case of intentional and victim breaches and more moral violations in case of accidental breaches (Ekman, 1992; Roberts et al., 2012). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications on the extant literature that concludes public engages on social media to seek emotional support for coping with negative emotions during crisis (e.g., Choi and Lin, 2009; Jin et al., 2010). Future research could, therefore, explore the links between initial public evaluations, corporate crisis response, and public emotional response to cope with a data breach.

Fourth, we extend the SCCT by considering the behavioral impact of reputation threats. Crisis communication scholars like Coombs who pioneered the SCCT has focused exclusively on reputation threats perceived due to public opinions, ignoring the behavioral aspect of spreading reputation-threatening content (Bundy et al., 2016: Coombs. 2007). In this study, we analyzed the immediate impact of reputation-threatening content (that is tweets that attribute data breach responsibility to the enterprise or relay negative emotions) on the behavior of social media users as they further generate reputation-threatening content. We found that responsibility-attribution tweets increase subsequent responsibility-attributions, and negative emotion tweets increase subsequent negative emotions (see Table 3). Further, while intentional and accidental frames increase subsequent responsibility-attributions and negative emotions, intentional frames lead to more reputation threats than accidental frames. Additionally, anger and disgust increase subsequent responsibility-attributions. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the more an enterprise is framed to be responsible for a data breach, the more social media users generate reputation-threatening content. Additionally, the more social media users relay anger and disgust, the more likely users will engage in generating reputation-threatening content. In light of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, it will be interesting to analyze the relation between crisis response strategy and public behavior.

Finally, this study contributes by providing insights into the unique pattern of reputation threats across the crisis stages as data breach situation evolves on social media. Prior studies report the prevalence of crisis stages on social media and the variability of the content across crisis stages (Nan and Lu, 2014; Spence et al., 2015; Sung and Hwang, 2014). However, the variability of reputation threats across crisis stages has not received attention. Consistent with prior research, we found four distinct stages of crisis di<sup>f</sup>usion in the event of a data breach (Spence et al., 2015). While intentional and accidental frames were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant before and after the breach, accidental frames were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant during all crisis stages. The responsibility-attributions and negative emotions were also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant before and after the data breach (see Table 2). Further, all four types of negative emotions were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant before and after the data breach. We also noticed responsibility-attributions and disgust were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant during the buildup and breakout stages whereas anger was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant during the breakout and chronic stages. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that reputation management may require enterprises to engage with the public as soon as the crisis builds up on social media. Examining speci<sup>fi</sup>c data breach response strategies at various crisis stages to contain further propagation of reputation-threatening content o<sup>f</sup>ers another interesting research opportunity.

## Practical implications

At the practical level, our study has implications for reputation management that require evidence-based guidance to facilitate post-crisis communication and decision-making (Coombs, 2007). While the initial data breach response should instruct information about what the a<sup>f</sup>ected public needs or wants to know, the crisis response should also mitigate any potential reputation threats. Research suggests that negative e<sup>f</sup>ect on enterprise reputation is reduced if the crisis response frames match the public attribution frames and emotional responses (Coombs, 2007). Our study explicates the constituent data breach frames and sub-frames, and the associated reputation-threatening characteristics. In light of the insights o<sup>f</sup>ered by our study, framing a data breach as a victim could lead to lower reputation threats than framing the data breach as an accidental or intentional crisis. Hence, data breach response can focus on the victim sub-frames to reinforce social media user's opinions in favor of the enterprise. Further, increased anger and disgust suggest that the enterprise is attributed to be responsible for the breach. Hence, crisis managers can develop responses focusing on relief and well-being of consumers to reduce anger and disgust (Kim and Cameron, 2011).

Our analysis also suggests that public opinions are most malleable at the crisis build-up stage and become highly sensitive as the information is shared and the public perceives to involve self-interest. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we found that the HD data breach was perceived as accidental crisis at the breakout stage. Pro and con factions formed immediately as the crisis started to build up. This is indicated by di<sup>f</sup>erent frames that emerged from our research context and the associated responsibility-attributions and emotional valence of tweet postings. As time lapses, a dominant public opinion that most of the people are willing to adopt forms. However, the public opinion may not represent the majority opinion; it could be a vocal negative opinion (Sturges, 1994). It is only with the passage of time and the absence of enterprise intervention to reinforce opinions in their favor that the public opinion begins to form su<sup>fi</sup>ciently to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the behavior of others towards the enterprise. This is indicative by the subsequent generation of reputation-threatening content in our research context. Hence, crisis managers need to intervene at the buildup stage to develop the favorable opinions in the aftermath of a data breach. Further, in the case of intentional and accidental framing of a data breach, it can be stated that enterprises need to act more vividly to reduce responsibility-attributions and negative emotions.

Finally, the insights o<sup>f</sup>ered by this study might bene<sup>fi</sup>t enterprises to improve their reputation scores. Consistent with prior research (Syed and Dhillon, 2015), we contend that social media provides an alternate means to assess the public perception of a company, particularly in the aftermath of a data breach. The publicized reputation ranks such as Fortune AMAC, are based on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of companies and do not consider the public perception (Deephouse, 2000). These weaknesses are well re-<sup>fl</sup>ected by HD’s Fortune ratings. Despite the negative press and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant drop in the pro<sup>fi</sup>t, HD, after its massive data breach in 2014, maintained the reputation score of 33, which was the same as that of 2013.<sup>7</sup> While other surveys such as Harris-Fombrun Reputation Quotient consider factors such as emotional appeal and perceptions of social responsibility of enterprise among the general public, the ratings are based on interview and survey data (Schwaiger, 2004). Hence, social media not only provides an alternate means to assess enterprise reputation based on public evaluations but also provides an opportunity for enterprises to strategically engage with the public to manage the enterprise perceptions. Although the empirical evidence of the impact of managing social media perceptions on the publicized reputation rank is yet to be established, crisis managers might <sup>fi</sup>nd the insights provided by this study helpful to frame adequate crisis response for enhancing positive enterprise reputation

## Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations, which give directions for future research. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on a single data breach at a large US-based retail enterprise, While crises of all types manifest intentional. accidental, and victim frames (Coombs. 2007). research suggests that information shared on social media varies from one crisis to another (Olteanu et al., 2015). Further, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings could vary by data breach type (small vs. big), or enterprise type (small vs. big, private vs. Govt., pro<sup>fi</sup>t or non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t). Nevertheless, we expect the framing taxonomy to emerge from other data breach incidents; however, the number of postings for each sub-frame may vary depending on how strongly public believes that the breach was intentional, accidental, or victim. Also, the results reported in this study are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the Twitter dataset. However, other social media sites may entail di<sup>f</sup>erent public reaction. Future research, therefore, could extend the research to other types of enterprises, social media sites, and crisis incidents to increase the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the data was collected over a period of three weeks. Although the discussion on Twitter started to decline, the crisis was not completely resolved. Future research can engage in longitudinal studies to understand the long-term e<sup>f</sup>ect

Another limitation is the use of an automated approach for identifying data breach framing taxonomy. Prior research notes that the LDA algorithm may not work well due to the short length of tweets (Zhao et al., 2011). To overcome this limitation, we followed the recommendations from previous studies and aggregated all the tweets before running the LDA (Weng et al., 2010). Although this treatment does not consider a single tweet as a topic (Zhao et al., 2011), the aggregation technique performs well for the purpose of this study. Additionally, while we followed qualitative research principles to map the sub-frames to intentional, accidental, and victim frames, the interpretive analysis could be biased due to researchers familiarity with the literature and data (Klein and Myers, 1999). Finally, this study does not analyze the emotional arousal, which presents another interesting dimension to understand the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the crisis. Future studies could also explore the relationship between emotional arousal and valence and the impact on enterprise reputation.

This study does not analyze the enterprise response to a data breach. An interesting research opportunity is to analyze the impact of data breach response strategy during di<sup>f</sup>erent crisis stages. Further, understanding the impact of di<sup>f</sup>erent crisis response strategies such as apology or denial on the subsequent generation of reputation-threatening content will help enterprises formulate an adequate social media response. Additionally, future research could explore whether the nWOM related to sub-frames a<sup>f</sup>ect user behavior towards the enterprise such as purchasing intentions or boycotting the enterprise. Finally, this study is limited to the content characteristics for understanding the reputation threats. Future research could also analyze social media user characteristics on the generation of reputation-threatening content.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, the essence of post-crisis reputation management is to know what is being said about an enterprise on social media. In this study, we analyzed enterprise reputation threats in the aftermath of a data breach as manifested by the negative evaluations on social media about the enterprise ability to prevent the data breach or harm caused by the breach. We analyzed the tweets related to the HD data breach to identify a taxonomy of data breach frames and the associated responsibility-attributions and negative emotions. We also analyzed the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of negative emotional responses associated with data breach frames. Further, we provide the <sup>fi</sup>rst evidence of the crisis stages and the evolving reputation threats on social media. We also provide empirical evidence of the immediate impact of tweets attributing data breach responsibility to the enterprise or relaying negative emotions on the subsequent generation of reputation-threatening tweets. Consequently, a response strategy to counteract negative evaluations of enterprises provides an interesting research opportunity. Furthermore, the availability of social media content coupled with innovations in data analytical technology o<sup>f</sup>ers an alternate medium for enterprises to assess and control their reputation as perceived by external stakeholders. While this study provides initial insights into threats to enterprise reputation on social media, it sets a foundation to further explore the impact of online reputation management on publicized corporate ranks.
