# An elaboration likelihood model of consumer respond action to facebook second-hand marketplace: Impulsiveness as a moderator

Hsin Hsin Chang<sup>⁎</sup>, Yu-Yu Lu, Shao Cian Lin

Department of Business Administration at National Cheng Kung University, Taiwan

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Elaboration likelihood mode Facebook second-hand marketplace Impulsiveness Product types

## A B S T R A C T

The Elaboration Likelihood Model is adopted in this study to examine the interaction of central route factors (information completeness and information accuracy) and peripheral route factors (post esthetics and post popularity) on consumer behavior in the Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. Impulsiveness served as a moderator a<sup>f</sup>ecting the relationship between consumer attitudes and behavior. Eight scenarios were designed to test the e<sup>f</sup>ects of post characteristics on consumer responses and purchase behavior. Hedonic and utilitarian products were used in the scenarios to compare the di<sup>f</sup>erences among di<sup>f</sup>erent product types. A total of 908 valid questionnaires were collected. The results of the data analysis indicated that consumers who process messages through the central route tend to respond to the post before initiating purchase intention. On the other hand, consumers who process messages through the peripheral route tend to initiate purchase intention directly if they feel favorable toward a post. Regarding the post characteristics, post popularity was found to be the most crucial factor related to enhancing perceived persuasiveness. According to the results and <sup>fi</sup>ndings, it is suggested that sellers on the Facebook Second-hand Marketplace should provide messages about their product as accurately as completely as possible to strengthen positive impressions of their posts. Otherwise, they need to provide an interesting introduction or esthetic pictures to attract consumer attention.

## 1. Introduction

There is a growing trend among Facebook users to create their own commercial groups that allow them to sell and buy items. Among Facebook users, this kind of commercial group is called marketplace. It is considered to be a platform where users are allowed to conduct consumer-to-consumer (C2C) commercial activities [1]. It represents a kind of group for Facebook users to sell their items and generated by them, while Facebook, the platform itself, is not involved but only provides functions. When users join these kinds of marketplace groups, they are able to see the selling posts created by other group members and directly communicate with the seller, either on the post or through private messaging [1]. There are di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of marketplaces among Facebook users; among them, the most popular ones are Second-hand Marketplaces. Accordingly, Facebook Second-hand Marketplace in this study refers to those commercial groups set by Facebook users, which allow them to sell and buy second-hand items directly with other involved group members [1]. Consumers who use other C2C second-hand shopping websites, such as Yahoo and PChome in Taiwan or eBay JV in the USA, tend to proactively visit the websites when they actually need to buy something. However, consumers can passively receive the information about the products sold on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace from the group after they join. When consumers log in to their Facebook account, once they have joined the group, if they tend to view the group often, the new group posts will be automatically shown on their Facebook homepage regardless of whether the products are needed or not. Moreover, if a consumer’s Facebook friends join the same group and respond to a post, that post will emerge on his homepage. This function causes Facebook users to be exposed to various types of shopping information and has become common in the daily lives of users.

In Taiwan, nearly 87.7% of inhabitants use Facebook every day according to a report conducted by E-ICP in 2016, and 40% of them use Facebook to search for speci<sup>fi</sup>c product information, introductions or comments before they make any purchase decisions [2]. A total of 75.5% of Facebook users have joined commercial groups or fan pages, and most of them tend to have higher purchase intention than those who are not in such groups [2]. A total of 10.3% of the users who join commercial groups tend to be a<sup>f</sup>ected by their Facebook friends and other users’ response behavior toward posts in the groups. Therefore, determining how to enhance consumer willingness to buy products from Facebook Second-hand Marketplace has become an increasingly important issue for sellers using that platform. Accordingly, the re search questions of this study are: How do consumers on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace evaluate a selling post? How do these evaluations a<sup>f</sup>ect consumer attitudes and behavior toward the post?

In discussions of commercial activities on Facebook, sellers should consider their post’s appearance and content as well as consumers’ perceptions and reactions toward the post as it is the main channel on which to communicate with consumers. Consumers evaluate the in formation provided by the post and also the post’s appearance, after which they form impressions and develop attitudes. Consequently, their reactions toward the post will be formed and lead to their purchase behavior. Therefore, the design of a post remains a crucial issue for sellers. A Facebook post on the Second-hand Marketplace usually provides product information, includes the product name, format, sales price, condition (new or used), and a picture of the product. It also includes other users’ feedback below the post if there is any. It is a <sup>fi</sup>xed format, so the content quality, pictures, and feedback are the only methods by which sellers can promote their products. Considering these variables, this study conducts a factorial experiment to evaluate consumer attitudes and behavior. Additionally, product type, i.e., whether it is utilitarian or hedonic, also has to be considered in a post design as consumers react di<sup>f</sup>erently toward di<sup>f</sup>erent product types [3–6].

In the current study, the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) is adopted to develop the conceptual model. The ELM model was pro posed by Petty and Cacioppo [7] and included central and peripheral routes. Determination of which one to choose when processing information is based on an individual’s elaboration pathway. Individuals who process information through central routes tend to rationally view the information, scrutinize the related information, and change thei attitude in a logical way [7]; on the other hand, individuals who process through the peripheral route are less thoughtful and deliberate about information and are only in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by simple cues outside the mes sages [8]. The main objective of signaling theory, which is another theory concerning information processing and behavior, is to discuss information symmetry during the communication process that occurs between an information sender and a receiver [9]. However, the ELM has been applied to explain how people process messages and change their beliefs or attitudes toward information [7,10,11] and compare the di<sup>f</sup>erences between people who process messages through the centra and peripheral routes [12–14]. This study draws upon the information presented only for consumers to assess instead of providing “signaling” to them. It is intended to determine how consumers react to the pre sented message. Thus, it is more suitable to use the ELM as the research model. Four characteristics (e.g., information completeness, informa tion accuracy, post esthetics, and post popularity) of a post are selected in this study as central and peripheral route factors. Individuals who process messages through the central route may tend to concentrate on the comprehensiveness and accuracy of product introduction [15]. In doing so, they tend to believe the post is a good source, which, in turn, drives them to buy the product. Bhattacherjee and Sanford [8] mentioned processing information through the peripheral route and suggested that the focus is mainly on source credibility. Purchase intention may be aroused because of a product’s esthetic appeal or the number of likes. Furthermore, impulsiveness is considered to play an important role in online consumer decisions [16]. As a factorial experiment i conducted in this study, where participants are asked to reply to questions after reading the designed scenarios, impulsiveness will be a crucial and suitable means by which to test its in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

To summarize, this study indicates some research gaps that are worth exploration. First, previous studies have rarely adopted factorial experiments related to central (high completeness × high accuracy and low completeness × low accuracy) and peripheral (high esthetics × high popularity and low esthetics × low popularity) routes and their in<sup>fl</sup>uences on consumer attitudes and behavior in the ELM on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. Chen et al. [17] discussed a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive elaborations through purchase-decision involvement as well as product categories and post sources and their e<sup>f</sup>ects on consumer attitudes toward posts and brands. Chang et al. [10] discussed the persuasiveness of messages in terms of argument quality, post popularity, and attractiveness on consumer attitudes and behavior on a Facebook fan page. These studies ignore how consumers perceive messages presented through central or peripheral routes and rather applied their studies to brands or fan pages. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls in this gap by providing discussions of combined factorial e<sup>f</sup>ects on central and peripheral routes speci<sup>fi</sup>cally as they relate to the Facebook Secondhand Marketplace.

Second, previous studies seldom include the e<sup>f</sup>ects of product types, utilitarian and hedonic, on consumer attitudes and behavior in the ELM on second-hand products. Although Chen et al. [17] provided di<sup>f</sup>erent elaborations of product categories (hedonic and utilitarian) in terms of their e<sup>f</sup>ects on consumer’ attitudes toward a brand in the Facebook setting, they did not focus on the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of product type on the product itself. Kivetz and Zheng [3] compared the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of promotion on utilitarian and hedonic purchases. They ignored how consumers processed the product presented. In addition, Guiot and Roux [18] discussed price as one of motivations leading to purchase of second-hand products. However, they also lacked in discussions on product type. Thus, this study <sup>fi</sup>lls in the gap to embed product type in the manipulations to indicate how product type a<sup>f</sup>ects consumer atti tudes and behavior on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace.

To address these research gaps, a framework is developed adopting the ELM model. The main objectives of this study are: (1) To examine the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of central route factors (information completeness and information accuracy) and peripheral route factors (post esthetics and post popularity) on consumers’ perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness; (2) to determine the causal relationship among perceived persuasiveness, perceived informativeness, and consumer attitudes and behaviors; (3) to examine the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of impulsiveness on consumer attitudes toward posts and their subsequent behavior; (4) to understand consumer responses to di<sup>f</sup>erent product types (i.e., utilitarian and hedonic) and the relationships between each of these construct.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Facebook second-hand marketplace

On Facebook, the group function is similar to a community, and it tends to connect members who share the same interests or particular businesses. Ng [19] posits that if consumers trust a social network community, they are more likely to purchase a product within the community, especially under conditions where the community is closely connected with their daily life, such as a Facebook group. Many Facebook users started to create commercial groups for the purpose of selling items [1]. In Taiwan, most of this type of commercial group is called Facebook Marketplace. Groups created by users that focus on selling second-hand items are called Second-hand Marketplace. Members in this kind of group tend to share items that are no longer of use for them, and the items are displayed in a “post” format.

A basic Facebook second-hand post includes information about the items for sale. The product information usually involves the product name, format, sales price, condition (new or used), and a picture of the product. Chen et al. [1] indicated that perceived information quality is the most important factor when consumers make a purchase decision. If the information about the product is accurate and complete, consumers will tend to generate higher purchase intention. Kwok and Yu [20] claimed that a photo with status information seems to receive more attention or reactions from Facebook users. Facebook also has a unique function by which to identify the popularity of a post, which is “number of likes.” Lee et al. [21] claimed that the number of likes is positively related to the sales volume. That is to say, if the post has high quality information or attractive content, consumers are more likely to be interested in the product. Consumers may be persuaded by the in formation about the product or merely the number of likes [10].

The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product types is also discussed in this study. Kivetz and Zheng [3] Pallas et al. [4] Novak et al. [5], and Voss et al. [6] pointed out that consumers generate di<sup>f</sup>erent emotions toward dif ferent types of products. Consumers on Facebook Second-hand Mar ketplace might expect the product sold there to be cheaper than a new one. Guiot and Roux [18] used wish to pay less as one of economic dimension on second-hand product shopping motivation. When consumers use Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, if the product of interest is a utilitarian product, they might already have done some research on the new product, so they might see a post on the marketplace with the same product, but it is second-hand and is cheaper. This might drive them to purchase this item. Concerning hedonic products, when consumers browse the marketplace or a post that has just shown up on their homepage, they might tend to be attracted by the price instead of considering if they actually need this product. As there are so many di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of products on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, it will be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to discuss all of them. To the best of our knowledge, laptops and apparel are the most frequent products that appear on the site. A laptop is considered to be a utilitarian product [22], and apparel is considered to be a hedonic product [23]. When consumers look for utilitarian products, they focus on their functional performance [24]. On the other hand, when consumers are evaluating a product hedonically, they will focus on their desire for the product [6]. Based on this, it is crucial to discuss product type in this study, where products are di vided into two types: utilitarian and hedonic. Chen and Lee [25] showed that people who purchase utilitarian products tend to process through the central route, while people who purchase hedonic products tend to process through the peripheral route. The purpose of this study is to verify the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the processes involved in purchases of hedonic and utilitarian products.

## 2.2. Elaboration likelihood model (ELM)

The ELM was proposed to explain the process of changes in con sumer attitudes toward messages [7]. They claimed that the ELM has dual routes, the central route and the peripheral route, and that whether individuals process through the central route or peripheral route is based on the depth of their cognitive information processing and degree of “elaboration.” When individuals perceive information as helpful, valuable, or persuasive, they tend to consider the information to be of high quality and thus receive the messages [26]. They <sup>fi</sup>nally change their attitude as a result of a deep analysis of related issues and com prehensive consideration. In contrast, individuals who process messages through the peripheral route tend to spend less time and e<sup>f</sup>ort scrutinizing related information or arguments against their target. In <sup>fl</sup>uences on their changes in attitude are temporary and short-term. Reputation is considered to be the most important signal for them to form beliefs or trust in the information presented to them [10].

Comparing the di<sup>f</sup>erences between the two routes, Bhattacherjee and Sanford [8] proposed that the two routes are distinct in terms of three dimensions. First, these two routes are used to judge how individuals process di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of information. Individuals utilizing the central route process the quality of the argument of the related message. In contrast, individuals utilizing the peripheral route are in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by some simple cues outside the message. Second, individuals using the central route spend more e<sup>f</sup>ort and time than those using the peripheral route. Third, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the central route is more stable than that of the peripheral route and thus changes attitudes in the longterm. Martina and Wang [27] claimed that the central route is the primary source of in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust building. Although both routes are important as they relate to changes in consumer attitudes, the central route is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential.

The ELM has been usually applied in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of social media, in cluding TV-advertising, printed-advertising, and word-of-mouth (WOM) in previous research [28,29]. More recently, the ELM has been used to investigate persuasive situations, such as e-marketing, including online-shopping, e-WOM, online communities, blogs, and online plat forms [12–14]. Gregory et al. [12] suggested how to design online recruitment websites to attract more jobseeker. Li [13] investigated employee acceptance of new <sup>fi</sup>rm information systems. Zhou [14] aimed at understanding the process by which mobile banking users form their initial trust.

This study investigates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of Facebook Second-hand Marketplace characteristics on consumers’ intention and behavior. Kim et al. [30] proposed the ELM as the research framework by which to investigate the relationship between online stimuli and consumer intentions or behavior. Although a Facebook post di<sup>f</sup>ers from other online advertising, the e<sup>f</sup>ects that stimulate consumers to process information are similar [31]. Chen et al. [17] indicated that both central route factors (argument quality) and peripheral route factors (post popularity and attractiveness) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact consumers’ like and share intentions. The same concept can also be applied to illustrate buyers’ changes in attitude toward posts on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. Therefore, the ELM was proposed as the research framework in this study.

This study uses some characteristics of Facebook to represent the central route factors and peripheral route factors, such as post popularity (number of likes and number of comments), posts esthetics (external appearance of the post), and the persuasive strength of post content (information accuracy and information completeness) to aim to determine whether individuals process the information through the central route or peripheral route under di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions and how they perceive the type of stimulation through that route. Finally, this study discusses whether consumers change their attitude toward a post and further take action or develop purchase intention. Impulsiveness is also adopted as a moderator to examine its moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect on consumer behavior.

## 2.2.1. Central route factors

Bhattacherjee and Sanford [8] Li [13], and Zhou et al. [26] mentioned that argument quality is the most representative of the central route as it re<sup>fl</sup>ects information reliability and the persuasive strength of an argument. The strength of an argument is the most important factor for the central route [32]. Argument quality is widely applied in advertising or online communities [10]; however, a Facebook post usually states the product information and includes the product name, format, sales price, condition (new or used), and a picture of the product. Ar gument quality as proposed by Bhattacherjee and Sanford [8] focused on the persuasive strength of an argument. In this study, the persuasive strength of posts in Facebook Second-hand Marketplace depends mainly on the product information in the post, and thus, it is more suitable and direct to use information quality. Information quality includes information relevance, completeness, accuracy, ease of understanding, format, and currency [1,11,14,33]). The most frequently used elements of information quality are completeness, accuracy, format, and currency [34]. Filieri and McLeay [11] used information understandability, information relevance, information timeliness, information accuracy, information format, and information completeness as central route information quality. In this study, information is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as product-related messages in a post provided by a seller including both text and picture content, such as the product’s name, format, price, transaction place, transaction function, and a photo of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c product. Additionally, information completeness and accuracy are adopted as central route factors.

<sup>Information completeness</sup> is the extent to which information is of su<sup>fi</sup>cient depth and breadth [35]. Applying this concept to online transactions, information completeness can be understood as the degree to which product information provided by a seller corresponds to its actual status [36]. As the objective of Facebook Second-hand Marketplace is mostly to sell second-hand goods, consumers may need to know more about a product’s actual status. Dutta-Bergman [37] argued that complete information requires all elements to establish. On the other hand, Filieri and McLeay [11] indicated information completeness in a hotel review context with essential features including location, clean liness, prices, quality of breakfast, and so on. Thus, in this study, when consumers seek laptops on the marketplace, if the posts content in volves all the necessary information about the laptops, such as brand, function, size, price, new or old condition, reason for selling, etc., consumers will consider it to provide complete information [11,37]. In other words, when the information presented on a selling post contains more aspects to re<sup>fl</sup>ect a selling item’s current status, it will lead consumers to believe the post to be more complete. Moreover, as the price of second-hand laptops is considered to be relatively high, consumers will carefully inspect the messages to evaluate their actual status and value. Therefore, if the sellers can provide comprehensive and exhaustive messages to consumers, their willingness to accept the mes sages will be enhanced [11].

<sup>Information accuracy</sup> leads to a powerful e<sup>f</sup>ect on consumers decision-making processes because accurate and correct information helps them avoid fraudulent or fake messages [11]. In this study, information accuracy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which information is correct, accurate, and unambiguous [38]. Information accuracy can be seen as the consistency between data and reality [39]. Accordingly, in a post of selling a second-hand item, accuracy is operationalized as whether the information presented is consistent and correct with the actual product, such as the selling price shall be in a reasonable range and the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations presented shall be the same as the product to be sold. Nelson et al. [40] further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that information accuracy is also perceived to be accurate. Wang and Diane [35] indicated that in formation accuracy is the degree to which information can provide correct, credible, and believable messages to consumers If the messages provided by the seller are correct, accurate, and unambiguous, consumers may perceive the information as trustworthy and credible and will, in turn, tend to be persuaded by the information [40,41]. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, the correctness of the messages provided by seller is an important issue for consumers when they are buying second-hand goods. As the fake online transactions are increasing, consumers will tend to be careful about the correctness of product messages.

## 2.2.2. Peripheral route factors

The peripheral route can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the source’s credibility and the attractiveness and likeability of the post. The most frequently used factor to represent the peripheral route is source credibility [8]. Source credibility has been regarded as the critical factor for peripheral cues [32]. In this study, the concept of source credibility is replaced by a similar construct, which is post popularity. Chang et al. [10] proposed post popularity and post attractiveness as peripheral route factors. They investigated the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of Facebook users intention to like and share a post. This study assumes that people will consider the popularity of the post as the basis for their perceived credibility of the information source. Also, consumers may have a positive impression toward a post with remarkable picture. Therefore, post popularity and post esthetics are used in this study as peripheral route factors.

<sup>Posts esthetics</sup> Nasermoadeli et al. [42] mentioned that when there are higher sensory experiences related to a product or service, consumers are more likely to generate purchase intention. Schmitt (1999) considered sensory as an esthetic perception in a shopping process. Therefore, consumers tend to purchase a product with higher esthetic perceptual value. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, pictures of the product are usually attached. Compared to texts, pictures may in-<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers more strongly, which may, in turn, lead them to form a social orientation. Kwok and Yu [20] claimed that a photo with status information seems to receive more attention or reactions from other Facebook users than those containing simply a link to a video or only text. Eftekhar et al. [43] also found that photo-related activities can highly connect to one’s personality. Chang et al [10] used post attractiveness as a factor to investigate what types of pictures would catch people’s eyes <sup>fi</sup>rst when they see a post on Facebook. Thus, in Facebook context, esthetic pictures are a critical attractor to consumers.

Reber et al. [44] proposed that perceived esthetics can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two dimensions: objective features and an individual’s previous experience. Objective features include the amount of information, symmetry, contrast, and clarity; previous experience refers to an individual’s historical contact with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of stimulation, such as repeated contact with the stimulation or previous awareness of rules. In terms of a Facebook group, the format of every post is the same because users are only allowed to use text and attach pictures. These are the only methods by which users can perceive esthetics. Because of the limitation of Facebook, the posts are in a <sup>fi</sup>xed form that texts are the same format with the only alternation of their arrangement. Yet, the platform allows users to add as many pictures as they want. In this study, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of post esthetics refers to Lavie and Tractinsky [45], where the design of the post is esthetic, clear, clean, and pleasant. Referring to Reber et al. [46] of objective features and individual experiences, when consumers see a second-hand post on Facebook, they tend to look for objective features such as clean, clear, and pleasant text arrangement and pictures, as well as using more pictures, to perceive the esthetics as they already know what the posts will look like. If a post contains clear photos and a delicate text arrangement, it will lead consumers to have higher perceptions of post esthetics [46].

<sup>Post</sup> <sup>popularity</sup>, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the quantity of online comments, is re garded as an important in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumers when they are processing messages [47]. Chatterjee [48] showed that the number of online comments is a signal of product popularity. In addition, the number of likes indicates the popularity of a post by displaying the number of people or friends who liked the post and encourages others to follow the post [31]. Chang et al. [10] used post popularity as a peripheral route factor. In their context, they used trustworthiness, reliability, and belief of recipes with more people pressing like, sharing, and responding to measure post popularity. The “number of likes” play an important role in changes in consumer attitudes [49]. Accordingly, in this study, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of post popularity follows De Vries et al. [31] and comprises the number of likes, comments, and shares of the post on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. When consumers see a post with more likes, comments, and shares, they tend to perceive it as popular [10,31].

## 2.3. Perceived informativeness

Perceived informativeness is the degree to which the traceability system can o<sup>f</sup>er online buyer helpful information [10]. In terms of an online transaction, this concept is viewed as the extent to which an online seller provides the product-related information required by consumers [30]. In this study, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of perceived informativeness refers to Aaker and Norris [50], where perceived informativeness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the post messages include informational content. When consumers look for a laptop on Facebook Secondhand Marketplace, they need a considerable amount of information to evaluate which laptop meets their requirements. If the messages correspond with consumer needs, they will pay more attention to the post and not <sup>fi</sup>lter out the messages [51]. Consumers will clearly ascertain the speci<sup>fi</sup>c status of the product if they view comprehensive messages. Thus, consumers will consider the messages to be useful, and their perceived informativeness toward the post will be higher [30].

Elliott and Speck [15] claimed that online reviews with informative content can assist consumers with reducing product uncertainty and increase purchase intention when online shopping [60]. These sales messages will be more important for those who have higher consumption motivation when making purchase decisions [52]. As this study focuses on purchasing second-hand goods, consumers may be more alert and cautious about the sale messages associated with used goods because some may be broken or defective. Therefore, if the content of the post includes more relevant, comprehensive, and detailed information about the speci<sup>fi</sup>c condition of the second-hand goods, consumers will be more con<sup>fi</sup>dent about the quality of the post content and will tend to change their attitude toward the post [53].

## 2.4. Perceived persuasiveness

Perceived persuasiveness refers to a process in which receivers beliefs or actions are changed in a rational and sensible way [54]. In classical research, this process can be explained by the information presented, received, and processed. If the process is successful, con sumers will change their attitude toward the target [55]. Drozd et al. [41] mentioned perceived persuasiveness as consumers’ acceptance of messages as well as a favorable feeling about such messages. When consumer favorability toward a product increases, they tend to feel good about the messages related to that product and accept them [56]. Consumers who frequently receive persuasive messages tend to process messages more super<sup>fi</sup>cially [57]. In terms of Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, if messages provided by a seller can make consumers feel safe, comfortable, convinced, and bene<sup>fi</sup>ted, perceptions of persuasiveness will tend to be higher [41,58]. Teng et al. [59] showed that when ac product description is more attractive, it is viewed as more persuasive. In this study, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of perceived persuasiveness is adapted from Zhang et al. [60], where perceived persuasiveness is de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the received information can persuade a person to believe something.

## 2.5. Attitude toward post and behavioral action

Chen et al. [17] indicated that attitude formation includes multiple processes. Consumer attitude can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by di<sup>f</sup>erent types of stimuli, such as advertising, TV programs, and Facebook posts. After receiving these stimuli, consumers will generate some message-related responses, including a<sup>f</sup>ective and cognitive responses. An individual’s cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective responses to a stimulus source result in attitudes toward that source, where the attitude toward ads is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as either a favorable or unfavorable feeling toward the advertising stimuli following exposure to the stimuli [31]. Attitude toward post is a consumer's “predisposition to respond in a consistently favorable or unfavorable manner toward the post in general [81].” When consumers read the content of the post, if the description of the product is clear and speci<sup>fi</sup>c, consumers may become more involved with the product. Chang et al. [10] posited that if consumers regard advertising messages to be useful, their perceived value of the advertising will be higher.

In this study, behavior includes two dimensions: a response and purchase intention. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, after seeing sale messages in a post, consumers can decide whether to respond to the post. If consumers feel favorable toward the post, they will tend to respond in some ways, such as clicking “like,” sharing, or commenting on the post [10]. Lee et al. [21] claimed that some recommendation functions of Facebook such as “number of likes” and comments made by other consumers will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence sales volume. In this study, consumer behavior after viewing post content is seen as an important result.

<sup>Response</sup> Facebook provides three functions with which to response a post: like, comment, and share. Clicking “like” is the most common action taken by Facebook users when they enjoy a post. Chen et al. [49] and Lee et al. [21] indicated that the “number of likes” plays an important role in changing consumer attitudes. When referring to the “comment” function, people tend to trust their actual friends on Facebook as opposed to strangers. When purchasing products in an online social commerce environment, consumers tend to seek out their friends recommendations or opinions [21]. Chang et al. [10] mentioned that commenting on a post will in<sup>fl</sup>uence people’s attitudes. The “share” function enables people to share a post on their own page, where the post can be seen by their friends. Hinz et al. [61] indicated that if a post receives more likes, the post may gain more attention and will be more likely to be shared. Therefore, this study posits that a response plays an important role on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. A response is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as when people take action related to a post, which includes clicking like, comment, and share.

<sup>Purchase intention</sup> re<sup>fl</sup>ects the likelihood of purchase in the future [62]. Determining how to enhance consumer purchase intention is a key issue for Facebook Second-hand Marketplace sellers. Nasermoadeli et al. [42] posited that emotional experience will in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer purchase intention. Kim and Han [51] suggested that if consumers consider the messages provided by advertisement content to be valu able, they will further generate some positive attitude toward the products advertised, and their intention to purchase will be higher. Under this condition, if customers feel a strong sense of joy and satisfaction with the post content, they will end up generating positive attitude toward the products and thus higher purchase intention.

## 2.6. Moderator- impulsiveness

In previous studies, many di<sup>f</sup>erent factors have been proposed as moderators of ELM, such as self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy [14], user expertise [8,10], job relevance [8], and prior experience [83]. Most of the moderators in these studies were proposed to test the di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uences of the cen tral and peripheral routes. As this study focuses on online shopping on Facebook, it is crucial to determine whether consumers purchase a product after reading the product information from the post. Therefore, impulsiveness is proposed as moderator to test the strength of its in <sup>fl</sup>uence on consumer attitudes and behavior.

According to Floh and Madlberger [16], with the growing trend of online shopping, impulsive purchases have become increasingly important in online transactions. Impulsiveness has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on online impulse purchasing. In this study, impulsiveness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual tendency that includes urges to engage in an unplanned action or to take action immediately without evaluation and deliberation of consequences [63]. Wells et al. [64] posited that impulsiveness can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two dimensions: individual characteristics and environmental characteristics.

Individual characteristics are common personality traits, one of which is making impulsive purchases [10]. Dittmar et al. [65] claimed that people with higher discrepancies between their real self and their ideal self tend to purchase impulsively, where the real self is who the person really is, and the ideal self is the ideal image that person wants to portray. These people tend to <sup>fi</sup>ll up this gap with materialism, so their motivation to make impulsive purchases, in turn, tends to be higher. On the other hand, environmental characteristics concern external cues from the shopping environment, such as store atmosphere or service quality [10]. In the case of online shopping, Parboteeah et al. [66] indicated that websites that are of high quality in areas such as ease of use, security, and navigability will increase consumer impulse behavior. Wells et al. [64] showed that both personal traits and environmental cues are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to impulsive online purchasing. This study only discusses individual characteristics as the precondition of the environment remains constant. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of environment stimuli in this study is “post content.” In this study, the consumers will be given a post prior to answering the questionnaire. After consumers read the same post content, where it is posited that those who are more impulsive will tend to take action compared to those who are less impulsive.

## 3. Conceptual framework and hypotheses development

This study is an attempt to investigate the characteristics of Facebook Second-hand Marketplace posts and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumer behavior. The conceptual framework in this study is based on the ELM, which is a dual-route theory proposed by Petty and Cacioppo [7].

Fig. 1. Research Framework

There are four characteristics used in this study to measure the e<sup>f</sup>ect of stimuli on consumer attitudes and behavior, including central route factors (information completeness, information accuracy) and peripheral route factors (post esthetics and post popularity). A 2 × 2 factorial design is conducted to measure how these characteristics a<sup>f</sup>ect consumer behavior. In addition, perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness are used to test consumers’ perceptions after reading the post content. Responses including clicking “like,” “share,” or “com ment” are used to measure how consumers respond to the post. Purchase intention refers to whether consumers want to buy the secondhand goods advertised on the post. Moreover, impulsiveness plays a role as a moderator in this study. Lastly, product type is applied as the product information provided on the post to examine in the di<sup>f</sup>erences of consumer behavior between hedonic products and utilitarian pro ducts. Overall, a total of eleven constructs are employed in this study, for which the conceptual framework is shown in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Central route vs. Perceived informativeness vs. Perceived persuasiveness (H1, H2)

Lee et al. [38] divided information quality into many dimensions to measure which aspect of information quality in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer acceptance willingness more. The results showed that both completeness and correctness of information are very important. Elliott and Speck [15] also suggested that product information should include both correct and complete information about the product on the website. If the website content provides complete and accurate messages about pro ducts, consumers will be able to make better decisions and will, in turn, tend to form a positive attitude toward the website [51].

This concept can also be used in Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. When there is an adequate quantity of information about a product, consumers will have more information to consider and will, in turn, tend to feel that the post includes informative content [67]. In contrast, if the post lacks some important information about a second hand product, such as price, condition, or a product picture, consumers may feel uncertain about the product, and their perceived informa tiveness will thus be lower [60].

In addition, the correctness of messages is also an important factor on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. Filieri and McLeay [11] claimed that information accuracy is the most powerful in<sup>fl</sup>uencer among all the information quality dimensions during the consumer decision-making process. Hence, if both the completeness and accuracy of the post are high, and consumers can easily determine the actual status and features of a second-hand product, they will be more likely to concentrate on the post and waste less time searching for other products [51]. On the other hand, if both the completeness and accuracy of the post are low, and the post lacks important information or provides incorrect information, consumers may feel that the post content is useless and tend to ignore the post. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is as follows:

H1. Central route factors, such as information completeness and information accuracy, have a positive relation to perceived informativeness; that is, high information completeness and information accuracy have stronger efects on perceived informativeness than low information completeness and information accuracy.

Persuasive strength is the extent to which information can induce consumers to believe something [60]. Persuasive strength can be in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced by how comprehensive information is as well as by its accu racy, relevance, and timeliness [68]. Whether sellers provide comprehensive information about a product is an important factor related to enhancing the persuasive strength of a post [59]. Providing the actual status of the products with defects will enhance the perceived trust of consumers [33]. In contrast, because of a post missing some important information, consumers’ trust in the post will be lower.

Accurate information can also provide a good foundation for a successful transaction [33]. If all the information on the post is correct, consumers may feel the post content to be believable, and the persuasive strength of the post will be higher [41,60]. Contradictorily, if the post has a lot of wrong information, consumers might consider the post messages to be fake and tend to ignore it. For instance, if the description of the product from a post is for Nike shorts, but the photo attached is an Adidas hoodie, consumers might perceive the text as not consistent with the attached photo, and their regard for the post will be reduced [52].

In this study, it is assumed that both completeness and accuracy are important factors that enhance the persuasive strength of a post. When both completeness and accuracy are high, consumers have comprehensive and correct information by which to evaluate the quality of a second-hand product and to determine whether the price is reasonable. In contrast, if both completeness and accuracy are low, consumers might think the message is not precise enough to determine the actual status of the product, which will, in turn, reduce their perceptions of persuasive strength. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2. Central route factors, such as information completeness and information accuracy, have a positive relation with perceived persuasiveness; that is, high information completeness and information accuracy have stronger efects on perceived persuasiveness than low information completeness and information accuracy.

## 3.2. Peripheral route vs. Perceived informativeness vs. Perceived persuasiveness (H3, H4)

Kwok and Yu [20] claimed that on Facebook, a post with an attached photo can attract more attention than those with links or video only. This means that a beautiful picture is an important stimulus to obtain consumer attention. Chang et al. [10] showed that post popularity is positively related to usefulness, which means people regard the number of likes as an important signal when they seek information required on Facebook. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, good esthetics helps consumers process messages more easily and makes them feel safe and comfortable [46]. Consumers may feel that an esthetic post provides clearer and more accurate information to them than would be the case with an untidy post, and thus, their perceived informativeness will be higher. Moreover, post popularity on Facebook implies that a great number of people liked the message and encouraged other users to focus on it [31]. Zhang et al. [60] showed that when consumers see a large quantity of online reviews, they expect and further perceive the review content to be more informative. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, consumers might think that a popular post is more worthy of referring than an unpopular one and further perceive the post content to be informative. In contrast, when both post esthetics and post popularity are low, consumers tend to ignore the post because they cannot process the content easily, and an ugly attached picture and messy text arrangement may also make them feel uncomfortable. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3. Peripheral route factors, such as post esthetics and post popularity, have a positive relation with perceived informativeness; that is, high post esthetic and post popularity have stronger efects on perceived informativeness than low post esthetics and post popularity.

Wang et al. [69] indicated that website esthetics positively impact consumer satisfaction with a website. Therefore, consumers tend to accept the information on the website [58]. On Facebook, if the post has a beautiful attached picture or the title of the post is attractive, consumers will focus on the post content [10]. In addition, post popularity is also an important in<sup>fl</sup>uence that convinces people to believe the post content. De Maeyer [70] has shown that popularity is positively related to sales volume. When consumers want to purchase a product, they may consult the product’s level of popularity or the quantity of online reviews about the product. As popularity implies that there are many other people who follow this product or issue, the perceived value of the product will thus be enhanced, which will, in turn, lead consumers to be more likely to make a purchase. [60] In particular, considering that online commerce has many uncertainties and information asymmetry, perceived popularity can help consumers feel safe and some of the risks are reduced [21].

When both esthetics and popularity are high, and the attached picture in the post clearly presents the second-hand product, consumers tend to be more likely to accept the content [46]. On the other hand, when a post has more likes, shares, and comments, consumers may thus feel that a popular post is more persuasive than an unpopular one be cause post popularity implies the extent to which a post is worth attending to [31]. If the text arrangement of the post and attached picture are messy, consumers cannot process the content easily, so they will tend to ignore the post content [46]. In addition, if the post has fewer likes, shares, or comments, consumers might suspect that the post content or the product has some problems, which will cause readers to ignore it and thus reduce its perceived persuasiveness. Thus, hypothesis H4 is proposed as follows:

H4. Peripheral route factors, such as post esthetics and post popularity, have a positive relation to perceived persuasiveness; that is, high post esthetics and post popularity have stronger efects on perceived persuasiveness than low post esthetics and post popularity.

3.3. Perceived informativeness and Perceived persuasiveness vs. Attitude toward post (H5, H6)

Consumers expressing favorable attitudes toward an advertisement are more likely to regard the advertisement as entertaining, informative, and acceptable. Chen et al. [17] and Sinkovics et al. [71] proposed that cognitive and a<sup>f</sup>ective responses elicited by Facebook post content may positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer attitudes toward posts. Kim and Han [51] suggested that if consumers believe advertising content is informative, they will think the advertising is a good channel on which to buy the product and focus on the ad content. Consumers prefer to spend more time and attention on an informative post, and their perceived utilitarian value of the post will be converted to a favorable attitude toward the post [25].

On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, if the post provides a high level of related information about a laptop, such as the product’s condition or defects, consumers will tend to believe that the post is a good source for buying a second-hand laptop. Source credibility usually affects consumers’ degree of perceived persuasiveness because it reduces perceived risk and uncertainty about messages and creates positive impressions of a post [41]. The greater the persuasive strength of the post, the more likely it will be that consumers will engage in further behavioral intention to purchase [60]. Therefore, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5. Consumer perceived informativeness has a positive relation to attitude toward a post.

H6. Consumer perceived persuasiveness has a positive relation to attitude toward a post.

3.4. Attitude toward post vs. Response action vs. Purchase intention (H7, H8, H9)

When consumers like or dislike online advertising, this will lead to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c attitude or behavior, such as clicking on the advertising [52]. Facebook has three functions that enable consumers to express their attitudes toward a post, as discussed earlier. When consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd a post to be complete and accurate or the attached photo is beautiful, they tend to feel favorable toward the post and use the “like” function to express their emotion. Consumers tend to share the online advertisement they like with others [10]. The “share” function enables consumers to share the post with their friends on their individual page or as a private message. If the description of a laptop is accurate, or the price of the laptop is much cheaper than the market price, consumers may feel the post content to be useful and helpful, and, in turn, may be more willing to share this second-hand post with their friends. The “comment” function enables consumers to express their opinions or ask some questions about the post. When consumers feel favorable toward the post content, they may use the “comment” function to ask for some further details about the transaction, such as the transaction place or payment method, or they may attempt to negotiate the price. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H7. Consumer attitudes toward a post have a positive relation to their response behavior.

Consumers who express favorable attitudes toward products or services are more likely to buy the product or services [51]. To enhance consumer willingness to purchase a second-hand product, raising the intensity of their emotional experience during the purchasing process is important [42]. If the advertising clearly presents a product’s features and functions, consumers might feel the ad content to be informative and entertaining. Zhang and Mao [52] claimed that whether an advertisement is e<sup>f</sup>ective depends on its ability to make consumers feel and think about the product in the advertisement before they actually make a purchase. This concept can also be used to illustrate consumer purchase intention toward Facebook second-hand products.

When looking for apparel on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, if the post content clearly presents the actual status of the apparel or the attached picture of the apparel is beautiful and appealing, consumers might have better feelings from these visual or informational cues [72]. When the post provides consumers with a more enjoyable and emo tionally pleasant experience while they browse $\mathbf { i t } ,$ they are more likely to feel delighted and to develop further intention toward purchase or engage in further behavior [73]. If consumers generate a strong positive a<sup>f</sup>ection for or a favorable feeling toward a post, they will be more likely to buy the second-hand product in the post. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H8. Consumer attitudes toward a post have a positive relation to their purchase intention.

The number of “likes” on a Facebook ad is positively related to its <sup>fi</sup>nal sales volume [21]. Total “likes” re<sup>fl</sup>ects the number of potential buyers who are expressing a positive attitude toward a post. Zhang and Mao [52] claimed that people who click “like” on a Facebook post are more likely to take further action than those who do not because they are willing to spend more attention and more time on the post. This concept can also be used to illustrate consumer behavior on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace. When consumers have interest in apparel in a post, they will tend to press “like” to express this favorable attitude. If consumers respond to the post to ask some questions about the apparel, it means that they want to obtain more information about the apparel, such as size, condition, or location. Sharing the post implies that con sumers have high recognition of the post content, so they share the post with their friends to express their support. Liking (disliking) a post is usually positively (negatively) related to purchase intention [52]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H9. Consumer responses have a positive relation to their purchase intention.

## 3.5. Moderator: impulsiveness (H10)

Impulsiveness is a social psychological trait that plays an important role in online consumer decisions [16,64,82]. Chen et al. [49] indicated that impulsiveness has a moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect on consumers’ perceived website quality and impulsive buying on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, and especially when the post provides impulsive consumers with high completeness of information, the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect will be stronger. When consumers are characterized with high impulsiveness, they might take action immediately without thinking or planning [63]. When highly impulsive people see a post they like, they will respond immediately by clicking “likes,” “share,” or “comment” to express their reaction.

Consumers with strong impulsiveness characteristics tend to urge others to buy products on websites compared to less impulsive individuals, especially in situations where an online shopping website causes them to feel a sense of joy or leads to other positive emotional responses [16]. That is, consumers who are highly impulsive tend to take action without thinking and planning and tend to buy a product suddenly rather than going through a deliberation process [63,74]. In addition, Kacen and Lee [74] claimed that highly impulsive people are comparatively more easily aroused by stimuli and have less control of their intellects than other people. In other words, highly impulsive people may buy apparel just because they feel the apparel is appealing and beautiful rather than because they actually need it. Thus, they are comparatively more likely to buy apparel without deliberation. Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H10. Impulsiveness has a positive moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect on the relationship between consumer attitude toward a (a) post and responses and (b) post and purchase intention.

## 3.6. Product types: utilitarian and hedonic

According to Crowley et al. [75], product value can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two dimensions: utilitarian value and hedonic value. Chen et al. [17] also claimed that among the numerous product categories, hedonic and utilitarian values have been most frequently tested and compared in previous studies. Products can be high or low in both the utilitarian and hedonic dimensions at the same time because utilitarianism and hedonism are not a one-dimensional scale [6]. Products are not absolutely classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as either utilitarian or hedonic but can rather simultaneously have these two values and only vary in terms of the degree to which they are either one. Pallas et al. [4] roughly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed products as four types: high utilitarian/high hedonic, high utilitarian/ low hedonic, low utilitarian/high hedonic, and low utilitarian/low hedonic. In this study, a utilitarian product is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as having high utilitarian value but low hedonic value, and a hedonic product is de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as having high hedonic value but low utilitarian value.

Previous studies have shown that product types can in<sup>fl</sup>uence both intention and attitude. Novak et al. [5] suggested that consumers are more likely to take unplanned action or engage in impulsive purchase when they are exposed to hedonic products, such as apparel, DVDs, and video games. Consumers who purchase hedonic goods are motivated by their senses, such as their hearing, sight, or touch [76]. In addition, hedonic products can provide consumers higher levels of delight than utilitarian products [4]. Therefore, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product types is discussed in this study. The research scenarios are divided into two types: utilitarian products and hedonic products, and the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the results for the two types of products are compared and discussed. It is assumed here that product types will have di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects on all the hypotheses in this study. Therefore, consumers might have di<sup>f</sup>erent reactions or focus on di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics of product types as they process messages.

<sup>Utilitarian product,</sup> utilitarian value originated from the functional performance of products such as personal computers or minivans [24]. People who purchase utilitarian products are motivated by their basic daily needs for such products [3]. Therefore, when consumers purchase a utilitarian product, they will be more deliberate about the product’s function [77]. Chen and Lee [25] used the ELM as the research model to test consumer attitudes toward utilitarian goods. Their results indicated that consumers who purchase a utilitarian product will deliberate about the functions of the product and will thus tend to process through the central route. In this study, a utilitarian product is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an instrument for which the focus is on functional performance [24] and a laptop is used as a utilitarian product [22]. The 3C-related marketplace is one of the most common marketplaces on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace where users usually sell cellphones, computers, or laptops they no longer need. Furthermore, a laptop is usually used to manage document processing, so the utilitarian orientation is higher than that of the hedonic orientation [22]. Therefore, a laptop is very suitable to represent a utilitarian product in this study.

<sup>Hedonic Product</sup>, hedonic value originates from a<sup>f</sup>ection related to the use experience with a product [6]. Hedonic products such as sports cars, luxury watches, and video games can deliver positive feelings to customers [3]. Thus, people purchase hedonic products in a more subjective and personal way, perhaps because such products provide them with more pleasure and a greater sense of playfulness than is the case with their functions [77]. Chen and Lee [25] also used ELM to measure consumer attitudes toward hedonic goods. Their results indicated that consumers who purchase hedonic goods focus on their a<sup>f</sup>ection for and feelings about the products and tend to process through the peripheral route. In this study, a hedonic product is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an instrument that can provide fun, joy, pleasure, excitement, and other emotional feelings to consumers [23,24]. The hedonic good used in this study is apparel. The second-hand apparel marketplace is the most common marketplace on Facebook. Additionally, apparel can not only serve as a necessity but also beauti<sup>fi</sup>es one’s appearance. Kushwaha and Shankar [23] also claimed that apparel’s hedonic value is higher than its utilitarian value. Therefore, apparel is suitable to be the research object in this study. Thus, the following hypothesis is pro posed:

H11. Product types (utilitarian and hedonic products) have di<sup>f</sup>erent e<sup>f</sup>ects on the relationship between each construct in the model.

## 4. Research design and results

Four di<sup>f</sup>erent $2 \times 2$ factorial designs based on the ELM were em ployed in this study to <sup>fi</sup>nd out whether di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of character istics of a post occur through the central route (i.e., information com pleteness and information accuracy) or the peripheral route (i.e., post esthetics and post popularity) and whether utilitarian or hedonic products in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness and then lead to impacts on their attitudes and behavioral intention. All the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and references for the constructs in this study are provided in Appendix A.

Posts of second-hand products with a laptop as a utilitarian product and apparel as a hedonic product on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace were used in the online questionnaire for the participants. As previously mentioned, 3C products and apparels are the most common products on this type of social media platform. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaire after reading the posts. All the questionnaires items were measured on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (7) strongly agree (Appendix B). The detail scenario designs are described in the next section.

## 4.1. Scenario design

There are two di<sup>f</sup>erent levels (high and low) of the characteristics of a second-hand product post. The experiment was designed to have sixteen conditions through the central and peripheral routes and utili tarian and hedonic products. For the central route, there were four scenarios for each product type: high information completeness and high information accuracy, high information completeness and low information accuracy, low information completeness and high information accuracy, and low information completeness and low information accuracy. For the peripheral route, there were also four scenarios for each product type: high post esthetics and high post popularity, high post esthetics and low post popularity, low post esthetics and high post popularity, and low post esthetics and low post popu larity.

Regarding the central route scenarios, the levels di<sup>f</sup>er in terms of information completeness and information accuracy as either high or low. It is assumed in this study that when these two factors are both high, their in<sup>fl</sup>uences will be stronger than those that are low on consumer’s perceived informativeness and persuasiveness and will further a<sup>f</sup>ect their attitudes and behavioral actions. These factors were tested with the laptop and apparel separately, where the former was utili tarian and the latter was hedonic ([22]; Kushwaha & Shankar. 2013). The laptop tested in this study was an Apple Macbook Air, which is one of the most popular brands in the world. The test object for apparel was an Adidas hoodie to avoid it being gender-speci<sup>fi</sup>c. In the design scenario, for information completeness, the scenarios were designed to provide detailed information. As for information accuracy, there were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant errors in the product description; for example, the price for each product was supposed to be the same, but there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant error of one more zero (NTD 27,000 and NTD 270,000). As for the peripheral route scenarios, the same product type was used in the assumption, and the di<sup>f</sup>erence from the central route is that when both post esthetics and post popularity are high, this tends to have stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumer’s attitude and behavioral actions. In the design scenario, for post esthetics, the scenarios were designed to provide more modi<sup>fi</sup>ed pictures to illustrate the product. As for post popularity, there were comments and like options under the post. There were a total of sixteen scenarios, for which the combination matrix is listed in Appendix C and each experimental scenario is shown in Appendix D.

## 4.2. Pilot test

To determine whether the questionnaire items were suitable to measure the construct and whether the scenario designs were appropriate, a pilot test was conducted before the formal test. The online questionnaire was distributed in the form of a Facebook post on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace in Taiwan. The key words of “second-hand marketplace” were used to search for groups on Facebook, and groups with more members and without speci<sup>fi</sup>c product types were chosen to distribute the survey. The post included a brief introduction to the questionnaire and attached sixteen links to the online questionnaires from the “mySurvey” website. When participants read this post about the questionnaire, they were able to choose freely and randomly from one of the sixteen links and thus to reply to one of the scenarios. The questionnaire included two questions to check the respondent’s experience with using Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, 3 questions to check whether they read the scenario carefully or not, 42 items in total designed to measure the constructs, and 7 questions to obtain the respondent’s demographics.

A total of 115 valid questionnaires were collected for the pilot test, which was used to con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the manipulations of the central route factors and peripheral route factors corresponded with expectations. An independent t-test was adopted, and the results indicated a statistical di<sup>f</sup>erence. Results for information completeness (F-value = 0.121, tvalue = 18.310, p-value = .000), information accuracy $( F = 1 . 7 6 7 ,$ t = 14.723, p = .000), post esthetics (F = 6.510, t = 15.243, p = .000), and post popularity (F = 0.891, t = 18.070, p = .000) indicated that the manipulations were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Additionally, the mean of information completeness (High= 5.152, Low= 2.227), information accuracy (H= 4.868, L= 2.642), post esthetics (H= 4.536, L= 2.289), and post popularity (H= 4.484, L= 1.623) all exhibited signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between the high and low levels of these manipulations. This indicated that these four manipulations were well-designed for the questionnaire. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the values of the item-to-total be more than 0.50. A higher value for the item total indicates a greater degree of reliability of a given item.

## 4.3. Research results

A similar process as that used for the pilot test was adopted for the formal questionnaires and distributed through a Facebook post and on Facebook Second-hand Marketplaces. A total of 63 questionnaires were collected for each scenario, and a total of 1008 questionnaires were collected. Among these, 100 questionnaires were considered to be in valid because these respondents either had no experience with using Facebook Second-hand Marketplace or chose the wrong answer in the scenario check. A total of 908 valid questionnaires were used to conduct the data analysis. The details of the valid respondents for each scenario are provided in Appendix C.

The demographical analysis showed that 66.6% of the respondents were female and 33.4% were male, with the majority ranging in age between 21 and 30 years old (69.3%). A total of 85.7% of the respondents were students, with education levels including college and university (68%). In terms of the average Facebook usage rate, most of the respondents spent less than 6 h on Facebook per day; their average purchase rate on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace was less than 3 times per month, and their average purchasing total was less than 500 NTD (approximately 17 USD) (Table 1).

## 5. Discussions and conclusions

## 5.1. Research finding and discussions

5.1.1. Central route vs. perceived informativeness (H1) and perceived persuasiveness (H2)

The result for H1 is consistent with Elliott and Speck [15]. Filieri and McLeay [11] and Kim and Han [51] showed direct or indirect re lationships between information completeness, information accuracy, and perceived informativeness. However, to the best of our under standing, no previous study has tested the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of information completeness and information accuracy on perceived informativeness. One of the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings here is that the completeness and accuracy of messages a<sup>f</sup>ect consumers’ perceived informativeness the most. Among the eight scenarios, perceived informativeness for scenario 1, the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of information completeness and information accuracy on perceived informativeness was supported. On Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, both the accuracy and completeness of the post information are important factors that contribute to provide consumers with informative content. Kim and Han [51] mentioned that when a post provides complete and accurate information about second-hand products, consumers can easily determine the actual status and features of the second-hand product.

H2 was supported. Both information completeness and information accuracy were found to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uences on consumers’ perceived persuasiveness, which is consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from Teng et al. [59] and Delone and McLean [68]. Moreover, the study proved the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of information completeness and information accuracy on consumers’ perceived persuasiveness. On Facebook Secondhand Marketplace, when a post provides comprehensive, correct messages about a product, consumers will be convinced that the description of the product is speci<sup>fi</sup>c enough to help them decide, and they will tend to believe the post content [60].

## 5.1.3. Perceived informativeness, perceived persuasiveness, attitude toward post, and response (H5-H9)

H5 and H6 were supported; Kim and Han [51] and Zhang et al. [60] proved that informativeness and persuasiveness are important determinants of consumer attitudes toward information sources especially in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of marketing. The results indicated that consumers tend to generate favorable attitudes toward Facebook second-hand posts when the post content is informative and persuasive. A post with informative content can present the speci<sup>fi</sup>c status of the product and reduce the time wasted on searching through other related messages about the same product, so they tend to regard the post as a good channel on which to purchase the product [51,60]. Therefore, if sellers provide detailed, comprehensive information in their posts, consumers will believe in the sincerity of the seller and trust that the actual status of the product has been described.

H7 was consistent with Zhang and Mao [52]. The results indicated consumers with more favorable feelings or positive moods related to a post will tend to transfer their attitude to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c action such as clicking like, comment, or share. The result of H8 was consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies ([51,79,73]). Therefore, whether the post induces positive feelings is an important determinant of a purchase decision [42]. Good feelings toward a Facebook post can be created through visual cues or informational cues [72]. Kim and Han [51] provided comprehensive messages about products, such as the product format, condition, and an accurate photo. A post with an attractive photo and neat arrangement can make consumers feel visually comfortable, leading them to feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed or generating positive emotions about the post [69]. All the above factors can induce positive attitudes toward posts, which will, in turn, make consumers more likely to develop further behavioral intention in the future [51,73]. Therefore, the most important target of a post is to enhance positive emotion and impressions.

H9 was supported, which was consistent with Zhang and Mao [52]. On Facebook, the number of likes usually implies the potential number of buyers who are expressing a reaction to a post [21]. Lee et al. [21] investigated the relationship between number of likes and sales volume related to Facebook advertisement posts and proved that posts with more “likes” led to higher sales volumes. Based on the results of this study, when consumers respond to a post by liking, commenting, or sharing, this implies that they are willing to spend more time interacting with the post. Therefore, consumers who respond have more interest and potentially more favorable attitudes about the second-hand product advertised on that post.

## 5.1.4. Moderator: impulsiveness (H10)

The result of H10a was consistent with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of impul siveness by Beatty and Ferrell [63]. When consumers are highly impulsive, they tend to take action immediately without thinking or planning. That is, when highly impulsive people have interest in a second-hand post, they will respond immediately without planning or thinking. H10b was not supported. Chen et al. [49] claimed that people’s impulsive purchase behavior on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace is strongly a<sup>f</sup>ected by their peers. According to SDT, consumers will regard unknown people as at a greater social distance and will, in turn, tend to ignore their opinions and their peers’ opinions or comments. Furthermore, highly impulsive people are more easily aroused by stimuli and less in control of their intellect [74]. Therefore, impulsive purchases are mainly a<sup>f</sup>ected by “who” recommend the product rather than “how” complete the product information is [49]. As the scenarios in this study were designed to provide consumers with product information but lacked other emotional stimuli such as “who clicked like or responded to the post,” this might explain why the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of impulsiveness was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

## 5.1.5. Efect of product types (H11)

H11 was partially supported; the term product type was proposed to test the di<sup>f</sup>erences between every hypothesis when consumers are choosing either utilitarian or hedonic products. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a hedonic product was found to be stronger than that of a utilitarian product for H1, H2, H3, H4, H6, H7, and H9. Among these, the di<sup>f</sup>erences between product types were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for H2, H3, and H7. On the other hand, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the utilitarian product was stronger than that of the hedonic product for H5 and H8, but the results showed that only the di<sup>f</sup>erence between product types for H5 was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In conclusion, there were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between hedonic products and utilitarian products on the four hypotheses in this study, including H2, H3, H5, and H7. Next, some explanations about the effects of product types as they relate to these hypotheses are provided.

For H1-H4, it seems that hedonic products have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence than utilitarian products, but only the in<sup>fl</sup>uence related to H2 and H3 indicated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences. That is, regardless of whether consumers process through the central route or the peripheral route, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of hedonic products will be stronger than that of utilitarian products on their perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness. In the case of H2, when consumers see apparel and a laptop on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, they will believe the post information for the apparel to be more persuasive with the equivalent information completeness and information accuracy. A reasonable explanation for this is the amount of information consumers need when purchasing utilitarian products or hedonic products. Consumers purchase utilitarian products to satisfy the need for some necessity or to accomplish some speci<sup>fi</sup>c task. Therefore, when consumers purchase a utilitarian product, they will be more deliberate about the product’s function [77].

For H3, when consumers see apparel and a laptop on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, they will consider the post information for the apparel to be more informative with equivalent post popularity and post esthetics. As people purchase hedonic products in a more subjective and personal way, they enjoy purchasing as a result of pleasure and as sense of playfulness rather than due to a need for it to have a speci<sup>fi</sup>c function [77]. On the other hand, popular posts will make consumers feel that the post is reliable [48]. Therefore, consumers are more likely to accept the information about a hedonic product because of a good impression or a favorable emotion [6].

For H5, Babin et al. [77] claimed that consumers will be more deliberate about the function of a utilitarian product because utilitarian products are regarded as instruments intended to provide functional performance [24]. Therefore, once consumers have more detailed information by which to determine if the function of the product is consistent with their expectations and requirements, they tend to generate favorable attitudes toward the post. For H7, according to Novak et al. [5], consumers are more likely to engage in unplanned action or impulsive purchasing when they are looking at hedonic products. That is, purchases of hedonic goods are motivated by consumers sensory reactions to the product, such as hearing, sight, or sense of touch [76]. Therefore, when consumers see a piece of apparel they like, the good sensation they experience may result in their interest in the product. They will thus want to use the like, comment, or share function to express their emotional reaction to the post.

## 5.1.6. Diferences between the central route and peripheral route

The e<sup>f</sup>ect of the peripheral route was stronger than it was for the central route for H7 and H8, but the di<sup>f</sup>erence was only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for H8. For H8, consumers tend to purchase a product when they are in duced to do so by some external cues such as post popularity and post esthetics rather than through deliberating about the information. The result of H9 indicated that after consumers respond to a post, they will tend to purchase the product through the post when they are deliberate about the informational content. The results indicate that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of the central route is stronger than that of the peripheral route for H5, H6, and H9, but the di<sup>f</sup>erence was only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for H9. There was a main <sup>fi</sup>nding that consumers who process through the peripheral route will directly develop further purchase intention if they like the post, and those who process through the central route will tend to respond before they decide to purchase. According to Bhattacherjee and Sanford [8], consumers who process through the central route will spend more time and e<sup>f</sup>ort evaluating the related opinions of the target. They will tend to respond by asking some questions about the product to evaluate their purchase decision, or they may click like or share the post to keep a record of the post. Therefore, consumers processing with central route will spend more time evaluating their purchase decision.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

The factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of central route factors and peripheral route factors was examined in this study. Chang et al. [10] and Filieri and McLeay [11] used the ELM as the research model. However, they only discussed the e<sup>f</sup>ect of central route factors and peripheral route factors on consumer message adoption, and few of these researchers tested the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of these routes as factors related to changes in consumer attitudes. In addition, this study successfully proved the factorial e<sup>f</sup>ect of the central route on consumers’ perceived informativeness and perceived persuasiveness.

Although many previous studies have used the ELM as a research framework to investigate consumer intention or changes in behavior toward online advertisements or website commentary [12,83,30], few of them have discussed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of Facebook Second-hand Mar ketplace characteristics on consumer intention and behavior. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls some gaps left by Chen et al. [49], who investigated impulsive purchasing behavior on Facebook Marketplace. The present study classi<sup>fi</sup>ed consumers’ decision-making processes as through either the central route or peripheral route and discussed which route in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers more under di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions. To test the e<sup>f</sup>ect of product types on consumer decision making under di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions, 16 scenarios for the central route and peripheral route were designed to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence strength on consumers perceived informative ness and the perceived persuasiveness of these characteristics at different levels. In conclusion, compared to the results of Chen et al. [49], there are some additional <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study.

The e<sup>f</sup>ect of product types on all the hypotheses in this study was tested. Most previous studies compared the di<sup>f</sup>erences between hedonic products and utilitarian products [4,6,75,76], but few of them discussed the di<sup>f</sup>erences in consumer attitudes, responses, and pur chase intention as they related to the type of product, i.e., whether it was hedonic or utilitarian. Thus, a laptop and apparel were chosen as the utilitarian product and hedonic products, respectively, to compare the e<sup>f</sup>ect of product type on each hypothesis. In conclusion, this study provided evidence that product type will a<sup>f</sup>ect consumer attitudes and responses, and these results may contribute to future study about the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of product type.

It was found that consumers who process messages through the peripheral route tend to purchase a second-hand product through the post immediately if they like the post. However, consumers who process messages through the central route tend to respond to the post before they purchase the product. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study may contribute to applications in future studies or to extended investigation about consumer purchase decisions within the ELM framework.

## 5.3. Managerial implications

The results of this study showed consumers will focus on a post with more complete and accurate information content and, in turn, believe the post is a good source from which to buy a product. Therefore, the sellers on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace are suggested to provide comprehensive messages about products to consumers as much as possible. Particularly, sellers usually sell second-hand products rather than new products on this marketplace, so consumers will be more careful about the product’s real status. Moreover, it is important to provide complete, accurate product information when selling utilitarian product as compared to when selling hedonic products. That is, consumers purchasing a utilitarian product focus on its functional performance because a utilitarian product is used to respond to some necessity or to accomplish mundane tasks. Therefore, the seller should provide detailed information about a utilitarian product’s function or actual status to reduce consumer suspicions.

Among the four manipulations, post popularity had the strongest e<sup>f</sup>ect on consumers’ perceived persuasiveness compared to the other characteristics such as information completeness, information accuracy, and post esthetics. As online transactions involve many uncertainties and risks, consumer decisions about whether to believe the post content depend on the persuasive strength of the post. Therefore, it is important for the Facebook Second-hand Marketplace seller to enhance the number of likes, comments, and shares. A higher degree of post popularity makes consumers believe the post is a good source from which to buy a product.

According to the results of the data analysis, consumers are more likely to respond to a post when they feel favorable toward the post. Therefore, this study suggests that Facebook Second-hand Marketplace sellers should make some e<sup>f</sup>ort to enhance consumers’ positive impression or favorable attitudes toward a post, such as providing an interesting introduction or a beautiful picture of the product. Highly impulsive people are especially more likely to respond to a post if the post makes them feel joyful or excited [16]. When consumers think a large number of people are following a post, they will tend to regard the post content as believable. In this situation, the persuasive strength of the post will be stronger.

A post on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace is di<sup>f</sup>erent from other Facebook posts because it is aimed at selling second-hand pro ducts. Therefore, a favorable attitude will be a<sup>f</sup>ected by whether consumers think the post content is trustworthy. It is important for sellers to make consumers believe that a given post is providing more informative or persuasive content than its counterparts. The relationship between perceived informativeness and attitude toward post is sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cantly stronger when consumers are looking at utilitarian products as compared to hedonic products. Therefore, the Facebook Second-hand Marketplace seller is suggested to provide more detailed, comprehensive introduction to highlight the functional performance of utilitarian products and their real status. More detailed and comprehensive information about a product can reduce consumer suspicions and the degree of uncertainty to a product’s real condition, and they are more likely to regard the post as a safe channel from which to buy the product.

Positive emotions toward a post will make consumers more likely to take further action such as responding or generating purchase intention. Highly impulsive people are more likely to respond to a post. It is suggested that when consumers ask questions about a product on a post, the seller responds to the questions with a patient, polite attitude. When consumers feel the seller is sincere and honest, they will tend to have good impression of the post. Moreover, this study suggests that sellers should pay attention to those who respond to the post because they are more likely to buy the product in the future. That is, consumers who respond to the post may have more interest in obtaining detailed information about the product. Therefore, good interaction will provide more information and also strengthen positive impressions of a post, in turn making it more likely that consumers will buy the product via the post.

Although Facebook Second-hand Marketplace di<sup>f</sup>ers from other online C2C platforms or online advertisements, the function of a Facebook post to stimulate purchases is similar to that of other online advertisements [31]. Therefore, all of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be applied to other online advertisements or shopping platforms. According to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, consumers tend to process through the central route when purchasing utilitarian products. Therefore, it is suggested that online sellers focus on providing complete, accurate messages to consumers when selling utilitarian products.

According to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, consumers care comparatively less about the degree of informativeness when purchasing hedonic products. Consumers tend to process through the peripheral route when purchasing hedonic products, and their purchase decision is mainly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their feelings or sensory experiences related to the product rather than by the functional performance of the product [76]. Therefore, it is suggested that online sellers provide esthetic pictures or interesting introductions to attract consumer attention when selling hedonic products. In conclusion, it is suggested that online sellers should focus on central route factors when selling utilitarian products and should focus on peripheral route factors when selling hedonic products.

## 5.4. Limitations and directions for future research

There are some limitations in this study. Regarding the product choice, product brand, and the moderator, the product types in this study were limited to two products: a laptop as a utilitarian and apparel as a hedonic product. These two products might not represent all hedonic and utilitarian products on Facebook Second-hand Marketplace as there are more di<sup>f</sup>erent products sold on the platform. In future studies, the discussion about product types could involve the use of other objects as products to test their e<sup>f</sup>ects on consumer attitudes and behavior. Additionally, this study chose Apple Macbook Air as utilitarian product and Adidas hoodie as hedonic product for scenario design. This study does not specify brand as a variable. Yet, brand could be a critical issue when discussing utilitarian and hedonic value of a product (e.g., [80]). Future studies can extend this research to discuss the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of di<sup>f</sup>erent brands within the same product type. For the selection of the moderator, as previously mentioned in the literature, prior experience might also be a moderator that strengthens or weakens the relationships between attitude and behavioral actions [83]. If consumers have experiences with purchasing through Facebook Second-hand Marketplace, they might tend to purchase again. Future studies can take this moderator into consideration.

As for the design of the questionnaires, when distributing the questionnaires to Facebook Second-hand Marketplace groups, the <sup>fi</sup>rst group with more members limited the area where the users were. This might be because of the Facebook algorithm. In this case, when conducting this study, the second-hand marketplace appeared is the area where the authors were located. Thus, future studies might be able to use the same framework to see whether there is an area di<sup>f</sup>erence related to consumer attitudes and behavioral actions. Moreover, concerning attitudes and behavioral action, in this study, di<sup>f</sup>erent scenarios were designed that required participants to reply to items related to attitude and action intention for that particular scenario. Future studies might extend this to a direct response or purchase behavior to test whether consumers will actually respond to the post or purchase the target items.
