# ARE FOREIGN AND DOMESTIC INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY PROFESSIONALS COMPLEMENTS OR SUBSTITUTES?<sup>1</sup>

Sunil Mithas Muma College of Business, University of South Florida Tampa, FL, U.S.A. {smithas@usf.edu}

Yanzhen Chen School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology Hong Kong, CHINA {imyanzhen@ust.hk}

Che-Wei Liu Kelley School of Business, Indiana University Bloomington, IN, U.S.A. {cwliu@iu.edu}

Kunsoo Han Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University Montréal, QC, CANADA {kunsoo.han@mcgill.ca}

The globalization of work raises important questions related to the employment of workers across geographies and how the complementarity or substitution of workers across country borders influences firm profitability. In particular, tension often exists regarding the substitution or complementarity of workers located outside the U.S. or within the U.S. for American firms. We investigate this question in the context of information technology (IT) professionals and assess how domestic and foreign IT professionals contribute to firm profit by utilizing a rare firm-level dataset with information on the locational composition of IT professionals within and outside the U.S. Exploiting a labor market supply-side exogenous shock induced by the American Competitiveness in the Twenty-First Century Act (AC21), which increased the availability of H-1B visas in the U.S. in 2001, we find that foreign IT professionals located offshore and American IT professionals located onshore complement each other in generating profits. Our model and empirical findings are important both for informing firm choices and for shaping and creating public policies that so far appear to have been informed more by emotion than by data and science.

Keywords: IT professionals, foreign workers, domestic workers, offshore, onshore, globalization, employment, geographic composition, profits, AC21 shock

## Introduction

The globalization of work has significant implications not only for firm profits but also for where firms employ workers. Prior work has argued that globalization allows firms to become more competitive, innovative, and profitable by acquiring ideas and inputs from multiple locations and by serving diverse markets (Ghemawat, 2007; Ravichandran et al., 2017). In

2019, the majority-owned foreign affiliates of U.S. parent companies generated nearly \$6.7 trillion in total sales by selling abroad to foreign customers.<sup>2</sup> Foreign sales accounted for 33% of aggregate revenue for the S&P 500 in 2014.<sup>3</sup> One of the key advantages of globalization is firms’ ability to access global talent to reduce costs and cycle time, as well as to improve productivity, quality, and innovation capacity (Mithas et al., 2017; Ravichandran et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2020b; Su & Levina, 2011). However, access to global talent outside the U.S. and hiring workers abroad raise questions about the substitution and complementarity between foreign workers located offshore and American workers located onshore, and how that may impact firm profitability.<sup>4</sup>

How the employment of foreign and domestic information technology (IT) professionals relates to firm profits is a question of significant interest to researchers and policymakers alike. From a research perspective, the effect of multinational enterprise (MNE) activities related to their hiring choices in the U.S. and foreign markets is of particular importance: such firms are key employers of IT workers in the U.S., as well as foreign workers in markets abroad to serve local needs there. Furthermore, the IT profession is often cited as one of the most globalized of all occupations, with significant mobility of work and workers across country borders (Slaughter, 2014; Whitaker et al., 2019). Although U.S. firms employ many foreign-born IT professionals within the U.S., they are treated as domestic American workers in this study if they are located within the U.S. Media reports suggest that U.S. firms hire workers abroad because of limits on how many foreign-born workers can be brought to the U.S. through immigration or work visas, the lack of needed skills or talent within the U.S., and the need to use dispersed resources to serve global markets in a more cost-effective manner.

From a policy perspective, there is concern regarding how hiring workers across geographies affects firm profitability, with broader concerns relating to the substitution or complementarity among workers. Implicitly, policymakers often assume that domestic workers in the U.S. and foreign workers located abroad are “perfect substitutes” for each other, sometimes leading to directives and/or visa/immigration and tax policies that can create serious disruption to individuals and firms (Hackman & Korn, 2020). This misconception is a manifestation of the “lump of labor” fallacy (Krugman, 2003), in that there exists a finite amount of work to do; therefore, jobs at different locations crowd out each other, or gains “there” equate to job losses “here.”<sup>5</sup> Unfortunately, despite the importance of this substitution versus complementarity question, there has been very little empirical research to verify these assumptions at the firm level, particularly with respect to high-skill IT professionals, partly due to the scarcity of good-quality firm-level data on the locational composition of workers.

Against this backdrop, the current study examines the focal research question: How do foreign and domestic IT professionals influence the profits of U.S. firms as potential substitutes or complements? Empirically, there are at least two challenges in investigating this question. First, high-quality firm-level data are rare. Firms are reluctant to share a breakdown of their employees between the U.S. and abroad (Yang, 2011), partly in response to negative public perceptions of the outsourcing and offshoring activities of MNEs (Lacity & Rudramuniyaiah, 2009). Some companies that used to provide such data earlier stopped doing so publicly later on; IBM is an example of a firm that stopped reporting its U.S. headcount in 2009 (Yang, 2011). Even if companies release such data, the decision to do so is usually strategic. For example, AT&T claimed that its merger with T-Mobile could create between 55,000 and 96,000 jobs in the U.S. (Schatz & Bensinger, 2011). Apple claimed that it created 514,000 jobs in the U.S. when it faced controversy over its labor practices overseas (Tsukayama, 2012). Due to the lack of firm-level data, prior studies in the economics literature have often investigated issues related to the complementarity versus substitution between foreign and American workers using industry-level data or broad classifications across occupations (e.g., skilled versus unskilled, production versus nonproduction, and routine versus nonroutine) (e.g., Ebenstein et al., 2015; Feenstra & Hanson, 1996, 1999). Because of the higher levels of aggregation involved in these studies, they either provide somewhat suggestive evidence or mixed findings, thereby leaving policy debates unresolved or inapplicable to firm-level employment questions that are often the subject of policy debates. No study to date has used firm-level data for a specific occupation (such as IT professionals) as we do in this paper; our dataset is one of the very few that provides direct observation on firm-level IT employment details across geographies. A second challenge in answering these questions is the general difficulty in assessing causality due to common omitted variables that may influence the employment of IT professionals across geographies and profit-related decisions, besides other potential concerns such as measurement error or reverse causality (Mithas et al., 2022).

We address these issues by leveraging a relatively unobtrusive and rare dataset on the geographic distribution of IT professionals for the first time, and by addressing causality issues using a variety of identification strategies such as an instrumental variable (IV) approach, a control function (CF) approach, and theory-driven 3SLS simultaneous equation models (Mithas et al., 2022). We leverage an exogenous shock to the supply of domestic IT employment in the United States resulting from an immigration act passed by Congress in October 2000—The American Competitiveness in the Twenty-

First Century Act (AC21 hereafter)<sup>6</sup>—which regulates hiring and visas for the employment of foreign professionals in the U.S. Our instrumental variable captures differences in the AC21 sensitivity of IT occupations across industries based on the science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) knowledge required across industries. Using a sample of 369 firm-year observations for firms in the U.S. over the 2000-2003 period, we found that foreign and domestic IT professionals complement each other with respect to influencing U.S. firms profits. From a theoretical perspective, this research contributes to our understanding of the complementarity versus substitution relationship between foreign and domestic jobs for skilled workers at a highly granular level (firm-level) for one specific occupation (i.e., IT professionals).

Empirically, this study provides a much more direct test and evidence for complementarity versus substitution, a significant improvement from prior studies that have used industry-level data or much broader occupational categories such as skilled versus unskilled, production versus nonproduction, and routine versus nonroutine (e.g., Ebenstein et al., 2015; Feenstra & Hanson, 1996, 1999). Because theoretical mechanisms invoked in the prior literature greatly rely on firm-level behaviors for a specific occupation, our study provides a more direct test of these mechanisms by studying a specific occupation at the firm level.

Our work also extends prior research leveraging data at the individual IT worker level to examine the labor market interactions of foreign and American IT professionals located in the U.S. (Mithas & Lucas, 2010) and the effect of MNE activity related to the offshoring and employment of IT professionals in Indian captive centers on U.S. IT professionals (Tambe & Hitt, 2010, 2012a). Our contribution is distinct from prior work that provides only indirect evidence for complementarity using salary data on American and foreignborn IT professionals located in the U.S. For example, Lucas and Mithas (2011) discussed likely complementarity in a short derivative article based on their 2010 Management Science article (Mithas & Lucas, 2010), which studied the compensation of foreign-born and American IT professionals located in the U.S. at the individual worker level. There have been other studies since Mithas and Lucas (2010); in general, they have documented relatively higher wages for temporary or immigrant professionals after controlling for observable human capital attributes such as education and experience (Drago, 2015; Hunt, 2011; Lofstrom & Hayes, 2011). Hunt (2011) uses data from NSCG; Drago (2015) uses LCA data from 2013 and NSCG data from 2003 and 2010; and Lofstrom and Hayes (2011) use data from the USCIS. In contrast, our work uses firm-level analysis and directly addresses the question about the substitution or complementarity of IT professionals located in the U.S. and abroad in generating profits.

Likewise, we also extend research linking IT use with firmlevel employment (Atasoy et al., 2016), in that we focus sharply on the employment of IT professionals and disaggregate firm-level employment in terms of American and foreign IT professionals. Finally, our work contributes to the broader complementarity research in IS: our evidence for the complementarity of domestic and foreign IT professionals augments other studies that focus on the complementarity of IT investments with organizational practices such as human resource and information systems practices (e.g., Aral et al., 2012; Tafti et al., 2022), customer involvement (Saldanha et al., 2017), early versus later stage IT-business alignment (Saldanha et al., 2020a), and R&D investment (Ravichandran et al., 2017).

## Theoretical Framework

## Background and Prior Literature

Prior studies on MNE activities have examined employment transfer within MNEs (i.e., the share of foreign affiliate employment in MNEs’ total employment) by using either MNE data or industry-level data (aggregating the proxy for production transfer within MNEs at the industry level). The results of these studies with MNE-level data by Head and Ries (2002) for Japan, Hansson (2005) for Sweden, and Becker et al. (2013) for Germany generally show that the effects of employment transfer within MNEs are fairly small.

To the best of our knowledge, to date, no prior study has assessed the complementarity versus substitution arguments at the firm level for knowledge workers located within and outside the U.S. by focusing on a particular occupation. Our study makes a unique and distinct contribution by assessing the extent to which foreign IT professionals located outside the U.S. complement or substitute for IT professionals located in the U.S. in terms of influencing firm profits. As we previously noted, recent work in information systems has focused on the complementarity of IT investments with other practices and investments (Aral et al., 2012; Ravichandran et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2017), and this work implicitly recognizes the complementarity among workers across different functional areas or geographies. For example, Ravichandran et al. (2017) found that the mitigating effect of IT in diminishing returns to R&D is stronger when firms face greater geographical complexity, measured by the geographic distribution of inventors across countries.

The focus on IT professionals is consistent with scholarly interest in “studies employing finer disaggregations” (Crinò, 2009, p. 215) beyond the industry level or broad classifications across occupations (e.g., skilled versus unskilled or production versus nonproduction workers) that a majority of prior studies use. The period of this study from the early 2000s is also interesting because IT outsourcing was taking off around this time in a significant way due to the Y2K crisis and the dot-com bust, when firms were under a considerable amount of pressure to reduce costs (Carmel & Agarwal, 2002; Mithas & Whitaker, 2007; Tambe & Hitt, 2010). Most importantly, because the issues related to American and foreign employment were not as politicized then as they are now, using archival data that are relatively uncontaminated by political debates (which became far sharper later) makes this research (which answers a fundamental and enduring question) that much more valuable. Indeed, IS researchers often use archival data stretching back to the 1990s or early 2000s as the most suitable dataset for addressing enduring and fundamental questions (Karhade & Dong, 2021; Tambe et al., 2020). A recent paper in the Journal of Political Economy uses data from the 1930s in Germany (Huber et al., 2021), suggesting that, despite the age of the data, what matters more is the importance and relevance of the research question itself.

## Theoretical Development: How Foreign and American IT Professionals Influence Firm Profits

Our review of the prior literature in economics and information systems indicates greater support for complementarity in the case of high-skilled professionals across countries than for perfect substitution among them. Although some studies have found that domestic and foreign labor are substitutes in MNE activities (see Mankiw & Swagel, 2006 and references cited therein), the strength of this relationship is weak. Quite a few studies reviewed by Mankiw and Swagel (2006) use BEA data on the activities of U.S. and foreign MNEs to indirectly infer complementarity versus substitution among workers using the wages and employment of workers, but without considering firm profits, as we do in this study. The substitutability between domestic and foreign labor is mainly driven by affiliates in other high-income countries, resulting from investments aimed to serve the local market to avoid trade barriers and transportation costs; moreover, it is much weaker with respect to employment in low-income affiliates. This type of substitutability does not apply to foreign IT professionals because a large number of them are hired in other low-income countries such as India.

Brainard and Riker (1997) use 1983-1992 BEA data to report on the low substitution between foreign affiliate employment and U.S. parent employment and far greater substitution between affiliates in low-income countries at similar levels of economic development.

Studies have also suggested that short-run substitutability switches into long-run complementarity. For example, Slaughter (1995) used data on the parents and affiliates in 32 industries between 1977 and 1989, and found that domestic and foreign labor are complements in the long run, even though in the short run, they are substitutes. This situation is likely to apply to IT professionals because firms have been experimenting with the use of offshore IT professionals for a while. In the end, whether foreign and American IT professionals are complements or substitutes is ultimately an empirical question.

We next present four arguments for why foreign and American IT professionals are likely to be complements in the profit function of the firm, based on prior work, while acknowledging the possibility of some weak substitution. First, the availability of foreign professionals in other markets can allow U.S. firms to obtain access to high-skill labor that they could not otherwise find in the U.S. market due to visa restrictions or the unwillingness of some professionals to relocate to the U.S. (Whitaker et al., 2019). These high-skill professionals collaborate with American professionals to help firms create new and innovative high-technology products and services, which are eventually reflected in firms’ profit streams.

This reasoning is supported by prior work using the knowledge-based view and expertise-based arguments in studying dispersed collaboration (Boh et al., 2007), as well as more recent work that argues for the role of IT in enabling globally dispersed virtual teams of R&D professionals to collaborate effectively (Ravichandran et al., 2017). While U.S. firms need IT professionals to expand their business and tailor their goods and services for global markets, they cannot satisfy all of their needs by restricting themselves to only U.S.- based domestic IT professionals. This is partly due to the presumed shortage of such highly skilled IT professionals in the U.S. (see Agarwal & Ferratt, 1999; Lewin et al., 2009; U.S. Department of Commerce, 1997) and visa restrictions. Even if firms could find technically qualified U.S. IT professionals, technical skills alone may not be sufficient to meet firms’ needs. This is because local responsiveness may require firms to hire IT professionals located abroad who are familiar with foreign cultures, languages, and business practices to help coordinate with foreign vendors and customers more effectively. Han and Mithas (2013) report on the complementarity between internal IT labor (presumably located in developed markets) and IT outsourcing (presumably provided by service providers that employ a large fraction of their IT professionals in locations abroad) in reducing non-IT operating costs, thereby providing indirect support for the above arguments.<sup>7</sup> Foreign IT professionals and those located in the U.S. may also influence firms’ overall profits because of the arguments above; however, our argument here is that foreign and U.S. professionals also show a complementary effect, given that they bring complementary skills and assets to create new products and services.

Second, using foreign labor in other markets can allow U.S. firms to customize IT systems, products, and services for world markets at competitive costs, leading to higher sales abroad (Chang & Gurbaxani, 2012; Mann, 2004). Prior research has suggested that higher sales in foreign affiliates create a demand for labor in U.S. parents through scale and scope effects (Mankiw & Swagel, 2006). This is because expansion abroad can facilitate growth in the scale of firm-wide activities (both in foreign affiliates and U.S. parents), thereby expanding employment in the U.S. (Ottaviano et al., 2013) through a “productivity effect” and increasing cost savings. In addition, expansion abroad can also change the scope or mix of U.S. parent activities to become more specialized in high-skill higher-end IT development, which may increase the employment of U.S. IT professionals within U.S. parent firms. These mechanisms also create a virtuous cycle whereby foreign labor can work with American counterparts to design further line extensions for foreign markets; it can also create opportunities for “reverse innovation” through which some products and services that were initially meant for foreign markets can now be brought to developed markets (Immelt et al., 2009) with enhanced profit opportunities, thus lending support to the complementarity argument. We do not take a position on whether the complementarity effect is stronger due to the cost advantage created by using labor outside the U.S. or due to the innovation advantage enabled by collaboration among U.S. and foreign professionals.<sup>8</sup>

Third, to the extent that a few of the foreign professionals in other markets are likely to have worked in the U.S. previously on work visas (Sachitanand, 2019), they can play an important role in spotting new opportunities in emerging markets that may not otherwise be obvious to employees located in the U.S., or to those who have never traveled to the U.S. Prior studies have shown that international graduates make significant contributions to U.S. innovative activity in terms of the total number of patents and patents issued to U.S. universities and other entities (Chellaraj et al., 2008; Hunt & Gauthier-Loiselle, 2010; Kerr & Lincoln, 2010). Likewise, prior work has recognized the important complementary roles of globally dispersed R&D professionals in firms’ innovation (Ravichandran et al., 2017). In the same spirit, foreign IT professionals located abroad are likely to contribute to the innovation capacity of U.S. firms when collaborating with American IT professionals, and some of that innovation should be reflected in firm profits. This argument relates to the complementarity that arises due to familiarity and the boundary-spanning skills of emigrant foreign professionals.

Fourth, foreign professionals located abroad are often willing to travel for extended periods across country borders, thus providing greater flexibility for U.S. firms to coordinate operations in different parts of the world. They often act as boundary spanners to bridge cultural issues arising in interactions with customers located outside the U.S. This boundary-spanning work requires frequent travel and work at night or early morning hours to coordinate with other IT professionals or business partners in other time zones (Krishna et al., 2004).

Note that our focus here is on understanding the complementary or multiplicative effect of foreign and American IT professionals in firms’ profit-generation processes, beyond the additive effects that are also implied in the reasoning above. Although the notion of complementarity implies multiplicative effects beyond additive effects, from a theoretical perspective, the arguments for additive effects are not completely orthogonal to the arguments for multiplicative effects. In the context of firms’ profit-generation processes, our key argument is that the joint effects of foreign and American IT professionals are beyond their separate additive effects; furthermore, much of our theorizing shows why firms benefit from such complementarity, with the magnitude of such complementarity to be ascertained in empirical analyses. Thus, we posit that foreign IT professionals abroad and domestic IT professionals in the United States are complements in influencing overall firm profits.

## Method

## Data and Variables

As previously noted, one of the primary strengths of this study is the use of a rare firm-level dataset with the locational composition of IT professionals to answer questions that have so far not been answered at this level of granularity. We estimate our empirical models using secondary data from two sources. First, the sample is composed of firms that are publicly listed in the U.S. and have operations abroad. We obtained data on the locational composition (American or foreign-based) of IT professionals from InformationWeek, whose survey data have been used in several academic studies (Rai et al., 1997; Ravichandran et al., 2017; Tafti et al., 2013). IT professionals are those involved in career clusters such as programming and software engineering, technical support, enterprise systems, database development and administration, web development and administration, network design and administration, digital media, and technical writing (Moncarz, 2002). This definition of IT professionals is consistent with how the Information Technology Association of America defines an information technology worker, as well as prior academic studies (Mithas & Lucas, 2010). The data used in this study pertain to information about a firm’s IT employment, IT spending, and IT outsourcing for the 2000-2003 period. These data were collected by InformationWeek as part of a more comprehensive survey to benchmark a firm’s IT infrastructure and managerial practices in its industry.

We matched the InformationWeek data with profit and industry data from Compustat for publicly traded firms. We used firm size (measured by total assets) and return-onassets data consolidated across a firm’s subsidiaries operating abroad, as reported in Compustat. Combining the data from the two sources resulted in an unbalanced panel consisting of 369 observations. We used return on assets (ROA) as our performance measure (dependent variable). We measured the number of IT employees within and outside the U.S. by ITEMPUS and ITEMPF, respectively. The InformationWeek data provide the number of full-time IT employees in the IT organization of a firm “in the U.S.,” and “outside the U.S.” It does not include “consultants, parttimers or contract workers.” We accounted for other investments and expenditures that compete with IT hiring across locations; these include the total number of employees (TOTEMP), money spent on IT outsourcing (ITOUT), research and development (RDEXP), investments in fixed assets such as buildings, computers, and machinery (PPE), and the dividend paid to shareholders (DIVIDEND).

## Empirical Models and Identification Strategies

We exploit an exogenous shock created by legislation passed by Congress that temporarily increased the supply of domestic IT labor located in the U.S. The key idea is to investigate how profitability responds to the foreign-U.S. IT employment interaction induced by an exogenously increased supply of IT employees in the United States. The exogenous shock we exploit is the American Competitiveness in the Twenty-First Century Act (AC21), which increased the H1-B annual limit by 70%, compared to that of the year 2000 for 2001-2003. Specifically, AC21 increased the maximum number of new H-1B visas issued per year from an annual cap of 115,000 in 2000 to 195,000 from 2001 to 2003 (AC21 Act, 2000).<sup>9</sup>

Because of the significant increase in the visa cap in 2001 to 195,000 from 115,000 in 2000, AC21 facilitated a huge new supply of H-1B visa workers in 2001, and indeed the number of H-1B petitions went up significantly in 2001, compared to 2000 (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2011).

However, in the following two years (years 2002 and 2003), due to the dot-com collapse, the 9/11 incident (the 9 September 2001 attack at the World Trade Center in New York City), and economic recession, the demand for IT professionals fell significantly and was significantly below the H-1B cap (U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2011; Mithas & Lucas, 2010). Therefore, we coded our AC21-based shock as a binary variable that takes the value of 1 in the year 2001 only and 0 otherwise.

Our empirical specification models profitability (ROA) as a function of all inputs, especially U.S. IT employees (ITEMPUS), foreign IT employees (ITEMPF), and their interactions (ITEMPUS×ITEMPF) as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} R O A _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} I T E M P U S _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} I T E M P F _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} I T E M P U S _ {i, t - 1} \times I T E M P F _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} I T O U T _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} T O T E M P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {6} R D E X P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {7} P P E _ {i, t - 1} + a _ {t} + c _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

We account for IT outsourcing expenditures (ITOUT), the total number of employees (TOTEMP), research and development expenditures (RDEXP), and investments in fixed assets such as buildings, computers, and machinery captured by PPE.

Note that we utilized the lagged values of foreign and American IT professionals in our ROA model to alleviate reverse causality concerns (i.e., ROA determining IT employment complementarity). We further ruled out concerns due to reverse causality by formally conducting a test in which we flipped the dependent variable, profitability (ROA), with our variable of interest: IT employment complementarity (ITEMPUS×ITEMPF). As expected, this test shows no relationship between the prior period ROA and the complementarity variable.

We used an instrumental variable (IV) to help identify the interaction between American and foreign IT professionals in generating profits. The IV approach addresses potential endogeneity arising from the simultaneity of the interaction term involving American and foreign IT employment and ROA due to omitted variables (e.g., a firm culture that emphasizes collaboration across geographies if one assumes such firms to be more profitable), and it can also help to address any other issues that may arise due to measurement error or reverse causality due to unknown or unavailable lagged effects (Greene, 2000). Because our variable of interest is the interaction term involving American and foreign IT employment, we used the interaction of the “AC21 sensitivity” variable and foreign IT employment to instrument the interaction term involving American and foreign IT employment.

We defined “AC21 sensitivity” as refining the exogenous shock with the STEM intensity of industries. In other words, AC21 sensitivity captures the interaction of AC21 and STEM intensities of industries. The key idea here is that although AC21 increases the supply of IT labor in the U.S, its impact varies across industries according to the extent to which STEM knowledge is required. Specifically, we adopted the shares of industry workers in STEM occupations from Muro et al. (2015) and Rothwell (2013) to measure the STEM intensities of industries, which take continuous values from 0 to 1. Muro et al. (2015) and Rothwell (2013) constructed this measure from two sources. They first measured the STEM knowledge intensity for each occupation using a detailed survey created by the Department of Labor’s Employment and Training Association regarding the level of knowledge in STEM and other domains needed in occupations. Then, they converted the STEM knowledge required in occupations into four-digit NAICS industries using the industry-occupation matrices from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Thus, “AC21 sensitivity” affects the IT labor supply from the U.S. by quantifying the effects of a supply shock from the domestic employment side on different industries.

Note that our instrumental variable is an interaction term involving AC21 and the STEM intensity of an industry. Even though the AC21 variable has a low correlation with ITEMPUS and ITEMPF (see Table 3), we found evidence for the relevance of the instrumental variable: it is significantly correlated with the interaction term involving foreign and American IT employment in our first-stage results with the expected sign, consistent with economic intuition. In addition, the first-stage F statistic is above 10, which further demonstrates the instrument’s relevance. Moreover, the instrumental variable satisfies the exclusion restriction on conceptual grounds because we do not expect AC21 to directly influence ROA; any effect on ROA mediated through IT employment is consistent with our arguments.

With the AC21 sensitivity-based instrumental variable, we investigate the relationship between IT employment in and outside the U.S. using two econometric methods of estimation: the two-stage least squares (2SLS) and control function approach.<sup>10</sup> Although 2SLS has been widely used in prior work, recent studies in IS use a control function approach as a technique to address endogeneity (Choi et al., 2021; Ho et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2017). This approach generalizes Heckman’s two-stage estimator (Garen, 1984; Wooldridge, 2015) for continuous treatment variables. In this approach, after the first stage, as in 2SLS, we obtained residuals $( \tilde { \eta } _ { c o m p } )$ . We then controlled for selection bias $( \tilde { \eta } _ { c o m p }$ and $\tilde { \eta } _ { \ c o m p } ^ { 2 } )$ and unobserved heterogeneity $( I T E M P U S _ { t - 1 } \times I T E M P F _ { t - 1 } \times \tilde { \eta } _ { c o m p }$ and $I T E M P U S _ { t - 1 } \times$ $I T E M P F _ { t - 1 } \times \tilde { \eta } _ { ~ c o m p } ^ { 2 } )$ in the second stage using these residuals.

As an additional exploratory analysis and robustness check, we estimated a three-stage least squares (3SLS) model utilizing the exogenous shock of AC21. This structural equation model approach evaluates the impact of foreign IT professionals on profitability by combining the shock-based identification strategy with the theory-based specification. The model specification relies on causal assumptions from production theory and growth theory. The former describes firm investment, while the latter depicts production. In this robustness check, we modeled profitability (ROA) as a function of all inputs, especially U.S. IT employees (ITEMPUS), employment induced by the exogenous shock $( E X O \_ I T U S \ = \ A C 2 I { \times } I T E M P U S )$ , foreign IT employees (ITEMPF), and exogenous event-augmented IT interactions among IT professionals (EXO\_ITUS×ITEMPF) using the following empirical specification:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} R O A _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} I T E M P U S _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} I T E M P F _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} E X O \_ I T U S _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} E X O \_ I T U S _ {i, t - 1} \times I T E M P F _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} I T O U T _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {6} T O T E M P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {7} R D E X P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {8} P P E _ {i, t - 1} + a _ {t} + c _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where EXO\_ITUS captures the exogenous increase in IT employment in the U.S. induced by the AC21 law. In other words, it is proxied by AC21×ITEMPUS. Our key coefficient of interest is $\beta _ { 4 } ,$ which shows the impact of an exogenous change on the interaction between IT professionals in and outside the U.S. with respect to firm profitability.

For the 3SLS estimation, we also modeled investment in foreign IT professionals and estimated that model jointly with Equation (2). This model includes ROA (because firms’ profits fund investments in IT professionals and other inputs) and dividends. Following Atasoy et al. (2016), we also controlled for firm size and sales growth in the same equation using the following specification:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} I T E M P F _ {i, t} & = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} R O A _ {t} + \alpha_ {2} I T E M P U S _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {3} A C 2 1 \times I T E M P U S _ {i, t} \\ & + \alpha_ {4} I T O U T _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {5} T O T E M P _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {6} R D E X P _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {7} P P E _ {i, t} \\ & + \alpha_ {8} D I V I D E N D _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {9} S I Z E _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {1 0} S A L E S G R O W T H _ {i, t} \\ & + b _ {t} + d _ {i} + \mu_ {i, t}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

## Discussion

## Main Findings

This study provides several new insights by using highly granular firm-level data for the locational composition of IT professionals for the first time, and by leveraging an instrumental variable approach using an exogenous shock in the form of a legislative act that temporarily increased the supply of IT professionals in the U.S. Our findings provide a more definitive confirmation for the tentative evidence of a similar relationship suggested in prior work.

For example, Mankiw and Swagel (2006, p. 1027) specifically note the tentative nature of the evidence: “The empirical evidence to date, while still tentative, actually suggests that increased employment in the overseas affiliates of U.S. multinationals is associated with more employment in the U.S. parent rather than less.”

Given the continued importance of the question regarding whether foreign and American IT professionals are complements or substitutes as reflected in public and policy discourses on this topic, along with managerial choices, our findings should provide a basis for informing policies and decisions for deploying offshore and onshore workers. We find evidence for complementarity between American and foreign IT professionals in terms of influencing firm profits. The results imply that IT professionals in foreign countries and the United States contribute to profitability by working synergistically.

Our findings are robust to alternative explanations. For instance, we alleviate concerns about reverse causality as an alternative explanation by using the lagged values of foreign and American IT professionals in our ROA model, and by conducting a formal test, as described in the Method section. However, because our sample period coincides with the dotcom bubble crash, we suggest caution when generalizing our findings. We do not believe that the dot-com phenomenon itself can account for our findings, given that we already include year-fixed effects in our models, which consider year-specific events (such as the dot-com bubble) that may affect IT employment decisions.<sup>11</sup> Nevertheless, we call for further research to test the generalizability of our findings in other settings and periods as part of “normal” science (Kuhn, 1996; Shadish et al., 2002). On the whole, our main findings are supported by theoretical arguments with justifications from prior work and instrumental variable approaches; the triangulation of conclusions from multiple methods points to the robustness of our findings while creating opportunities for further theoretical or empirical research.

## Implications for Research

We note several research implications of our study. First, the finding that IT human capital propels profits complements the findings of prior research on the business value of IT and outsourcing literature (e.g., Dedrick et al., 2003; Dibbern et al., 2004), which suggests a positive association between IT investment and productivity (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1996; Tambe & Hitt, 2012b), market value (Bharadwaj et al., 1999; Kohli et al., 2012), profitability (Mithas et al., 2012), product variety (Gao & Hitt, 2012), innovation (Kleis et al., 2012; Ravichandran et al., 2017), and customer satisfaction (Mithas et al., 2016). In contrast to prior work, which generally focuses more on investments in hardware or software, this work focuses more sharply on IT human capital and provides evidence for its importance in today’s knowledge economy, based on the information and intangible capital to which IT professionals contribute (Colvin, 2015; Tafti et al., 2022).

We propose several theoretical arguments and explanations for the complementary roles of foreign and domestic IT professionals in firms’ profit function by drawing on prior work in international business, economics, and information systems. The finding documenting the complementarity of IT professionals abroad and those in the U.S. contradicts arguments that treat them as perfect substitutes, as is often assumed in emotionally charged debates on the subject. Our results, showing the complementarity of American and foreign IT professionals using firm-level data on the locational composition of professionals, extend the findings of prior work, which indirectly show the reciprocity of American and foreign-born professionals located only within the U.S. by examining their relative compensation (Mithas & Lucas, 2010) at the individual level. Other studies provide indirect evidence for complementarity by comparing the compensation of IT professionals employed by MNEs across India and the U.S. For example, recent work finds a greater marginal effect of firm-specific IT experience in India but a greater marginal effect of MBA education in the U.S. for IT professionals, suggesting that MNEs do not value the human capital of IT professionals across geographies in the same way (Whitaker et al., 2019). To the extent that many of the policy debates relate to firms’ choices and behavior regarding the employment of workers in and outside the U.S., our theoretical arguments and empirical results can spur further evidence-based research, dialogue, and policy formulation on this topic.

## Implications for Practice

Our findings have important policy and managerial implications. One implication from a policy perspective is that the hiring of foreign IT professionals by U.S. firms abroad does not automatically mean that doing so will adversely impact the employment of domestic IT professionals in the United States. Any policies that presume perfect substitution between foreign professionals located abroad and American IT professionals located in the U.S. can discourage MNEs from leveraging potential synergies; this mistaken zero-sum belief can be counterproductive for both American IT professionals and American firms. Despite prior research showing complementarity between foreign-born professionals located in the U.S. and native American professionals (e.g., Lofstrom & Hayes, 2011; Mithas & Lucas, 2010; Ottaviano et al., 2013; Zavodny, 2018), obsolete assumptions about substitution among workers persist (Abi-Habib & Singh, 2020; Rosenthal & Smith, 2020). Even in intracountry settings, generalizing from our findings would suggest that one should consider the asymmetric effects and relative costs and benefits of policy choices. Policies restricting the supply of highly skilled professionals for U.S. firms may force them to increase hiring overseas (Glennon, 2020; Lewin et al., 2009), contrary to the espoused rationale to save jobs in the U.S.

From a managerial perspective, our findings suggest that foreign and American IT professionals do not perfectly substitute for each other in the profit functions of firms, despite the similarity of their job descriptions when one considers the trajectory of business growth. The finding that foreign and American IT professionals complement each other with respect to firms’ profits suggests that American and foreign workers together can make their firms more profitable, thereby allowing them to reinvest some of those profits into further innovation and subsequent job growth. Managers should treat foreign and American IT professionals as complements and should use their complementary skills to create products and services for global markets.

Finally, our study also has implications for educational institutions and universities; they need to revise their curricula to impart an understanding of foreign languages, cultures, and business practices while enhancing practical and cultural intelligence so that American workers can more effectively collaborate with business partners and colleagues in other geographies and time zones (Joseph et al., 2010; Langer et al., 2014; Rockstuhl et al., 2013). Equally, educational institutions should equip American workers with the STEM skills that are needed by employers; such a STEM-proficient workforce is also likely to better meet workplace needs as the economy is further digitized, beyond the benefits of STEM education to individual workers (Ge et al., 2020).

## Limitations and Suggestions for Further Research

First, this study focuses on the employment of American and foreign IT professionals. Further research is needed to understand how firms should optimize the composition of their labor force across countries, and which skills should be sourced from where. If business skills are valued more than technical skills for IT professionals in the U.S. (Mithas & Krishnan, 2008), does it mean that firms are sourcing technical skills from other countries by hiring IT professionals overseas? If so, is that reflected in premiums for technical skills in other countries such as China, Ireland, and Israel? There is also a need for studies in other professions that consider skills such as behavioral and customer interaction skills (Ramasubbu et al., 2008). Second, the microdata on firms’ decisions and timing to simultaneously lay off workers in the U.S. and expand overseas, without any associated changes in business activity abroad, would help answer the counterfactual questions of complementarity and substitutability even more clearly.

Finally, to the extent that the complementarity between foreign and American IT professionals results from collaboration between them, one would expect the level of collaboration intensity to influence the complementarity effects.<sup>12</sup> We call for further research to explore such a potential three-way interaction effect by operationalizing the extent of collaboration, and by considering the use of configurational approaches that may be promising in terms of exploring complex interaction effects (Ragin, 2008).

To conclude, this study explores how the employment of domestic IT professionals located in the U.S. and foreign IT professionals located abroad jointly influence firm profits. Using data on the locational composition of IT professionals for U.S. firms, we find that foreign and domestic IT professionals complement each other with respect to U.S. firms’ profits. Put differently, a job in Bangalore can very well create or save a job in Buffalo while also improving firm profits, potentially creating a win-win for everyone.

## About the Authors

Sunil Mithas is a World Class Scholar and Professor at the Muma College of Business. He also serves as a director (Rankings and Reputation) at the Muma College of Business. Identified as an MSI Young Scholar by the Marketing Science Institute, Sunil is a Distinguished Fellow of the Information Systems Society of INFORMS, and he ranks among the foremost information systems scholars in the world. Previously, Sunil was the Ralph J. Tyser Professor of Information Systems at the Robert H. Smith School of Business at the University of Maryland, where he served as associate chair, and co-directed two centers, namely the Center for

Digital Innovation, Technology and Strategy and the Center for Excellence in Service. He is a visiting professorial fellow at the UNSW Business School, Sydney, and has held visiting positions at the University of California, Davis, UNSW, University of Mannheim, and HKUST, Hong Kong. He is the author of two books, and his research published in top journals and conferences have won multiple best-paper awards, and featured in practiceoriented publications such as the MIT Sloan Management Review, Management Business Review, and Bloomberg. He has consulted and conducted research with a range of organizations including A. T. Kearney, EY, Johnson & Johnson, the Social Security Administration, U.S. Census Bureau, and the Tata Group.

Yanzhen Chen is an assistant professor at HKUST. She received her Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Austin. Her research focuses on the future of labor markets, fintech, health IT, and statistical learning. Her studies have been published in MIS Quarterly, POMS and featured by Bloomberg Businessweek. She has been invited to present her work to the Federal Reserve Bank in Philadelphia and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, U.S. Department of Treasury. She also has methodological work published in premier journals of machine learning and statistics such as JMLR and Biometrika.

Che-Wei Liu is an assistant professor of information systems at the Kelley School of Business, Indiana University. He completed his Ph.D. at the University of Maryland. His research interests include healthcare IT, business value of IT, IT labor, and IT adoptions (fintech and artificial intelligence). His research has been accepted in premier journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, and he has also been awarded the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) Best Paper runner-up.

Kunsoo Han is a Bensadoun Faculty Scholar and an associate professor of information systems. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota, and his B.S. and M.S. from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). Prior to joining academia, he worked at a large IT consulting company in Korea. He teaches undergraduate courses on systems analysis and modeling and information systems, and a Ph.D. seminar on the economics of IT. His research interests include IT outsourcing, the business value of IT, societal impacts of IT, and IT-enabled channels. His work has been published in Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Production and Operations Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and MIT Sloan Management Review, among other outlets. He has won Best-Paper awards at several conferences, including the INFORMS Conference on Information Systems and Technology (CIST).
