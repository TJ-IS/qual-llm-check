# Facilitating Complex Product Choices on E-commerce Sites: An Unconscious Thought and Circadian Preference Perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Unconscious thought theory Online shopping Consumer choice Circadian preference Human-computer interaction

## A B S T R A C T

E-commerce consumers often face complex product choices and have to make decisions at various times of the day. Extant research has primarily focused on providing decision aids and eliminating “distracting” features to help consumers' conscious thought when making complex decisions. Our research adopts a novel perspective that consumers' unconscious thought may have some advantages and be exploited to help consumers make complex purchase decisions, especially when one's circadian preference (optimal time of day for decision-making, e.g., morning vs. evening) is not met. Drawing on the Unconscious Thought Theory (UTT), we developed two hypotheses regarding the efects of consumers' unconscious thought and its interaction with consumer circadian preference on decision quality. A controlled experiment was conducted to test our hypotheses. The findings show that unconscious thought outperforms conscious thought for making complex product choices in general and such efects are amplified when there is an asynchrony between one's circadian preference and the time of decision. The new circadian synchrony perspective enriches our understanding of the efects of unconscious thought on decision-making, helps explain the somewhat mixed findings in prior work on UTT, and thus contributes back to the UTT literature. The findings have implications about how unconscious thought can be leveraged to facilitate online shopping, and underscores the importance of considering consumer's circadian preference and time of decision.

## 1. Introduction

Consumers do not always achieve their goal of making optimal product choices, largely due to limited information processing capacity [35]. One reason is the complexity of purchase decisions: e-commerce consumers often are bombarded with a colossal amount of information about products and their attributes, other consumers' evaluations and opinions, and personalized recommendations from the online retailers (e.g., Amazon's Gold Box). Moreover, distracting website features — such as pop-ups, banner ads, and animation — are commonly employed by online retailers [15.36]. Such distracting website features further increase the dificulty for consumers to make optimal purchase deci sions. Choosing a sub-optimal product can result in consumer regret and product return [51,94]. Hence, it has been a focal topic in Information Systems (IS) research to study how to help consumers make better purchase decisions in the face of complexity in online shopping [e.g., 19, 36, 63, 64].

To date, IS research has primarily focused on how to reduce the complexity and resulting uncertainty associated with online purchases and how to promote consumers' learning in conscious ways. For example, IS researchers have studied how the design features of e-commerce websites can aid or impede consumers' information processing [53,54,88]. Jiang and Benbasat [54] showed that presentation formats (e.g., virtual product experience and video) on an e-commerce website outperformed static pictures in facilitating consumers' learning of products. Accordingly, extant research typically cautions against the rampant use of distracting website features, such as pop-ups, banner ads, and animations, because they tend to disrupt consumers' conscious information processing process [e.g., 11, 79].

Unlike extant IS research that has focused on consumers' conscious information processing, this research adopts a novel perspective that sheds light on unconscious thought in facilitating complex decision making in online shopping. Research on cognitive and social psy chology acknowledges two distinct modes of thought, namely, conscious thought and unconscious thought [29]. Conscious thought refers to “the thought or deliberation while conscious attention is directed at the problem at hand”, whereas unconscious thought is “the thought or deliberation in the absence of conscious attention directed at the problem” [19 ,p.1005]. A person can have both conscious and unconscious thoughts at the same time. The critical point of distinction between conscious and unconscious thought lies in whether the attention is di rected to the primary task or other irrelevant non-primary tasks [21].

Still in its nascent stage, research on Unconscious Thought Theory [UTT; 27] has suggested that conscious thought outperforms un conscious thought in some circumstances such as when the decision is simple [e.g., 1, 24, 25, 103]. However, the findings regarding the advantages of unconscious thought for complex decisions are mixed. On the one hand, evidence supporting the superiority of unconscious thought over conscious thought can be found in several recent em pirical studies [e.g., 18, 23, 25, 72, 92]. Other research has also established the existence of unconscious thought in the e-commerce context [35,90]. On the other hand, some empirical studies have found little support for the advantages of unconscious thought [e.g., 59, 71, 90, 100]. Therefore, the advantages of unconscious thought over conscious thought deserves further investigation, especially in the complex decision context [47–49].

This research aims to investigate the advantages of unconscious thought by exploring the impacts of contextual factors of consumers complex decision-making. Specifically, we believe that consumers' circadian preference, defined as one's optimal time of the day for decisionmaking, should be taken into account when studying the efects of unconscious thought in the context of complex decisions. It has been ob served that people have a 24-h cycle (referred to as “circadian rhythm”) of increases and decreases in a range of biological, physiological, and cognitive functions, including body temperature, heart rate, and hormone secretion [32,40,50,71]. Prior research demonstrates that most people have a certain time of day when they are most alert (i.e., circadian preference) and able to perform at their best in various tasks. As will be discussed in detail in our hypotheses development, we believe that the advantages of unconscious thought on complex decisions is contingent upon whether one's circadian preference is met.

Studying consumer's circadian preference should be of interest to ecommerce practitioners. Most if not all e-commerce sites allow transactions day and night. Consumers make purchases round the clock, not necessarily at times coinciding with their circadian preference, with the peak time being 8 pm to 9 pm.<sup>1</sup> Moreover, increasingly ubiquitous mobile technologies have empowered consumers with much flexibility in making purchases across diferent times of a day [6,58]. Hence, it is critical to model time-related factors so that time-based strategies can be employed to facilitate consumers' purchase decision-making.

In summary, this research aims to investigate how to help consumers make complex decisions, from a novel perspective of un conscious thought and advancing this perspective with circadian pre ference. Hence, our paper aims to address a research question: How does the interaction between consumers'unconscious thought and circadian pre ference influence making complex decisions?

This research contributes to IS research in two critical ways. First, it highlights the importance of unconscious thought in online purchase decision-making. Notwithstanding the exclusive focus on cognitive information processing, consumers have limited cognitive capacities to make complex product decisions [4]. This research calls for more attention to unconscious decision-making processes, and thus opens new opportunities for IS research. Second, this research introduces a vital temporal factor that has been largely overlooked in extant research on UTT: consumer's circadian preference. Accounting for circadian preference may help elucidate the mixed findings in prior research on unconscious thought and thus contributes back to original unconscious thought theory.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Unconscious thought theory (UTT)

In recent years, Unconscious Thought Theory (UTT) [19,21] has been proposed to provide an alternative perspective and a possible solution to the long-lasting problem associated with human being's limited cognitive capacity. The motif of UTT is that the capacity of unconscious thinking is virtually infinite and thus can lead to a more satisfying or better decision in the context of complex decisions. UTT has attracted a lot of interest in recent years because it fundamentally changes how we view decision-making. Researchers have traditionally focused on how to facilitate conscious thought processes such as learning and reasoning in decision-making. If the motif of UTT holds true, the advantages of the infinite, unbiased unconscious thought can be leveraged to greatly empower optimal decision-making.

The UTT is rooted in the diference between two distinct modes of thought: conscious thought and unconscious thought [29]. Conscious thought is defined as “the thought or deliberation while conscious attention is directed at the problem at hand,” whereas unconscious thought refers to “the thought or deliberation in the absence of conscious attention directed at the problem” [19 ,p.1005]. Conscious thought and unconscious thought function in diferent ways in the brain and are diferent in many fundamental ways, as summarized in Table 1.).

First, unconscious thought works when attention is not directed at the primary task (or with attention directed elsewhere). In other words, it is not guided thinking. Unconscious thought can occur at any time. It is worth noting that a person has conscious and unconscious thoughts at the same time. The conscious thought is applied to the task that receives the individual's direct attention. At the same time, other tasks are processed by unconscious thought. As noted by Dijksterhuis and Nordgren [21], “attention” is the key to diferentiate between unconscious thought and conscious thought.

Second, unconscious thought has a much larger capacity than conscious thought. Conscious thought is restricted by a human being's limited cognitive capacity [69]. Hence, people can consciously process only a limited subset of all relevant information at one time [18]. This would cause a problem for making complex decisions that involve a large number of attributes. Unconscious thought, on the other hand, has a virtually infinite capacity, which allows people to process much more information at the same time. Furthermore, unconscious thought can occur even when conscious thought does not work. This further increases the capacity of unconscious thought.

Third, unconscious thought has a natural ability to assign appropriate weights to all attributes while incorporating one's preferences. This helps form more holistic evaluations [21]. Conscious thought, on the other hand, is primarily based on an over-emphasized subset of preferred attributes, partially because of its limited capacity and may lead to suboptimal or biased weighting of some attributes [18,19,22].

Despite the seemingly apparent advantages of unconscious thought, empirical studies to date have yielded mixed findings. For simple de cisions that involve very few (e.g., less than 7) attributes, prior studies have consistently favored the conscious thought over the unconscious thought process [19,21]. In the same vein, a meta-analysis on UTT [87] concluded that conscious thought outperforms unconscious thought when the decision problem was simple. The results for complex decision tasks (i.e., those involving many product attributes), however, are far from conclusive. While some studies have found support for the superiority of unconscious thought [e.g., 18, 23, 25, 72, 92], others have failed to do so [e.g., 59, 71, 90, 100].

The conflicting empirical findings pertaining to the efect of unconscious thought on complex decisions have driven researchers to look for possible explanations to reconcile the diferences in prior research. For instance, researchers have studied several individual diference factors and reported that individuals perform better under unconscious thought than under conscious thought if they are in the state of feeling less powerful [86], are subject experts [17,20], or have coherent representations of product in memory [61]. Other researchers have ex amined the impacts of important contextual factors, suggesting that unconscious thought has an advantage over conscious thought under time constraints [78], in the presence of a large quantity of high-quality information [35], or when a first impression is available [99]. Little, if any research has been done to examine how time — a contextual factor of apparent importance in today's full-day cycle e-commerce [47,48] — plays a role in facilitating unconscious thought to making complex decisions. This research aims to bridge this gap.

## 2.2. Synchronizing consumer circadian preference and time of decision

Circadian rhythm is found to be a fundamental and universal factor of human behavior [10,60]. It refers to the 24-h cycles of increases and decreases in a range of biological, physiological, and cognitive functions, including body temperature, heart rate, and hormone secretion [32,40,50,71]. When the time of decision coincides with peak arousal period, an individual experiences “circadian synchrony” [66,67]. Prior research demonstrates that most individuals have a certain time of day when they are most alert and able to perform at their best in various tasks especially cognitive tasks, such as proactive interference [40], long term memory access [3], reaction time and concentration [9], recall and recognition [52,80], visual search [72], and other reasoning tasks [8]. It is believed that circadian rhythm afects the energy dimension of activation and alertness [92], which is an antecedent to attention and cognitive resource availability [64] and information processing [48].

Individuals display a wide range of circadian rhythm, with peak times ranging from morning to evening hours, corresponding to the common categorization of individuals into morning types (i.e., “early birds”) and evening types (i.e., “night owls”) [49]. The Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire [45] has been developed to assess individual circadian preferences. Measured by self-reported assessment of intellectual and physical peak times.

Time-of-day has received attention in consumer behavior literature as a contextual factor [5]. According to Smith [85], diferences in consumer decision-making performance vary with the time of day. An interaction efect on memory recall tasks has been observed between individual circadian preference and time-of-day [70]. Specifically, there may be a synchrony efect [66] between an individual's circadian preference and the time of day at which decisions are made. In general, morning types perform better in the morning than in the evening, evening types performing better in the evening. Specifically, the syn chrony efect influences one's cognitive eficiency through afecting cognitive inhibition, a process critical for control of thoughts [e.g., 48, 76, 77, 118]. Cognitive inhibition refers to the mind's ability to suppress stimuli that are irrelevant to the task at hand $[ 2 , 4 2 , 7 4 ]$ . Prior literature suggests that cognitive inhibition controls the content of individuals' working memory through three main functions [14,37,41,74,75,84,102]. First, the access function prevents irrelevant, of-task information from entering the working memory, and hence limiting access to goal-relevant information. Second, the deletion function of cognitive inhibition dampens activation of information that is marginally relevant, or that was once relevant but has become obsolete or no longer relevant for a new task. Last, cognitive inhibition restrains strong responses to be evaluated for appropriateness. In sum, the access, deletion, and restrain functions of cognitive inhibition operate to minimize the interference from distracting materials during information processing [101].

Individuals are expected to experience deficits in cognitive inhibition at asynchronous time of decision (i.e., of-peak time), especially fo complex tasks that rely heavily on working memory [66]. Individuals who experience ineficient cognitive inhibition would be more susceptible to distracting and irrelevant information, such as from external sources (e.g., advertisements, animated banners, pop-up messages, and other distracting website features) or internal sources (e.g., task-unrelated thoughts). Furthermore, the impaired deletion function which is supposed to void previously relevant but currently irrelevant information may lead to heightened interference, resulting in dificulties in attending to, encoding, and retrieving more relevant information [101].

As the eficiency in cognitive inhibition is a critical determinant of the content of individuals' working memory, complex consumer product choices that heavily rely on working memory would be more susceptible to synchrony efects. Inhibition eficiency is particularly important in making complex purchase decisions, in which relevant product information is likely to be emitted quickly due to the limited capacity of working memory and the interference from distracting oftask stimuli. Thus, it is important that we explore whether consumers would leverage another mode of thought, unconscious thought, to ofset the consequences of deficiencies in cognitive inhibition at ofpeak times of decision.

## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Mode of thought and quality of online purchase decision

Previous studies on UTT have shown the advantages of unconscious thought in making complex decisions with the goal to identify optimal decisions. The conceptualization of decision quality, consistent with the UTT paradigm, is based on the idea of making the “right” (or “best”) choice [19]. Hence, one objective indicator of purchase decision quality is whether a consumer chooses an optimal product choice based on predefined decision-making criteria. It is worth noting that our variable of interest is not the probability of choosing the “best” option based on their whims and fancies, but rather identifying product options with most relevant information (i.e., better-ranking attributes). Making optimal purchase decisions is important as it increases the possibility that consumers' expectations of the product are well met and thus leads to greater consumer satisfaction [11,98].

Consistent with UTT, we expect that an unconscious mode of thought can lead to better purchase decisions for complex products $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ those with > 7 attributes) [18,19,23]. For example, smartphones are considered complex products because they often have many attributes (such as memory capacity, battery standby, weight, camera, etc.) that consumers need to consider when making choices. Evaluating complex products is dificult for conscious thought, because of its limited processing capability, which can store and process only about seven items at one time [69]. Such a limited processing capability could lead consumers to focus on only a subset of all relevant information when making a purchase decision. In contrast, aforementioned, unconscious thought has a virtually infinite capacity, allowing consumers to expand their attentional span to incorporate all product information that should be taken into account [18,19,21]. Furthermore, conscious thought often leads to a biased focus on a few attributes which are weighted heavily. Unconscious thought, on the contrary, operates on the holistic representation of product attributes and is good at weighing the relative importance of all product attributes [18,21]. Hence, we posit that:

H1. : For complex decisions, unconscious thought will lead to higher decision quality than conscious thought.

## 3.2. The moderating efects of circadian asynchrony

Processing product information often requires consumers to retrieve information from long-term memory and process it in working memory efectively [79]. Prior research suggests that people reach an optimal performance of memory recall when there is a synchrony (i.e., circadian synchrony) between peak circadian periods and time of decision be. cause it facilitates eficient retrieval of information in memory [47,48]. Circadian synchrony triggers a high level of arousal (i.e., psychological state of activation) [30]. Arousal is an antecedent to attention and re source availability [64], which in turn determines information processing capacity [100]. People actively process information when their arousal level is high. Hence, when the time of decision coincides with peak arousal periods, individuals will likely achieve optimal perfor mance involving cognitive tasks, especially those tasks that can be handled suficiently by human being's limited cognitive ability. Furthermore, circadian synchrony between one's circadian preference and time of decision can enhance his/her cognitive inhibition which operates to suppress irrelevant of-task information, and thereby facilitates concentration on task-relevant cognitive thoughts [41,74,75]. Consumer decision-making exhibits circadian efects because it requires cognitive inhibition for decision quality to be optimal [40]. Prior literature has shown that the access, deletion, and restrain functions of cognitive inhibition tend to be more eficient for individuals tested during synchrony times as compared to asynchrony times [65,66,101].

In summary, circadian synchrony between consumer circadian preference and time of decision implies a higher arousal level, meaning high capacity and eficiency of working memory [3,33,73]. Furthermore, circadian synchrony also means that consumers have better control over the content of working memory and are less likely to be disrupted by any distractions [42,65,66]. Therefore, this synchrony efect may result in better decision-making outcomes under conscious thought, making the relative advantages of unconscious thought less significant.

The opposite of circadian synchrony is circadian asynchrony, which means a person is working outside of his/her preferred time of day and therefore is cognitively less alert. Circadian asynchrony between cir cadian preference and time of decision may lead to a low level of arousal, and consequently deterioration of conscious thought [46]. It also would lead to a constraint in one's cognitive efficiency and more dependence on the capacity of working memory [21]. Moreover, con scious thought and unconscious thought rely on two different routes of retrieval of information from memory [34]. Conscious thought undertakes a deliberate route that requires careful and purposeful attempts to retrieve product information stored in the explicit memory, whereas unconscious thought takes an unintentional route from implicit memory, as consumers' behavior is activated without consciou awareness [21]. Hence, the disadvantages of conscious thought in term of low capacity of working memory and suboptimal weighting of product attributes become more salient when circadian preference is not synchronized with the time of decision, that is, circadian asynchrony.

While circadian synchrony enhances conscious thought through enhancing cognitive inhibition, there is little or no theory or empirical findings regarding how circadian synchrony influences unconscious thought. We argue that unconscious thought outperforms conscious thought when circadian asynchrony is present. The rationale is that unlike those based on conscious thought, purchase decisions based on unconscious thought depend less on working memory [21] and thus sufer less from the deficiencies in cognitive inhibition resulted from circadian asynchrony. Consequently, automatic, unconscious eforts to retrieve and process information should not be afected by circadian asynchrony, at least not as much as that for conscious thought. Hence, the advantages of unconscious thought over conscious thought for complex purchase decisions are likely more salient when there is circadian asynchrony. Hence, we hypothesize the following moderating efect:

H2. : For complex decisions, unconscious thought will have more advantages over conscious thought for decision quality when circadian preference is not synchronized with the time of decision than when circadian preference is synchronized with the time of decision.

## 4. Research methods

We conducted a controlled lab experiment to test our hypotheses. The experiment was about consumer purchase decisions of smartphones in online auctions. We set the context as an online auction because the efects of unconscious thought are more salient under a time constraint [78]. The online auction closure mechanism is a natural time constraint without compromising the realism of experimental settings [90]. Indeed, shopping under time restriction is not uncommon. Many e-commerce sites provide flash sales: consumers have to finish shopping within a limited period of time to enjoy promotions. For example, Amazon.com provides personalized Gold Box deals and “lightning deals” that only appear in a limited time period. Similarity, Tmall ofers flash sale deals during Double 11 Online Shopping Festival. Therefore, setting time constraints represents many real-world scenarios.

We created two identical online auction websites to represent the two treatment conditions, namely Conscious Thought (CT) condition and Unconscious Thought (UT) condition. Appendix A depicts the screenshots of the websites. This design was consistent with the UTT experiment paradigm [e.g., 24, 25, 29]. The UTT experiment paradigm typically utilizes a decision-making task in which individuals are presented information about product alternatives (e.g., cars or apartments). Each alternative is described by the same set of attributes. but with diferent attribute values. Several studies [e.g., 24, 25, 29] use four product alternatives each described by 12 attributes (i.e., a total of 48 pieces of information) as complex decision-making. Based on the principle that a conscious information processing capacity is limited to a temporary “store” of seven items [69] and the manipulation of product complexity in the UTT paradigm [e.g., 24], we have decided to opt for smartphones with 12 attributes as the product, given that the scope of this research is on complex decisions. The attributes had ordinal values (e.g., low vs. high camera resolutions). The four smartphones were ranked based on the aggregate of all their attribute rankings (See Appendix A for details).

## 4.1. Procedure and manipulations

Our task essentially simulates a gift purchase task: to purchase a smartphone for someone else as a gift. This is critical because it justifies the making of choices based on an objective standard rather than participants' own preferences or whims and fancies. Participants were introduced with the instructions that (a) during the experiment, they will be asked to evaluate several smartphones from an online auction website; (b) to help them make product judgment, a set of product attributes will be provided (noteworthy, to control for individual preferences, we emphatically asked subjects to choose the “best” smartphone based on the attributes provided, not based on their own preferences); (c) they need to choose the “best” smartphone among the four options in terms of their “overall quality” (not one or two attributes); (d) each product attribute should be weighted with equal im portance (noteworthy, they were cautioned against biasing themselves to prefer one attribute (e.g., battery life) over another attribute (e.g., camera resolution)); and (e) the task is an online auction with a time limit (see Appendix B for details).

Smartphones were chosen as the stimulus in this study because they are “search products,” based on the distinction defined in prior research [76]. Specifically, search products are associated with a higher degree of standardization compared with experience products and thus can be compared based on explicitly identified attributes [39]. For example, books, furniture, and well-described electronic devices are search pro ducts because their information can be easily acquired before the usage [63]. A 128 MB internal memory can be reasonably considered better than a 64 MB internal memory, ceteris paribus. Thus, irrespective of an individual's preference or utility function, the purchase of the alternative that has more better-ranking attributes should indicate a better decision.

To control for possible confounding efects of brand preference, prior brand awareness, or pricing (e.g., participants may simply choose the cheapest phone), we used hypothetical phones (M series) and all prices were set at USD 450. To motivate participants to pick the best choice (i.e., with most better-ranking attributes), participants were informed that in addition to the \$5 for show-up, they could earn an additional \$6 if they correctly chose the smartphone with the most betterranking attributes. Such an incentive also helps overcome the potential influence of personal preferences.

Fig. 1 summarizes the two diferent conditions: CT and UT. The respective website had four pages, representing the four smartphones respectively. The sequence in which the four smartphone pages were presented was randomized to avoid possible order efects. Furthermore, to control for possible order or recency efect, the order of 12 attributes was randomized for each smartphone. Consistent with prior UTT studies [e.g., 24, 25, 92], each product was presented for 15 s so that the absorption of all relevant information is adequate but insuficient for systematic deliberation (which is supposed to be manipulated later).<sup>2</sup> Subjects were automatically directed to the next smartphone page specification page after 15 s.

Mode of thought was manipulated by an n-back task, which aims at limiting conscious thought [55] and has been proven efective in triggering unconscious thought in both ofline decision-making settings [e.g., 24, 25, 80] as well as online shopping context [90]. Participants in the UT condition completed a two-minute n-back task presented in the form of a pop-up window (Appendix C). In the n-back task, a participant was presented with a sequence of stimuli and required to indicate whether a current stimulus matched the one presented n steps earlier in the sequence [55]. It is designed to enhance subjects' attention shifts as the success of mode of thought manipulation lies in the participants' attention shifts between the primary task and non-primary task [21]. It is a well-developed procedure to evoke unconscious thought as the task is irrelevant to the primary task of online shopping and thereby distracts the subjects [18,57]. It has been widely used as a measure of the funationality of working memory in cognitive neuroscience [57] and a distractor task in the UTT paradigm [1]. After the n-back task, participants were directed back to the auction site and asked to indicate their product choices before the auction closed in 15 s.

Participants in the CT condition did not do the n-back task. Instead, they used the same two minutes to deliberate on the products, employing conscious thought. They were instructed to carefully think about the product information and their decision for 2 min, without any distraction or access to product information again.

## 4.2. Measures

After the experiment, participants completed a survey that included items from Home and Ostberg's [44] Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ) to measure the participants' circadian preference, measures for satisfaction, and questions on demographic information. The quality of online product decision was measured by whether a participant chose the optimal smartphone (i.e., with most betterranking attributes). This is analogous to maximizing the overall probability of winning something in decision-making literature [78]. If a participant chose the optimal smartphone, the decision quality is coded as “1” and “0” otherwise. The measurement of decision quality in the present study, consistent with the UTT paradigm [e.g., 24, 25, 92], is based on the idea of the existence of a relatively “objective” standard for decision quality. Specifically. more better-ranked attribute values indicate a better product. As a result, one phone is identified as the best option, with 9 better-ranking attributes out of 12 attributes; two phones are flagged as the mediocre options, with 7 and 5 better-ranking attributes out of 12 attributes, respectively; one phone is marked as the poor option, with only 3 better-ranking attributes out of 12 attributes (Appendix A). Reiterating, choosing a smartphone (i.e., a search product rather than an experienced product) as the stimulus in our study helps make comparisons relatively objective.

To check the circadian synchrony, we recorded the experiment time for each participant and matched it with his/her circadian preference measured by MEQ. There is synchrony when, for example, a morning type participant completed the experiment in the morning, and there is asynchrony when, for example, an evening-type participant did in the morning.

The percentages of participants who chose the best, mediocre, and poor products in diferent conditions are shown in Table 3. As predicted by H1, a greater proportion of participants (27.0%) in the UT condition, as compared with those in the CT condition (14.6%) made the best choice, and the diference was significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } \ : ( 1 , N = 1 7 1 ) = \ : 3 . 9 0 5$ $p \ < \ . 0 5 , \varphi \ = \ 0 . 1 5 1 )$ ). According to the power analysis, this ensures a suficient statistical power of 0.8 for medium efect size [12]. We found participants in the UT group were more likely to choose the optimal smartphone. Therefore, H1 is supported.

It is worth noting that a substantial number of participants (especially those in the CT condition) failed to choose the best option but instead chose one of the two mediocre options. This is not totally un expected given the complexity of the purchase decision and consumers' limited cognitive capacity. After all, unconscious thought is only one possible factor afecting complex decisions. In addition, it has been known that people sometimes make choices by “satisficing” (rather than optimizing): choosing a “good enough” option $[ 5 6 , 8 9 , 9 3 , 9 5 ] .$ Hence, we suspect that participants in the conscious thought condition might have adopted the satisficing strategy. Also, decision-making is essentially a complex process and many other factors other than mode of thought and circadian synchrony would play a role.

## 5.3. Post-hoc analysis of consumer satisfaction

To explore if unconscious thought may adversely afect consumers satisfaction with their online shopping experience, we measured consumer satisfaction in the post-experiment survey using four measurement items adapted from prior literature [31,103], on a 7-point Likert scale. Cronbach's alpha of the measure of consumer satisfaction was 0.94, indicating adequate reliability.

Results of a one-way ANOVA test on consumer satisfaction indicated that the mode of thought (i.e., CT versus UT) did not have a significant efect on consumer satisfaction $( M _ { \mathrm { C T } } = 4 . 7 3 / 7 , M _ { \mathrm { U T } } = 4 . 5 6 / 7 ,$ F(1, $1 6 9 ) \ = \ 0 . 8 2 , p = \ \mathrm { n } . s . )$ . Furthermore, scores on consumer satisfaction were submitted to a 2 (mode of thought: CT vs. UT) by 2 (circadian synchrony: synchrony vs. asynchrony) ANOVA analysis. Results from the two-way ANOVA shown that neither the main efect of mode of thought $( F ( 1 , 1 6 7 ) = 0 . 6 3 , p = \mathrm { n } . s . )$ nor the interaction efect (F(1, $1 6 7 ) = 1 . 4 6 , p = { \tt n } . s . )$ was significant. Together, our findings indicate that unconscious thought (UT) could lead to better decision quality, without compromising consumers' satisfaction with their online shopping experience. Hence, we conjecture that the benefits of unconscious thought may make consumers less frustrated during complex decisionmaking [68] and thus ofset the potential annoyance caused by distractions.

## 5.5. Additional analysis: Chi-Square tests with repeated random samples

Some researchers have raised concerns over the reliability and validity of prior UTT experiments. For example, Acker argued that those experiments had “the largest efect sizes but at the same time the smallest sample sizes” [1 ,p. 299]. Likewise, Nieuwenstein et al. [77] questioned whether prior UTT studies employed under-powered small sample sizes that are insuficient to balance out potential confounding factors.

To address these potential concerns about sample size, we conducted additional bootstrapping tests. Bootstrapping test resamples the original experiment data and generates multiple bootstrap samples as a proxy to the independent real samples [25,26]. It has been used widely in applied statistics [24,38]. We followed the suggested technique and procedures $[ 2 5 , 2 6 , 6 2 ]$ . First, we determined the suitable sizes of each resampled dataset. The meta-analysis of UTT studies conducted by Acker [1] shows that the number of participants assigned to each experimental condition was around 20 to 50. Furthermore, our review of recent studies on UTT also confirms that UTT experiments typically employ a group size of around 20 to 50. Thus, we set random samples of 20, 30, 40, and 50. Second, for each group size, we constructed 1000 resamples under three conditions: (1) resampling from the original experiment dataset, (2) resampling from the subset of participants in the circadian synchrony group, and (3) resampling from the partici pants in the circadian asynchrony group.

We conducted chi-square tests for each resample and tested whether the efect of unconscious thought is significantly better than that of conscious thought for each sample size. Table 5 summarizes the results. First, as expected, bigger samples lead to higher chances of finding a statistical significance. Furthermore, Columns 1 and 2 jointly suggest that when circadian synchrony is not considered at all (as in previous research), there is, in general, an advantage of UT over CT, and such efect increases when the sample size increases.

The last four columns (i.e., Columns 3 to 6) show that considering circadian synchrony enhances the explanatory power of our prediction of purchase quality. However, when there is circadian asynchrony, the advantages of unconscious thought are more obvious. Based on the post hoc analyses, we speculate the importance of considering the synchrony between circadian preference and time of decision when explaining under what conditions unconscious thought is more likely to outperform conscious thought.

## 6. Discussion and contributions

Existing IS research has focused on how to facilitate e-commerce consumers' conscious information processing to make better purchase decisions. This research adopts a novel approach by switching our attention to the advantages of unconscious thought, based on the rela tively recent unconscious thought theory [19,21]. Instead of insisting on enhancing the conscious information process, we can take advantage of consumers' virtually infinite unconscious thought capacities. Furthermore, we hypothesized that the advantages of unconscious thought are contingent upon the synchrony of individual circadian preference and time of decision so that unconscious thought has more advantages over conscious thought when circadian asynchrony is present. We conducted a controlled lab experiment to test our hypotheses. The findings are in general consistent with our hypotheses that for complex purchase decisions, unconscious thought has some advantages over conscious thought when the asynchrony between a consumer's circa dian preference and time of decision is present.

## 6.1. Limitations

Despite our eforts to design a rigorous lab experiment and conduct additional statistical tests, there are limitations in our study. First, we followed many IS and decision-making research in using college students as subjects for experiments [13,19,21] because they are typical users of e-commerce websites. However, future works may want to triangulate the results for individuals of more diverse age groups because aging may afect cognitive ability and thus technology-related human behavior [43]. Also, college students (i.e., young subjects) may exhibit potentially diferent circadian preference from older adults [67].

Second, in order to enhance the internal validity of our experiment, there is unavoidably some sacrifice of realism in our lab experiment to control for the possible influence of environmental factors. In most experimental designs, environmental factors have to be strictly controlled, especially in the context of e-commerce [27,28,59,83]. We thus designed our experiment in a way that minimizes the influence of common environmental factors such as the ability to surf the Internet, multiple advertisements, etc. For example, to manifest the efects of unconscious thought, we impose a time constraint for the decisionmaking. Further research can explore how to re-test our findings in a more realistic online shopping setting. For example, the n-back task can be replaced by a real pop-up advertisement window in order to study the efect of the content (e.g., relevant or irrelevant to the product being purchased) and the timing of the advertisements.

Third, although the lab experiment provides some evidence for our conjected advantages of unconscious thought over conscious thought in circadian asynchrony conditions, there might be alternative explanations for our findings that were not fully captured in the experiment and analyses. Although the empirical results are in general consistent with our hypotheses, our main study, post hoc analysis, robustness checks, and bootstrapping analyses may not be able to fully rule out many other alternative explanations. This issue is especially salient in light of the fact that many participants in the unconscious thought group failed to make the optimal choice. Decision-making is a complex process and many factors can influence a person's decision. This indicates the possibility that there may exist alternative factors or mechanisms about decision-making this research does not account for. For example, the criticality of decision may be studied: a person may be more serious and less likely to take the aforementioned satisficing strategy if the decision is highly critical to his/her life or career. Therefore, future studies could formally and rigorously test alternative mechanisms of unconscious thought, e.g., by tracing actual consumer decision-making using magnetic resonance imaging methods. Considering such factors can help clarify how unconscious thought works.

A promising topic for future research is to study how circadian synchrony and unconscious thought works for simple decision tasks. This research is focused on complex decision tasks, in light of the fact that existing findings regarding unconscious thought for making complex decisions are inconclusive [e.g., 59, 71, 90, 100]. Future research can consider both simple products with few attributes (e.g., toilet paper, toothpaste, etc.) and complex products with more attributes and the interplay among task complexity, circadian synchrony, and mode of thought. This would further enhance our understanding of how circa dian synchrony functions for unconscious thought.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions and research implications

Our study ofers several important theoretical contributions. First, we introduce new opportunities for IS researchers to study unconscious thought. The extant IS research on online shopping typically assumes that a conscious, distraction-free, and systematic information processing allows individuals to focus their attention on the most relevant information [e.g., 101] and improves decision quality [7]. Drawing upon UTT [19,21], this research elucidates a novel perspective by speculating that unconscious thought can, under certain conditions, lead to consumers' optimal purchase decisions. By shedding light on how consumers' online shopping could be also shaped by unconscious thought, we advocate the need to factor unconscious thought into the design and evaluation of IS.

Second, our work also contributes back to the UTT literature by underscoring the importance of considering circadian synchrony as a boundary condition for UTT. Based on the literature on circadian preference and cognitive function, we conjecture that the advantages of unconscious thought over conscious thought is more salient when cir cadian asynchrony is present. As demonstrated in the resampling statistics (see Additional Analysis in Section 5.5), accounting for circadian synchrony can partially help explain the seemingly mixed and equivocal findings from past works [e.g., 59, 71, 90, 100]. The emerging interests in studying the direct and moderating efects of time-related individual and contextual variables on consumer cognition and behavior have cautioned that prior static models of consumer decisionmaking or static website design principles are insuficient [47,48]. This research underscores the importance of considering individuals' circadian preference and its potential influence on online shopping.

This research has implications for IS research on user adoption and continued use of technology. Many information technologies are complex innovations and the adoption decisions are dificult. Prior research has focused on utilitarian and hedonic factors such as perceived usefulness and perceived enjoyment [16,96,97] or social factors such as other people's behavior or influences, i.e., herding [89]. Most of the research in this area has assumed a conscious thought mode: users consciously develop perceptions about technology and subsequently form their behavioral intention to use it. Our research suggests that adoption decisions may also be made in the unconscious thought mode. We beleive that such adoption decisions may at times be better than those made completely in the conscious thought mode. Therefore, how users make adoption and continued use decisions unconsciously is a topic of value. One fruitful way to do this maybe through business analytics. Data-mining or text-mining techniques can be applied to estimate a consumer's circadian preference. Accordingly, diferent strategies (UT or CT) can be used to facilitate decision-making, considering the time of decision and complexity of the technology.

The findings of this research also have implications on how we view the “distracting” features displayed on e-commerce websites. Distracting website features, such as pop-ups, banner ads, and animation, are commonly employed by online retailers to attract Internet users' attention and convey commercial marketing messages [15,36]. IS research generally cautions against the rampant use of distracting website features [e.g., 8, 50, 115]. However, with the rapid develop ment of multimedia and Internet technologies, multitudes of seemingly “distracting” features have been introduced to support varying aspects of consumer information processing and evaluation. For example, live “help” pop-ups have been employed to mediate consumer purchase decisions and build trust [82]. Instead of insisting on the riddance of all distracting features, this research conjectures that we can think about how to strategically employ them to make the best use of unconscious thought. This is in line with some recent HCI research that hinted at the possibilities of usefulness in distracting features under certain circumstances [e.g., 91, 106].

## 6.3. Practical implications

Our research provides important implications for e-commerce practitioners. First, we highlight to e-commerce practitioners the importance of considering consumer's circadian preference and time of decision. In the present data-rich online environment, online retailers can collect consumers' circadian preference information and accord ingly employ our findings to e-commerce practices. For example, an ecommerce site can track consumers' online shopping activities and identify the temporal patterns or circadian rhythms in consumers propensity to make purchase decisions. Along with the advances in mobile technologies, consumers are able to make purchase decisions at diferent times of a day in accordance with their circadian preferences [81]. This creates the possibility of “perpetual contact” whereby con sumers are always with their mobile devices and can access information and services regardless of time and location, thus reflecting their real circadian preferences [6,58]. Hence, information on consumers' circa dian preferences can be gathered by collecting and analyzing consumers' clickstream data. Also, demographic information (e.g., age) and interaction records might serve as proxies for MEQ measurement when the latter is not available. For example, prior research has found that older adults are likely to prefer activities early in the morning, whereas younger adults prefer the evening [67], indicating that age may be a proxy for circadian preference. Therefore, strategically designing distractions at an appropriate time of decision is feasible by tracking consumers' online shopping behaviors, taking into account diferences in individuals characteristics and time zones.

Second, this research suggests to e-commerce retailers that strate gically designed interventions have the possibility to help consumers leverage the advantages of unconscious thought to make complex purchase decisions. For example, for better decision quality to arise through the benefits of unconscious thought, seemingly distracting website features (e.g., pop-up advertisements or coupon vouchers in pop-ups) could be employed when consumers are making complex purchase decisions with time constraints $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ online auctions, flash sales, live broadcasting sales, etc.). This is in line with the interests of ecommerce retailers, who may want to create a sense of urgency to entice consumers to buy on the spot. Based on the findings, we conjecture that the advantages of unconscious thought could be leveraged to help e-commerce consumers who are bewildered by the overwhelming amount of product information to make better decisions without adversely afecting their satisfaction.

In summary, this research conjectures that unconscious thought may be leveraged by practitioners to help customers make better decisions, especially when the decisions are complex. Such advantages are especially salient when customers' circadian preference is not met. Making better decisions can benefit practitioners by reducing product return and increasing customer retention.
