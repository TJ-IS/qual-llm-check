# The Attitude Cube – A three-dimensional model of situational factors in IS adoption and their impact on the attitude-behavior relationship

## Abstract

Since the introduction of the TAM by [1], user attitudes have been considered an important determinant of IS adoption. Their impact on behavior is rooted in behavioral frameworks such as the theory of reasoned action [2] and the theory of planned behavior [3]. However, in IS adoption models, the predictive power of attitudes for behavior as an endogenous variable has proven strong in some circumstances and weak in others [4,5]. These inconsistencies resulted in an exclusion of the construct attitude from further modified versions of the TAM and other models explaining individual IS adoption and use. To better understand the prevalence of inconsistent attitude behavior relationships in IS adoption models, this research follows social psychological research practices focusing on situational factors prone to result in insignificant attitude-behavior relationships. To test for the impact of these factors on the attitude-behavior relationship, the article raises the level of analysis to a higher level of abstraction. A scientometric literature review and a meta-analysis of fourteen top IS journals from 1989 to 2014 finds that three out of four situational factors positively influence the attitude-behavior relationship in the IS fields: voluntariness, technology type and adoption context. These factors constitute “the attitude cube”, which provides conceptual guidance for researchers in assessing the conditions under which the attitude-behavior relationship is likely to be weak or strong.

One that might be the most important is that humans are likely to behave in accordance with their attitudes, which in turn makes the assessment of attitudes key to assessing and predicting human behavior [8,9]. Theoretical frameworks and theories that build on this relationship between attitudes and behavior, such as the theory of reasoned action [2] and the theory of planned behavior [3], have been applied in a wide range of research disciplines. However, the prediction of behaviors by attitudes has proven less consistent than initially assumed and depends on a variety of factors external to an individual [10]. The considerable variability in the degree to which attitudes predict behavior was illustrated by [9], who reported that the mean correlations between attitudes and actual behaviors range from -0.2 [11] to 0.73 [12]. Given this variability, in addition to the issue of attitude measurement, research in social psychology set a second focus on the conditions under which attitudes predict behavior to the highest extent possible [10,12,13]. One quite intuitive finding of this research is that attitudes are better predictors of behavior if the relevant behavior affects the private rather than professional life of an individual [10].

In the IS discipline, user attitudes is among the top three independent variables in IS adoption models [14] that explain IT usage and thus individual behavior as an endogenous variable. Regarding the large body of research indicating great variability in the ability of attitude to predict individual behavior in social psychology, it is hardly surprising that these inconsistencies in the relationship between attitude and behavior have also occurred and been noticed in the IS discipline [1,15]. The originally modeled mediation of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use by attitude in the original TAM was found to be at best modest [4], and the direct effects of the two beliefs perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use were found to be more predictive of behavior. As a consequence, attitude was removed from further developments of the TAM and related models [16,17]. In other adoption models and studies, however, attitude continued to be a core construct with high predictive validity [5,18].

The overall conclusion drawn from the observed inconsistencies in the IS discipline – similar to social psychology – was that attitudes predict behavior but not under all circumstances [15,16]. However, unlike researchers in the discipline of social psychology, IS research has not placed much attention on the particular circumstances that potentially make attitude highly predictive of individual IT usage behavior and those that lower the ability of attitudes to predict IT usage behavior. The issue that attitude may deserve more attention in the IS discipline has been raised by some authors [19-21] who also question if the ways in which we measure and estimate attitudes are still consistent with contemporary social psychological research and take the particularities of IS adoption scenarios into consideration.

This research is meant as a response to that call and aims to reintroduce the construct of user attitude by addressing the following research questions:

RQ1: From a theoretical standpoint, which conditions/external factors of technology adoption scenarios are likely to impact the predictive validity of user attitude for usage behavior?

RQ2: From a meta-analytical standpoint, do these factors significantly impact the relationship between attitude and behavior in IS adoption models?

To address the first question, we review the social psychology literature on attitudes and transfer the insights into influential factors to adoption scenarios. We develop a framework designed to serve as a guideline that indicates under what conditions user attitudes, as a construct in adoption models, is likely to have a high impact on usage/adoption behavior and under what conditions attitude is likely to have no significant impact on behavior. In line with the second research question, we then test this framework by analyzing scientometric data from fourteen top IS journals from 1989-2014, contributing to the IS discipline by providing basic theoretical insights into the particularities of the construct user attitude in general and into the impact of external conditions on its predictive validity with reference to behavior in particular. Further, the meta-analytic approach enables us to derive implications of higher general validity than those taken from single studies. Our results indicate that the inconsistencies of the predictive validity of user attitude for adoption behaviors are not of a random but rather a systematic nature and can thus be addressed and eliminated by better comprehending the relationship between attitude and behavior.

## The attitude-behavior relationship – the social psychological perspective

The most likely reason that attitudes are frequently studied is the assumption that attitudes guide and influence behavior [22]. Intuitively, this assumption seems obvious and correct. We surround ourselves with people who we

#

like, vote for the political party we find most appealing, eat food that we like, etc. However, we also drive cars although we know that driving contributes to climate change, which we want to end, and we cheat on our partners, although we value loyalty. Thus, it is hardly surprising that early research on attitudes in social psychology that attempted to identify a causal relationship between attitudes and behavior produced contradictory results, reflecting the considerable variability in the degree to which attitudes influence behavior [3,10]. In some studies, attitudes predicted behavior to a great extent (see e.g.[12]), while in others it was negatively correlated with behavior, which implies, assuming the absence of measurement error and methodological bias, that subjects’ behavior is inconsistent with their attitudes [11]. The first attempts to explain this variability almost exclusively relied on measurement issues and resulted in the improvement of measurement and scaling techniques (see [23] for an overview). However, the new measurement techniques and methods also complicated matters and brought in new problem areas and biases [24].

A second generation of research addressed the problem of inconsistent attitude-behavior relationships from a different viewpoint and was devoted to the definition and examination of factors and conditions under which attitudes are more likely to predict behavior [10,25-28]. According to Glasman and Albarracín (2006), however, these approaches also had two basic limitations: First, the factors and conditions identified were often correlational and convergent [29-31], and, second, research based on experimental methods was often unable to provide a process-based and theoretically grounded picture of causal relationships between attitude and behavior [32-34]. To address these shortcomings and with the intent to provide conclusive insight into the factors that influence the attitude-behavior relationship, Glasman and Albarracín [10] increased the level of analysis to a higher level of abstraction and conducted a meta-analysis of the attitude-behavior relationship that basically resulted in two central factors that could be regarded as theoretical surrogates and indications for the predictive validity of the attitudebehavior relationship: attitude accessibility and attitude stability. Thus, to examine the inconsistencies of the attitude-behavior relationship in the IS discipline, these two factors will be introduced as theoretical surrogates in the following.

An intuitive example of attitude accessibility is a public opinion survey of how people feel or think about different issues. Some people will answer before the interviewer has even finished the question, while others take several minutes before they can answer [35]. This phenomenon is referred to as attitude accessibility and is primarily associated with the work of Russell H. Fazio. According to Fazio et al. [32], attitudes are more likely to predict behavior when they are easy to retrieve from memory. In the present example, this implies that a quick response is an indicator for highly accessible attitudes because it does not take long to retrieve the attitude from memory, whereas a slow response suggests that more effort is needed to do so, thus representing an indicator for low attitude accessibility.

This accessibility concept is built on two main premises. The first is that more accessible attitudes are likely to be available as criteria for later behavioral decisions [32,36,37]. The second is that more accessible attitudes influence the interpretation of present information relevant to the behavior in question. Thus, the accessibility of attitudes increases their predictive power for behavior because they are more likely to be activated upon exposure to the attitude object or other relevant cues [38].

The concept of attitude accessibility follows from an underlying associative learning model in which attitude is regarded as an association between the attitude object and its evaluation. With regard to the attitude-behavior relationship, a high degree of attitude accessibility represents a highly predictive attitude-behavior relationship. To address the inconsistencies in the attitude-behavior relationship, it is necessary to identify factors that impact its theoretical surrogate of attitude accessibility.

According to Glasman and Albarracín [10] and Bassili [39], five factors influence attitude accessibility and thus the attitude-behavior relationship: direct behavioral experience, cognitive effort, affective extremity, personal involvement, and behavioral relevance.

Direct behavioral experience represents the determinant that has been linked to attitude accessibility from the outset [32]. Fazio has provided much evidence for his proposal that direct experience increases attitude accessibility (see [38], p. 196ff for an overview). People whose attitudes are based on direct experience respond to inquiries about their attitudes with shorter latencies than do people whose attitudes are based on indirect experience [40]. The prevailing theory about why this is the case is that the human mind needs more time to virtually create and evaluate a situation than to retrieve an experience that has been filed in memory [38].

Research into the second determinant of attitude accessibility, cognitive effort, suggests that the greater the amount of thought about an attitude object or behavior, the greater the number of expressions about the attitude and its availability in memory [10,12,37]. Attitudes resulting from a large amount of cognitive effort are based on solid information. Another argument regarding why a large amount of cognitive effort increases attitude accessibility is that reflecting often and intensively about an attitude object leads to repetitive associations in memory that also increase their accessibility [38]. Hence, if an attitude is based on solid information and thorough reflection, it should be stronger and presented with more confidence, which according to Fazio [12,32] makes it more accessible and thus the attitude-behavior relationship more predictive.

According to Bassili [39], affective extremity refers to the extent to which a person’s affective evaluation of an attitude object deviates from the midpoint of a scale in either a positive or a negative direction. Another less theoretical expression would be the extent of emotional attachment that is induced by an attitude object. One of the consequences of extreme affective evaluations is that they are very accessible and thus support a highly predictive attitude-behavior relationship [39].

To a certain extent, affective extremity is related to the fourth determinant of attitude accessibility, personal involvement. If a person is personally involved in the direct consequences of an attitude and/or the resulting behavior, the attitude is more accessible than if involvement is created by virtually creating involvement in the mind [38,39]. Further, personal involvement has also been shown to have a strong direct impact on the attitude-behavior relationship. Political demonstrations might serve as an illustrative example here. If an individual is personally affected by the consequences of a legal initiative and is personally affected by unemployment or shortages, a negative attitude is much more likely to result in the participation in a demonstration [38].

Although easily confused with personal involvement, the last determinant of attitude accessibility, behavioral relevance, is conceptually distinct from personal involvement. Behavioral relevance is the degree to which attitudes are relevant to behavioral decisions [38]. For instance, if a behavior is performed under pressure by someone else or is primarily the consequence of some sort of strong social influence, an individual’s attitude is not relevant to the behavior, which implies that it is neither accessible nor predictive with respect to this behavior. If the individual’s attitude is relevant to the behavior, in contrast, this attitude is assumed to be more accessible and thus easier to retrieve from memory than attitudes that are less relevant for behavioral decisions.

The second surrogate for the predictive validity of the attitude-behavior relationship is attitude stability [10]. Stable attitudes are defined as attitudes that show little ambivalence over a period of time [41]. A variety of studies have demonstrated that strong attitudes are stable over time [30,42,43]. Thus, in contrast to attitude accessibility, attitude stability is a long-term concept.

Like attitude accessibility, high attitude stability results in a stronger and more consistent attitudebehavior relationship and, according to Glasman and Albarracín [10], is influenced by public-private correspondence and intra-attitudinal correspondence.

The first factor influencing attitude stability is public-private correspondence. In general, correspondence and attitude stability are intercorrelated. This is because the opposite of correspondence is conflict, which in almost every manifestation, be it technical, social, or biological, creates instability. Public-private correspondence is low when individuals report their attitudes to others (e.g., colleagues) but perform different actions privately (e.g., at home) or vice-versa [10]. In this case, attitudes are assumed to be poor predictors of behavior and vice versa. Another example would be if a company policy or business contradicts one’s attitudes, such as when someone is working for a nuclear power company but buying clean energy in private. This correspondence is high in private environments if there is a free democratic political environment.

Intra-attitudinal correspondence appears to operate in a similar way [10,19], unless it refers to internal psychological rather than external conditions. Intra-attitudinal correspondence refers to the consistency between the attitudinal antecedents affect and cognition [38]. In general, affective and cognitive evaluations of attitude objects correspond with one another. Attitudes can be affectively motivated, such as for hedonic attitude objects, or cognitively motivated attitudes, such as for utilitarian attitude objects [19,44,45]. Thus, basing one’s attitude on feelings when the behavior is hedonic creates intra-attitudinal correspondence and thus stable and predictable attitudes [10]. Comparing the components affect and cognition, attitudes based on emotions (affect) are assumed to be more predictive of behavior than

#

attitudes based on cognition [19]. Internal inconsistency that destabilizes attitudes occurs when affect and cognition are in conflict with each other, for instance, when we respond positively and affectively toward an attitude object and at the same time evaluate it negatively from a cognitive perspective. This results in a less stable and predictive attitude [38].

## Components of IS adoption scenarios and their impact on the attitude-behavior relationship

Attitudes toward using and the actual use of a particular technology have been a subject in the area of technology adoption at least since the appearance of the technology acceptance model (TAM) in 1989 [1]. Based on the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [2,46] and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [3], Davis et al. [1] defined attitude as a person’s affective evaluation of a specific object. This definition and the according measurement scales were adapted by various authors in the IS literature (e.g., [15,47]) and have remained basically unchanged ever since. Although irregularities in the construct’s explicative power and significance have been identified (e.g., [17]), the causes of these irregularities have not yet been investigated.

In addressing the same sort of attitude-behavior inconsistency, which poses a challenge in social psychology research, this research focuses on situational factors as potential reasons for the observed inconsistencies in the IS discipline following the example of Glasman and Albarracín [10]. The IS discipline has achieved some important results in measuring attitudes and in reducing inconsistencies between attitude and behavior by correcting misconceptions and errors in measuring attitudes (see [19,21,48]). It is now understood that it is better to consider affect and cognition as separate constructs with significantly different predictive validity with respect to different attitude objects (hedonic or utilitarian technologies). Another important outcome is that the predictive validity attitude toward objects is different than the predictive validity of attitude toward behavior (see [21]).

However, IS scholars have not considered the situational factors of adoption scenarios in helping to explain the inconsistencies in the attitude-behavior relationship in the IS discipline, although various situational factors have been identified as significant moderators of adoption behavior [16,49-51].

In the following, we extract different situational factors that have been found to be influential in adoption decisions and evaluate their potential impact on the attitude-behavior relationship based on the theoretical background of the preceding section. We then derive hypotheses for the attitude-behavior relationship and test them using scientometric data from 14 top IS journals spanning 25 years.

## Adoption context

The majority of studies on IS adoption in the IS literature were applied to explain individual adoption and usage of technologies in the workplace [49,52]. According to Venkatesh and Brown [52], adoption decisions in households are expected to differ from those in a business or institutional environment due to the personal nature of expense and the goals for technology use. Against the backdrop of influences in the attitude-behavior relationship, the following section aligns adoption studies in the institutional sector (e.g., adoption in institutions subject to public law, the health care system or public administration agencies) with studies in the business sector.

Comparing adoption decisions in the household and in organizations, two main factors affect the predictability of adoption decisions by attitudes. The first is personal involvement. It is presumed that the personal involvement in adoption decisions is generally higher in the own household than in organizations because the consequences of the adoption decision generally involve the individual more personally in the household than in an organization. Thus, attitudes are assumed to be more predictive of behavior, when adoption decisions are made in the households. If people are personally involved, according to Fazio et al. ([36,37,40], the accessibility of attitude is greater, resulting in a potentially stronger attitude-behavior relationship. Thus, adoption decisions that affect people personally, which is more likely in the household environment, are more likely to be based on behavior-relevant attitudes.

The second factor associated with the adoption context that is likely to affect the attitude-behavior relationship is the private-public correspondence of attitudes and the corresponding behavior [10]. According to the preceding section, public-private correspondence is high and results in stable and thus predictable attitudes if behavior performed in public corresponds to a person’s private attitudes or vice-versa. If this is not the case, the resulting inconsistency causes less stable and thus less predictable attitudes.

Given the adoption context of business environments, the adoption behavior is performed in a public environment. However, employees will most likely not express their attitudes about a new IT artifact publically, especially if they do not comply with organizational policy [10]. Although employee surveillance in organizations is uncommon, employee attitudes can be at odds with their employer’s policy or business model. Thus attitudes expressed in private might deviate from public actions in the organizational environment.

Regarding adoption decisions in a household environment, by contrast, attitudes and behaviors are likely to be expressed and carried out in private. In this case, attitudes and respective behaviors are assumed to be more consistent, which implies that private-public correspondence is high and attitudes are more stable and thus more predictive of behavior. Another reason why the public-private correspondence is generally higher in household environments relates to compliance. Employment often implicitly imposes compliance with company policies or business models that are not necessarily entirely consistent with an employee’s attitudes. Such inconsistency can lead to instable attitudes and consequently weaken the attitude-behavior relationship [10].

In summary, we expect higher levels of public-private correspondence and personal involvement in the household context to lead to a higher predictive validity of attitude for behavior compared to the business context. Thus, we derive the following hypothesis:

H1: The adoption context significantly influences the attitude-behavior relationship, and the impact is higher if the context is private.

## Technology type

In 2004, van der Heijden [45] classified information technology as hedonic and utilitarian systems. Drawing on the consumer behavior literature, he stated that “hedonic systems aim to provide self-fulfilling value to the user, in contrast to utilitarian systems, which aim to provide instrumental value to the user” ([54], p. 696). In this case, instrumentality implies the existence of an objective external to the interaction between the user and a system, such as higher productivity. In contrast, hedonic systems, van der Heijden argues, do not aim to facilitate the achievement of a comparable objective [54].

Like public-private correspondence, intra-attitudinal correspondence is assumed to result in stable and thus predictive attitudes. As outlined in the previous section, intra-attitudinal correspondence has two preconditions: First, the affective and cognitive evaluation of an object should reflect each other, and second, the attitudinal antecedent on the basis of which the attitude is built (e.g., affect or cognition) should comply with the nature of the attitude object for which the behavior is performed (e.g., hedonic and affect vs. utilitarian and cognition) [8].

In the IS adoption context, this implies that the adoption of hedonic systems is basically affectively/emotionally driven, while the adoption of utilitarian systems is basically cognitively driven [10,19,38,54]. Therefore, assuming an affect-based measurement of attitude, the level of hedonic-utilitarian consistency would be high, and thus, attitude would be a strong predictor of behavior in the case of hedonic systems, and the level of hedonic-utilitarian consistency would be low in the case of utilitarian systems. Analogously, cognitive-based measures of attitudes would result in a high intra-attitudinal correspondence for the adoption of utilitarian systems.

Attitude as a construct in IS adoption models was defined by Davis et al. [1] as a person’s affective evaluation of a specific object. In keeping with this definition, affect-based scales have been developed for the construct and adopted by various IS adoption scholars, e.g., [17,47]. In contrast to this primacy of affect in conceptualizing attitudes in the IS adoption literature, social psychology, the core discipline for attitudinal research, has a long tradition of implementing an attitude concept based on affect, cognition and behavior. In the IS literature, affective scales of attitude were also applied to measure user attitudes toward both hedonic and utilitarian systems. In line with van der Heijden [54], this should result in a very high predictive validity of attitudes for behavior regarding the adoption of hedonic information systems and a very low predictive validity regarding the adoption of utilitarian systems.

A good example of this effect is the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) devised by Venkatesh et al. [17]. During the development phase, the authors tested various IS adoption models and included constructs under various conditions to determine constructs that perform well across all variations of settings. Attitude was measured and affectively scaled following Davis et al. [1] 44 times and proved insignificant in 24 cases. In all of these 24 cases, affective-based attitude scales were applied to measure the adoption of utilitarian systems (see [15]).

In addition to intra-attitudinal correspondence that basically affects attitude stability, the technology type also affects the accessibility of attitude via cognitive effort and affective extremity. As the discussion above indicates, these two variables are also related to the manifestations that are hedonic or utilitarian. Cognitive effort is assumed to be higher with regard to utilitarian technologies, whereas affective extremity is higher as related to the adoption of hedonic information systems [19,54]. This relationship has been proven in various studies in both social psychology and the IS discipline (see, e.g., [19,38,54]) and leads to the hypothesis that the technology type is of high importance for the attitude-behavior relationship even more if the scaling of attitude that in general is based on affect [1] is assumed. We thus derive the following hypothesis:

H2: The type of technology significantly influences the attitude-behavior relationship, and the impact is higher if the technology type is hedonic.

## Degree of Voluntariness[TM1]

As outlined in the previous section, the behavioral relevance of an attitude affects its accessibility and thus the predictive validity of the attitude on behavior. When attitudes have little or no impact on the decision process of conducting a behavior, they are assumed to be less accessible and certainly less predictive of the respective behavior.

Regarding user attitudes in IS adoption scenarios, behavioral relevance seems to be linked to the degree of voluntariness of an adoption decision. The distinction between a voluntary or mandatory usage context has been identified as a key characteristic by IS scholars, e.g., [15,16,51,52]. Wu and Lederer [55] even investigated the role of environment-based voluntariness in information technology acceptance, revealing that user-based and environment-based voluntariness moderate the relationships between the determinants in behavioral models.

It seems natural that the behavioral relevance of attitudes in mandatory settings is relatively low. However, empirical adoption studies in mandatory usage settings indicate that user attitudes can coincidentally correspond to the behavior of interest. In this case, the parameters of a structural model would show a correlation between attitude and behavior, which could occur as a workaround for, compliance with, or even resistance to the mandated usage [51]. The alignment of user attitudes with the imposed usage in mandatory settings would be measureable as a positive and significant relationship between user attitude and behavior, although user attitudes cannot be assumed to be a determinant of behavior in this case. To measure the true causal effect of attitude on behavioral intention/behavior in a situation of mandatory usage, a situation should be artificially created whereby users are required to use an IT that they definitely do not like or even oppose. Such a situation avoids the measurement of mutual compliance. In this specific scenario, the predictive validity of attitudes should be nearly zero because it is not relevant for the behavioral decision [38]. In the opposite case, users would engage in resistance [56].

Based on these considerations, the following hypothesis is derived:

H3: The degree of voluntariness significantly influences the attitude-behavior relationship, and the impact is higher if the usage is voluntary.

## Temporal adoption status

As explained in the previous section, a very important factor that influences the accessibility of attitude is direct behavioral experience [8,10,12]. If an individual has direct experience with an attitude object, the attitude is assumed to be highly accessible when the individual is confronted with the attitude object again. Consequently, the attitude is assumed to be more predictive with respect to the behavior at stake [12].

Regarding direct behavioral experience in adoption scenarios, some IS scholars [50,57,58] have addressed the influences of pre- and post-adoption behaviors on the endogenous variable (mostly behavioral intention) in IS adoption models in addition to differences in the behaviors. Pre-adoption is defined as a stage prior to the initia usage of a technology [50],while post-adoption refers to a temporal status, according to which an IS artifact has already been used by the individuals observed. Thus, in post-adoption scenarios, users are able to include direct experience in decision-making processes. Therefore, IS researchers tend to conceptualize post-adoptive use of an IS application as increasing because individuals gain experience in using the application. However, the effects of diminishing interest, limited fashion, and routinized work processes oppose the positive effect of direct experience on usage behavior in post-adoptive usage situations [50], implying that in these scenarios, behavior is likely to be driven by habitual patterns instead of accessible attitudes [28,59].

#

Given the importance of direct experience for the accessibility of an attitude and thus the attitude-behavior relationship in pre-adoption scenarios compared to post-adoption scenarios, users are initially confronted with an IS application. With reference to user attitudes, this implies that when confronted with a new IS artifact, users most likely engage in active cognitive processing [50,60]. Thus, the amount of thought involved in pre-adoption behavior is higher than in post-adoptive behavior. It also implies that users in pre-adoptive situations are not capable of retrieving prior attitudes from memory as a basis for making a decision. User attitudes in these situations are most likely constructed on the spot on the basis of information present at the time [22]. With reference to the predictive validity of attitudes for behavior, this implies that user attitudes in pre-adoptive settings are not expected to be very accessible and therefore likely to result in a weak attitude-behavior relationship.

Nonetheless, given the importance of direct experience, we hypothesize that temporal adoption status has an impact on the attitude-behavior relationship and derive the following hypothesis:

H4: The temporal adoption status significantly influences the attitude-behavior relationship, and the impact is higher if the temporal adoption status is post-adoption than if it is pre-adoption.

## Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we combined scientometric data collection with a meta-analytical analysis approach. This approach was chosen to reflect systematic influences of the situational factors on the attitude-behavior relationship in IS adoption studies. According to Hassan and Loebbecke [61], the research opportunities available as a result of applying scientometrics in IS research approaches include identifying research fronts, associating ideas, and understanding how IS knowledge is transformed from other disciplines and within the IS field itself. Furthermore, as Glass [79] notes, a single empirical investigation would not serve the intended purpose, and analysis at the secondary level (i.e., across multiple studies) will be necessary in the future.

Collecting data using a scientometric approach provides useful insights into how IS adoption researchers publish their content. Leyesdorff defines scientometrics as “the quantitative study of scientific communication” [62], while Lowry et al. [63] regard it as ”the scientific study of the process of science” [63], p. 30). Lewis et al. [64] lauded the contribution of the scientometric approach to facilitating the ongoing evaluation and improvement of an academic discipline. In IS research, Straub [65] emphasized the importance of these self-studies in developing and making progress in the IS field. Scientometric approaches and studies have been conducted on a broad range of topics, including the epistemological structure of the IS field in general [66]. Scientometric studies differ from regular surveys in that they focus on the article itself and not on people’s behavior or background [67,68]. Thus, scientometric studies differ from regular literature reviews [81] in terms of the depth of the analysis. Using tools such as citation analysis or meta-analysis, scientometric studies observe paper titles, author names, paper abstracts and texts or references and appendices, as well as numeric values [80].

Scientometric studies are often conflated with meta-analysis, but we argue that the relation between scientometric approaches and meta-analysis can be described as a stepwise differentiation. Whereas scientometric studies and approaches generate literature-based quantitative data, meta-analysis as “the analysis of analysis” (Glass [79], p.3) and “(…) useful tool for analysis testing combining the results from many individual studies” (Wu and Lederer [55], p. 423) detect and analyze causal and/or correlational relationships in the scientometric data set [79]. Thus, single quantitative studies, scientometric literature reviews, and meta-analysis have a three-step hierarchical relationship.

In the following subsections, the collection of the scientometric data sample, the coding of variables and the analysis approach and results are described in detail.

## Data collection

To ensure the meaningfulness of the results and a large sample size, we included 14 top peer-reviewed journals of the IS field, namely, Management Information Systems Quarterly (MISQ), Information Systems Research (ISR), European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Journal of the Association of Information Systems (JAIS), Journal of Information Technology (JIT), Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), Management Science (MS), Communications of the AIS (CAIS), Decision Sciences (DSI), Decision Support Systems (DSS), Communications of the ACM (CACM) and Information and Management (I&M). In addition to the fact that these journals are part of the AIS Journal Basket, the journals were chosen because of their topical relevance and their having been ranked among the top ten journals by Peffers and Ken [69] and Lowry et al. [70].

We chose a timeframe of 25 years to include the early beginnings of IS adoption research and the introduction of TAM in 1989. Our two-person research team analyzed every issue of the journals selected between spring 1989 and winter 2014, accessing 29,271 articles via Business Source® Complete by EBSCOhost.

We searched the databases using the Boolean operators “AND”, “OR” and “NOT” to combine search items. Like the procedures in other literature research approaches, this technique allowed us to use a continuing procedure with consistent results and without any confusion.

For the purpose of identifying all relevant articles, the following inclusion criteria had to be fulfilled:

1. The study had to have been published in one of the selected journals.

2. The study had to have been published between the introduction of the Technology Acceptance Model in August 1989 [1] and the second issue of 2014, depending on availability.

3. The study had to contain attitude or affiliated terms found in the IS literature [2,22].

4. The study had to be empirical and based on survey data. Purely conceptual models and research approaches using other research methods [71-73] were excluded.

5. Further, the content of the study had to fit within the context of adoption and diffusion.

6. The study had to measure or hypothesize attitudes in some way.

The scientometric search was limited to incidences of the chosen search term attitude appearing in the body, abstract or title of the article, thus fulfilling the inclusion criteria; in total, 29,271 articles were accessed, and 1,255 articles were preliminarily extracted that provided topics and content related to an individual’s attitudes and that met the inclusion criteria 1-4. To check for fulfillment of criteria 5-6, these 1,255 articles were accessed manually, and the content requirements of the criteria were verified, resulting in a pre-final sample of 229 articles

Next, these 229 articles were manually crosschecked for relevance and potential duplication. The findings were then categorized by title, author, year of publication, publication outlet, research subject, context, place and point of time of data collection, type of technology, temporal adoption status, adoption context, voluntariness of usage, etc. The individual role of the construct attitude was assessed in terms of item measurement, methodological approach, beta value, significance (t-value), theoretical underpinnings and construct definition, and impact on other exogenous and endogenous variables. The results were stored and coded in a database. To avoid biased findings and ensure validity of the results, each identified article was crosschecked. We also captured single measurements of attitude and related determinants if an article contained more than one dataset with an attitude construct (e.g., Hsieh et al. 2008). For instance, if a causal model was tested on two different groups of people, we captured each single dataset. All statistical analyses were performed on the basis of the single measurements. The pre-final sample yielded 408 single

#

measurements. Further, because the focus of this research is the attitude-behavior relationship, all articles that had an endogenous variable other than behavior or intention as its representative were eliminated to avoid biases in the analysis.

## Coding procedure

To test our hypotheses, we used the significance level of the attitude-behavior relationship as an endogenous variable. We captured the significance level as thoroughly as possible, which implied that when an article provided exact t-values, we coded the corresponding significance level; if significance thresholds such as 0.01 were provided, we copied them accordingly. We were not initially sure how to handle non-significant relationships because few author teams provided exact t-values. We solved this problem by using a value of 0.15 as the minimum value, which without doubt represents insignificance. For a value of 0.14, one could argue that allowing only one digit would result in 0.1, which is referred to as significant in some publications. All articles for which no significance level was applicable were excluded from the analysis because we aimed at avoiding missing values related to the endogenous variable.

This last sorting step resulted in a final sample of 118 articles and 283 single measurements of attitude that constituted the basis of the analysis. The list of these articles is provided in the appendix.

We coded the exogenous variables according to the categories in Figure 3, reading every article and extracting the information about the situational factors. Following the advice of Van Raan [80], we attempted to minimize potential biases from interpretation and used only the information about the situational factors that was given in the article itself. If the article did not contain the information, we coded a missing value. Most of the article contained exact information about the factor technology type and adoption context, which made it easy to categorize the article into either one of the dichotomous manifestations (e.g., hedonic or utilitarian). Due to the nature of scientometric studies, procedures such as mean value replacement are not feasible for missing values of scientometric studies because the scientometric data are based exclusively on the original values of the reviewed studies themselves taking the original biases on the primary data level into account [79,80]. However, to minimize biases that occur during the coding procedure in the scientometric dataset, [79] and [80] recommend coding missing values if a piece of information cannot be reliably retrieved from the original article.

The coding of the factor voluntariness and temporal adoption status into dichotomous categories of the scientometric dataset was more complex than the coding procedure for technology type and temporal adoption status for several reasons. In the case of voluntariness, the first difficulty was given by the vagueness of the variable itself. Especially in business contexts, the usage of a system can sometimes be voluntary for some departments and mandatory for others. If a sample thus contained members of both groups, the resulting value in the scientometric dataset was a missing value because the article could not be coded as voluntary or mandatory for the complete sample. Thus, we only coded into one of the two categories when it was explicitly clear from the original article that the whole sample used a system either voluntarily or mandatorily. The continuous measurement of voluntariness in some of the original articles represented another difficulty in the coding procedure. According to [79,80], many biases in scientometric data sets occur due to ambiguous coding procedures in terms of scales. To avoid such ambiguity, we applied the following coding principle for voluntariness: An article was coded as voluntary only if either the article explicitly mentioned that usage of the artifact or system was voluntary in general or if the coding of the article as voluntary could be justified based on the nature of the artifact/system in combination with the adoption context (business vs. household), such as online shopping website adoption. Thus, the question that we answered for each article was "is the adoption and usage of the artifact or system in this case voluntary?". If this question could be answered unambiguously, the article was coded as voluntary or mandatory. If the answer was ambiguous, we considered the information to be not retrievable and coded a missing value, which was the case in 45 cases. This procedure guaranteed the smallest possible bias in the results [79].

For temporal adoption status we had to clarify the question regarding how to address longitudinal studies, which contain measurements at two points in time, such as pre- and post-adoption situations. In such cases, we applied the following principle: If an article contained two separate structural models with two points in time, such as a pre- and a post-adoption scenario, we coded the article twice with two different measurements (pre-adoption and postadoption), each with its own values from the model. In cases lacking separate structure models, we followed the same principle as for voluntariness. If explicit information was provided in the article as to whether participants had used an artifact/system in advance, we coded accordingly. If this information could not be retrieved unambiguously, we coded a missing value.

For each situational factor, we chose 0 and 1 values to reflect the respective manifestation (e.g., value 1 for the situational factor adoption context if the context of the respective article was coded as "household environment"). This transformation of nominal into dichotomous variables in a scientometric dataset thereby follows common practice and enables the application of meta-analytic techniques [67,80].

## Data analysis and results

The present section summarizes the results of the data analyses. The final sample consisted of 283 datasets. Because of the non-normal character of the endogenous variable, we conducted non-parametric tests to estimate the impact of the exogenous situational factors on the endogenous variable, employing the Kruskal-Wallis test (see [74] for details). This non-parametric test analyzes differences in the groups being defined by the endogenous variable (significance level of the attitude-behavior relationship) with respect to the categories of the exogenous variables. Small p-values thereby indicate such differences that are likely to originate in the exogenous variables. The results are provided in Table 2 below. For more details, we refer to Siegel and Castellan [75].

Because the Kruskal-Wallis test tests for the impact of the situational factors on the endogenous variable, we further applied a Spearman correlation coefficient to test for the direction of this impact. The results are depicted in Table 2 and indicate that two out of four hypotheses can be fully accepted and one partly accepted. According to the Kruskal-Wallis test, the adoption context, the technology type of the IT artifact in the study, and the degree of voluntariness significantly impact the significance level of the attitude-behavior relationship in IS adoption studies. The temporal adoption status, however, does not exhibit a significant impact in the attitude-behavior relationship.

As to the direction of the impact and the second part of our hypotheses, the adoption context and technology type significantly showed this impact. However, although the impact of voluntariness on the endogenous variable could be verified by the Kruskal-Wallis test, its direction could not be significantly verified by the correlation analysis.

To conceptualize these results with respect to further research from a theoretical standpoint, Figure 2 below illustrates the situational factors that have a significant impact in the attitude-behavior relationship. Due to its cubical format, we call the result the “attitude cube”. The sides of the cube represent the adoption characteristics and their categories, while the arrows within each dimension represent influences on the attitude-behavior relationship, as illustrated in Figure 1.

The arrows on the sides of the cube contain the relative tendencies (high/low) of each influence from Figure 1 within each adoption situational factor category (e.g., low behavioral relevance for mandatory usage). We only included factors that proved significant according to our analysis and hypotheses testing. The arrows also imply relative tendencies, i.e., in which combination of situational factor manifestations the predictive validity of attitude on behavior is assumed to be high, such as in situations with high public-private correspondence, high personal involvement, high internal correspondence, and high behavioral relevance. In terms of concrete adoption scenarios this includes situations in a household context with a voluntary usage environment of hedonic systems.

The cube thus represents the core contribution of this research, providing a structured guide to attitude-behavior consistency in adoption scenarios. Adoption studies can use the cube to preliminarily assess the predictive validity of user attitude on usage behavior with respect to the specific scenario they wish to study.

In addition to the Kruskal-Wallis tests and correlation analysis, the analysis of the descriptive statistics of our scientometric dataset with respect to the attitude cube further supports the results. Within our scientometric dataset, we observed 48 insignificant relationships between attitude and behavior, including 24 (50%) in scenarios with mandatory usage of a utilitarian IT artifact in the business environment. In scenarios with hedonic IT artifacts, voluntary usage, and private contexts, not a single insignificant relationship could be reported within our dataset. Assuming that measurement errors are equally distributed among the studies, the importance of situational factors for the consistency of the attitude-behavior relationship is evident.

## Discussion

The purpose of this research was to investigate reported attitude-behavior inconsistencies in the IS discipline using a structured approach based on situational factors. The results of our analysis of a scientometric dataset of fourteen top IS journals over twenty-five years provides strong evidence that three situational factors, namely, adoption context, technology type, and voluntariness, shape the relationship between an individual’s attitude and the relevant behavior. We’ll discuss the implications for theory and practice in the following.

## Implication for theory

According to Glasman and Albarracín [10] and a review by Bassili [39] in the field of social psychology, situational factors are central in describing attitude stability and attitude accessibility as representatives of the attitude-behavior relationship. To account for the challenge of several reported inconsistencies in the attitude-behavior relationship in the prior IS adoption literature [15], we transferred these insights from social psychology to the IS field and hypothesized the significant impacts of the four central adoption situation factors (adoption context, technology type, level of voluntariness and temporal adoption status) on the attitude-behavior relationship. While prior IS adoption research has focused on each of these constructs in single approaches [49-51,76], there has been no research accounting for all four of them at once. The reason for combining all four of them was our assumption that inconsistencies did not only occur due to the misspecification of one factor but also due to their interplay. The results of our metaanalytical study show that three of the four major situational factors (adoption context, technology type, level of voluntariness) in the prior research indeed played a very relevant role in determining the

#

significance of the attitude-behavior relationship, while temporal adoption status was insignificant. Based on our results, we can state that the attitude-behavior relationship is most significant in IS adoption scenarios in household contexts, when individuals use hedonic technologies on a voluntary basis.

The non-significant impact of the temporal adoption status on the attitude-behavior relationship also has useful implications. While prior research has shown that, especially in post-adoption scenarios [77], beliefs grow stronger over time as individuals experience greater utility and joy through the experience of using the IS artifacts, this impact appears to be weakened by other factors occurring in the scenarios described by the attitude cube. This may be explained by the importance of habits in post-adoption scenarios. While more experience could strengthen beliefs about a technology, it could also lead to more automatic processing in the individual’s technology usage. This also implies that the planned behavior of technology usage as hypothesized and predicted in several prior IS adoption research scenarios might only be valid for pre-adoption scenarios. Researchers focusing on attitudes in post-adoption scenarios in the future might therefore consider taking a closer look at individual habits either as determinants or as control variables for the endogenous variables in their approaches.

In conclusion, the central contribution of our research is twofold: First, although situational factors and context variables have been tested and operationalized in many adoption models and have proven to be significant moderators of many relationships in IS adoption [17,49,78], in terms of their role with respect to the reported insignificance of the attitude-behavior relationship, the IS research has primarily focused on construct operationalization issues and empirical studies [19,21,48] and neglected a systematic problem with respect to a potential influence of situational factors. Our descriptive results indicate that 50% of all insignificant attitudebehavior relationships have the precise situational characteristics that, according to the attitude cube, would predict a low attitude-behavior consistency. Second, our study indicates that attitude-behavior inconsistencies may have a systematic character that is impossible to detect in single empirical studies. The attitude cube reveals that attitudebehavior inconsistency is not a random phenomenon attributable to measurement error but rather substantially dependent on the characteristics and attitude-relevant factors in a situation. As the literature in social psychology indicates, the attitude-behavior relationship is a highly complex process with many influential factors, and this study identifies and structures three factors significant for the IS context.

## Implications for practice

Our research has several implications for practice as well, such as the importance of employee attitudes for their usage behavior. Internal and business campaigns can be planned and managed based on the importance of technology type, adoption context, and voluntariness. Assessing and influencing user attitudes toward the adoption of hedonic IT artifacts used voluntarily in a private environment is important for successfully implementing such IT artifacts.

Creating personal involvement, affective extremity and intra-attitudinal correspondence by emphasizing the usefulness and hedonic value of an IT creates stable accessible attitudes that are key to successfully marketing information systems. In different scenarios, such as implementing a utilitarian IT in the business context, it is essential to focus on cognitive-based trainings and if possible provide voluntary environments because adoption decisions based on attitudes formed under voluntary conditions have proven to be strong and stable [8,10].

## Limitations and future research

As with other studies, our research has limitations that point to opportunities for future research. Meta-analytic studies have the inherent limitation that they contain the errors of their samples. We tried to account for this by keeping the analysis and interpretation broad. Further, although we crosschecked the scientometric dataset, the complexity of the dataset and coding procedure may have led to errors.

We also acknowledge that the interconnectedness between the factor adoption status and habits represent a limitation. In post-adoptive settings, the impact of habit-driven decisions cannot be excluded, especially if habit and attitude operate in the same direction and behavior is consciously planned but in reality directed by some sort of goal-directed automaticity [28, 82].

Like the interconnectedness with habit, the predictive validity of attitude for behavior is also limited if strong external influences are present. For instance, if a behavior is performed under pressure by someone else or is primarily the consequence of some sort of strong social influence, an individual’s attitude is not relevant to the

#

behavior, which implies that it is neither accessible nor predictive with respect to this behavior [83]. Due to the scientometric approach, this research did not account for these interaction effects.

Further, our dataset contained a small number of cases that could not be clearly subordinated to a category of situational factors. To minimize potential biases in the dataset and results, these data points were left out of the analysis. However, given the growing prevalence of technologies with multiple characteristics, such as social media, an additional category of situational factors is needed.

Finally, given the omnipresence of social media and the expansion of the home office, more and more mixed approaches should be expected, including the use of hedonic IT in the business environment, which is an excellent opportunity for future research.
