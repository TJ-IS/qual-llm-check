# What levels of moral reasoning and values explain adherence to information security rules? An empirical study

Liisa Myyry<sup>1</sup>, Mikko Siponen<sup>2</sup>, Seppo Pahnila<sup>2</sup>, Tero Vartiainen<sup>3</sup> and Anthony Vance<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

It is widely agreed that employee non-adherence to information security policies poses a major problem for organizations. Previous research has pointed to the potential of theories of moral reasoning to better understand this problem. However, we find no empirical studies that examine the influence of moral reasoning on compliance with information security policies. We address this research gap by proposing a theoretical model that explains noncompliance in terms of moral reasoning and values. The model integrates two well-known psychological theories: the Theory of Cognitive Moral Development by Kohlberg and the Theory of Motivational Types of Values by Schwartz. Our empirical findings largely support the proposed model and suggest implications for practice and research on how to improve information security policy compliance.

## Introduction

The importance of information security has been highlighted by recent surveys, which suggest that more than 90% of organizations confront at least one serious information security incident in a given year (Lee & Lee, 2002; Willison, 2006). Researchers agree that a majority of such security problems are indirectly caused by employees who violate or neglect the information security polices of their organizations (Warkentin et al., 2007; Whitman & Mattord, 2008). Overcoming this issue has received considerable attention in the literature. Puhakainen (2006) reviewed 60 different approaches designed to improve employee information security policy compliance. These range from security awareness programs (Siponen, 2000) to criminology-based models to explain computer abuse (Straub, 1990; Straub & Welke, 1998; Lee & Lee, 2002), to social-cognitive models, such as Protection Motivation Theory (Pahnila et al., 2007; Siponen et al., 2007).

Interestingly, while previous research has suggested that theories of moral reasoning can explain employee’s adherence to information security policies (Siponen, 2000, 2002), and research on psychology suggests that moral reasoning has an effect on behaviour in general (Blasi, 1980) and policy violations in particular (Greenberg, 2002; King & Mayhew, 2002), we did not find any empirical studies that investigated the effect of moral behaviour on employee’s adherence to information security policies.

Theories of moral reasoning are relevant to information security policies because the decision to violate an information security policy can be understood as a moral conflict, ‘a morally relevant decision-making situation in which the fulfilment of a moral requirement is at stake’ (Vartiainen, 2007, p. 684).<sup>1</sup> For example, a moral conflict arises when one has the obligation to follow a security policy, but feels obligated to assist another individual (a co-worker, manager, or customer) at the cost of breaking or obviating the security policy. A common instance of this is the violation of tailgating, in which an employee with an access control card opens the door to an unauthorized person out of a perceived obligation to be polite (Finne, 1996). Whether such conflicts are minor or severe, theories of moral reasoning and values can help explain why people choose to behave in a certain way (Kohlberg, 1969).

To investigate the potential of moral reasoning and values theories to explain compliance with IS security policies, we developed a theoretical model based on two well-known psychological theories: the Theory of Cognitive Moral Development by Kohlberg (1969) and the Theory of Motivational Types of Values by Schwartz (1992). An empirical test supports the model, which significantly explains employee’s compliance with information security policies.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section ‘Theoretical framework’ advances the research model and hypotheses. Section ‘Methods and measures’ discusses the research method, and Section ‘Results’ presents the empirical results. Section ‘Discussion’ discusses the results, as well as the limitations and implications of the study for practice and research. Finally, Section ‘Conclusion’ summarizes the key findings of the article.

## Theoretical framework

We propose a theoretical model that combines Kohlberg’s (1969) Theory of Cognitive Moral Development and Schwartz’s (1992) Theory of Motivational Types of Values.

Accordingly, our model includes two components: moral reasoning and values.

Moral reasoning refers to the process in which an individual applies moral principles to determine a course of action. In moral reasoning research, the cognitive developmental approach has made the most convincing contribution to this area. The structure of moral thought is most often assessed in terms of Kohlberg’s (1969) Theory of Cognitive Moral Development (Rest, 1994; Siponen & Vartiainen, 2004). We selected this theory for the moral reasoning component of our model because of its comprehensiveness and because it is so widely applied (Macquarrie & Childress, 1986).

In order to understand whether normative beliefs affect employees’ information security behaviour, we cannot overlook the role of values. In fact, previous research in moral psychology suggests that values inform individual behaviour or attitudes (Allport, 1963; Kristiansen & Hotte, 1996). Although a variety of value theories exist, the Theory of Motivational Types of Values by Schwartz (1992) was selected to form the value part of our model for two reasons. First, Schwartz’s (1992) model is regarded as the most comprehensive approach compared with other value models and theories, such those of Hofstede (1980) and Inglehart (1977). Second, Schwartz’s theory has been successfully tested in more than 60 countries, demonstrating strong generalizability. We next describe these two theories in greater detail.

## Kohlberg’s theory of cognitive moral development

Kohlberg’s (1969) theory suggests that moral judgment is developed through six stages (see Table 1).

Together, the moral judgment stages form three levels of moral reasoning: the preconventional level (stages 1 and 2); the conventional level (stages 3 and 4); and the postconventional level (stages 5 and 6). At the preconventional level, individuals act in order to avoid sanctions and penalties (stage 1) or receiving something in exchange, such as a salary raise or reward (stage 2). At the conventional level, individuals comply with the rules and expectations of society and significant others, and this level of moral reasoning is typical for adolescents and adults (Kohlberg, 1984). At stage 3, individual behaviour is based on conforming to the expectations of others, as well as the expectations associated with a social role or a profession. At stage 4, individuals follow the laws and norms for their own sake. In this stage, individuals use arguments like ‘I comply with the policy because it is the policy of our company’ or ‘I don’t commit jaywalking, because it is illegal’. At stage 5, the decision is based on a utilitarian calculation, the aim being to select an act that produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people. In stage 5, an individual should decide whether compliance with security policies produces more satisfaction for the organization and its clients than noncompliance. Stage 6 thinking reflects impartiality. To be more precise, at stage 6 a person applies the principle of universality, in which if one judges that one’s action in a particular situation is right, one must then acknowledge that a similar act by anybody else in a similar situation would also be right (Hare, 1981).

To test Kohlberg’s theory, Rest (1979) established the defining issues test (DIT). The DIT is a multiple-choice test with 12 items representing different stages of Kohlberg’s theory. For the DIT, respondents are asked to rate the relative importance of each item on a five-point scale, and then to rank the four most important items. The DIT is a recognition task where the scenario is first presented to a respondent and then followed by a standard set of items to evaluate. The DIT has been the most widely utilized method in moral research. It has been used in over 40 countries in over 1000 studies with several hundred thousand respondents (Rest, 1994).

Previous research has found that the level of moral judgment relates positively to behaviour. To start with, Blasi’s (1980) review revealed a positive correlation between moral reasoning and actual behaviour. Similarly, with respect to the DIT, Rest (1986) reported a fairly moderate relationship between Kohlberg’s postconventional moral reasoning and moral behaviour. In addition, King & Mayhew’s (2002) review of the relationship between moral judgment and behaviour revealed that behaviour was positively related to the level of moral reasoning. They found that respondents giving high preference to postconventional items in their rank of the most important items in the DIT (the so-called ‘P-score’) were more willing to blow the whistle by calling the potential error to the attention of the investigator in an experimental design and less likely to cheat, for instance. In many studies, a conventional level of moral reasoning has been positively associated with morally relevant behaviour. For example, in his study about employee theft, Greenberg (2002) found that employees at the conventional level of moral reasoning were less likely to steal from their employers – especially when they worked in an office with an ethics program – than employees at the preconventional level. Renwick & Emler (1984) showed that delinquent and antisocial behaviour was negatively related to conventional reasoning and positively related to postconventional reasoning. Moreover, Emler et al. (2007) found that a preference for conventional moral reasoning related positively to moral responsibility, whereas the correlation with postconventional reasoning was insignificant and in fact negative.

In summation, at the preconventional level of moral judgment, individuals are sensitive to rewards and punishments, and immediate self-interest guides their behaviour. At the conventional level, individuals are sensitive to the rules and norms that guide behaviour, as discussed earlier. People attaining this level do not define right and wrong in terms of self-interest, but tend to adopt and internalize the rules of larger social units, like organizations, and make their moral judgments based on what others expect of them. At the postconventional level, individuals understand the purpose of the rules and norms, but can adopt a critical viewpoint of them (Kohlberg, 1984; Narvaez & Bock, 2002).

Moral judgment instruments such as the DIT assess hypothetical behavioural choices (what should the protagonist do?). However, in the context of information security, it is also worthwhile to investigate actual behavioural choices (what does the respondent do?), referred to as actual compliance. In the domain of moral research, the findings concerning the relationship between actual and hypothetical behaviour are mixed (Denton & Krebs, 1990; Carpendale & Krebs, 1995). In real-life situations, several factors might influence behaviour. Therefore, to estimate the relationship between behaviour and moral components, it is essential to measure actual behaviour.

## Value priorities

There is widespread agreement that people’s value priorities play an important role in predicting their attitudinal and behavioural decisions. Gordon Allport (1963), for example, suggested that human activity is directed toward the realization of values. Because the level of moral judgment may be insufficient, per se, to provoke moral behaviour (Blasi, 1980; Rest, 1986), Kristiansen & Hotte (1996), for instance, claimed that values may provide transsituational ideals regulating beliefs about what people ought to do and, thereby, their attitudes and behaviour.

Human values can be defined as enduring beliefs that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence (Rokeach, 1973). In this study, values are assessed in terms of Schwartz’s (1992) Theory of Motivational Types of Values. This theory defines values as goals and motivations that serve as guiding principles in people’s lives. The original Schwartz Value Survey contains 56 single values that can be divided into 10 distinct motivational types that serve different interests or motivational goals. The single values, which are each followed by an explanatory phrase, are measured on a nine-point scale (-1 ¼ opposite my values; 0 ¼ not at all important; 7 ¼ of supreme importance). Value types and their contents (the interests they serve) are as follows (the single values included in each value type are in the parentheses):

1. Power: societal prestige and controlling others (social power, wealth, authority).

2. Achievement: personal success and competence according to social norms (successful, capable, ambitious, influential).

3. Hedonism: pleasure and satisfaction of sensual needs (pleasure, enjoying life).

4. Stimulation: excitement, novelty, and challenge in life (daring activities, a varied and/or exciting life).

5. Self-direction: independent action and thought, making one’s own choices (creative, emphasis on freedom, curious, independent, choosing one’s own goals).

6. Universalism: understanding, tolerance, and protection for the welfare of all people and for nature (social justice, broadmindedness, world at peace, wisdom, a world of beauty, unity with nature, protecting the environment, equality).

7. Benevolence: protecting the welfare of close others in everyday interactions (helpful, forgiving, honest, loyal, responsible).

8. Tradition: respect, commitment, and acceptance of the customs and ideas that one’s culture or religion imposes on the individual (accepting one’s share in life, devotion, respect of tradition, humility, moderation).

9. Conformity: restraint of actions, inclinations, and impulses likely to upset or harm others, or violate social expectations or norms (obedience, selfdiscipline, politeness, honouring parents and elders).

10. Security: safety, harmony, and stability of society, of relationships, and of self (family security, national security, social order, cleanliness, reciprocation of favours).

The 10 distinct value types form a special structure based on the goals and interests that the value types serve. Multidimensional structural analyses of the relationships between the single values of several cultures, including Finland, support the distinctiveness of the 10 value types, as well as the circular structure of relations among them (Verkasalo et al., 1994; Schwartz & Boehnke, 2004; see Figure 1). Competing values are located in opposite directions from the centre, while complementary values are adjacent in the circle. The value types form a two-dimensional continuum, and the first dimension is Openness to Change versus Conservation. People can either show the motivation to follow their own intellectual and emotional interests (self-direction, stimulation, and hedonism value types) or they can prefer the status quo and the certainty provided by relationships with close others, institutions, and traditions (tradition, conformity, and security value types). The second dimension is called Self-Transcendence versus Self-Enhancement. The former relates to the extent to which people are motivated to transcend selfish concerns and promote the welfare of others (including the values of benevolence and universalism). Self-Enhancement comprises values that motivate people to further their own personal interests, even at the expense of others (power and achievement values) (Schwartz, 1992). Adjacent value types overlap conceptually in the motivations they express, and are sometimes mixed, such as tradition and conformity, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Value can be seen as a part of self-presentation, which informs others of the quality of the individual. Values are claimed to be ‘intimately bound up with a person’s sense of self’ (Feather, 1992, p. 112) and ‘a type of personality disposition’ (Bilsky & Schwartz, 1994, p. 178). Thus, values’ connection to one’s identity – both personal and professional – should make values preference a motivational force affecting behaviour, evaluations, and one’s choice of alternatives.

For instance, in situations where people are faced with a temptation to violate a norm, for example, the decision not to comply with an information security policy, willpower, and the value of self-discipline, which is one of Schwartz’s conformity values, are required. Of the value dimensions, conformity is involved in Conservation, which also includes security values. Openness to Change serves goals that conflict with Conservation. Previous studies have revealed, for instance, that conformity and tradition correlate positively with trust in different kinds of institutions (such as the judicial system, the police force, and the economic system), whereas Openness to Change value types, such as self-direction and hedonism, showed negative correlations with trust in these institutions (Devos et al., 2002). Sagiv & Schwartz (2004) found that the independent behaviour of career counselling clients was negatively related to Conservation values and positively related to self-direction. In addition, value types in general have been found to correlate positively with the corresponding behaviours through a measurement of behaviour expressions for the 10 value types (such as ‘Obey my parents’ for conformity and ‘Examine the ideas behind rules and regulations before obeying them’ for self-direction) (Bardi & Schwartz, 2003). Hence, in this study, we concentrate on the relationships between Conservation and Openness to Change in examining compliance with information security policy.

Reviews of the findings from a dozen studies that have related value priorities to Kohlbergian measures of moral judgment development and postconventional reasoning based on the Schwartz model show that positive associations are limited to three values. Around two-thirds of the positive associations were with universalism values, while the remaining associations were distributed equally between the two values adjacent to universalism in the Schwartz circular model: benevolence and self-direction. Most of the negative associations between values and postconventional moral reasoning have been found for conformity and security values, even though negative associations have been reported for all remaining values, except tradition (Kristiansen & Hotte, 1996; Helkama et al., 2003). Moreover, Feather (1990) has reported a positive correlation between obedience and the stage 4 moral judgment and Lan et al. (2008) have found a positive link between conformity and a preference for stage 4 reasoning.

To summarize, based on the conceptual links between levels of moral reasoning and adherence to information security policy, as well as the previous empirical findings, the following hypotheses will be tested.

H1a Preconventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a hypothetical situation.

H1b Preconventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a real-life situation.

H2a Conventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a hypothetical situation.

H2b Conventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a real-life situation.

H3a Postconventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a hypothetical situation.

H3b Postconventional moral reasoning is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a real-life situation.

Furthermore, based on the previously reported associations of values with social conformity and moral reasoning, the following hypotheses were posited.

H4a Openness to change is negatively related to compliance with the information security policy in a hypothetical situation.

H4b Openness to change is negatively related to compliance with the information security policy in a real-life situation.

H5a Conservation is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a hypothetical situation.

H5b Conservation is positively related to compliance with the information security policy in a real-life situation.

The theoretical models we tested in this study are presented in Figure 2. The hypotheses were tested with multivariate regression analysis. Moral reasoning and values explained hypothetical and actual compliance behavioural choices in the first and second models.

## Primary data collection

The actual data was gathered from two populations in Finland. The first population consisted of clerical employees of the technical service centre of a town in West Finland. This population consisted of 250 employees, 97 of whom answered the questionnaire (38 females, 55 males, and 4 who did not disclose their gender), with the response rate being 38.8%. The second population consisted of part-time master’s students with work experience from Central Finland. The population consisted of 66 individuals who all answered the questionnaire (34 females, 28 males, and 4 who did not disclose their gender) in the classroom. Altogether, the questionnaire was delivered to 163 individuals. Of these 163 respondents, 31 provided incomplete data, leaving a final data set of 132 respondents. This technique uses scenarios that ‘present subjects with written descriptions of realistic situations and then request responses on a number of rating scales that measure the dependent variables of interest’ (Trevino, 1992, p. 127–128). In the field of IS, the scenario method has been used to study ethical issues, such as software piracy (Moores & Chang, 2006), computer abuse (Harrington, 1996; Banerjee et al., 1998; D’Arcy et al., 2009), and privacy concerns (Malhotra et al., 2004).

To apply this method, we first designed a scenario that describes a situation in which a person violates an information security policy of his organization:

Nurses use a healthcare information system. They obtain patient information from this system, and the nurses have to use a personal user name and password in the system. Many nurses find the use of personal user names and passwords to be cumbersome because they have difficulty remembering their passwords. One of the nurses wondered whether he should share his username and password with his co-workers.

Respondents were then asked, ‘Should the nurse share his password with his co-workers?’ To answer this question, respondents were asked to choose between ‘Definitely should not share’ and ‘Definitely should share’ on a scale of one to nine. This answer is used to measure respondents’ perceptions of hypothetical behaviour. Concerning their own behaviour (actual compliance with information security policies), the respondents were asked to choose an answer between ‘I don’t ever share my password with my co-workers’ and ‘I always share my passwords with co-workers if it helps their work’ on a scale of one to nine. For the analyses, the scales were revised so that higher scores indicated adherence to an information security policy (1 ¼ ‘Definitely should share’ and 9 ¼ ‘Definitely should not share’ for the hypothetical behaviour and 1 ¼ ‘I always share my passwords with coworkers’ while 9 ¼ ‘I don’t ever share my password’). These items were followed by 11 items representing different stages of moral judgment adapted from the DIT instrument that required respondents to rate their importance (1 ¼ very important; 5 ¼ insignificant). Furthermore, they were asked to rank the four most important items in deciding what to do in the hypothetical situation (for more details about the questionnaire, see Appendix A).

After revising the scales of the rating items (1 ¼ insignificant; 5 ¼ very important), we adapted three items representing the moral reasoning levels: preconventional (two items: ‘Is there a penalty or sanction for sharing passwords?’ representing stage 1 and ‘Does the nurse understand that he may lose his right to use the information system due to sharing his user name and password with his colleagues?’ representing stage 2), conventional (five items representing stages 3 and 4; for example, ‘Should the nurse share his personal user name and password because, in this way, he is able to decrease his colleagues’ workloads?’ for stage 3 and ‘Is the sharing of the user name and the password with colleagues in accordance with the data security policies of the hospital and the laws of society?’ for stage 4), and postconventional (three items representing stages 5 and 6; for example, ‘As a whole, will the giving of the user name and password cause more bad than good for the work community and the society?’ for stage 5, and ‘Is it possible for the users of a healthcare information system to accept as a general principle that a user name and password can be shared among colleagues?’ for stage 6). The DIT also includes meaningless but complex-sounding items (the ‘M-items’) in order to detect respondents who prefer items for their complexity and pretentiousness rather than for their meaning (Rest, 1979). We used one M-item: ‘Are the essential contents of the user names connected to the granularity of the passwords?’, which was not included with the moral reasoning variables.

The reliabilities of the moral reasoning variables (Cronbach’s alphas) were 0.67 for preconventional, 0.63 for conventional, and 0.57 for postconventional reasoning. Although the Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.57 is low, it can be considered acceptable in new research contexts (Nunnally, 1967).

Values. Value priorities were measured using the Portrait Value Questionnaire (PVQ) (Schwartz et al., 1999), which was designed by Schwartz et al. for an indirect measure of values. It presents descriptions of 40 different people. Each portrait consists of two sentences that characterize the person’s goals, aspirations, and wishes, and they are all expressive of a single value type. Respondents make similarity judgments by comparing the portrait to themselves and indicating how much the characterized person is like them, based on a six-point scale (1 ¼ not like me at all; 6 ¼ very much like me). The data collected with the PVQ have replicated the circular structure of the 10 value types previously found with the SVS (Schwartz et al., 1999; Schwartz & Rubel, 2005).

From these 40 portraits, two value dimensions were formed: Openness to Change (10 portraits, such as ‘It is important to him to make his own decisions about what he does. He likes to be free and not depend on others’ and ‘He thinks it’s important to be interested in things. He likes to be curious and to try to understand all sorts of things’), and Conservation (13 portraits, such as ‘He believes that people should do what they are told. He thinks people should follow rules at all times, even when no one is watching’ and ‘He thinks it is best to do things in traditional ways. It is important to him to keep up the customs he has learned’). To control differential use of the scale, centralized sum variables were utilized in the analysis; a personal mean of all 40 portraits was calculated for each subject separately, and the means of the sum variables were divided by the personal mean. The reliabilities (Cronbach’s alphas) of the values were good: Openness to Change was 0.81, while Conservation was 0.79.

## Results

Regression analysis is the most common method of analyzing the DIT instrument (Rest et al., 1997) and values in terms of Schwartz (e.g., Sagiv & Schwartz, 2000; Schwartz et al., 2000; Schwartz, 2007). Accordingly, regression was used to provide better comparability to these studies.

Because our data comprised two subsamples, we performed several tests to ensure that the data met the assumption of normality required for regression analysis. First, we examined the distribution of the dependent and independent variables, as well as the residuals, all of which were normal. We then compared the two samples using a T-test to see if they differed in terms of dependent variable measurement. No significant difference was found in this comparison. Finally, we created a dummy variable for the subsample to control for the possible effect of a difference in subsamples. We entered this control variable into a hierarchical regression analysis in which the first block contained the control variable and the second block contained the independent variables of the model. The control variable had no significant effect on the dependent variables, both in terms of the b-coefficient and the amount of variance. The results of these tests therefore confirmed that the sample was homogenous.

## Discussion

We next highlight four findings based on our empirical results. First, preconventional moral reasoning is positively related to both hypothetical and actual compliance in the information security context, while conventional moral reasoning (both hypothetical and actual) correlates negatively with behaviour. Preconventional moral reasoning focuses on fear of sanctions (the first stage in Kohlberg’s theory) and ‘What’s in it for me?’ thinking (stage 2). These findings on conventional reasoning were contrary to previous findings with respect to the DIT. Usually, conventional or postconventional reasoning is positively related to morally relevant behaviours (e.g., Blasi, 1980; Rest, 1986; Greenberg, 2002; Emler et al., 2007). Preconventional reasoning has been considered to be immature and selfish. For instance, a series of studies conducted by Rest and his colleagues (Rest, 1994) and Walker et al. (1984) have concluded that ‘higher is better’ in moral judgment by revealing that respondents preferred higher-stage arguments over lower ones when asked to rate the adequacy of arguments representing the different stages. Even though respondents comprehended the arguments below their own stage, they did not give them high preference ratings, but considered them simple-minded or childish. Our results imply, however, that in the context of information security – specifically regarding the issue of giving a password to others – lower is better. This may indicate that our respondents considered the information security policy that one should not share one’s password to co-workers to be an externally forced policy that they may comply with only when it is in their immediate interest to do so, not as a principle that would uphold cooperation between friends and allies (representing stage 3) or for society in general (representing stage 4). In addition, they might consider the act of giving one’s password to others as a threat to themselves, that is, as an act that might violate their own security (representing stage 2). The finding that people prefer low stages in terms of Kohlberg’s Theory of Cognitive Moral Development also supports previous findings in the area of user information security behaviour, suggesting that sanctions deter computer abuse (Straub, 1990) and that the threat of sanctions increases employee compliance with information security polices (Siponen et al., 2007).

The other possible theoretical explanation for this finding is that the context of information security represents a non-structuralist moral order (Harre´, 1983) of preconventional reasoning. Moral order refers to implicit expectations of appropriate behaviour in different social contexts, meaning that in different social contexts, different behaviours and judgments are expected (Carpendale & Krebs, 1995). For instance, according to previous studies (Carpendale & Krebs, 1992, 1995), the moral order of business represents predominantly stage 2 judgments, that is, the morality of instrumental egoism and simple exchange. Applying this view to our situation, it may be claimed that when it comes to giving password to a co-worker in a hospital context, the moral order resembles that of the preconventional level (stages 1 and 2). Perhaps because it is commonly known that privacy laws in Europe in general and Finland in particular pose strict requirements for the hospital environment, respondents reacted strongly to the threat of sanctions.

In the area of illegal software copying, scholars have also reported unexpected findings with respect to moral reasoning. For example, in their study of illegal software copying, Logsdon et al. (1994) found no positive relationship between the levels of moral development and attitudes toward software copying. Their explanation of the results is that perhaps individuals who are copying software do not perceive copying as a moral issue. The same explanation might apply to our case. To continue this line of reasoning, it could be postulated that perhaps the respondents were only afraid of sanctions for noncompliance with information security policies, and did not perceive compliance with policies to be a moral decision. This would explain the finding that only the preconventional level is positively related to both hypothetical and actual behaviour in the case of giving one’s password to a co-worker.

Third, of the value dimensions, Openness to Change was negatively related to behavioural choices in the information security context. The association makes sense conceptually, given that Openness to Change values refer to following one’s own intellectual and emotional interests, such as choosing one’s own goals (self-direction), seeking excitement, novelty, and challenge in life (stimulation), or seeking pleasure and the satisfaction of sensual needs (hedonism). In other words, people who exhibit Openness to Change do not respect rules in general (Bardi & Schwartz, 2003) and, therefore, may display a critical attitude toward information security policies as well. Our result is in line with previous findings showing that respondents high in Openness to Change values recognized fewer moral issues in professional dilemmas (Myyry & Helkama, 2002), used less complex decision-making strategies in resolving value conflicts (Myyry, 2002), and were less prone to feeling guilty over transgressions (Silfver et al., 2008).

Fourth, our results suggest that hypothetical and actual behavioural choices are very significantly related to each other, at least in the case of giving one’s password to others. The high means of hypothetical and actual compliance (M ¼ 8.22 and 7.77, respectively, on a scale of one to nine) indicate that most of our respondents agreed that the password should not be shared with others and reported their own behaviour in accordance with that policy. Nevertheless, the hypothetical behavioural choices (hypothetical compliance) were more convergent with the information security policy than the respondents’ actual behavioural choices (actual compliance).

## Implications for future research

Our findings highlight four opportunities for future research on employee information security policy compliance and moral motivation and reasoning. First, there is a need to study the influence of other components of ethical decision-making on an employee’s compliance with information security policies. It has been suggested (Rest, 1986, 1994) that there are at least four major psychological processes that must have occurred in order for moral behaviour to take place: (1) interpreting the particular situation as involving moral issues (moral sensitivity); (2) making a judgment about which course of action is morally right or fair (moral reasoning); (3) being motivated to behave morally, for example, by giving priority to moral values above other personal values (moral motivation); and (4) having the courage and skill to carry out a line of moral action, even under pressure (moral character). In this study, we focused on moral reasoning in order to explain employee behaviour as it relates to information security. Future research is needed to study the role of these other components on employee compliance with information security policies.

A second avenue for future research is to study whether individuals’ moral judgment with respect to following or breaking information security policies is context-bounded. Carpendale & Krebs (1995) stated that individual moral judgment is situational, that is, it varies on a case-by-case basis. Hence, there is a need to study whether or not this applies to employees’ compliance with information security policies. The obvious way to accomplish this is to design a number of scenarios that cover different situations where an employee fails to comply with information security policies. However, before this research avenue is approached, it would be useful to explore what the most common and problematic information security policy violations are.

Third, with respect to the postulation that perhaps respondents did not perceive non-compliance with security policies as a moral issue at all, future research should evaluate whether different computer crimes, such as violation of email privacy or hacking, make a difference in terms of individuals’ moral reasoning. Here, the idea is that perhaps violation of email privacy, for example, is regarded as a more (serious) moral issue (than compliance with common information security policies), which awakens individuals’ moral reasoning. Another research opportunity is to study qualitative interviews regarding whether employees perceive compliance with security policies as a moral issue.

## Implications for practice

The findings of our study have implications for organizations that are interested in improving the information security behaviour of their employees. Our results suggest that people who exhibit preconventional moral reasoning (i.e., those who believe that they will be punished if they do not follow IS security policies, or those who see the act of divulging their password to others as posing a personal risk) are more likely to obey the policies. This result suggests that, for information security staff in organizations, punishment is a viable solution to ensure that employees comply with the information security policies of their organization, at least in the case of sharing passwords. Deterrence Theory suggest that sanctions work if employees believe that negligence in terms of adherence to information security policies will certainly be detected and that severe sanctions will occur immediately after non-compliance (Akers & Sellers, 1994).

While proper punishment activities are important in light of our findings, our findings also suggest that information security policies should be better rationalized, so that the importance of these policies to the organization and the work community is clarified. In that way, compliance with information security policies would be more attractive for those employees who are non-conformist or who do not act only with their own interests in mind. In addition, organizations should focus on ensuring that their employees internalize the importance of complying with information security policies (Siponen, 2000). If information security policies are seen as external by employees, and employees only comply with the policies if they are punished – as our findings suggest – employee motivation for complying with information security policies in the long run may be problematic. The dysfunctional effects of punishment or power assertion (punishment-oriented upbringing) have long been established, both experimentally and longitudinally (Hoffman, 1970). In addition, several experiments have shown that a nonpunitive strategy is most effective in eliciting co-operative behaviour, whereas a deterrent strategy elicits more aggressive and self-protective, and less co-operative, behaviours than other strategies (Deutsch, 1985). There are additional reasons for organizations to invest in actions that help employees to internalize information security policies (Siponen, 2000). For example, the effective use of sanctions requires constant monitoring since sanctions are effective only if people believe that they may be caught (Akers & Sellers, 1994). If there is no adequate monitoring present, the risk of getting caught is not high enough from the employee viewpoint. Constant monitoring requires resources, which cost the organization, whereas compliance with information security policies, based on the moral duty to follow the security policies, is less costly to the organization. Finally, organizations might prefer employees who are non-conformist, that is, self-directed and creative, and who give high priority to Openness to Change values. This is based on the postulation that organizations want to have employees that are self-directed and creative, rather than employees who act based on a fear of sanctions.

## Limitations of the study

This study is subjected to some typical limitations. The study used two populations. Following Popper (1985), care should be taken in generalizing these findings to other populations (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Our study used a case wherein a nurse pondered whether he should share his password with co-workers and, again, care should be taken in generalizing these findings to other situations.

Another limitation of our study is its use of a single scenario. In studying hypothetical moral reasoning, one finds that the use of at least three scenarios is recommended (Rest, 1979; Colby & Kohlberg, 1987). However, deVries & Walker (1986) based their scoring of moral judgment on a single case. Therefore, while the use of three scenarios is common, previous research has also utilized just one scenario.

## Conclusion

Negligent employee attitudes toward information security policies constitute a vital concern for organizations. A number of methods to improve employee adherence to security policies have been suggested in the information security literature. While previous studies have suggested that normative theories may explain employee adherence to information security policies, we did not find any studies in the literature that have accomplished this. To address this shortcoming in the literature, we presented a new theoretical model combining wellknown psychological theories: the theory of Cognitive Moral Development by Kohlberg and a theory of motivational types of values by Schwartz (1992). We suggested that this model explains employee adherence to information security policies, at least in situations where an employee is giving his or her personal passwords to his or her co-workers.

Our empirical findings suggest that preconventional moral reasoning is positively related to both hypothetical and actual compliance in the given information security context. In turn, conventional moral reasoning (both hypothetical and actual) correlates negatively with behaviour, while the value dimension of Openness to Change is negatively associated with behaviour. Our findings suggest that respondents who prioritize Openness to Change values (to follow their own intellectual and emotional interests) were less likely to follow the information security policy than respondents with a lower regard for these values. Finally, we found that hypothetical and actual behavioural choices are very significantly related to one another. Overall, the results suggest that people who exhibit preconventional moral reasoning (i.e., those who believe that they will be punished if they do not follow IS security policies, or those who see the act of divulging their password to others as posing a personal risk) are more likely to obey the policies. On the basis of our findings, implications for the practice of improving employees’ information security behaviour are advanced. While sanctions seem to work well, at least in the case of providing a personal password to the co-workers, we suggest that organizations also employ other means, such as education, to ensure that people internalize information security policies.
