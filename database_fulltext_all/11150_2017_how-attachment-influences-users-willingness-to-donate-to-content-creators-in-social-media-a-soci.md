## A B S T R A C T

As a relatively new behavior, donation to content creators in social media has become very popular in the last few years. Different from traditional donation to nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organization or victims, donation to content creators in social media has received little attention from academic researchers. On the basis of the socio-technical systems framework and attachment theory, this study develops a model to investigate the effects of social and technological factors on users’ donation behavior. Our results indicate that donation intention is determined by the emotional attachment to the content creator and functional dependence on social media, which are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by both social factors (identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interaction, and information value) and technical factors (sociability and personalization).

## 1. Introduction

Social media encompass a wide range of websites such as social networking sites, blogs, microblogs, and websites such as YouTube and Flickr that support content sharing [88]. Social media play a vital role in the creation and sharing of information through usergenerated content (UGC) within online social networks [130]. In recent years, social media have become increasingly popular and attracted not only businesses’ participation and contribution but also researchers to investigate the motivation behind their use [114].

To be an attractive platform to users, especially content creators, social media provide various functions such as allowing users to create their own channels, post contents that can be instantaneously shared with a wide audience, choose to be friends with others or subscribe to other channels, and comment on or choose favorite contents [37]. Because encouraging UGC and opinion formation are the core goals, some social media sites in China provide a new function—donation—that allows users or the audience to donate to content creators. Users donate on the basis of the knowledge and service provided by content creators, and content creators can gain monetary or nonmonetary rewards as a result. Therefore, such a function provides an incentive for the content creators. In addition, content creators also share the revenue generated from users’ donation with the platform, which can support social media in ensuring their innovativeness and long-term survival [81].

The donation function as a business strategy has become quite popular, particularly in China. For example, Sina Weibo, the most well-known microblogging platform in China, has provided the donation function since 2014, and the revenues through donation reached more than 40 million yuan in 2015. YY platform, a usergenerated live video streaming site similar to Twitch.tv, obtained 676.2 million yuan in revenue from donation in the fourth quarter of 2014 and attracted more than 200,000 content creators in its music channel [90,148]. In 2015, WeChat Public Accounts also tested the feasibility of the donation function by allowing personal public accounts to add the donation button at the end of the contents they propagated. However, social media are usually free of charge, and most users do not need to pay to view contents [86]. Therefore, an understanding of users’ motivation behind donation in social media is important for researchers and social media developers to help businesses to unleash their potential. Although donation to social media content creators is not prevalent in the Western culture, its success in China makes it an attractive feature to consider for social media providers there. Our research provides social media providers around the world with important practical implications on how to understand and motivate users’ donation behavior.

Given that donation is a relatively new function in social media and no research has investigated this phenomenon yet, our study <sup>fi</sup>rst compares it to donation to street performance or busking, and then we focus on the charitable and consumptive attributes of this behavior. This study draws upon the socio-technical systems framework to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social and technological characteristics on users’ affection toward and intention to donate to content creators in social media. As social media is a sociotechnical information platform [140], the socio-technical systems framework allows us to capture both the social and technical aspects of social media in our understanding of donation behaviors there. In addition, we investigate users’ motivation by applying the attachment theory, a theoretical framework that has been used to study the relationship between attachment and donation. Attachment theory helps us to empirically test the distinction between emotional attachment and functional dependence considering that such attachments are induced by different aspects of users’ affection. In addition, we examine the effects of emotional attachment to the content creator and functional dependence on social media on donation intention. Moreover, the combination of the socio-technical systems framework and attachment theory can provide us a better understanding of which type of social and technical factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence attachment to predict social media donation behavior.

The present research makes the following contributions. First, this study extends the literature on donation behavior to social media, whereas previous research mainly focuses on charitable organizations and victims. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine the Pay-What-You-Want (PWYW) donation model in social media, where the original motivation of the knowledge contributor or content creator is not <sup>fi</sup>nancial gains. Second, we extend the understanding of the motivation of users’ donation intention using attachment theory and examine the crucial roles of social and technical aspects as antecedents of attachment on the basis of socio-technical systems.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, the theoretical background of the research is presented. In the third section, we discuss the research model and hypothesis, followed by the research methodology including measurement development, data collection, and analysis in the fourth section. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal section, discussion of the results, the theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and future research are presented.

## 2. Literature review

In this section, we review the theoretical underpinnings of our research by <sup>fi</sup>rst providing an overview of donation in social media and its two attributes. Then we introduce the attachment theory and the socio-technical systems framework.

## 2.1. Donation in social media

Researchers in various social science disciplines including psychology [77,146], economics [28,60], marketing [94,98], and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organization domain [19,116] have examined the donation behavior. In these literatures, donation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as charitable giving behavior to organizations or others beyond one’s own family. Donation may be both monetary and nonmonetary such as donating organs, blood, clothes, and toys [15,19,53,56]. In social media, the donation function is used by social media platforms as an incentive to improve the richness and quality of UGC [35].

Donation in social media is similar to busking or street performance because a performer or content creator <sup>fi</sup>rst delivers service or knowledge, and then the audience gives a voluntary amount [84]. As a joint product, basking includes both music and charity, whereas charity is exhibited by the audience only and is considered as the price paid for the music. Therefore, performers even though freeriding behavior exists (e.g. [84]). “Busking” in social media also involves a joint product: charity and the service or knowledge provided by the content creator. Thus, we argue that donation in social media has two inherent attributes: the charitable attribute and the consumptive attribute.

The consumptive attribute of donation implies that the donors pay for the consumption of goods or service, such as contents or service provided by the creators. From this perspective, the performers have to make sure their performance appeals to the prospective audience, visitors, or buyers. Some researchers have investigated how intercultural and intracultural identities exhibited by street performers increase the attention of the audience (e.g. [25]). The audience can set the price for the street performance and use the PWYW pricing scheme. However, as street performance lacks the mechanisms to ensure payments or donations and are vulnerable to freeriding, some researchers noticed that charitable causes may also be the major motivations for the audience’s donation behavior [65]. Kushner and Brooks [84] modeled the audience’s payment intention and found both musical service and reputation in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision to pay. Similar to the donation behavior examined in the busking context, donation in social media also presents freeriding opportunities and employs the PWYW pricing scheme. However, the lack of social pressure in such a virtual context might further decrease users’ donation intention. Considering more and more social media platforms have started to support the donation function as a revenue source, it is important to understand what motivates users’ willingness to donate to content creators.

## 2.1.1. Charitable attribute of donation in social media

Charitable- or gift-giving behaviors as a type of donation behavior have been studied in various social science disciplines including psychology, economics, and anthropology, and in marketing and the nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organization domain [15,19,81,95,116]. Researchers view the act of gift giving as symbolic or even a “labor of love” for some. When individuals make a payment or donation, they usually do not receive direct commensurate tangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [118].

Research on donation has studied why people give or donate by applying the motivation theory. Such research has classi<sup>fi</sup>ed motivation to donate into two categories: intrinsic and extrinsic. Intrinsic motivations to donate can be closely associated with the “warm effect,” the positive emotions associated with doing good and helping others, which is also called altruism or bene<sup>fi</sup>t to others [5,6,146]. Many individuals donate to nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations out of a desire to help these organizations to achieve their mission or to support a particular cause. That is, they enjoy the satisfaction from the experiences of making the donation [69,127]. Sargeant et al. [116] concluded that empathy, sympathy, and emotions such as guilt, pity, and fear can considerably affect individuals’ intrinsic motivation. In contrast, extrinsic motivations are the means through which individuals can gain rewards or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from engaging in a behavior. These bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be tangible such as tax breaks [124] and monetary rewards [54] or intangible such as praise, recognition, or grades [59,79]. For example, an individual who donates to an arts organization may gain a better seat at an event, and the “purchase” of a brick in a community garden can have one’s name displayed as a donor. Extrinsic motivations promote donation because individuals will consider them before donating [118].

In addition to motivation theory, research suggests relation ships between donors and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations in terms of trust, commitment, and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in<sup>fl</sup>uence the donation behavior [81,116,117]. One of the effective tools to let individuals know what a nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organization does and how it helps its victims and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries is storytelling, and many nonpro<sup>fi</sup>ts deem storytelling as a means to differentiate themselves from others in the minds of (prospective) donors and the public. Merchant et al. [94] studied the relationship between charitable organizations’ storytelling and emotion and found that problem description can indeed arouse negative emotions, which in turn can be alleviated through the opportunity to donate to the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed cause, after which, the donor would experience greater positive emotions and lesser negative emotions.

In prior research, bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries of donations can be people who suffer painful experiences such as those going through a natural disaster or victims of traumatizing experiences [52,123,149,150]. They can also be organizations such as alumni organizations [60,104] and open source software platforms [81]. In social media, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are individuals who volunteer their time to create contents, contribute knowledge, and provide service. On the one hand, users who donate to content creators want to help the creators to achieve their mission, have a better life, or encourage them to provide high-quality contents or service. Such voluntary donation provides an incentive for content creators. On the other hand, users decide whether to donate to content creators after consuming the creators’ contents or service. It is similar to paying in exchange for such contents or service, which is different from the traditional donation behavior. Therefore, donation in social media is not only a charitable behavior but also a consumptive behavior.

## 2.1.2. Consumptive attribute of donation behavior in social media

The consumptive attribute of donation behavior implies that when users consume creators’ contents or service, they can pay any price they want. It is similar to the PWYW pricing model, which entails the consumer setting the price of a product or service [82]. Consumers can set any price equal to or above zero, and the seller may not refuse this price [76]. PWYW as a marketing strategy has been popular in real-world applications and increases customers purchase intention, especially for service and digital goods [75]. Academic researchers have explored customers’ motivation of paying. For example, Kim et al.’s [76] pioneering research on the factors affecting PWYW payments revealed that consumers’ personal characteristics such as fairness, altruism, satisfaction, loyalty, and income in<sup>fl</sup>uence the PWYW prices they pay. Building on Kim et al.’s [76] research, Kunter [82] found that avoiding the feeling of guilt and social norm are important factors that also drive PWYW payments. Furthermore, Marett et al. [93] distinguished economic drivers including price consciousness and prior usage of the app and social factors including fairness and loyalty while investigating online PWYW for electronic device applications. Because sellers using PWYW face the risk that customers may exploit their advantage and pay either nothing at all or a price below the costs, other researchers have explored the pricing mechanisms under PWYW. For example, Schons et al. [120] examined the potential dynamics in prices paid from individuals perspective using latent growth modeling. They found that repetition negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences customers’ price behavior.

In a PWYW process, the customers may offer what they consider to be fair prices for a product or service because the customers and sellers will tend to maintain a fair relationship to avoid the pressure of unfair transactions [64,76,93]. In social media, this can also be a reason that explains the motivation of the donation behavior. Furthermore, the key objective of PWYW is to maximize the seller’s pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and achieve competition advantage, where some sellers use it as a promotional tool and others use it as a sustainable pricing mechanism [76,120]. In other words, PWYW is a marketing strategy designed by the sellers. However, in social media, content creators do not provide knowledge or service for their <sup>fi</sup>nancial gains, and users generally consume such knowledge or service for free. Users donate to content creators to show their appreciation for the knowledge or service provided, encourage future content creation behaviors, and help content creators enjoy a better life. As a result, donation in social media is more than just PWYW.

Finally, donation behavior in social media describes the behavior where a user is willing to make a monetary or nonmonetary donation to a content creator, which involves both charitable and consumptive attributes. That is, the donor pays the content creator any price she wants depending on her charitable emotion, consumption of the knowledge or service, or both.

## 2.2. Attachment theory

Attachment theory is developed to explain interpersonal bonds that connect an individual with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c target [22]. Attachment to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c target such as a person or object affects one’s allocation of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral resources to the target [61]. In early attachment studies, the desire to make an emotional attachment to a particular target serves as a basic need [1], beginning from an infant or child’s attachment to her primary caretakers [21] and going into the adulthood with kinships, friendships, and romantic relationships [134].

Attachment leads to strong motivational and behavioral outcomes such as the desire to maintain physical proximity and the willingness to defend and invest cognitive and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources in the attachment target [9,43,143]. Thus, a person who is strongly attached to a particular target will be dedicated to the target and will strive to protect and preserve interactions with the object [42].

In the marketing literature, many researchers conceptualized the relationship between consumers and a brand as emotional brand attachment and investigated its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on consumers purchases [40,62,121,143], positive word-of-mouth [42,138], repurchase [91], brand loyalty [92,138,139], and forgiveness [42,50].

In the information systems (IS) literature, Choi [30] indicated that users’ attachment to IS products has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on their intention to get involved in online communities for the products. On the basis of Ren et al. [113], IS researchers identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two dimensions of attachment, namely identity-based attachment and bond-based attachment, and they regard them as the main factors that affect user participation, willingness to help, retention, and knowledge sharing behavior in virtual communities [32,113]. Choi [31] showed that attachment is a more in<sup>fl</sup>uential predictor of demanding contributory behaviors (e.g. community involvement) than loyalty and satisfaction.

In the fan–celebrity relationship context, Stever [125] revealed that once a fan develops an attachment to a celebrity, her proximity-seeking system will be activated as if she is in a relationship with a child or a romantic partner. As a result, fans can spend hours looking at the celebrity’s photographs and videos, collecting memorabilia, and becoming passionate consumers of the celebrity’s work. In particular, sports fans have been shown to endorse their favorite teams against all odds [12]. Although this behavior of supporting highly unlikely events seems irrational, it is reminiscent of a typical attachment.

In the donation literature, a number of university fund-raising and alumni donation studies have shown that attachment to the university can be a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant predictor of donation [38,132]. In addition, as a construct similar to attachment [40,55,132], commitment is also found to positively impact donation behavior. For example, Sargeant et al. [116] argued that commitment signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences donors’ nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t giving behavior. Krishnamurthy and Tripathi [81] found that commitment to open source software platform contributes to the decision to donate.

In summary, attachment is one of the factors that drive commitment, loyalty, and consumer’s helping behavior. When users are emotionally attached to the target object, they are likely to expend more of their own resources such as time, energy, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources to strengthen the relationship [9]. In the virtual context, because of the lack of social pressure and because freeriding is more prevalent, content creators have to ensure their knowledge and service appeal to prospective followers and donors. Therefore, we apply the attachment theory to explain why users donate to content creators in social media.

## 2.3. Socio-technical systems

We use the socio-technical systems perspective [135], a concept initially introduced to understand an organization from both social and technological perspectives, to investigate user motivation to donate to content creators in social media. In a socio-technical system, the social system focuses on the relationship among people and their attributes such as attitude, value, and skills, whereas the technical system emphasizes the processes, tasks, and technologies to produce the designated output [20]. These two sets of factors affect each other in producing goods and service and operating a business [107]. Socio-technical systems as a framework have been widely used in previous studies. For example, Kim et al. [74] argued that society, culture, and technology play core roles in building the information and communication technology ecosystem. Yu et al. [147] employed the socio-technical systems framework to study users’ Smart TV adoption. Some IS researchers argued that social media such as microblog are socio-technical information platforms [140], and users can obtain knowledge or achieve interpersonal outcomes through the platforms in addition to communicating with others [13,57,58]. Therefore, many studies have applied the socio-technical systems framework to study user participation and contribution behavior. For example, Chai and Kim [27] investigated users’ knowledge sharing on social networking sites from both social and technical aspects. Wang et al. [140] examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of technical and network effect factors on microblog users’ behavior through the lens of the sociotechnical systems framework.

In our study, users donated to content creators when and after they consume the contents or service in social media. The behavior of users during the process of content/service consumption and donation will provide insights into the nature of their interactions, and social media platforms will provide and guide users to accomplish such experiences and behaviors. Thus, this study investigates how social aspects motivate user donation behavior in social media along with the consideration of technical and environmental factors. In addition, the combination of the socio-technical systems framework and attachment theory allows us to examine the effects of both social and technical factors on attachment in explaining social media donation behavior.

## 3.1. Users’ emotional attachment

According to self-expansion theory [9,143], users’ emotional attachment has strong motivational and behavioral implications because an individual who is deeply attached to a person or object is more willing to invest in that target to sustain or strengthen the relationship with it. In the extant literature, many researchers have studied emotional brand attachment. For example, bonds and connections with a brand can be regarded as long-term outcomes of the brand relationship that strongly predicts the frequency of past purchases and the likelihood of future purchases [42,55,132]. If a user is attached to a product, person, or an object, she will be more likely to engage in demanding behaviors such as expending more resources (e.g., time, energy) to answer other users questions or providing suggestions and developing user-driven marketing campaign [30]. In consumer–celebrity relationship research, emotional attachment plays an important role in explaining consumer and fan behavior [12,47,125]. For example, Ilicic and Webster [63] revealed that consumers respond more strongly to endorsements by celebrities to whom they are emotionally attached than those to whom they are not emotionally attached. Fans are willing to spend hours to review each piece of information and purchase goods about the celebrities they like [125].

In the donation context, it has been shown that donors are willing to donate to organizations they trust and are committed to [89,98,116,117]. Similarly, Brady et al. [23] showed that people donate because they are attached to an organization or a cause. In addition, loyal customers pay higher prices than others in a PWYW setting because they want to avoid the potential embarrassment associated with paying a low price, and they have a desire to maintain and strengthen the relationship with the seller [76,93]. Therefore, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne emotional attachment to a content creator in this research as the emotion-laden bonds and connections with a content creator that re<sup>fl</sup>ects a user’s affective commitment and desire to maintain and strengthen the relationship with the content creator. Such emotional attachment can produce a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the user’s donation intention. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Emotional attachment to a content creator is positively associated with a user’s intention to donate to the content creator.

## 3.2. Functional dependence

Functional dependence as user attachment has been used to investigate the interdependence among group members in performing tasks. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects group structure, and the functional dependence level will affect an employee’s work style and attitude toward the organization and other members [97]. Interdependence theory suggests that such dependence is an essential part in determining how and what type of relationships will be developed [73]. It also in<sup>fl</sup>uences coordination and communication patterns, trust, and members’ commitment to organizations as high functional dependence makes group members to expect their roles to be important to the group [36,111,126]. Similar to functional dependence, functional attachment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as how well a setting and condition support goal achievement [66,133] and in<sup>fl</sup>uence a consumer’s emotional states and behavior [144]. The strength of an individual’s dependence on an object may be associated with the investment in the object and the willingness to make <sup>fi</sup>nancial sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces to obtain it or help it to achieve its goals such as paying a premium price or performing additional tasks [70,132]. Social media provide technological support for users so that the users can communicate, share, consume knowledge, and follow other users whom they like [14,29,48].

In this study, we used functional dependence on the social media platform as the technical factor for characterizing the extent of interdependence between a user and the social media platform for communicating, sharing, consuming knowledge, and following other users. Thus, users with different degrees of functional dependence on social media will differ in their attitudes and behaviors. For example, a high level of dependence in social media as perceived by a user may lead to customer loyalty and encourage behaviors such as performing additional tasks that are not required, committing extra time in social media, and intending to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the social media [70]. Therefore, users’ willingness to make <sup>fi</sup>nancial sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces and intention to donate to content creators on the social media that they depend on will be higher because this behavior is a way to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the social media. Moreover, users who rely heavily on social media would have more time and chance to share and consume knowledge and <sup>fi</sup>nd friends or other users they want to connect with because the platform provides necessary resources and capabilities that enable them to achieve their goals and build relationships with other users or content creators. Such functional dependence will lead to users’ emotional attachment to content creators and increase users donation intention. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2a. Functional dependence on social media is positively related to a user’s intention to donate to a content creator.

H2b. Functional dependence on social media is positively related to a user’s emotional attachment to a content creator.

## 3.3. Social system

As discussed earlier, the social system refers to the relationships among people and their attributes such as attitude, value, and skills [20]. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three constructs, namely identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interaction, and information value, as characteristics of the social system. In our context, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation refers to users’ identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with a content creator, which will result in their positive attitude toward the content creator and increase their willingness to build long-term relationships with her; interaction focuses on users’ communication with a content creator, which is regarded as a type of user–content creator relationship; information value refers to users’ perception of acquiring knowledge, experience, and service from the content creator. It is related to a content creator’s skill level and can increase users’ skill levels [107].

## 3.3.1. Identification

Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is a cognitive assessment of the degree of overlap between the self and a target organization, group, or another individual [24,102]. It has been studied as an important construct in the organizational behavior and marketing literature. The consequences of an individual’s identi<sup>fi</sup>cation include support for the social group, social attraction to members of the group, and willingness to build long-term relationships with companies, organizations, or other objects they identify with [11,17]. For instance, a high level of perceived overlap between self-image and organizational image will make one psychologically attached to and care about the organization, which motivates them to commit to the achievement of the goals, willingly exert more effort, and actively collaborate with organizational members [26,136,141]. In this research, we argue that if users perceive a high degree of overlap between self-personality and content creators’ personality, they will have a sense of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the content creators, which makes the users to like the content creators more and want

H3. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is positively related to a user’s emotional attachment to a content creator.

## 3.3.2. Interaction

According to the extant literature, interactive characteristics of social media and online communities include three major aspects: knowledge sharing, interactivity, and engagement [83]. Knowledge sharing refers to users’ sharing and the discussion of information and personal experiences [67,99]. Interactivity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which users can interact with each other on the online platform [67,99]. Engagement refers to users’ positive perceptions of their participation in activities hosted by the online platform [2]. In this research, we focus on interaction because it allows users to interact and communicate with content creators and other users in social media. Such interaction makes them feel more socially connected, increases the chance that others can weigh in, and puts more focus on content creators [16,33,41]. Thomson [131] and Lin and Chen [91] argued that interaction between consumers and brands helps create attachment to brands and predict consumers’ commitment and speci<sup>fi</sup>c purchases such as paying high prices and repurchase behavior. Similarly, interaction with other people drives the development of interpersonal attraction: when individuals’ interactions increase in frequency, the likelihood of liking others will also increase [45,113]. In online communities, social interaction gives users the chance for selfdisclosure and friendship building with others, which increases common bond attachment [46]. Therefore, the more a user interacts with a content creator and with other users who consume the same creator’s contents, the stronger an emotional bond will be established. Therefore,

H4. Interaction is positively related to a user’s emotional attachment to a content creator.

## 3.3.3. Information value

Perceived value refers to “a consumer’s assessment of the utility of a product based on perception of what is received and what is given” [151]. Perceived value has been applied to investigate business issues since the 1990s [49,151]. As a context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c perception, it can drive an individual’s attitude and behavior such as satisfaction [39], trust [112], relationship quality [10], loyalty [85], purchase intention [108], and word of mouth [96]. In addition, creating greater value for consumers through a better understanding of their needs and desires is at the heart of the relationship– marketing paradigm because perceived value directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer commitment [100]. Perceived value incorporates three theoretical types of value: functional value (acquire functional, utilitarian, and physical performance), emotional value (acquire feelings and affective states), and social value (enhancement of one’s self-concept) [128]. In this study, we focus on information value, which is a functional value regarding users’ perception of obtaining knowledge, experience, and service from content creators in social media. When users perceive a high information value of the knowledge or service provided by a content creator, they will follow the creator and build a long-term relationship to enhance their experience and further obtain more knowledge, innovative information, or interesting news. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H5. Information value is positively related to a user’s emotional attachment to a content creator.

## 3.4. Technical system

According to the pervious literature, the technical system consists of the tools, techniques, procedures, and devices used by users to accomplish the tasks in social media [107]. Therefore, we propose competitiveness, sociability, and personalization as characteristics of the technical system. They denote features of the functional tools and distinctive mechanisms provided by the social media platform to the users to accomplish the tasks and increase their engagement in social media.

## 3.4.1. Competitiveness

Competitive mechanics are an effective and important tool in game design [101]. Salen and Zimmerman [115] described that “the competitive striving toward a goal is fundamental in giving shape to the structure of a game and the way that the game creates meaning” ([115], p. 255). In particular, competitiveness in games can evoke several cognitive and affective effects including perceived importance of ability [3], involvement [137], satisfaction [68], and Internet addiction [18]. For example, Ozcelik et al. [106] indicated that the elements of play, competition, and challenge in computer and video games increase players’ motivation, engagement, and willingness to spend more time on knowledge tasks within the game. Thus, attracting players through comparison processes is a basic game design principle [71]. Social media platforms imitate game design to provide competitive features that arouse users affection and behavior. For example, YY platform ranks content creators by the number of virtual gifts they have received. Once a user donates a virtual gift to a performer, the donation information including the donor’s name and amount of donation will be broadcasted live to all subscribers. Twitch.tv ranks content creators by the amount of donations they have received and inserts the donation link on the performer’s home page. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne competitiveness in this study as the degree to which a user feels that social media involves competition [4]. According to social comparison theory, if users perceive competitiveness and detect a discrepancy in abilities or opinions in social media, they will spend more efforts to enhance their abilities, reduce the discrepancy, and protect their superiority [44]. In this process, users will become more attached to social media. Therefore, we have the following hypothesis:

H6. Competitiveness is positively associated with a user’s functional dependence on social media.

## 3.4.2. Sociability

Sociability refers to the characteristics of a social media system that support “a state of being sociable,” where users enjoy interacting with each other through the technology-enabled space [110]. The level of perceived sociability feature determines whether the technology-enabled environment allows users to interact with each other comfortably [109]. It gives users the opportunity to present and build their own virtual images and requires users to spend more time, effort, and money in such image-building activities in social media [7]. Therefore, such technology-enabled environment exhibiting a greater level of sociability may create stronger feelings of affection, trust, and belongingness [122], which in turn will reinforce the perception of attachment to social media [105]. In this study, sociability of social media facilitates users’ communication and interaction, and it brings convenience to users in completing their routine tasks such as building virtual images. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H7. Sociability is positively related to a user’s functional dependence on social media.

## 3.4.3. Personalization

Personalization implies that contents in social media will effectively articulate a user’s personal preferences and needs [80]. Platforms provide relevant and customized recommendations to users to match their idiosyncratic preferences and needs [80] and make a good impression on the users [152]. In social media, these recommended items are put under a distinct category with labels such as “personalized recommendations,” “unique offers for you,” and “the users you are following also like,” which encourage users exploration [129]. In addition, personalization also tailors unique services to users using information technology. For example, some social media platforms provide customized functions for users to alter their interfaces and interactions to match their preferences. In this case, users will spend time, effort, and money to actively customize including constructing their pro<sup>fi</sup>les and presenting their preferred identities. Users’ investment in the personalization process may lead them to develop a strong sense of connection with the platform [30,153]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H8. Personalization is positively related to a user’s functional dependence on social media.

## 4. Methodology

YY platform (YY.com), a live video streaming website, is one of the most successful social media websites and the <sup>fi</sup>rst platform to provide the donation function in China. In the present research, we tested our proposed research model on the basis of a questionnaire survey of YY platform users. We next discuss the details of our research methodology.

## 4.1. Questionnaire design

When developing the questionnaire, we adapted existing validated scales to suit the context of this study. All constructs were measured using multi-item perceptual scales and were measured on a seven-point Likert scale with the two anchors being “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree.” Items for donation intention were adapted from Merchant et al. [94] and Ye et al. [146]; items for emotional attachment were adapted from Ren et al. [113]; and items for functional dependence were adapted from Antioco et al. [8]. The measures for the antecedents of emotional attachment and functional dependence were adapted as follows: identi<sup>fi</sup>cation based on Brown et al. [24] and Tuškej et al. [136]; interaction from Kuo and Feng [83]; information value from Lee et al. [86]; competitiveness based on Anderson and Carnagey [4] and Nebel et al. [101]; sociability based on Animesh et al. [7]; and personalization based on Zhou et al. [153].

As the respondents were Chinese, we followed the back translation method to ensure that the English and Chinese questionnaires were similar. First, a researcher whose native language is Chinese translated these items into Chinese. Next, to prevent altering the semantic contents of the measurements during translation, different researchers translated the Chinese version back to English. Subsequently, these researchers compared the two English versions. The translation process did not induce any language bias into the survey design, and the degree of correspondence between the original and the translated English versions was very high. We then invited 25 participants to check for any ambiguity in wording and format and established the validity of the questionnaire. ## 4.2. Data collection

The data for this study were collected from users of YY platform. YY’s users donate to content creators (live broadcasters) by giving virtual gifts purchased on the platform. The content creators will get a portion of the value of the gifts they receive as their income. Donation is also a major source of revenue for YY. According to YY’s 2014 fourth quarter <sup>fi</sup>nancial report [148], its revenues from online music and entertainment donation increased by 100.9% to 676.2 million yuan (109.0 million US dollars) from a year ago. ## 5. Discussion and implications

The current study investigates the motivation behind users’ donation behavior in social media. Using a questionnaire survey of social media users of YY.com, we examined the complex relationships between emotional attachment, functional dependence, and donation intention through the lens of attachment theory and socio-technical systems framework. Our research has the following <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications.

## 5.1. Discussion of results

This study examines users’ donation behavior by integrating the socio-technical systems framework with attachment theory. The results depict a high degree of explained variances for all dependent variables. The empirical results also reveal interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, we found that it is useful to explain users’ donation behavior using both social and technological factors based on socio-technical systems. Second, drawing on attachment theory, we found that users develop emotional attachment to content creators and depend on social media platform functionally. These two types of attachment signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ donation intention. In addition, functional dependence also indirectly affects donation intention through emotional attachment. This indicates that social media as the platform provide an environment for users to present themselves and develop relationships, and they play a core role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing users’ affection and behavior. Third, among social factors, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interaction, and information value positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence emotional attachment. For example, social media with high interactivity can better meet users’ needs [83] and allow them to spend more time to discuss the topics they are interested in. During this process, users’ intention to maintain long-term relationships with other users will strengthen. Finally, regarding technological features, sociability and personalization positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence functional dependence. For example, according to the use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation theory, one medium is chosen over others because of its ability to better serve a user’s need [145], and such perceived personalization in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences users’ medium selection decision.

Although most of our hypotheses are supported, the relationship between competitiveness and function dependence is found to be nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. One plausible explanation is that users perception of competitiveness on YY platform can be either a positive or negative emotion because it is impossible to make each user win and rank higher. In other words, it will make some users feel discouraged when ranked low; consequently, they may not develop a positive attitude toward the social media platform. Therefore, competitiveness may not affect users’ dependence on social media.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

From the theoretical perspective, our study presents a few interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, we extend the research on donation behavior to the social media context by integrating charitable behavior and PWYW, a <sup>fi</sup>rst in academic research. In traditional donation, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are often people who suffer painful experiences. In this research, we study users’ donation to content creators in social media. In addition, we examined PWYW in social media, a setting where content creators’ behaviors are not primarily driven by <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives. As most studies on donation behavior focus on donor motivation and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organization performance [95,146] and research on PWYW examines the pricing mechanism and product sales, this study contributes to the donation and PWYW literature by understanding donation behavior in the consumption of “free” contents through the lens of the attachment theory.

Second, we <sup>fi</sup>ll a gap in the study of consumer behavior by exploring how the characteristics of user perception in<sup>fl</sup>uence donation in social media. On the basis of the attachment theory, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two types of attachments—emotional attachment and functional dependence—that in<sup>fl</sup>uence users’ donation intention in social media. Emotional attachment is an important factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences users’ donation behavior, and functional dependence has direct and indirect impacts on donation. These empirical results reveal that the theoretical perspectives in our study are effective in explaining and predicting future donation intention.

Third, our study extends the understanding of how user attachment in<sup>fl</sup>uences donation behavior from social and technical perspectives on the basis of socio-technical systems. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that both social and technological factors of the social media are important in promoting users’ donation intention. Although studies have shown the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of social factors on traditional donation behavior [78,89], our research revealed the importance of technological factors in social media. The present study found that three social factors (identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interaction, and information value) and two technical factors (sociability and personalization) are indirect predictors of donation intention in social media.

## 5.3. Practical implications

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study also offer a few interesting practical implications. To predict whether people are willing to donate, social media providers and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations should pay attention to both social and technical factors. For example, nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations can develop their platforms or draw support from social media, which can boost the organizations’ ability to strategically and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently engage a large audience [119] and build relationships through communication or other mechanisms [142]. Emotional attachment and functional dependence provide goals or guidelines for practitioners. Social media providers should take strategic actions to enhance users’ attachment to content creators or bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and their reliance on the social media platform to solve their routine problems and achieve their goals.

In addition, because technical factors are good indicators of the willingness to donate, social media providers and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations should manage the technological features and test the impacts of these features on users’ donation behavior. Practitioners can increase sociability by providing comfortable and convenient channels of communication for users and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries to build and strengthen relationships. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, practitioners should spend more efforts to increase personalization that signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhances users’ perceptions. For instance, they can provide different types of users with personalized settings and recommend other users or content creators based on users’ usage patterns and tasks.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings advise that social media providers and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations should increase the opportunities for users including prospective donors and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries to recognize each other, and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries or content creators should focus on building strong attachment relationships with users who may be donors. For example, bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries or content creators can offer valuable information to increase attention and foster positive affection from users. Moreover, bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries can increase interaction with users by discussing common interests and providing prompt responses.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

We acknowledge the limitations of this study. First, given the scope of the study, there are other constructs that were not included in the model. Future research can identify other important antecedents that may explain social media donation behavior from different perspectives. Second, we did not incorporate actual donation behaviors in our research model, which is one of the limitations of all cross-sectional studies. Future research can extend our research by considering actual donation behaviors or the amount of the donation. Third, our results may be speci<sup>fi</sup>c to China as social media donation gains traction. Future research may also extend our model to other geographical contexts or cultures. Finally, we did not examine moderating factors such as gender, income, and personality that may provide better insights into the understanding of donation behavior in different situations. These factors can be considered in future research.

## 6. Conclusion

This study investigates the roles of social factors (identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, interaction, and information value) and technological factors (sociability, competitiveness, and personalization) on users attachment consisting of emotional attachment and functional dependence and how such factors further in<sup>fl</sup>uence their donation intention in social media. Most of the hypotheses on the impacts of social and technical factors on attachment and donation behavior are supported by our empirical study. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that user attachment enhances donation intention. Social media providers and nonpro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations should focus on the different effects of the two types of attachment and pay attention to both social and technical factors to encourage user donation in social media.
