# Reflections and opinions on 25 years with the ISJ

David Avison\* & Guy Fitzgerald<sup>†</sup>

\*ESSEC Business School, Department of Information Systems and Decision Sciences, av Bernard Hirsch, Cergy-Pontoise 95021, France, email: avison@essec.fr, and <sup>†</sup>Brune University, Department of Information Systems and Computing, Kingston Lane, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK, email guy.fitzgerald@brunel.ac.uk

Abstract. Reflecting on 25 years as editors of the Information Systems Journal-(ISJ), we analyse some statistics on the ISJ over this period. These include the origins of papers published in terms of author gender, geographical region and academic department, the type of research in terms of positivist or interpretive (and empirical, critical and descriptive, if the latter) and qualitative, quantitative, mixed or descriptive, research method and research category. The paper also consists of personal reflections on the ISJ discussing issues such as motivation for journal editing, impact of research on practice (and vice versa) and loss of control as the journal develops. We also use the ISJ lens to reflect on the discipline of IS as a whole. We hope therefore that this paper might also form part of a collection on the history of the IS discipline.

Keywords: Information Systems Journal, journal editors, journal editing, informa tion systems discipline, information systems history

## INTRODUCTION

This issue marks the handover of our role as editors-in-chief of the Information Systems Journal (ISJ) to Robert Davison, Philip Powell and Eileen Trauth as from the next issue. Reflecting on our 25 years with the journal will help make sense of their plans for the next phase of the ISJ. Although the paper analyses some statistics on the ISJ over this period, the paper mainly consists of personal reflections on the ISJ and using the ISJ lens to reflect on the discipline of IS as a whole. We hope therefore that this paper might also form part of a collection on the history of the IS discipline.

Although the first issue of the ISJ was launched at the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) in December 1990 (officially volume 1, issue 1, 1991), this handover does indeed represent 25 years of our association with the ISJ, as we initially discussed the possibility of putting together a new journal as far back as 1987 with the publishers. We had just completed the proofs of the first edition of our book on IS development (Avison &

Fitzgerald, 1988) and agreed to edit a series of books on IS of which this book would be a part (published by Blackwell Scientific, Oxford), but we also approached Blackwell with a proposa for a new journal called the Journal of Information Systems, that later became the Information Systems Journal. The name change was to avoid confusion with the American Accounting Association publication of the same name and to avoid similar confusion we will hereafter refe only to the ISJ. In the next section, we discuss our motivations for setting up the journal.

## MOTIVATION

Our motivation was laid out in an initial paper (Avison et al., 2001). We argued that there were no high-quality research journals publishing the whole range of research that we regarded as within the scope of IS. We saw IS as wider than the purely technical aspects, which seemed to be the view of the existing journals of the time. Indeed, we saw the impact of information technology (IT) on people, organisations and society as not only appropriate but of critica importance. We defined IS as ‘the effective analysis, design, delivery and use of information and information technology in organizations and society’, and in our paper, we gave an overview of IS practice, education and research as we saw it in 1990. We identified one task of the ISJ to integrate the technological disciplines with management and other disciplines, e.g. psychology, philosophy and sociology were also specifically mentioned.

Rather than attempt to narrow the discipline to a few core issues (see this discussion in Benbasat & Zmud (2003) and King & Lyytinen (2006)), we sought to embrace its wide-ranging and interdisciplinary nature and make it a feature of the ISJ. A paper by Backhouse et al. (1991) in our first edition was our best ‘publicity’ for this. Indeed, they stress the importance of sociology and semiology to IS, a far cry from a more conventional view of the discipline at the time (and perhaps even now). The paper of Lyytinen et al. (1991) also in that first issue clearly indicated our social rather than technical ‘bias’.

Lyytinen et al. (1991) also suggested that the roots of IS lie in practice, as did Backhouse et al. (1991, p. 20): ‘the study of information systems has come about in the first instance to meet practical needs in commerce, industry and administration’. We also strongly believed in this and stated that we intended to include papers that both reflected and impacted on practice. As we will see in the next section, when we look at papers actually published in the ISJ over the years, we have been more successful at publishing the researchers’ views of practice (via case studies, for example) than we have at providing ‘a practitioner’s view on a subject of current concern’ (as we solicited in our original editorial).

However, we hope that our papers have impacted on practice to some extent, perhaps via a mediator reporting on our findings. Our students, for example, might learn from these articles and use the lessons learnt in their practice with others. But we should concede that we have had very few papers actually written by practitioners and those that are submitted tend to fall in the double-blind refereeing process that a top research journal demands, We also have had few practitioners (who were not at least part-time academics and/or researchers) subscribing to the journal. Thus, the impact on practice is indirect as practitioner papers do not seem to be compatible with a high-quality journal, and potential practitioner readers would seem to find the language of the researcher a barrier (or too much of an effort).

Another reason for starting an alternative research journal in IS concerned the related issue of research approaches used in IS. Typically, many papers that were published in top IS journals at the time tended to be based on the results of a statistical analysis of data obtained from a survey questionnaire. If done well and the contribution is of interest, then this research can be very valuable, but it is not the only valuable research approach in IS and we have particularly championed interpretive and qualitative research. This was a feature of research carried out in Europe, Australia and New Zealand, but rare in North America at the time. Indeed, it was difficult to get this research published in leading IS research journals. Much has changed in the intervening period, and we like to think that the ISJ has been a major influence in this change.

As individual delegates at the time attending research conferences, such as the ICIS, we saw this cultural difference between North American and European researchers and we Europeans were certainly in the minority! But this has changed also thanks to the majo influence of such organisations as the Association for Information Systems (AIS) and the International Federation for Information Processing (IFIP) Working Group 8.2.

The wide range of topics covered in the ISJ and our emphasis on practice meant embracing different research approaches. Case studies are probably the most well-used research approach that reflects practice and our first edition also heralded our intention to publish a wide range of research approaches, e.g. through the exemplar paper of Mansell (1991) on action research in IS development. At that time, we were brave (some might have suggested foolhardy) to give this predominance to action research, where it is clear that the researcher may not be ‘objective’ in the traditional sense. Thirteen years later, there was a special issue of Management Information Systems (MIS) Quarterly devoted to action research Baskerville & Myers (2004) showing that it had become almost mainstream in terms of acceptability, although it is still a comparatively rarely used research approach in IS.

As we have already suggested, the ISJ has been associated with interpretive research more generally, although we are not exclusive and the best positivist research is welcomed (as we will see in the following section analysing the published papers in the Journal).

This also suggests our own willingness to take calculated risks in our choice of papers. We did not take a risk on quality. All papers went through a rigorous refereeing process. But we did take risks in the papers that we published in terms of topic and research approach because not all were generally accepted within the community. We had to balance these risks against ou wish to further the Journal in terms of readership, impact factor and regard in the community.

We can not express this better than by quoting Ron Weber. In his book on the ontological foundation of IS, Weber (1997, p. xiii) writes that we as editors of the ISJ gave his papers ‘a chance’ and found that we were ‘risk-takers’. But the thorough reviewing process and the wish for us to place the ISJ as one of the top-ranked research journals, which has been successful judged by impact factor and AIS recognition, to give just two examples, has probably (and regrettably) reduced our effectiveness as risk takers over our period of the ISJ. In the long term, the ISJ has perhaps become itself part of the ‘establishment’.

This may also be partly due to our reliance on others. In short, we found ourselves losing control of the ISJ, at least to some extent. As the ISJ grew, we needed help to cope with the numbers of submissions. Sometimes, in writing the editorials, we were ‘uncomfortable’ with a few of the papers that we were publishing. None were of poor quality, our processes and the excellence of our associate editors and senior editors ensured that, but the Journal in becoming established had perhaps lost some ‘edge’. It is ironic that perhaps the discipline of IS, despite having such up-to-date and continually changing topics of interest, is ultimately quite conservative.

We can not be objective of course, and the ISJ might not have progressed in the way it has if we had continued to exert that control. But some might think that we have stayed on too long as editors and impeded its progress in some respects. Our reluctance to give up has been because of our genuine enjoyment of the role and the satisfaction we have had in steering the journal all this time. However, we are now convinced that it should be steered by others, especially as we have managed to attract three excellent editors to follow us. You may be correct to accuse us of hypocrisy, however, in that we and the publishers have suggested tha the contracts of those that follow us are time limited!

Although we were both based in the UK at the time of setting up the Journal, we saw it as very important that the journal had an international audience. Indeed, the ISJ has been identified by Galliers & Meadows (2003) as being one of the most international IS journals, significantly more so than other journals, such as MISQ and Information Systems Research. In retrospect, our decision to include leading academics from the whole international community on the Board of Editors was also crucial to our mission. Bob Galliers, Kenneth Kendall, Frank Land and Trevor Wood-Harper remain on our Board of Editors to this day from the original set and we are saddened to note that Heinz Klein and Enid Mumford are no longer with us. Both were major positive influences for the discipline and the ISJ in particular.

The Board were very helpful to us in providing advice on strategy, but as editors, we had the day-to-day tasks of dealing with the increasing load of submissions: keeping a record of these, deciding on referees, dealing with their feedback, deciding on what requirements were necessary, dealing with resubmissions, getting accepted papers typeset, putting each issue together and writing the editorials. As the work increased, we needed extra help and asked Richard Baskerville and Michael Myers to act in the role of associate editors and we also increased the size of our panel of referees. All did an outstanding job

Now we have 5 senior editors, 13 associate editors as well as a large panel of referees and a Board of 39 members. We are forever grateful to these excellent colleagues who have helped us shape the ISJ and make it one of the top research journals in IS. It is disappointing that such major contributions as being a board member, associate editor or referee of a journa receive so little recognition from Deans when they are such important roles in terms of furthering the discipline and the academic community as a whole. This lack of encouragemen of Deans may have been the cause of us being let down on a very few occasions by referees and even associate editors. On occasion, we have had to intervene when a paper has been delayed too long in the process. Thankfully, these occasions have been rare and the enthusiasm from our colleagues to support the discipline through this and other ways has always remained high

We have also had the consistent support of our publishers Blackwell Science and most recently Wiley, as a result of the recent merger, and over the last few years in the person of James Sullivan, who we would particularly like to thank. The publishers have always been available to advise and support in any way they could but they never interfered in or tried to change our decisions; indeed, they gave us almost total freedom. Rather like discussions in the open source community about commercialisation (Fitzgerald, 2006), there are many in the community who argue that academic journals should be run and owned by academics. The production and business sides of the ISJ, i.e. typesetting, printing, distribution (and more usually nowadays database update and downloads), dealing with libraries and consolidators, the financial issues and so on, has always been in the hands of the publishers. We will be eve grateful for that! The ISJ would have failed very early in its life if that had not been the case. Your editors are better academics than entrepreneurs!

Sometimes, the publishers have pushed for change against our conservatism! For example, they suggested moving from four to six issues annually long before we agreed to make that change (we were unduly concerned – as it turned out – about the possibility of lowering overall quality). Other practical concerns include the annual agreed page count and whether to include book reviews or other sections such as opinion pieces and so on. Again, we have perhaps been conservative and kept the journal to a simple and short editorial along with the papers themselves. The publishers have accepted our suggestions for the annual page count but again we have usually been conservative in our predictions and actual pages have usually been greater than predicted (thus increasing publisher costs). We have been, until recently, relatively lenient on word count as we felt that interpretive papers need space to evidence the interpretations made. However, word counts are sometimes unnecessarily high and perhaps insistence on a maximum might have proved a better policy.

The evidence does suggest that we have been successful in our aims of establishing an outstanding research journal that publishes the best of IS research defined broadly as described above and in particular provide an outlet for the best research where such openings were not previously available in the top IS research journals. Although impact factor has its limitations, it is one indicator of research excellence of a journal, and Table 1 shows the steady progress of our impact factor. The dip in 2009 was due to us changing from four to six issues per year but we recovered the following year and preliminary simulations indicate that this improvement will continue for 2011. Another crucial indicator has been the inclusion of the ISJ amongst the six (and eight) top research journals of the AIS and being recognised by them as ‘an official journal of the AIS’. We now turn to an analysis of the ISJ publications.

## ANALYSIS OF ISJ PUBLICATIONS

In this section, we develop the paper of Avison et al. (2008) by including and emphasising the four most recent volumes of the ISJ, taking us up to the end of 2011. We are grateful to our previous co-authors Philip Powell and Yogesh Dwivedi who produced the original statistics. This was not the first time we have done such reflecting, and in 2001, when the journal was 10 years old, we published a piece analysing those first 10 years Avison et al. (2001). In Table 2, we see that the volumes of the ISJ divide fairly equally into four groups, with the last group representing 24% of published papers. The first three groups were analysed in the previous paper and we discussed the trends that they illustrated. We will comment in particular on those trends which have changed in this fourth quartile.

We begin our analysis by looking at the origins of the papers submitted and published over the period, in relation to authors, their geographical regions, gender and departments. Of the papers published, 32.4% were single authored in groups 1–3 and 13.6% in group 4; 44.4% and 40.9%, respectively, were by two authors, 17.8% and 33%, respectively, by three authors and 5.1% and 13.5% by four authors or more. For reasons difficult to explain, the trend is clearly for more joint authorship of papers. It might seem surprising because many promotion and probation (tenure) committees still favour single-authored papers. On the other hand, the present authors also prefer joint authorships following the dictum ‘the whole is greater than the sum of the parts’, and it might show that academics in the field are becoming more collaborative. Being more personal, we find co-authorship more fun, less lonely, more interesting, more likely to produce better results and just part of our friendship of nearly 40 years (we first met as young students doing a Masters’ course on computing).

In terms of the number of contributors from different geographical regions, our original analysis separated the UK from the rest of Europe. Most (33.4%) were located in North America, closely followed by the UK, with 32.5%. The third largest category (19.7%) was formed by European (non-UK) contributors followed by the Asia-Pacific region with 13.3% contributors. The remaining 1.1% contributors were from outside these broad regions. As seen in Table 3, since that time, the number of articles from the UK has decreased even further so that authors from North America now exceed those from the whole of Europe. This suggests that the ISJ left its UK roots some time ago and has been truly international for some years.

Authors from the Asia-Pacific region have increased significantly, and this reflects a growing number of successful submissions from China and the immediate region (there was also a special issue devoted to IS issues in China edited by Robert Davison, Sia Siew Kien and Dong Xiao Ying – ISJ 18:4 July 2008). We are still concerned that we seem to be missing significant parts of the world including South America and Africa (we did, however, have our first papers from authors from the Lebanon and Ghana in this last quartile). We strongly encourage research papers from regions not well represented in this analysis of papers published. Such papers are likely to provide different and interesting perspectives. On the other hand, like practitioner papers, all will have to pass our stringent review system, which is based on the Western system for reviewing papers in the top academic journals.

In terms of the number of contributors from the academia or industry, the vast majority in our original analysis (92.9%) were from the academic world with only a small proportion from industry (7.1%). This has declined even further in these latest four volumes so that only 2.5% of published authors were from practice, and even then as joint authors with academics (there were no solely authored practitioner papers in the last period). We commented on this in our introductory section. We do not think this situation will change, but we encourage academics to co-write papers with practitioners as the different perspectives can enlighten both theory and practice and the ‘trickle-down’ effect onto our teaching is also important.

A gender imbalance also concerns us. The fact that the percentage of female authors has increased from 14.4% (volumes 1–6) to 19.8% (volumes 13–17) and now further to 28% are moves in the right direction but still indicates a structural problem; probably a reflection of a wider issue which is the gender imbalance in universities, including in our domain. However, we are hopeful that our special issue on ‘Women and IT’ that will be published later this year (edited by Liisa von Hellens, Julie Fisher and Eileen Trauth), will at least partly address this issue.

Special issues as a whole have been well cited in the ISJ in the past. The ability to find in one issue different perspectives of one important topic is compelling. On the other hand, nowadays, downloads are by far the most important source of a paper, so readers tend to retrieve paper-by-paper rather than by whole issue (even if the publishers continue to produce a paper version of the ISJ), so this might change. Nevertheless, we look forward to proposals for some interesting special issues in the future. We already have in the pipeline special issues on ‘Theorising development and technological change’ (edited by Niall Hayes, Gianluca Miscione, Leiser Silva and Chris Westrup), ‘Interpreting digital enabled social networks’ (edited by Eoin Whelan, Brian Butler, Robin Teigland and Emmanuelle Vaast) and ‘New trends in IS development’ (edited by Karlheinz Kautz, Linda Dawson, Peter Axel Nielsen and Nancy Russo) as well as that on ‘Women and IT’ mentioned above. We thank our special issues edito Jan Ondrus.

Analysing the information provided by authors as to the departments in which they are located (Table 4), we found that in our original analysis, the largest number of contributors were located in departments of IS (or similar title; 43.4%), followed by business/management departments (34.9%) and then by computer science departments (11.1%). In the later analysis of the last quartile only, the respective statistics are 45.8%, 43.3% and 2.0%.

The decline in contributions from researchers in computer science departments might reflect both the increasing trends of IS academics moving away from the technical to the more management spheres and the greater submissions from North America where IS researchers tend to be in independent IS departments or management schools rather than in computing departments (which had been the tradition in the UK, for example). It seems that the ISJ is becoming more IS with a management focus than IS with a computer science one, and maybe this reflects also the way that the subject area as a whole has evolved with a move away from the technical issues towards the application and effectiveness issues within organisations and society (at least from the evidence of the ISJ).

The percentage of papers written by academics from other departments almost doubled even if from a low base and included members of research laboratories in the energy and aerospace sectors. They also included researchers from departments of oceanography, communications, psychology and education. As we commented earlier, authors in the practitioner community actually decreased from a low base but included a chief information officer (CIO), project manager, management consultant and practitioners in finance. These practitioners were frequently doing PhDs with IS academics or whose organisations were the subject of case study or action research, so a jointly authored paper would be appropriate.

The discussion above does enable us to give readers a warning signal regarding our statistics. For example, if an author works in an IS department within a management school, we have allocated the author to the IS department. So in general, we would argue that the trends that we are highlighting do reflect the real situation, but the placement of every author and therefore the exact percentages are certainly open to question.

Having looked at some demographic information concerning authors, we now turn to look at the papers themselves. We begin by examining the general research paradigm used in the research reflected in the published papers. Initially, we categorise the papers into two basic groups: positivist and interpretive. As we argued in our earlier paper (Avison et al., 2008): ‘In undertaking the analysis we had to be somewhat interpretive ourselves, as authors do not always specifically categorize their work in this way’ (again, a warning signal with regard to our statistics!). In making such decisions, we followed the Chen & Hirschheim (2004) criteria fo categorising positivist articles as such if they had indications of hypotheses, propositions, model formation, quantifiable measures of variables and the inferences drawn from samples to populations (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991), and demonstrated the belief that ‘objective’ data could be collected to predict the relationship amongst factors and to test hypotheses or theories (Walsham, 1995b).

The criteria for determining interpretive articles were threefold. First and foremost, articles should not involve any positivist indicators as described above, i.e. no deterministic perspec tives imposed by the researchers. Second, participants’ perspectives are taken as the primary sources of understanding and investigating the phenomena. Third, the phenomena are exam ined with respect to cultural or contextual circumstances (Walsham, 1995a).

As can be seen in Table 5, the ISJ has published predominantly interpretive research. This is no surprise as one of our original objectives was to provide a natural home for interpretive research papers. We felt that such research was not well catered for in terms of outlets at that time. However, within the groups, there is a small indication of an increase in the number of positivist papers over the initial period, originally 25:75 approximately, then nearer 30:70 and now 38:62. It was never the intention to publish interpretive papers only as we believe in a pluralism of research methods and paradigms as being appropriate for the relatively young subject area. Over the years, since the creation of the journal, there have been a number of debates, and indeed proposals, that the ISJ become a purely interpretive journal. This has been rejected because of our pluralist views, and the ISJ continues to welcome the best of positivist research (indeed all research in IS).

Given the predominance of papers in the interpretive classification, in Table 6 we look at these in more detail and further refine the analysis. Our first subcategory of interpretive includes papers that are essentially empirical and comprised 42% of the interpretive total in ou original analysis, and this has increased to 61.8% in the fourth quartile. As editors, we have encouraged authors to provide evidence to illustrate ideas and concepts. Referees and associate editors have been similarly demanding. Thus, this category has increased relative to the descriptive/conceptual/theoretical category without empirical material. It has proved difficult to write a convincing account of IS phenomena without some empirica evidence.

The subcategory of critical research, which adopts an evaluative dimension (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991), has also increased largely thanks to an excellent double special issue of the ISJ edited by Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic, Heinz Klein and Carole Brooke (ISJ 18:2 March 2008 and 19:2 March 2009). The critical researcher attempts to critically evaluate and transform the social reality under investigation not merely just to predict or explain the status quo, and in our view, this proves to be an exciting dimension for IS research.

Descriptive/conceptual/theoretical papers are those which are essentially the non-empirica papers, composed of either descriptive (or argumentative) approaches, conceptual or theoretical deliberations. As a discipline becomes more mature, one might expect theory-testing to outweigh theory-building efforts. Therefore, a comparison between empirical and nonempirical studies might serve as a meaningful indication of the progress made in this context.

The next step of the analysis relates to the research method adopted in the papers. The analysis began by asking whether the research was quantitative or qualitative. Again, we utilise the definitions of Chen & Hirschheim (2004) here and classify papers as quantitative if they use numerical analysis to illustrate the relationship amongst factors in the phenomenon studied, and we define it as qualitative if the emphasis is on the description and understanding of the situation behind the factors. It is possible that a paper could use both quantitative and qualitative methods, maybe in different stages of the research, in which case we categorise them as mixed. Some papers are non-empirical and somewhat diverse and we categorise these as descriptive/theoretical/meta analysis. These non-empirical studies typically involve describing or arguing for theories and frameworks.

Table 7 shows the outcome of this analysis, and we see that around 40% were classified as descriptive/theoretical/meta analysis in the first three groups of ISJ volumes, but there was a sharp decrease to 9% in the fourth quartile. This would seem to reflect the situation discussed above where referees are requiring evidence and/or the maturing of the discipline. So 36% of papers published were qualitative (but 49.4% in the last quartile) and there is a small increase in the mixed category (but still only around 10%) with the rest quantitative. Thus, quantitative papers have also increased as a proportion. This is quite a significant increase, from around 18.5% to 31.5%. Nevertheless, there are clear parallels between this analysis and the positivist/interpretive discussion above and that these figures are not dissimilar to the quantitative/qualitative percentages, which were also surprising to the present authors.

Nevertheless, our view is that the most important aspect is that the research method chosen must be appropriate for the research question (which must be interesting and significant to readers) and also employed correctly. Whatever the research approach used, over this time the evidence of research quality has become more apparent through papers having more focus, thought, rigor and explanation of the adopted research method.

We now turn to research designs evident in the papers. We have partly adopted the classifications of Orlikowski & Baroudi (1991) and partly Galliers (1991). These are survey, case study, laboratory experiment, field experiment, action research, ethnography, grounded theory and ‘others’. We defined each of these in our previous paper (Avison et al., 2008). As seen in Table 8 (adapted from Palvia & Pinjani, 2007), we classify the papers into a total of 15 categories based on their research method.

There has been a steady increase throughout the four periods in papers that use question naires and surveys and statistically analyse the data collected as the research approach. This is a particularly interesting finding to us as one of the motivations for us setting up the ISJ was the wish to find a home for papers that did not use this research method (although we never, of course, asserted or implied that this research method was inappropriate to IS research). We think that this change in its influence on the ISJ is not inappropriate. It shows that the ISJ is perceived as a general IS research journal willing to publish the best IS research whatever the method. But it might also show that the greater recognition, impact factor, longevity and so on has in fact made the ISJ more conventional. This is not an argument with which we are particularly comfortable, but it might yet be valid. We discussed earlier in this paper the loss of some of our personal control that has reduced risk taking and been an additional factor in this process.

Another ‘health’ warning should be given on these statistics in relation to the increase in the interviewing category from around 5% to over 12%. Papers are included in this research category if the authors emphasised interviewing and quoted from the interviews in their papers, but some of these papers could also have been categorised as case studies, action research or ethnography instead. Interviewing also include focus groups, which are essentially group interviews.

The original list is worth reassessing, nevertheless, because it shows the range of interests of IS academics and also might show some ‘hot topics’ that are now somewhat cooler.

## FUTURE

Having discussed our motivation for the ISJ and looked at what it publishes, in particular reflecting on changes in trends over the last four volumes compared with the previous 17, we now turn to its future. One of the reasons for the success of the ISJ has been our personal friendship and cooperative spirit. We are handing over the reins to three outstanding academ ics, all three having held major positions with the ISJ for some time. Philip Powell has been managing editor and senior editor for several years and was associate editor before that; Robert Davison has been senior editor for some years and also editor of a special issue; and Eileen Trauth also edited a special issue and has been a referee over many years. Their outstanding academic credentials are well known, but they were chosen also to reflect the international nature of the ISJ.

The next issue of the ISJ will be their responsibility. They will outline their plans in their first editorial. Based on the above analysis, however, we issue the following challenges and questions to them:

1 Can the ISJ attract more papers from regions not well represented in its past issues, including most of South America and Africa that are eventually accepted in the ISJ?

2 Can the gender balance of ISJ articles be more representational of the population as a whole?

3 Should the ISJ publish only interpretive research or continue to be generalist (with a particular bias for interpretive research)?

4 Should the ISJ be more risk taking? If so, how can this happen whilst keeping its high standards?

5 Should it have one major goal (e.g. a higher impact factor) to the detriment of other measures?

6 Should it publish more papers, more issues, larger papers and more special issues?

7 Should it have different sections apart from research articles, e.g. opinion pieces, review articles and so on?

8 Should the ISJ remain a combined print and electronic journal or might it move to electronic only?

9 How can it further improve its impact in the IS academic community and attract the best submissions?

10 Can it, and should it, become more relevant to the practitioner community as we originally envisaged?

Every good wish to Eileen, Philip and Robert!
