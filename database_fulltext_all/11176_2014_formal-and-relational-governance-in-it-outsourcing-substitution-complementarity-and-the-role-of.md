# Formal and relational governance in IT outsourcing: substitution, complementarity and the role of the psychological contract Eleni Lioliou,\* Angelika Zimmermann,\* Leslie Willcocks<sup>†</sup> & Lan Gao

\*School of Business and Economics, University of Loughborough, Loughborough, UK, email: e.lioliou@lboro.ac.uk, and <sup>†</sup>Information Systems and Innovation Group, London School of Economics, London, UK

Abstract. This study aims to contribute to the literature on IToutsourcing governance by advancing our understanding of mechanisms of substitution and complementarity between formal and relational governance. Our study illustrates certain conditions under which substitution and complementarity can occur and depicts a two-way causal relationship between them. Our examination further provides a more indepth assessment of relational governance by using the concept of the psychological contract. Our results demonstrate that the psychologically binding nature of the psy chological contract makes it a particularly forceful substitute to formal governance while its associations with other relationship aspects make it a forceful complement.

Keywords: IToutsourcing, outsourcing governance, contract, relational governance, psychological contract, case study

## INTRODUCTION

IToutsourcing has emerged as an important tool for enabling organizations around the world to enhance their competitiveness by gaining access to the distinctive expertise and technologica competencies of external suppliers. Its growth during the past 15 years has been massive, and the trends are further increasing. The global IT outsourcing market reached \$288bn in 2013, and Garther predicts a 5.4 compound annual growth rate in the global outsourcing marke through 2017 (IT Outsourcing News, 2013).

Stories of successes and failures have made the headlines of public media (e.g. Mourdoukoutas, 2012), and an extensive academic literature has discussed best practices, success factors and hidden costs of outsourcing (e.g. Dibbern et al., 2008). As part of these debates, recent statistics from Equaterra (2010) and Mercer (2010) have connected poor outsourcing outcomes to ineffective governance of the IT outsourcing ventures. Furthermore, a recent survey from the International Association of Outsourcing Professionals showed that IT outsourcing governance and control is the top challenge that IT outsourcing professionals are facing today (IAOP, 2008). In addition to commercial research, a number of academic studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed outsourcing governance as a key mechanism for achieving the purpose of outsourcing arrangements (e.g. Kern & Willcocks, 2000; Lacity et al., 2010).

Researchers now agree that successful IT outsourcing partnerships rely not only on formal governance through contracts but also on relational governance through interpersonal relationships between the outsourcing partners. Furthermore, an increasing stream of research has acknowledged that these two forms of governance are interrelated and can both substitute and complement each other (Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Mellewigt et al., 2007; Goo et al., 2009; Gopal & Koka, 2012). Researchers have also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several conditions under which either substitution o complementarity effects occur. Most recently, Huber et al. (2013) argued that substitution and complementarity effects are themselves interrelated. Research on this topic is howeve inconclusive, owing to two main reasons.

Firstly, research on the mechanisms by which substitution and complementarity occur, and links between the two, is still sparse, and the quantitative nature of most prior studies does not allow for in-depth explanations of substitution and complementarity effects. While there is solid statistical evidence for these effects. there is little qualitative research on how and under what conditions formal contracts and aspects of relational governance substitute each other or work as complementary mechanisms.

Secondly, relational governance has been examined in a fragmented manner, which makes it hard to compare <sup>fi</sup>ndings and draw conclusions from prior research. Relational governance involves a whole range of relationship aspects, including interpersonal trust, commitment, informal controls, communication and mutual dependence (e.g. Mellewigt et al., 2007; Vlaar et al., 2007; Goo et al., 2009; Gopal & Koka, 2012). Prior research does not examine these elements jointly and does there fore not provide an integrated view of relational governance and its links to formal governance.

We aim to <sup>fi</sup>ll these research gaps by presenting a study that examines the interrelations between formal and relational governance in an in-depth, qualitative manner. Firstly, we will demonstrate certain conditions under which substitution and complementarity can occur, and we will argue for a twoway causal relationship between substitution and complementarity. Secondly, to broaden our view on relational governance, we examine a whole range of relationship aspects. To go into yet more depth in the examination of relational governance, we additionally use the concept of the ‘psychological contract’. We will argue that the psychological contract is a mutual agreement on obligations of both outsourcing partners that is psychologically binding and can therefore act as a particularly forceful substitute to the formal contract. We will then illustrate how the associations of the psycho logical contract to other relationship aspects make it a forceful complement to the formal contract.

In the following, we will elaborate on this argument by reviewing prior insights into the interrela tions between formal and relational governance and by introducing the concept of the psychologica contract. This will be followed by our methodology section and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We will then highlight the main contributions to research and practice, limitations and directions for future research.

## FORMAL AND RELATIONAL GOVERNANCE IN OUTSOURCING ARRANGEMENTS

Outsourcing governance has been regarded as a key mechanism stirring the outsourcing arrangement towards the achievements of its objectives. Previous research distinguished between the formal and relational aspects of outsourcing governance. The formal aspect of governance refers to the development of a good contract and other formal instruments of governance, such as service level agreements (SLAs), key performance indicators (KPIs) and other formal control mechanisms. Formal governance aims to coordinate activities betweer partners and prevent opportunistic behaviour through the creation of a mutually agreed upon and legally binding set of acceptable behaviours (Vlaar et al., 2007; Hoetker & Mellewigt, 2009). Relational governance, on the other hand, refers to the softer, more ‘human’ elements of the outsourcing relationship, which provide a form of ‘social control’ and serve to coordinate activities and mitigate risks of opportunistic behaviour. Table 1 provides an overview of several formal and relational governance elements that have been examined in previous studies. From this table, it also becomes clear that prior research does not present a coherent view of relational governance elements, but different studies examine relational governance in terms of different aspects of the outsourcing relationship. Perhaps most frequently, relational governance has been operationalized in terms of trust (e.g. Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Carson et al., 2006; Mellewigt et al., 2007; Gulati & Nickerson, 2008; Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009; Goo et al., 2009; Huber et al., 2013). Some researchers have also investigated relational governance in terms of informal o social control mechanisms (Das & Teng, 2001; Kirsch, 2004; Tiwana, 2010; Huber et al., 2013). A large range of other relational elements have been investigated less frequently, for example commitment (Mohr & Speckman, 1994), communication (Mohr & Speckman, 1994; Kern & Willcocks, 2000; Poppo & Zenger, 2002) and mutual dependence (Goo et al., 2009). ## Interrelations between formal and relational governance

More recently, there has been an effort towards developing a more thorough understanding of the interrelationship between the formal and relational elements of outsourcing governance (Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009; Goo et al., 2009; Srivastava & Thompson, 2012). In this stream of research, a major focus has been on the ‘substitution’ vs. ‘complementary’ nature of the relationship between contractual and relational governance (Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Vlaar et al., 2007; Gulati & Nickerson, 2008). The substitution stream of research views formal and relational governance as functional equivalents, and because both formal and relational governance are doing the same thing, one can replace the other (Malhotra & Mirnighan, 2002; Huber et al., 2013). Furthermore, the workings of formal governance can dampen the levels of relational governance. For instance, the existence of rigid contractual processes can be perceived as a signal of distrust. Conversely, the workings of relational governance can dampen the functioning of formal governance (Huber et al., 2013). Organizations may for instance downplay and bypass formal governance processes because they are overdepending on their prior good relationship.

The complementarity stream of research, on the other hand, argues that formal and relational governance have unique strengths and that one compensates for the weaknesses of the other (Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Carson et al., 2006; Huber et al., 2013). ## Conditions of substitution and complementarity

Several researchers have gone a step further and suggested that the substitution and comple mentarity between formal and relational governance depend on specific conditions (Table 1 last two columns). With regard to substitution effects, Vlaar et al. (2007) observe that forma governance substitutes relational governance if a certain degree of distrust exists between the outsourcing partners. The authors suggest that starting conditions of trust and distrust can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of formal coordination and control (high or low) and how managers interpret the behaviour of their partners (positive or negative). In a similar vein, Gulati & Nickerson (2008) claim that high levels of pre-existing interorganizational trust increase the probability that partners choose a less formal mode of governance.

The substitution between formal and relational governance depends on further conditions, including levels of interorganizational performance, type of outsourced assets and the nature of the desired outcomes. Thus, low levels of interorganizational performance will lead to a perceived need for an increase in formal coordination and control (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003; Vlaar et al., 2007). Moreover, in the presence of knowledge-based assets, <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to substitute formal by relational governance, while in the presence of property-based assets, <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to substitute relational by formal governance (Hoetker & Mellewigt, 2009). In addition, as argued by Miranda & Kavan (2005), the choice of governance is affected by the nature of desired outcomes, which in turn depends on whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm pursues a strategy of value creation or value capture. Value creation is inherently more uncertain, and outcomes are frequently unknown. In this case, the psychological contract (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘beliefs regarding terms and conditions of a reciprocal exchange agreement between that person and another party’) takes over after signing the formal contract, and relational governance becomes important at this stage, involving trust, shared identity, beliefs, expectations and understanding. By contrast, a strategy that aims to capture value is less uncertain, and outcomes and processes are therefore easier to outline. After signing the formal contract, a psychological contract takes over, but formal associations and processes regulate the outsourcing arrangement in this scenario. In other words, relational governance substitutes formal governance in the case of uncertain outcomes, while formal governance takes over in the case of certain outcomes. Uncertainty as a substitution condition is explored further by Carson et al. (2006), who distinguish between ‘ambiguous’ situations where contractual governance is more effective than relational governance and situations of ‘volatility’ where relational governance is more effective.

Certain substitution conditions are also tied to different phases in the development of the outsourcing arrangement. Kirsch (2004) found that in earlier stages of an outsourcing venture, rela tional governance tends to substitute formal governance, because of the <sup>fl</sup>uidity in goals, expecta tions and requirements. During the so-called development stage, however, there is a better understanding of tasks, processes and objectives, and both parties are therefore better able to estab lish formal rules and procedures. Formal governance then tends to substitute relational governance.

While the larger stream of research focused on substitution conditions, there are also a few stud ies on the conditions of complementarity between formal and relational governance. Goo et al. (2009) demonstrate that the content of formal governance practices (as a condition) can reinforce the development of effective relational governance. Conversely, the quality of relational gover nance (as a condition) can set the basis for the creation of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient formal controls (Vlaar et al., 2007; Chua et al., 2012). Another condition of complementarity between formal and relational governance resides in previous interactions between the client and the vendor. Frequent interactions and close relationships between the two can lead to the generation of more complete con tracts (Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009). Complementation between formal and relational governances is further supported by a lack of clear goals and requirements (Kirsch, 2004; Huber et al., 2013).

In sum, prior research has distinguished between several conditions of substitution between formal and relational governance and a smaller number of conditions of their complementarity. In our study, we are continuing this line of investigation, by identifying the conditions under which formal and relational governance substituted or complemented each other within the participating company cases.

The majority of the studies on conditions of substitution and complementarity have taken a dichotomous position and assume that formal and relational governance work ‘either’ as substitutes ‘or’ as complements. This ‘either/or’ dichotomy of complementarity and substitution between formal and relational governance has recently been questioned, as outlined in the following section.

## Interrelations between substitution and complementarity

While theory has framed complementarity and substitution as incompatible with one another, empirical research found support that they may co-exist in reality (Tiwana, 2010; Mellewig et al., 2007; Huber et al., 2013). In this vein of research, it has been illustrated that some informal control mechanisms can strengthen the effectiveness of certain formal control mechanisms and therefore produce complementary effects, but at the same time, they can weaken the effec tiveness of others and therefore produce substitution effects (Tiwana, 2010; Huber et al., 2013).

In their effort to provide a greater consensus with previous research results, Huber et al. (2013) conducted an in-depth investigation of formal and relational governance mechanisms and their ability to replace, dampen, compensate and enable one another. The authors argue that complementarity and substitution between formal and relational governance mechanisms can be causally connected, as they found that complementarity can be a necessary precondi tion for substitution. In our study, we are going to extend this argument and demonstrate that there is in fact a two-way causal relationship between the complementarity and substitution of formal and relational governance.

## THE PSYCHOLOGICAL CONTRACT

In order to take a more in-depth perspective on the interrelations between formal and relational governance, we will take a broader view of relational governance and apply the concept of the psychological contract. A psychological contract is related to the aspects of relational governance that were included in prior studies, such as trust and commitment, but is also differen from them. The distinguishing characteristic of the psychological contract lies in its contractual nature, which makes it psychologically binding and implies mutuality between the exchange partners. In the following paragraphs, we will illuminate these characteristics in comparison with the other elements of relational governance.

Argyris (1960) was the <sup>fi</sup>rst to introduce the term ‘psychological contract’, describing it as an agreement in which both parties have to have a shared understanding of what they are expected to contribute to each other. Levinson et al. (1962) developed these ideas further and emphasized expectations as a fundamental element of the psychological contract, de<sup>fi</sup>ning it as ‘a series of mutual expectations of which the parties to the relationship may not themselves even be dimly aware but which nonetheless govern their relationship’. Schein (1978) underlined that these expectations cover a whole set of obligations, rights and privileges. Robinson & Rousseau (1994), in turn, strongly emphasize the subjective nature of the psychologica contract, by de<sup>fi</sup>ning it as beliefs or perceptions of reciprocal and promised obligations (p. 246).

Research in the human resources literature has generated various categorizations of psychological contracts. Rousseau (1995) introduced one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential categorizations in terms of ‘transactional’ vs. ‘relational’ types of contracts. This categorization was based on the dimensions of time frame and tangibility. Transactional contracts refer to short-term, well de<sup>fi</sup>ned, performance-related arrangements where there is usually limited commitment, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, involvement and investment. Relational contracts, on the other hand, refer to longer-term, more ambiguous arrangements where there is high emotional involvement and investment, dynamic working conditions and subjectively understood terms. As we will show later, it is this relational type of psychological contract that takes part in relational governance

Mutuality plays an important role in the psychological contract. Firstly, the psychologica contract is based on perceived promises of reciprocal exchange and therefore refers to obligations of each partner rather than only one (Rousseau, 1989). Secondly, each partner in the agreement believes that the agreement is mutual, ‘that is, a common understanding exists that binds the parties involved to a particular course of action’ (Rousseau, 2001: 512). Thirdly, the psychologica contract only ‘actually’ exists and is not just subjective if both partners hold the same expectations regarding their obligations to each other (Rousseau, 2001: 534).

Psychological contracts also create a strong sense of accountability and are therefore psychologically binding. While formal contracts can be imposed on individuals, the psychological contract refers, per de<sup>fi</sup>nition, to individuals’ own beliefs about their mutual obligations. For this reason, individuals tend to identify with and hold themselves accountable against these perceived obligations. The psychological contract is therefore a ‘self-regulating mechanism’ that can motivate partners to perform to the conditions of their agreement (Rousseau, 2001: 528).

Following previous conceptualizations, we therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ne the psychological contract as a perceived mutual agreement between two parties on the obligations of both sides, which creates a strong sense of accountability and is therefore psychologically binding. The partners who hold a psychological contract will believe that they share the same perceptions and expectations of their mutual obligations, even if their actual perceptions and expectations differ.

Other elements of relational governance, such as trust and commitment, can be one sided and/or less psychologically binding. To make the distinction between the psychological contract and other relationship aspects more clear, we have to look at them in greater detail. Trust is perhaps the most frequently examined aspect in relational governance. Following Mayer et al. (1995: 712), trust in an inter<sup>fi</sup>rm partnership can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the willingness of each partner <sup>fi</sup>rm to be vulnerable to the actions of the other party, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party. Interestingly, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition by itself implies that trust can substitute monitoring and control mechanisms. Furthermore, we can distinguish between trust in the partner’s ability, benevolence and integrity. Ability-based trust refers to ‘the trustees’ skills, competencies, and characteristics that enable them to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their tasks’ (Mayer et al., 1995: 716). Benevolencebased trust depends on the extent to which a trustee is believed to want to do good to the trustor, aside from an egocentric pro<sup>fi</sup>t motive (Mayer et al., 1995: 718), and integrity-based trust relies on the perception that ‘the trustee adheres to a set of principles that the trustor <sup>fi</sup>nds acceptable’ (Mayer et al., 1995: 719). In our case, these principles concern primarily an adherence to formal contractual obligations. As highlighted by Schoorman et al. (2007), trust (based on ability, benevolence or integrity) is not necessarily reciprocal but can be unidirectional.

Commitment, in turn, can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the willingness of partners to exert effort on behalf of the relationship (Mohr & Speckman, 1994: 137). In some partnerships, this may be the case for one partner, but not the other. Commitment need therefore not be mutual. Communication, including communication quality and the extent of information sharing, is particularly important in relational governance (Mohr & Speckman. 1994: Kern & Willcocks. 2000). Effective communication does of course involve both partners, because the sender and the receiver of a message have to achieve a consistency between the intended message and the received message. Communication is therefore only effective if it is reciprocal. However, effective communication does not necessarily create high levels of psychological accountability. Mutual dependence, on the other hand, is mutual by de<sup>fi</sup>nition. It has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the recognition by both partners in an exchange relationship that the relationship provides bene<sup>fi</sup>ts greater than either partner could attain alone or with some other partner’ (Goo et al., 2009: 125). Although mutual dependence is likely to create a strong sense of commitment (Goo et al., 2009), it is not necessarily psychologically binding.

It is important to note that the psychological contract is related to other aspects of the relationship between outsourcing partners. Firstly, a psychological contract relies on a degree of trust in the other party’s integrity; in other words, a belief that the partners will commit to their promises and obligations (Rousseau, 1995; Atkinson, 2007). Moreover, the psychological contract affects other relationship aspects. Empirical studies on the effects of psychological contract breach on various employer–employee relations are conclusive with regard to its negative impact. In particular, breach perceptions were found to have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on employees’ trust in their employer, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as beliefs regarding the employer’s integrity, motives and intentions, behav joural consistency, openness and discreteness (Robinson & Rousseau. 1994). Breach perceptions have also been shown to negatively affect employees’ organizational commitment (Coyle-Shapiro & Kessler, 2000), which typically comprises identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, involvement with and loyalty towards the organization (Buchanan, 1974).

In view of this prior research, it becomes clear that a relational (as opposed to transactional) psychological contract is part of relational governance. If two collaborating parties hold a psychological contract, it means that they believe they have a mutual agreement, based on a shared understanding of what they are expected to contribute to each other. This perceived agreement creates strong psychological accountability, entailing high levels of self-control by the contract partners. The psychological contract does therefore not control partners’ behaviour formally, like a contract. However, because of its mutual and psychologically binding nature, the psychological contract functions in a similar way to formal governance, namely in the manner of a contract. These characteristics distinguish the psychological contract from the other elements of relational governance, which can be not mutual and/or not binding. We will later demonstrate that it is the mutuality and psychologically binding nature of the psychological contract that make it a particularly forceful complement and substitute to formal governance.

Only a few researchers have applied the notion of the psychological contract to the context of Information Systems (IS) outsourcing. Koh et al. (2004) focus on de<sup>fi</sup>ning the obligations within a psychological contract in an IS outsourcing relationship. Using a grounded theory approach, they examine the psychological contract in terms of perceived mutual obligations by vendors and suppliers, and the relationship between ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of obligations and IS outsourcing success

In the same line of research, Lane & Agerfalk (2007) describe the set of obligations that make up a psychological contract between parties in a global software collaboration.

A few authors have also examined the role of the psychological contract within either formal or relational governance. Pri<sup>fl</sup>ing et al. (2009) demonstrate how the establishment, re<sup>fi</sup>nement and amendment of psychological contracts over time in<sup>fl</sup>uence the selection of the type of control exercised, either formal or informal. Miranda & Kavan (2005), in turn, see the psychologica contract as a ‘moment’ of governance in IS outsourcing relationship, which follows the initial moment of the ‘promissory contract’. Depending on the structure and process of the promissory contract (governance by market vs. hierarchies), the psychological contract can be part of either formal (‘arms-length’) or relational (‘embedded’) governance and thereby support either value capture or value creation, respectively. Our study continues this line of research. However, as explained earlier, we focus on the relational psychological contract, which is an aspect of relationa rather than formal governance.

Based on these considerations, we aim to contribute to the literature on outsourcing governance by providing an in-depth, qualitative account of the interrelations between formal and relational governance. For this purpose, we include a broad range of relationship aspects and, additionally, examine the psychological contract between outsourcing partners. To analyse the interrelations between formal and informal governance, we will <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the characteristics, functions and limitations of formal as well as relational governance. We will then investi gate how and under what conditions formal and relational governance function as substitutes o work as complementary mechanisms. This will lead to an analysis of the interrelations between substitution and complementarity effects.

## RESEARCH METHODS

The chosen research strategy for our study is the case study research method. The case study research method is considered suitable because it enables the researcher to study contemporary phenomena over which he or she has little or no control and examine the context within which these phenomena take place (Yin, 2003). Benbasat et al. (1987) suggested that it is a particularly useful research strategy primarily for two reasons. First of all, the researcher is able to study information systems in a natural setting and achieve a better grasp of reality. Secondly, through the case study method, the researcher is given the ability to answer ‘how’ and ‘why questions and understand the nature and complexity of the processes taking place (Benbasat et al., 1987). Along these lines, our intention was to conduct one in-depth case study that would enable us to examine and generate an in-depth understanding of how and when formal contracts and aspects of relational governance substitute each other or work as complementary mechanisms.

The case study is a maior bank based in the UK, Our examination was focused on a contract with a major Indian supplier. The contract included the support and maintenance of applications that were of relatively low strategic importance to the bank. The bank will be named GIB and its supplier PV. In total, we gathered 28 interviews during the period of October 2009 to April 2010. Out of these interviews, 14 were our primary interviews (conducted during October 2009 to

December 2009), and 14 were follow-up discussions for further clari<sup>fi</sup>cations on emergent themes (these were conducted during January 2010 to March 2010). Table 3 provides an overview of the research participants from GIB and PV.

GIB is a major European bank with operations in more than 75 countries spread across Europe, the USA and the Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c. It has more than 80 000 employees and provides a range of investment and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services to individuals, corporations and institutional clients. PV is a major IT company that provides a variety of products and services including software, business process outsourcing and infrastructure management services, and it is one of the primary vendors of GIB. PV is based in India. It employs about 55 000 people and is one of the country’s largest IT services exporters. GIB and PV had been working with each other for over 15 years. The project under examination was called X-RAY and had three objectives:

1 support of a set of non-strategic applications under one managed service;

2 having a <sup>fi</sup>xed cost for maintaining and running these applications; and

3 decommissioning any of these non-strategic applications where appropriate.

The project started of<sup>fi</sup>cially on 1 August 2008 and run under a renewed 3-year contract. The formal governance between GIB and PV was run by their master service agreement (MSA), which was an umbrella contract that encompassed individual project descriptions, which were essentially individual contractual agreements.

We used multiple data collection methods in order to provide a stronger substantiation of our themes. We used interviews to collect the re<sup>fl</sup>ections of our research participants on the established formal governance mechanisms as well as their views on the relational aspects of the governance of their arrangement (for a list of questions around which our interviews were centred, please see the APPENDIX). The interviews were based on participants’ recollections. With regard to our examination of the formal governance in place, we further viewed the MSA, the project description and documents on the outsourcing practices that the client and supplier organizations had been following. Having the chance to view these enabled us to obtain a good understanding of the contractual apparatus of governance and the working practices that were in place. However, for reasons of con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, we could not include any of these company documents in our results.

## Formal governance

The financial services industry is a highly regulated sector. Well-documented and detailed governance practices in the outsourcing domain are regarded as essential across the industry. On this basis, GIB held a very detailed apparatus of governance in all of its outsourcing projects, including the projects outsourced to PV. As mentioned, the high-level apparatus of governance was set out in the formal contract, called MSA, between GIB and PV. The MSA included an outline of some fundamental rights and obligations for each organization (i.e. acceptance of service; service augmentation; benchmarking; monitoring, reporting, regulatory, communication, operational, infrastructural and disaster recovery obligations; change procedures; and termination rights). In essence, the MSA constituted an umbrella contract for individual project descriptions. The X-RAY project description included the specifics of the X-RAY in terms of SLA. KPIs and measurement charters for deliverables, application support, maintenance and times.

An important function of this formal governance was to create a common understanding of the obligations and expectations of each partner and resolve misinterpretations and differences in expectations. On this basis, according to our interviewees, there were a number of forums in place between the outsourcing partners, where the client described its needs and the supplie aimed to understand them and build a plan. B2 (PV global account manager) commented: ‘The purpose is that there are no differences between what GIB expects from PV and what GIB thinks PV expects’.

In addition to the facilitation of a common understanding, formal governance was important for the alignment of visions between the outsourcing partners. B1 (PV head of integrated services delivery) elaborated on this issue: ‘We discuss what our vision of our account is… At the same time, GIB is sharing with us at which direction they are moving and what is expected from us. We constantly align our objectives and goals to make sure that both are working towards achieving those objectives’.

## Limitations of formal governance

While formal governance was important, its effectiveness to re<sup>fl</sup>ect practical situations was at times constrained. While legal documents could provide a clear description of the obligations of each party, in practice, there were often dependence between clients and suppliers that made the obligations and expectations of each party more vague. As an example, the supplier was sometimes not able to deliver to a particular service level owing to its own inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (i.e. because of lack of people with appropriate expertise, lack of infrastructure and lack of resources). But very often, this was due to certain dependence with the customer, in other words, because some client-input were not received on time. A5 (GIB lawyer) explained: ‘It might be the case that GIB had to give a document or had to write something and has provided it a day late so therefore the supplier is a day late. So when they get to the end of the service level it is not completely their fault, there is a joint… things happen, days to go, time delays that results in the supplier not meeting the service level. So when GIB comes to the supplier and says “Well look you have not met the service level” the supplier’s answer will always be “Yes but there was a dependency on you so therefore it is the two of us together that have done this.” So then service levels are not always followed to the letter’. In such situations, a shared understanding of obligations was necessary beyond the wording of the formal contract. This was achieved through relational governance, as will be discussed later on.

Conversely, SLAs and KPIs were not always de<sup>fi</sup>ned in line with the users’ expectations. For this reason, it was possible that the supplier was performing according to the SLA, but the end-users within the bank were dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed because their expectation was different. B3 (PV X-RAY global transformation manager) gave an example of where GIB was not receiving proper service although PV was reaching the SLAs that were de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the contract: ‘I know that there are KPIs between GIB and PV where if there is a major incident it has to be resolved by PV within 24 hours. But the user’s expectation will be that if there is a majo incident it should be resolved in two hours. Thus, the KPI was not tied into the business expectation but contractually, we did not have a problem and we would not be charged a penalty if we addressed the incident within 24 hours… But again, the user would not be happy… even if we solved the issue within 6 hours, the user will be dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed because the expectation is that the issue is addressed in two hours’. Again, a shared understanding of each partner’s obligations beyond the formal contract was here required.

According to our respondents, it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of SLAs and KPIs that made them functional. A very complicated SLA could turn out to be completely ineffective simply because it could not be understood. On this issue, A5 (GIB lawyer) noted: ‘I have seen service levels where they’ve got a huge matrix of different results and there are ten or twenty factors that go into determining whether or not a service level has met a particular result and because there are so many individuals moving parts to the service level that for a person to actually work out whether the service level has been met, they have to do all these tiny evaluations and sometimes it just does not work because if something goes wrong in one of them it means you effectively cannot get the end result’. On the other hand, a very broad SLA created the risk that it did not safeguard the interests of the customer. Some areas were easy to measure (e.g. downtime of a system), yet other areas were more vague (e.g. quality of code). Against this frame, the idea was to have a <sup>fi</sup>ne balance in the information necessary to measure a service level, and this would create effective service levels.

Furthermore, as outlined earlier, it could be secondary whether an SLA or a KPI had been met. It was the user’s expectations that needed to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. On this basis, if an SLA had been met, however the users were not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the service, the end result was dissatisfaction with the supplier. Conversely, if users were satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the service, there would be no dissatisfaction with the supplier, even if an SLA had not been met.

Our respondents also explained that over-governance could create too much bureaucracy and inhibit the smooth execution of operations. A4 (GIB IBIT sourcing and vendor management of IT portfolio) noted on the issue: ‘You have to make sure you are not over-governing things. You need to have proper governance structures in place to get issues to be on top of informa tion, to recognize issues early up front, but not getting too much details’.

Therefore, an amount of <sup>fl</sup>exibility appeared to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. A7 (GIB global technology outsourcing vendor management) elaborated on this issue and illustrated that over-governance through formal contracts could be harmful, if it was not combined with some level of <sup>fl</sup>exibility: ‘Over-governance can become a problem if you are not <sup>fl</sup>exible, if you are putting someone in charge of governance that is becoming very bureaucratic… Bad governance or ineffective gover nance would be governance that would become bureaucratic, completely disconnected from the business and the services, existing for itself… that would be very negative’. Such <sup>fl</sup>exibility did, however, require complementary control through relational governance, as outlined later on.

Our respondents further explained that formal governance mechanisms needed to be revisited and adjusted with regard to how issues were spotted, how they were resolved and when and how issues were escalated form one governance layer to another. For example, the scope of a project could change, and as a result of this, formal governance processes would also have to change. With regard the X-RAY project, when the scope of applications grew, the formal contract and other elements of formal governance would also have to change, depending on the needs of the business and the type of applications that were in the portfolio. Against this background, formal governance needed to be dynamic.

This <sup>fl</sup>exibility was also required with regard to business drivers that kept changing. For example, when there was a downturn (like the crisis in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry during 2008), cost reduction was a primary objective of formal governance. Against the background of business drivers, organizational members had to take a fresh look and re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon their expectations and the objectives of formal governance.

## Relational governance

The respondents described the relationship between GIB and PV in terms of the outsourcing partners’ shared understanding of the system, their trust, open communication, commitmen and mutual dependence. Moreover, through the respondents’ narratives, we were able to discern a psychological contract between the partners. Figure 2 represents the precise linkages between different relationship aspects and between the psychological contract and the other relationship aspects. We will now present the respondents’ explanations of relational governance through these relationship aspects and the psychological contract between the partners.

GIB and PV had maintained a very long relationship before the X-RAY project. GIB had in the past created an organization called GIB Software, which was an offshore captive. This captive was bought over by PV (a stake of 49% was bought in 2001, and the remaining 51% was bought in 2004). Because of this, many PVemployees were part of the old GIB Software organization. On the same basis, according to our interviewees, there had been interpersonal relationships between members of the two organizations over the last 20 years. It appeared that there was fo this reason a climate of closeness and familiarity among the employees of GIB and PV.

Because of their cooperation over the years, PV had developed a very good understanding of GIB’s system. GIB could therefore trust PV’s ability and competence, which refers to ability-based trust (see arrow linking shared understanding trust in Figure 2). This was a very important reason why PV had won the contract over its competitors and also a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor for the effective progression of the venture. B3 (PV X-RAY global transformation manager) elaborated on the importance of the knowledge that PV possessed with regard to GIB’s technology: ‘We used to already support about 35%–40% of this portfolio, so this gave us an edge over a few othe competitors… We could use this experience to our advantage because we had demonstrated a lot of competency in the support of these applications… So we came with the background that GIB was already happy with our service on 40% of these applications… For this reason we could leverage some of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of that deep relation outside into the wider portfolio’.

Over the long course of the collaboration, strong trust had also been created with regard to the other side’s benevolence, as far as honesty and openness beyond the necessary were concerned. This had been facilitated by open communication between the partners (see arrow linking open communication with trust in Figure 2). As A1 (GIB vendor decisions/relationship management) commented: ‘Transparency helps a lot and they have been pretty open and honest with us. I am not convinced that other vendors would be as open and honest and I think that this helps the relationship a lot…’.

Trust in the vendor’s competence, integrity and benevolence had also grown because the vendor had demonstrated their effort and commitment to the client <sup>fi</sup>rm (see arrows linking commitment with ability-based, benevolence-based and integrity-based trust in Figure 2). B1 (PV head of integrated services delivery) noted: ‘The other thing is that when you start working with the partner and you see them delivering products and working hard to deliver to you, obviously the trust factor will improve in the engagement. That trust factor will ultimately lead to higher collaboration levels’.

Throughout the interviews, it became apparent that the partners had also developed a psychological contract. Most strikingly, our respondents explained that managers tended to focus not merely on the letter of the formal contract (i.e. what it is written) but rather on the ‘spirit of the contract, which points to a perceived (as opposed to a written) agreement on obligations of both sides, which de<sup>fi</sup>nes a psychological contract. The willingness of employees to follow this spirit of the contract, more than its literal wording, implies that they also believed they had a shared understanding of their obligations and felt accountable to their perceived agreement. The psychological contract was in this sense mutual and created psychologica accountability. For example. B1 (PV head of integrated services delivery) illustrated the role of unwritten obligations between the outsourcing partners in the face of vague written obligations: ‘When you start executing, you realize that the [formal] contract has covered some areas and has not covered other areas – has not examined some features. It is important for the very contract to know the spirit… how do I interpret tomorrow if I end up having issues. The contract, because it is a legal document, we theoretically want to be very speci<sup>fi</sup>c on how we put matters in. But I have seen most of the contracts falling short – they will never cover 100%. So it is important for the parties to understand the spirit of the contract, not just only the letter. Otherwise, when the relationship is going through turbulent times, vou may end up in conflict'.

Over time, vendor <sup>fi</sup>rm members had demonstrated their commitment by making a great effort to reach an accurate understanding of the client’s expectations and ensure that these expectations were met, thereby developing shared perceptions of obligations and stabilizing the psychological contract. In this manner, their commitment supported the psychologica contract (see arrow linking commitment to psychological contract in Figure 1). Conversely, the vendor’s great commitment was driven by their strong sense of accountability, which was part of the psychological contract (see arrow linking psychological contract to commitment in Figure 2). These interlinkages between commitment and the psychological contract are illustrated in a quote from B3 (PV X-RAY global transformation manager): ‘We gave GIB the feeling that we want to work closely with them and we want to make the project a success… We wanted to show them that we would go to any extent to ensure that their expectations from us would be met… We wanted to ensure that all issues would be covered in the proposal and this is why we put a lot of effort into understanding what the business wanted from GIB ITand in effect what GIB ITwanted from us’.

On the side of the client <sup>fi</sup>rm, in turn, employees had communicated explicitly what level of help the vendor <sup>fi</sup>rm could expect from them. Client <sup>fi</sup>rm members had thereby helped develop shared expectations of their own obligations towards the vendor, thus strengthening the psychological contract (see arrow linking open communication to psychological contract in Figure 2). They had also demonstrated their commitment to the relationship (arrow linking open communication to commitment in Figure 2) and thereby further reinforced the shared expectations of obligations as part of the psychological contract (arrow linking commitment to psychological contract in Figure 2). To illustrate, B4 (PV X-RAY European programme manager) emphasized how supportive his counterparts at GIB had been: ‘On a personal note, speaking with some of the GIB managers they have said to me “If you ever need any help, even though we are expecting not to be involved, come over and shout if there is problem. It might be something that we are aware or of something that we have a point of view on”…’.

At the same time, such open communication and demonstration of commitment supported the benevolence-based trust between the two sides. by allowing members of the vendor firm to feel comfortable in asking the client for help when necessary and ensuring client <sup>fi</sup>rm members that their vendors would do so if necessary (see arrows linking open communication and commitment with trust in Figure 2). This is illustrated in a comment by B4 (PV X-RAY European programme manager): ‘Because you need to be able to say “I need your help in this”… or “I do not know, I really do not know the answer to that and thus I need your support”…. If I am comfortable to be able to go over and do that, then they will feel the same kind of trust… they will trust in the fact that I can go over there and say to them “I have got a problem”’.

The psychological contract supported the partners’ trust in each others’ integrity and benevolence (see arrows linking psychological contract with trust in Figure 2). It was easy for the partners to trust that the other would adhere to their tasks and go beyond them, because they believed that they had agreed on their obligations and had a strong sense of psychological accountability. The strong sense of accountability also went hand in hand with the partners commitment to ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their tasks, as mentioned before (see arrow linking psychological contract with commitment in Figure 2), which again strengthened trust. It is therefore clear that the psychological contract fed back into certain aspects of the relationship between vendor and client and that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence between relationship aspects and psychological contract was reciprocal rather than unidirectional.

The mutuality in the psychological contract was reinforced by the strong mutual dependence between the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. GIB was dependent on PV to carry on with its operations, as a big transition would be required to move to another vendor. Such a transition would be time-consuming, expensive and possibly complex to carry out for GIB. In respect, PV was highly dependent on GIB for a great revenue stream. In other words, it appears that there was a power balance that limited the possibility for the demonstration of opportunistic behaviour by the vendor or a vendor ‘lock-in’ scenario, as well as the possibility that the client would try to exploit the vendor (e.g. by diminishing the vendor’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t potential). Furthermore, GIB and PV managed to make this programme a win-win situation. GIB had been able to lower its costs, and PV had made a satisfactory (at that point in time) amount of pro<sup>fi</sup>t out of the contract.

Such mutual dependence supported the expectations of mutual obligations, which are part of the psychological contract (arrow linking mutual dependence with psychological contract in Figure 2). The mutual dependence also raised the levels of commitment on both sides (arrow linking mutua dependence with commitment in Figure 2). As A4 (GIB IBIT sourcing and vendor management of IT portfolio) noted: ‘If they decide to go away, GIB cannot work… and it GIB decides to go away from them it will need a transition. But there is a dependency on both sides… GIB makes big revenue to them, it will have a big impact on PV’s revenues to lose GIB as a customer… this is why they are highly committed and they are trying to deliver…’.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings thus suggest that the partners had developed a strong psychological contract, i.e. a perceived mutual agreement on obligations of both sides, which was psychologically binding. Its mutuality and psychologically binding nature distinguished the psychological contract from the other aspects of relational governance – shared understanding, trust, open communication, commitment and mutual dependence. At the same time, however, the psychological contract was supported by these other aspects of relational governance, and it fed back into them (Figure 2). This implies that psychological contract and other relationa elements reinforced each other. Moreover, some of the relational elements fed into other elements (e.g. shared understanding fed into trust). We found evidence only for certain interrelationships between relational elements, but we assume that many others are likely to occur. For example, it seems plausible that trust feeds into open communication, commitment and the psychological contract, even though our respondents did not mention this. These observations imply that that if some relational elements are present, others will be created, and they together feed into the psychological contract. The psychological contract will in turn strengthen the relational elements. Through such mutual reinforcement, the relational elements are likely to co-emerge with each other and with the psychological contract.

## Limitations of relational governance

The respondents’ reports suggest that relational governance was hard to maintain under certain circumstances. Firstly, it was not possible to rely on relational governance in the case of workforce changes, where personal relationships were unstable. Under these circumstances, governance had to revert to formal contracts. A7 (GIB global technology outsourcing vendor management) emphasized that formal contracts were necessary in order to make the collaboration work independent of the people involved: ‘People may change…. So to have a good managed relation ship which is not dependent on people you need to have a framework’.

Respondents further explained that relationships would sometimes not endure dif<sup>fi</sup>cult times. Formal governance would in this case be given greater prominence. A5 (GIB lawyer)

noted: ‘It is because of the unknown, you do not know what the relationship is going to be, so the best thing is to have something formalistic so that if something does go wrong you can always accommodate each other by going back to the schedule… it is when problems happen that a good complex governance schedule is important’. This implies that when there are disagreements or con<sup>fl</sup>icts between the outsourcing partners and relationa governance therefore becomes weaker and is no longer suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to settle down the issues, the outsourcing partners need to have formal governance in place. Formal governance will then provide the outsourcing partners with a framework for action.

## Conditions of substitution and complementarity

Our results showed that both formal and relational governance had important roles in governing the offshoring collaboration, but they functioned within certain limitations (Table 4). These limitations created important conditions under which formal and relational governance compensated for each other (which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as one type of complementarity, Table 2), and or dampened or replaced each other (which are the two types of substitution, Table 2). Overall, managers tended to use relational governance mechanisms to compensate or substitute for the formal governance, rather than vice versa.

Our results suggest that formal governance had a legally binding character with regard to the obligations and expectations of each party. Against this background, proper boundaries of acceptable and non-acceptable behaviours were being established. However, formal governance mechanisms were limited to accommodate for all situations and unforeseen contingencies. In our case study company, relational governance took over in these cases, and compensated for, dampened or even replaced the workings of the formal contract. In the following, we will concentrate on the role of the psychological contract in this matter. The psychological contract created a sense of mutual obligation that produced a whole set of acceptable and non-acceptable behaviours, which went beyond the formal contract, yet were deemed as fundamental for the execution of the arrangement.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings <sup>fi</sup>rstly illustrate that formal governance, while important, tended to be very mechanistic in nature. This means that contractual clauses and legal interpretations produced insights that did not necessarily re<sup>fl</sup>ect practical realities (Table 4, <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation of formal governance). In practice, it appeared that while the obligations of the supplier to deliver a particular service level could be clear and accurate, the contributions of the client to that end, or unexpected contingencies that alter the practical side of the service delivery, as well as the feasibility of certain plans, could not be captured by the formal governance. In such instances, the psychological contract between the participating <sup>fi</sup>rms acted as an alternative governance mechanism that produced a shared understanding of each party’s obligations, as well as the feasibility of adhering to these. Our results indicate that while service delivery could be accurate according to the formal plan, the end result was not necessarily satisfactory, again because of the inadequacy of the formal arrangement to capture all angles of service delivery, including changing requirements. A <sup>fi</sup>t in the psychological contracts of both sides accommodated for such situations and made both parties more <sup>fl</sup>exible: the client in instances that the supplier had not ticked all the boxes of the levels of service delivery and the supplier in instances where service delivery had been appropriate according to the legal arrangement, yet not satisfactory for the client’s business. In this manner, the strengths of the psychological contract compensated for certain weaknesses of the formal governance mechanisms. By falling back onto the psychological contract, the partners dampened the impact of formal governance and in certain situations replaced it by the psychological contract and its associated relational elements.

Secondly, it appears that for most people, it was impossible to know the <sup>fi</sup>ne details set out in the formal governance mechanisms contract (Table 2, second limitation of formal governance). Certain contractual regimes were particularly complicated, and not all people involved in the work were aware of them. Thirdly, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings further suggest that the formal governance mechanisms were only effective to the extent that they did not lead to over-governing and unnecessary bureaucracies (Table 4, third limitation of formal governance). From our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we can infer that governance through a psychological contract could compensate for these limitations of formal governance mechanisms, as an effective means of avoiding the timeconsuming interpretation of overcomplicated formal contracts, as well as cumbersome bureaucracy. The respondents also highlighted that formal governance mechanisms needed to be adjusted to changing parameters in the project and the business environment (Table 4, column two, fourth condition). Our results imply that even if the legal adjustment was not carried out immediately, the psychological contract could compensate for the weakness of formal governance and allowed partners to govern their collaboration effectively. We can infer that formal governance would under these conditions be dampened or even replaced by the relational governance mechanisms.

Relational governance (psychological contract as well as other relational elements), in turn, was also subject to certain limitations. Being built on personal relationships, relational governance could be weakened through workforce changes (Table 4, <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation of relational governance), which forced partners to draw on the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of roles and obligations in the formal governance mechanisms. Interestingly, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that dif<sup>fi</sup>cult times could lead the partners to revert either to relational governance or to formal mechanisms as a more effective means of governance, depending on whether relationships survived the dif<sup>fi</sup>cult situa tion (For this reason, ‘dif<sup>fi</sup>cult times’ is the only condition that appears in two columns of Table 4.) With respect to the psychological contract, it clearly helped the partners to cope with arising dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in the collaboration, such as performance problems. The partners’ shared expectations of their mutual obligations, along with the high levels of psychological accountability, allowed them to revert to informal means of control in cases were mistakes occurred, instead of adhering to the letter of the formal contract. Rather than pointing the counterparts to their obligations in terms of formal contract, GIB members could rely on PV counterparts to respond willingly to verbal criticism of their performance. As A1 (GIB vendor decisions/relationship management) explained: ‘If I go to the vendor management and say “These things are not working” they take it on board, they come back and they will say and actually write down a number of things…. They will analyze what they did wrongly and what they are going to do’.

In these situations, the psychological contract and the associated relational elements could act as the more useful governance mechanism and were taken as a replacement for forma governance. However, this was only possible as long as the psychological contract withstood the dif<sup>fi</sup>cult times, which we regard as a ‘subcondition’ of substitution and complementarity.

If relationships were weakened through disagreements, and the psychological contract was thereby lost, the partners had to fall back on formal governance mechanisms. The formal de<sup>fi</sup>nition of obligations and rights as part of the contract could then function as a safeguard for both parties and compensate for the weakness of relational governance. With increasing severity of the disagreements, the formal contract could dampen or even replace the use of the psychological contract. To illustrate, A5 (GIB lawyer) noted: ‘So a detailed governance is put up at the outset to ensure that if the relationship does go bad, if it doesn’t turn out to be what everybody thought it would be, at least there’s a very formalistic process that everyone can say “Fine let’s adhere to this process, let’s go through this very structured method and see if we can <sup>fi</sup>x something that has gone wrong.” That’s the only real reason what I can see is to have such a formalistic governance process at the start’.

While the managers in this company tended to use formal and relational governance either to compensate for each other or as substitutes (dampening and replacement), there were also strong voices to argue that formal governance could and should enable relational governance, which is the other type of complementarity. The enabling in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the formal governance on relational governance elements is illustrated in Figure 2 (arrows linking formal governance directly or indirectly with aspects of relational governance). To be more speci<sup>fi</sup>c, A2 (GIB director of risk) commented that the outsourcing partners should not pay attention to formal governance only during the dif<sup>fi</sup>cult times of the relationship. He suggested that close monitoring and governance could enhance communication and a tighter <sup>fi</sup>t between the outsourcing partners: ‘These meetings, these processes… they are useful not only during bad times… this is very common in the outsourcing industry, ignoring governance because things go well… All these processes can actually help you boost your relationship, communicate better, collaborate better and leverage the relationship towards innovation’.

Firstly, and not surprisingly, clear formal governance mechanisms helped the partners to achieve a better visibility and transparency of obligations and, through this, a shared perception of obligations of both sides. In this manner, the formal governance mechanisms fed into the psychological contract (Figure 2, arrows linking formal governance with visibility/transparency and, through this, the psychological contract). Additionally, a number of interviewees agreed that formal governance mechanisms facilitated relations of trust (in the sense of benevolence-based and integrity-based trust) between the outsourcing partners through the transparency and visibility they created (Figure 2, arrow linking formal governance with visibility/transparency and trust). B1 (PV head of integrated services delivery) made an explicit link between formal governance and trust by arguing that ‘…the governance in place HAS to facilitate trust, otherwise you are not working with the right governance.

A4 (GIB IBIT sourcing and vendor management of IT portfolio) went a step further and illustrated that in essence, formal governance mechanisms should be aimed at creating transparency, but not only for the generation of trust. Transparency and visibility enabled the outsourcing partners to bring issues up front and examine how they could sort them, thus facilitating open communica tion about such issues (Figure 2, arrow linking visibility/transparency with open communication): ‘Governance mechanisms really ought to create transparency. Whenever you look or think about sourcing you recognize that the more you are able to create transparency across your sourcing portfolio the better you become in supporting issues, managing issues’.

Formal governance was further seen to yield mutual interest and dependence and, through this, high levels of commitment on both sides (Figure 2, arrows linking formal governance mechanisms with mutual dependence and with commitment). For example, the contract between the partners was set in such a way that when one partner was doing well, the other one was also bene<sup>fi</sup>tting; conversely, if one partner was having a problem, the other partner shared the problem. B2 (PV global account manager) hence called the relationship between the two organizations a ‘very symbiotic relationship’. As mentioned before, such mutual dependence and strong commitment both strengthened the psychological contract (Figure 2).

In sum, the potential enabling effects of formal governance on relational governance were apparent as our interviewees emphasized that formal governance aimed to precisely outline the obligations and create clarity in terms of the expectations of each outsourcing party. Through this, formal governance was seen to enable the two partners to avoid misunderstandings and communicate better. Formal governance thereby fed into the psychological contract and helped the partners to develop trust (benevolence based and integrity based) and communicate abou issues openly. Formal governance also reinforced perceptions of mutual dependence and mutua commitment, thereby again feeding into the psychological contract.

## DISCUSSION

In this section, we will <sup>fi</sup>rst illustrate some risks of using formal and relational governance as substitutes as well as bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using them as complements and consider potential interrelations between substitution and complementarity effects. We then demonstrate that the either/or dichotomy between formal and relational governance is relatively limited and illuminate the two-way causa relationship between substitution and complementarity. This is followed by a re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the role of the psychological contract with regard to complementarity and substitution. After this, we highlight implications for practice, the limitations of our study and avenues for future research.

## Risks of using formal and relational governance as substitutes and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using them as complements

Our results indicate that using formal and relational governance as substitutes (dampening or replacing the other form of governance), rather than complements (enabling or compensating for the other form), leads to undesirable outcomes. We have demonstrated that both forma and relational governance mechanisms have distinctive limitations that the other can compen sate for. We can therefore conclude that using excessive formal governance can result in unnecessary bureaucracies that reduce the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of the partnership and make it less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. In a similar way, excessive formal governance can overcomplicate the way things are carried out and impose rules and procedures that are not possible for people to fully comprehend and implement. On this basis, over-reliance on formal governance can be proven to be counterproductive.

We suggest that using excessive relational governance, on the other hand, can also have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly undesirable results. In particular, when managers rely exclusively on their psychologica contracts, formal governance mechanisms tend to be neglected (dampening or replacement effect). We argue that this bears the danger that managers may not re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their formal governance mechanisms and whether their objectives are realistic and in line with business drivers and expectations and, therefore, stick to a ‘casual’ way of conducting business. Even more important is the fact that over time, managers may not examine whether amendments in the formal governance mechanisms should be made in order to safeguard their own interests. Furthermore, people involved in the arrangement may also change. The formal governance is then a safeguard to ensure that irrespective of such changes, the outsourcing venture can be continued.

Similar to previous research (Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Goo et al., 2009), our results therefore indicate that using formal and relational governance in a complementary way (enabling or compensating) breeds better outsourcing outcomes than using them as substitutes (dampening or replacing). We suggest that a combined use of formal and relational governance can enhance the arrangement in a positive way, because they can compensate for each other’s limitations and they can enable each other. Conversely, we suggest that relational governance, and the psychological contract in particular, can facilitate the drafting, amendments and therefore the effectiveness of formal governance. This suggestion is consistent with that of Poppo & Zenger (2002), who found that outsourcing clients develop greater relational norms as their contracts become more detailed and customized, and respectively, as outsourcing clients employ greater contractual complexity (i.e. more customized and detailed contracts), they develop greater levels of relational governance.

## Either/or dichotomy of substitution and complementarity

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that the either/or dichotomy between substitution and complementarity is relatively constrained and that there appears to be a two-way causal connection between substitution and complementarity. In this direction, <sup>fi</sup>rst, we discussed how each type of governance compensates for the limitations of the other. Furthermore, we found that compensation occurred under certain conditions, where the weaknesses of one or the other form of governance became more relevant. For example, certain practical realities made the vagueness of the formal governance mechanisms apparent, leading the partners to compensate fo this weakness by relying on their psychological contract and other elements of relational governance. However, with increasing severity and relevance of the weaknesses of one form of governance, such compensations could lead to situations of dampening or even full replacements by the other form of governance. In other words, complementarities were leading to substitution of one type of governance by the other. For example, when relationships were entirely strong and the workforce stable, the limitations of formal governance would become most prominent, and the partners could draw fully on their psychological contract and use it as the sole form of governance, with all its advantages over formal governance mechanisms. Conversely, in the case of con<sup>fl</sup>icts or workforce changes, the psychological contract would not be effective anymore and would have to be substituted by the formal contract. In this way, similarly to Huber et al. (2013), we illustrated how substitution can result from preceding complementarity. Our result therefore reinforces the claim of Huber et al. (2013) that there is a causal relationship where complementarity may lead to substitution.

However, extending the contribution by Huber et al. (2013), we demonstrated that this causal relationship between complementarity and substitution is in essence two-way. In particular, our results show that the complete replacement of one type of governance by the other becomes ineffective over time, because under certain conditions (or contextual triggers), the strengths of the type of governance that has been replaced may become more relevant. This situation leads in effect to complementarity resulting from preceding substitution. For example, situations of over-governance by formal processes were producing bureaucracies, and they were inhibiting the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of the venture. In such situations, our interviewees acknowledged that more informal governance was needed. This result not only reinforces the view by Huber et al. (2013) on the causal connection between complementarity and substitution but also highlights that this connection is two-way.

## Complementarity and substitution in the light of the psychological contract

By re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on the role of the psychological contract, we can construe the substitution and complementarity effects in yet more depth. Psychological contracts are similar to formal contracts with regard to their mutual and binding nature. Formal contracts have a binding character tha stems from their legal nature, while psychological contracts have a psychologically binding character based on the sense of accountability they create. We argue that these characteristics make it easier for partners to rely fully on the psychological contract as a substitute of the formal contract, i.e. dampening or replacing the role of the formal contract. By contrast, other relationship aspects, such as trust and commitment, are not necessarily mutual or psychologically binding and could for this reason not as easily substitute for the mutual and binding characteristics of the formal contract. The mutual and psychologically binding nature of the psychological contract can therefore be seen as a strength, which it shares with the formal contract. Other aspects of relational governance do not necessarily possess this strength and can therefore not serve as equally strong substitutes.

At the same time, the psychological contract is strongly related to other relationship aspects (as shown in Figure 2). For this reason, it has ‘relational’ strengths that are different to a formal contract and can compensate for the weaknesses of the formal contract. In our study, for example, the psychological contract strengthened trust and commitment, which made it possible to work around vague specifications in the contract. Under conditions where weaknesses of relational governance were less relevant and weaknesses of the formal contract were more relevant (e.g. stable relation ships and few workforce changes), the psychological contract could therefore comfortably be taken as a replacement and therefore substitute for formal governance. It thereby underscored the causality between complementarity and substitution. We can conclude that the similarities of the psychological contract to the formal contract in terms of strengths (its contractual nature) made it a forceful substitute to formal governance. By contrast, its additional, relational strengths (its associations with other relationship aspects), made it a forceful complement.

## Managerial implications

Our research has important practical implications. It has illuminated that over-reliance on forma governance as well as over-reliance on relational governance are ineffective. While during different moments in the outsourcing venture, managers tend to favour one type of governance over the other, both of them are equally signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The strengths of each type of governance provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant safeguards against the weaknesses of the other.

For outsourcing <sup>fi</sup>rms, it is therefore not enough that legal experts set up sophisticated forma contracts to cover as many prospective circumstances as possible. For the formal contract to work, it is necessary that managers and employees who are involved in the outsourcing arrangement understand and commit to the ‘spirit’ of the formal contract and thus develop a psychological contract that complements the formal contract. Conversely, when the collaboration goes smoothly, managers should not be complacent about the legal contract but should review it regularly in light of changes in the business environment as well as the actual expectations of obligations that are part of the psychological contract.

In this frame, practitioners should be aware of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c advantages of each type of governance and utilize them accordingly. A good contract cannot make up for a bad relationship, as the very own distinctive strengths of relational governance can become relevant unde certain conditions (e.g. unpredictable environments), which a good contract cannot make up for. Conversely, a good relationship cannot make up for a bad contract, because the very own distinctive strengths of formal governance can become more relevant under certain conditions (e.g. workforce changes). Last but not least, as the exercise of governance is costly, managers should be aware of the conditions under which one type of governance weakens the effectiveness of the other, as well as the conditions that facilitate the production of synergistic results.

## Limitations and future research

The main limitation of this study is that it focused only on one IToutsourcing contract and thus has limited power to provide statistically generalizable results, although analytical generalization is possible (Yin, 2003). However, as mentioned in the methodology section, the primary objective of this research was to provide an in-depth assessment of the interrelations between formal and relational governance in IT outsourcing, utilizing an approach that focuses on the natural setting of the phenomenon rather than providing generalizable results. For this reason, we decided to follow an in-depth qualitative case study approach, in order to yield more profound and richer insights into the governance of IT outsourcing arrangements. Based on our case study results, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed conditions of substitution and complementarity (Table 4) and interrelationship between elements of relational governance (Figure 2). These suggestions need yet to be corroborated by further research in similar and different settings.

One important characteristic of our case was the prior history of the partnership. The collaboration had emerged from a previous captive arrangement, which strongly facilitated the development of a psychological contract and relationships between the partners. Because of their prior collaboration as part of the same organization, the foundations of the relationships and the psychological contract between the partners had been set before the contract was ever signed. Future research could examine whether an equally strong psychological contract can be developed in an offshoring partnership that cannot draw on such a shared history.

A further limitation of our research is that our data were collected during 2009–2010, which is a period very close to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis of 2008 that had a big impact on the outsourcing market and supplier–client relationships. It is possible that in the period right after the crisis, managers were particularly aware of the risks of over-relying on only one form of governance at the expense of the other, because they were just experiencing such times. This consideration could apply to the case of the more recent <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis within Europe, as well. In this frame, it can be assumed that the risks of over-relying on one form of governance over the other may be less apparent – but still latent – during better economic times.

Future research could further examine the role of wider institutional factors for formal and relational governance, and psychological contracts in particular. While psychological contracts tend to be ‘venture speci<sup>fi</sup>c’, we maintain that wider institutional practices can have a profound effect on the evolution of psychological contracts. As examples, competitor activity, the performance of the service provider with other organizations, and wider industry practices and standards can all be factors contributing to the evolution of psychological contracts. Such assessments can yield signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights into the use and effectiveness of relational governance, as, according to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, psychological contracts go beyond the relationship aspects that previous research has focused upon.

Future research could further address the psychological contracts of individual employees and examine how these are interrelated with the ongoing governance. We agree with Blaskovich & Mintchik (2011) that further research on the employee level can provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights into effective governance, and primarily within a context of a heterogeneous workforce, which is often the case in outsourcing settings. As an example, Zimmermann et al. (2013) provided an in-depth account of onshore employees’ offshoring attitudes and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on relational behaviours towards their offshore colleagues (including the engagement of collaborative behaviours, communication, knowledge transfer and support). We argue that this type of research that focuses on the level of individuals can provide further fruitful considerations around the use and effectiveness of relational governance within organizations

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

Through our qualitative research design, we were able to gain a deeper insight into the mechanisms by which formal and relational governance are interrelated. We have explained how and under what conditions formal and relational governance substituted or complemented for each other, and we have outlined potential enabling effects. We were further able to discern a mutual causality between complementarity (in terms of compensation and enabling effects) and substitution (in terms of replacement/dampening effects).

To our knowledge, this piece of research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst that applies the psychological contract lens to the study of relational governance in an outsourcing context, allowing us to gain a more comprehensive perspective on relational governance and a stronger explanation of substitution and complementarity effects between relational and formal governance. The binding nature of relational governance explains why at times the outsourcing partners used the two forms of governance as substitutes rather than enabling mechanisms. The psychological contract was, owing to its mutually binding nature, suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently strong to fully govern the collaboration. This forceful psychological contract allowed the partners to navigate through their collaboration effectively without consulting the formal contract. At the same time, however, it became clear that this approach was risky. Most importantly, over-reliance on the psychological contract meant that managers would not review and update the formal contract and other formal governance mechanisms during times of smooth collaboration, which could back<sup>fi</sup>re in case the relationship became weaker.

We therefore conclude that the potential of the formal and relational governance to facilitate the effectiveness of each other has to be fully realized. Outsourcing partnerships could be governed more robustly, and under changing conditions, if managers focus on the potentia for compensating and enabling effects between formal and relational governance, rather than substituting one for the other.
