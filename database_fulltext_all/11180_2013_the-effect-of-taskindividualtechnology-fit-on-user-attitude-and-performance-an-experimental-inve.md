# The effect of task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t on user attitude and performance: An experimental investigation

Alison Parkes ⁎

Department of Accounting, The University of Melbourne, Australia

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 29 September 2011 Received in revised form 23 July 2012 Accepted 7 October 2012 Available online 16 October 2012

Keywords: Task–individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t Decisional guidance Task complexity Decision support User attitude Performance

## a b s t r a c t

Decision support research explores interactions between individuals, tasks, and technology. In this paper, I deconstruct the task–technology–individual <sup>fi</sup>t model into three two-way interactions and ascertain how these interactions affect user attitude and performance. Performance is conceptualized as consisting of two dimensions, technology performance and task performance. The paper reports a controlled laboratory experiment involving 94 subjects using a purpose built decision support system. The results demonstrate several important principles. User attitude is affected by the <sup>fi</sup>t between individual and technology whereas technology performance is affected by the <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology, and task and individual. Users of technology <sup>fi</sup>tted to them as an individual can perceive it as more useful than it actually is, in terms of improving task performance. Finally, technology performance translates into task performance. Technology performance is a necessary but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient precursor to task performance.

## 1. Introduction

This paper uses a well established model [23] to frame an in-depth investigation of the effects of <sup>fi</sup>t on decision support outcomes. Goodhue and Thompsons' [23] task–technology–individual <sup>fi</sup>t model is deconstructed into three two-way interactions, task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF), individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF), and task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (TaIF) after Liu et al. [34]. The degree of granularity obtained by using this deconstructed model creates an opportunity to address fundamental decision support questions from a fresh perspective.

DSS performance is contextualized into two dimensions. One relates to the use of the DSS to obtain a better outcome or recommendation from the technology (technology performance), the other relates to performance on the decision task (task performance). Technology performance is about using the system; task performance is about using the outputs of the system. Prior empirical evidence demonstrates that improved technology performance leads to improved task performance [26,50]. This relationship is not necessarily direct given that a user could use the system but not rely on the information it provides, in which case the DSS would affect technology performance but not task performance. Alternatively, a user could elect to use the system and to rely on the outputs, affecting both technology performance and task performance.

If technology performance is a necessary, but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient, condition for improved task performance other questions arise. What prompts users of DSS to reply on DSS outputs? Under what conditions does technology performance affect task performance? The answer appears to lie in the attitude of users towards the DSS, with many prior studies linking users' attitude to technology with their performance. [18,19,23,29,31,34]. In particular, the consideration of users' beliefs and attitude is particularly important where the use of the DSS is not mandated, and where the task solution is non-normative [27]. This paper looks at the impact of <sup>fi</sup>t and user attitude on performance, exploring both technology and task performance. The primary research question addressed is:

How do task–technology–individual fit affect DSS user attitude and performance?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The research background and theoretical foundations for the work are presented in Section 2. Section 3 details the speci<sup>fi</sup>c hypotheses while Section 4 discusses the methods and materials used to test these hypotheses. Results are presented in Section 5: Section 6 contains a discussion of the results and limitations, and concludes the study.

## 2. Research background and theoretical bases

## 2.1. Considerations of fit, user attitude and performance

Conceptually achieving “<sup>fi</sup>t” is about aligning the interrelationships between individuals, tasks and technologies. Existing studies recognize that <sup>fi</sup>t affects both user attitude and performance [10,23,34,53]. The task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t model [23] suggests that performance depends on <sup>fi</sup>t, identifying a weakly positive link between usage and performance. Recent work by Liu et al. [34] explored task– technology–individual <sup>fi</sup>t by employing three differing <sup>fi</sup>t interactions (individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF), task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (TaIF), and task– technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF)). These two-way interactions provide a valuable opportunity to explore the effect and implications of each type of <sup>fi</sup>t separately and independently. These dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t are discussed in more detail in Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3 of this paper.

The use of any technology will vary based on the <sup>fi</sup>t achieved between the task, technology and individual [14,22,23,48]. Good <sup>fi</sup>t is required before technology can positively impact task and/or technology performance [23]. Technology performance and task performance are not directly related; a user could elect to complete a decision task without using the DSS, or they could use the DSS but ignore any outputs generated by that DSS when making their decision. Prior literature unequivocally establishes the necessity for a user to perceive a system as useful before they will use it, if use is not mandated [5,15,16,27,29]. Perceptions of usefulness of the technology affect task performance via the degree of reliance achieved on the outputs of the technology. Selecting perception of usefulness as the user attitude of interest to this paper complements and extends Liu's exploration of user attitude and DSS models.

This paper explores performance related to use of the technology (producing a better output) and performance related to the ultimate decision (making a better decision). The paper explores relationships between perceptions of usefulness and performance for varying <sup>fi</sup>t and performance dimensions, posing several related questions. Do perceptions of usefulness change technology performance and/or task performance? Does technology performance translate into task performance? These questions underpin the hypotheses posed in Section 3 of this paper.

## 2.2. Characteristics of tasks, individuals and technology

The following sections identify and detail the task, individual and technology characteristics central to this work.

## 2.2.1. Task characteristic — task complexity

DSS are generally considered to be more gainfully deployed when supporting complex tasks [9,38] however it is unclear whether this holds when user and technology characteristics are considered concurrently with task complexity. Task complexity needs to be calibrated independently of the decision-maker [51]; an experienced decisionmaker will render even a highly complex task less dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, whereas a novice may perceive a relatively simple task as quite dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Both the complexity (a task characteristic) and dif<sup>fi</sup>culty (a function of <sup>fi</sup>t) of a task should be considered when exploring user attitude and performance. This paper examines user attitude and performance in light of the differential impacts of <sup>fi</sup>t between the complexity of the task and the technology, (TTF); and the complexity of the task and the individual (TaIF).

## 2.2.2. Individual characteristic — task expertise

Task performance declines when there is a gap between the task expertise of an individual decision-maker, and the task expertise required to perform the task. DSSs bridge this task expertise gap, and are therefore of greater value where a decision-maker is not a task expert [2,4,35]. When a decision-maker draws on task expertise supplied by a decision support system it helps them to perform the decision task in an expert-like manner [7,12].

Novice decision makers lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient task expertise to be able to determine the relevance of information offered so tend to collect information cues indiscriminately, and inappropriately weight those information cues which they do collect [13,43]. Novices also deal with individual information cues, as they have not yet begun to develop the heuristics or pattern knowledge that experts possess [6,38,40,42].

Experienced, but not expert, practitioners are better able to determine information saliency but have not fully developed the pattern knowledge required to discerningly reduce the number of information cues they endeavor to consider [43,44]. Experienced decision-makers generally seek to validate and extend their existing patterns. This paper considers user attitude and performance in light of the differential impacts of <sup>fi</sup>t between task expertise and technology (ITeF), and task expertise and the task being undertaking (TaIF).

## 2.2.3. Technology characteristic — decisional guidance design

Design affects how, and how much, a technology will be used [21], so design choices should be deliberate and purposeful [46]. Poor or unintended design choices can create suboptimal <sup>fi</sup>t and result in a technology being ignored or overridden [16,17,21]. A key issue in DSS design is whether a DSS has suf<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanisms in place to effectively guide the decision-maker and support their human judgment [46]. Silver (1990) identi<sup>fi</sup>es two broad types of decisional guidance, informative and suggestive. Informative guidance enlightens a decisionmaker by providing additional information pertinent to the decision task. Suggestive guidance sways a decision-maker by providing a recommendation on how to proceed during interactions with the system. Empirical studies have established that the form of decisional guidance provided is an important explanatory variable in relation to both user attitude and performance [26,33,36,39,41,46,50]. This paper considers user attitude and performance in light of <sup>fi</sup>t between complexity of the task being undertaken and the decisional guidance embedded in the technology (TTF); and task expertise and the decisional guidance embedded in the technology (ITeF).

## 3. Hypotheses

The three two-way <sup>fi</sup>t interactions involving the task technology and individual characteristic discussed in Section 2 are examined in this section by considering direct effects of individual <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions, and by examining relationships between <sup>fi</sup>t, user attitude and performance as shown in the research model contained in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Individual–technology fit (ITeF)

Individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF) is the extent to which the technology (decisional guidance) <sup>fi</sup>ts the individual (task expertise). ITeF does not include the task characteristic of task complexity. When considering the <sup>fi</sup>t between individual and technology ITeF will affect user attitude more than performance, due to the inability of novices to process cues ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. Novices are likely to perceive a technology providing additional information cues as useful, despite the fact that they are unlikely to be able to use that additional information to improve their performance, consequently it is argued that ITeF will affect attitude rather than performance. When experienced practitioners are provided with pattern knowledge (suggestive guidance) it helps extend and con<sup>fi</sup>rm their existing pattern knowledge; however unlike novices they already possess some pattern knowledge so the potential for performance improvements is minimal. Performance improvements ensue where the DSS provides a more expert pattern than the individual already possesses. In this case of good ITeF, it is anticipated that DSS will be perceived as less useful, due to the perception that it is offering knowledge the individual already possesses. ITeF primarily affects perceptions, not reality. I hypothesize therefore that <sup>fi</sup>t between the technology and the user will directly affect user attitude towards the technology.

Hypothesis 1. Individual/technology <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF) affects user attitude (perceptions of usefulness) of the technology, rather than task and/ or technology performance.

## 3.2. Task–individual Fit (TaIF)

Task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (TaIF) is the extent to which the complexity of the task being undertaken matches the expertise of the individual. TaIF does not consider the technology characteristic of decisional guidance. As discussed in Section 2.2.1 the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of a task is related to the task expertise of an individual and the complexity of the task they are undertaking. TaIF will be poor where a task is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and good where a task is not dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Performance has the potential to improve where performance is currently sub-optimal, i.e. in those situations where the complexity of a task exceeds an individual's skills and experience. The impact of TaIF on user attitude is less well understood; however Kamis et al. [27] found that perceptions of usefulness increase in line with task complexity up to the point where the user becomes overwhelmed by choice, which then triggers a decline in perceived usefulness. Liu et al. [34] found that TaIF directly affected user attitude in a semi or unstructured task setting, and report mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings for the performance effects of TaIF. Accordingly Hypothesis 2 proposes that <sup>fi</sup>t between the task and individual will affect performance, rather than user attitude.

Hypothesis 2. Task/individual <sup>fi</sup>t (TaIF) affects performance (technology performance; task performance), rather than attitude towards the technology (perceived usefulness).

## 3.3. Task–technology fit (TTF)

Task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) is the extent to which the complexity of the task being undertaken matches the decisional guidance provided by the technology. TTF does not take in the individual characteristic of task expertise. Informative guidance provides additional information cues, while suggestive guidance provides pattern knowledge [45], as discussed in Section 2.2.3. Where additional cues in the form of informative guidance are provided for a more complex task TTF will be low. Complex tasks by de<sup>fi</sup>nition already contain multiple cues [51] so providing additional cues is unlikely to improve TTF. For that same complex task, pattern knowledge in the form of suggestive guidance provides a means to consolidate cues and offset task complexity. Past studies provide evidence of positive links between <sup>fi</sup>t, user attitude to technology, and performance [19,23,29,34]. In a related study looking at consumers' online behavior [49] Hong et al. <sup>fi</sup>nd that the <sup>fi</sup>t between task (searching vs. browsing) and technology (list format vs. matrix format) affects performance but not attitude. Liu et al. [34] <sup>fi</sup>nds that TTF is related to attitude in a structured task setting, and that TTF is more important to improving performance than either TaIF or ITeF. Where tasks are semi- or un-structured however a relationship between <sup>fi</sup>t and attitude is more likely to be found in either of TaIF or ITeF rather than TTF [34]. Given the unstructured nature of the task employed in this paper (refer to Section 4.1 for details of the task and context), and the prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings linking TTF and performance, it appears likely that TTF will directly affect performance, rather than user attitude (perceptions of usefulness) of the technology.

Hypothesis 3. Task/technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) affects performance (technology performance; task performance), rather than attitude towards the technology (perceived usefulness).

## 3.4. Performance, attitude and fit

The research model in Fig. 1 depicts interactions between <sup>fi</sup>t, attitude and performance, with dimensions of performance including technology performance (the quality of the recommendation generated by the technology), and task performance (the quality of the decision ultimately made by the user). In order for use of the technology to affect task performance, the user must incorporate any recommendation generated by the technology into their decision. Where a technology is not perceived as being useful an individual is unlikely to elect to incorporate the DSS recommendation into their decision, hence task performance will be affected only where an individual perceives the technology as being useful. It is anticipated that perceptions of usefulness and technology performance will directly, and in combination, affect task performance. Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis in relation to the performance effects of attitude and <sup>fi</sup>t.

Hypothesis 4. User attitude towards the technology (perceived usefulness), and technology performance (recommendation quality), separately and jointly affect task performance (decision quality).

## 4. Methods and materials

## 4.1. Experimental task and context

The context of this study is a non-normative judgment task that requires subjects to make a recommendation about the future of a company that has recently entered into voluntary administration. The task was framed within relevant corporate insolvency legislation. Subjects were cast as a voluntary administrator making a recommendation about the

future of a company; they had to decide whether to allow the business to continue to trade or to close the business down and sell off the assets. The relevance and importance of this insolvency decision task have been established in a stream of prior research [1–4,11,30,31]. The task is unstructured and lacks a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed decision making process, yet it re-<sup>fl</sup>ects a current and important real world business decision. The DSS (discussed in detail in Section 4.2) provided a logically framed decision model (refer to Fig. 2 for details) designed to motivate decision makers to traverse a broad span of relevant issues prior to making their decision. This DSS is not intended to be a substitute for a human decision maker; it is designed to function as an electronic colleague or additional source of advice for the decision maker. It is anticipated that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this paper would be equally applicable to any unstructured non-normative judgment task that requires domain expertise and critical assessment of diverse information cues to achieve a decision outcome.

## 4.2.1. Suggestive guidance design

The pre-cursor questions embed suggestive guidance dynamically based on decision-makers responses to the pre-cursor questions. Suggestive guidance was constructed to mimic the expert pattern knowledge of decision-makers with extensive experience in this task. An example screenshot containing suggestive guidance is shown in Fig. 3. The guidance was operationalized by leveraging the existing hierarchical structure of the decision model. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, where multiple underlying factors contributed to an interim judgment within the decision model, the system asked users to make an interim judgment, and provided suggestive guidance for that interim judgment. After extensive modeling exercises, an additive model was found to be the most effective way of generating suggestive guidance.

## 4.2.2. Informative guidance design

Informative guidance in the form of de<sup>fi</sup>nitional text was embedded into precursor question screens. An example screenshot containing informative guidance is shown in Fig. 4. The informative guidance text was constructed by the author with advice from researchers involved with the INSOLVE project [3,11,30].

## 4.2.3. Recommendation report

InsolveDG produces a recommendation at the exit point of the decision process, as shown in the example recommendation report contained in Fig. 5. This exit point is reached when InsolveDG has gathered enough responses to be able to make a recommendation, as shown in Fig. 2, which depicts the decision model.

## 4.2.4. Testing and validating InsolveDG

The software functionality was extensively tested, including a full pilot test, prior to use in the laboratory sessions. All new text content also underwent a validation process, which involved sending copies of the text and decision model to three expert insolvency practitioners, each of whom had in excess of 10 years experience in senior insolvency roles. These expert practitioners reviewed the materials and con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that all content re<sup>fl</sup>ected current commercial practice and language. After allowing preparation time the author conducted semi-structured phone interviews with the three experts to discuss the materials. As a result of this process proves some minor content changes were made. The decision model in InsolveDG was also validated by three expert insolvency practitioners who indicated that the model accorded with their ‘real world’ view of the insolvency decision-making process. The overall feedback from the practitioners was that the materials were comprehensive and realistic in both form and content. In addition close to 90% of subjects involved in the research indicated that they would use the aid if it were made available in their workplace (see instrument in Appendix 1 for details), indicating that InsolveDG had a high level of face validity.

## 4.3. Case studies

The experimental task was a judgment task requiring subjects to make an insolvency related trade on/liquidate decision. In order to support the task complexity operationalization two case studies were developed. Much prior research exploring task complexity uses a single case study crafted into two versions with one version more complex than the other [see for example 24,37]. In those designs, each participant is required to complete only one case study. The research design in this paper uses two case studies with differing levels of complexity; every participant completed both case studies. The within subjects design improves the power of the statistical analyses conducted and reduces error due to individual variability.

The companies were both portrayed as operating in high tech industries with a new product identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being currently in the <sup>fi</sup>nal stages of development. Standard sub-headings were developed which grouped information cues along similar lines to the questions in InsolveDG. The intention of grouping cues identically and using similar business contexts was to reduce the possibility of confounding the task complexity manipulation. Presenting a standardized content structure and business context helped ensure task complexity variance was related solely to the cue engineering undertaken.

Task complexity variability was achieved by employing a theory driven approach [52] to engineer cues within the cases. The cue engineering altered both the number of information cues provided to participants (component complexity), and the interdependencies between the number of types of information provided (coordinative complexity) [52]. Examples of information cues embedded in the case studies and the question they relate to are contained in Table 2.

## 4.3.1. The less complex case

The less complex case was originally developed by King [28] and later adapted for use in research by Arnold et al. [1,2,4]. The author revised the narrative to accommodate the task complexity manipulation and re<sup>fl</sup>ect contemporary dates, products and technology. The seven pages of the less complex case contain 22 purposely-embedded cues (component complexity), written in deliberately unambiguous language such as “there are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient funds”; or “You are con<sup>fi</sup>dent your fees will be paid”. None of the cues in the less complex case contains contradictory or interdependent statements (coordinative complexity).

## 4.3.2. The more complex case

The more complex case contains appendix materials collected by Leech et al. [32]. An 18 page case narrative based on these materials was developed and written by the author. Case development included identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and insertion of appropriate information cues for the task complexity manipulation. Where necessary additional original material was devised by the author. The more complex case contains 37 purposely embedded cues (component complexity), with nearly half of these cues (17 or 48%) using ambiguous language such as “however this possibility cannot be de<sup>fi</sup>nitively ruled out” or “it is anticipated that”. Interdependencies between cues were created in the more complex case by connecting sets of related information cues. As an example the subject needed to concurrently consider two cues; “there doesn't currently seem to be a risk” and “if a new product is launched the risk pro<sup>fi</sup>le for the products will change” before they could answer the question “Is there a greater than acceptable risk of liability for the practitioner?”

## 4.3.3. Validation of the case studies

The face validity and realism of the case studies was validated by the same expert practitioners who validated the InsolveDG materials described in Section 4.2.4. The experts read the cases and provided feedback on content and style; they also supplied answers to the questions in the InsolveDG decision model for each case. All three con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the content of the cases was both reasonable and realistic. The completed question sets indicated that the cues embedded within the case were operating as expected. The recommendations of the expert practitioners for these cases became the expert opinions used. All three of the experts advised that they would recommend liquidation for the more complex case, and trading on for the less complex case.

## 4.4. Subjects

The author recruited subject with differing levels of task expertise speci<sup>fi</sup>cally avoiding “experts” for several reasons. Firstly, both novice and experienced decision-makers are common in any population whereas, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, real experts are rare [6]. Consequently, the subjects selected encompass a broad population. Secondly, experts have less need for decision support as their task expertise is already well developed. Finally, while numerous studies have contrasted novice and expert behavior, experienced practitioners have been neglected in prior studies. It is likely that their behaviors are likely to differ from both novices and experts, rather than simply being the “middle ground” between these two ends of the task expertise continuum.

These consisted of experienced practitioners currently employed in an insolvency related role and novices who were <sup>fi</sup>nal year students undertaking an accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance degree. Novices were recruited by means of an announcement at the commencement of lectures in an advanced <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement analysis subject. The author was not involved in teaching or assessing the classes from which students were recruited. Recruitment was timed so that the students had just completed a directly relevant section of the subject, which covered the formation of going concern opinions. Experienced practitioners were also directly recruited by the author. Several open sessions were held under the auspices of the Insolvency Practitioners Association of Australia (IPAA) that attracted practitioners from a number of their member insolvency <sup>fi</sup>rms. Practitioners who attended these sessions were later contacted by the author and asked if their <sup>fi</sup>rm would like to participate in an in-house session. Firms involved in the in-house sessions were mostly medium to large in size, whereas subjects at the IPAA sessions came from <sup>fi</sup>rms ranging in size from sole practitioner practices to large internationals. Individuals and <sup>fi</sup>rms were incentivized to participate by offering a debrief session comparing their decision-making processes and outcomes with those developed from a panel of expert practitioners. The debrief session was generic for the IPAA open sessions and customized to suit individual <sup>fi</sup>rm requirements for the in-house sessions.

## 4.5. Operationalization of the task, individual and technology characteristics

The manipulations applied to task, technology, and individual dif ferences are summarized in Table 3 and explained in detail in the remainder of this section.

Task complexity was operationalized as described in Section 4.3. To ensure that the task complexity manipulation performed as expected subjects were asked to assess the perceived complexity of each of the cases using a seven point Likert scale, as shown in Appendix 1. The less complex task recorded a mean perceived complexity of 2.99; the mean of the more complex task was 4.90. A repeated measure ANOVA showed that perceived task complexity differed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between the two cases (pb.001), indicating that subjects correctly detected the task complexity manipulation. A one-way ANOVA showed that unaided performance of subjects also varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between the two cases (pb.000). 89% of individuals were able to produce an unaided recommendation that agreed with the expert opinion for the less complex task, this dropped to 61% for the more complex task. The more complex task was correctly perceived as more complex than the less complex task. Unaided performance on the less complex task was better than that on the more complex task. These results con<sup>fi</sup>rm the success of the task complexity manipulation.

## 4.6. Operationalization of TTF, ITeF and TaIF

Operationalization of the three dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF, TaIF and TTF) is shown in Table 4; details of ‘good’ and ‘poor’ <sup>fi</sup>t were previously discussed in Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3. As an example of <sup>fi</sup>t, a more complex task is a poor <sup>fi</sup>t to a novice user (as they have insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient task expertise to complete the task well), and to informative decisional guidance (as additional cues increase the risk of information overload). ## 4.7. Other measures

## 4.7.1. User attitude — perceived usefulness

Users' perceptions of the usefulness of the technology were measured immediately after subjects completed each of the two tasks. The standard six item scale [15] was adapted to suit the context as shown in the questionnaire instrument contained in Appendix 1. Consistent with prior research, scale reliability using Cronbach's alpha was very high, returning values of .96 for the less complex task, and .95 for the more complex task. Descriptive statistics show that perceptions of usefulness varied considerably, a minimum value of 1.0 and a maximum of 6.8 was recorded on a scale of 7. Additional ANOVA analysis indicated that perceptions of usefulness varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly (p.012) between subjects with novices perceiving InsolveDG as being more useful to them than experienced practitioners. A repeated measure ANOVA con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that perceptions of usefulness did not vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly between treatments indicating that the perceived usefulness variable was capturing user attitude towards the technology and con<sup>fi</sup>rming the appropriateness of the within subject research design chosen.

## 4.7.2. Technology performance — recommendation quality

The recommendation produced by the aid provided a short report (discussed in Section 4.2.3) containing information about the decision logic employed and advice to either trade on, or liquidate. The user interface for InsolveDG contains data logging functionality to monitor and record user keystrokes, including identifying which recommendation was delivered to each user for each task. Data was extracted from the session logs and coded using a value of zero where the recommendation differed from the expert opinion and one where the recommendation aligned with the expert opinion. This variable is used to measure technology performance.

## 4.7.3. Task performance — decision quality

A decision outcome was recorded after the subject used InsolveDG. Subjects were asked whether they would liquidate the business or trade on, as per the questionnaire instrument in Appendix 1. Decisions were coded “0” where they differed from the expert opinion and “1” where they aligned with the expert opinion. This post-use decision is referred to as an “aided” decision and is used to measure task performance.

## 4.7.4. Control variable — unaided performance

Technology performance and task performance are potentially prejudiced by the a priori expectations of the decision-maker. Given the desire to isolate the effect of <sup>fi</sup>t on attitude, it is helpful to be able to differentiate whether and how subjects' decisions were impacted by their innate belief related to the ‘correct’ outcome for the case. An initial decision was recorded after the subject worked through the case unaided i.e. before using InsolveDG. The decision taken prior to using InsolveDG is referred to as the “unaided” decision, it was recorded and coded in the same way as the aided decision described in Section 4.7.3 and was controlled for in all analyses.

## 4.7.5. Control variable — decision agreement

Liu et al. [34] note that where a DSS recommendation contradicts a user's unaided decision, that user will likely perceive the DSS as being less helpful, confounding the perception of usefulness measure. In addition, the user is less likely to rely on the recommendation, reducing the likelihood that a relationship between technology performance and task performance will be observed. By reference to keystroke logs and questionnaire answers I established whether the recommendation generated by InsolveDG was consistent with the users' unaided decision, then coded this decision agreement variable as “0” where the recommendation aligned with the users' unaided decision, and “1” where the recommendation differed. To eliminate any potential confounding effect on user attitude and performance, decision agreement was controlled for in all analyses.

## 4.8. Experiment design and procedure

Hypotheses were tested in a laboratory based experimental setting, using a 2 (novice vs. experienced practitioner)×2 (informative vs. suggestive guidance)×2 (more complex vs. less complex task) research design. Subjects were recruited and incentivized as described in detail in Section 4.4 and randomly assigned to one of the two versions of InsolveDG, as shown in Table 5. As every subject completed both cases, 94 subjects and 188 treatments are reported.

The descriptive statistics shown in Table 6 were collected using the questionnaire contained in Appendix 1. Novice subjects were on average younger than experienced practitioners were and had less work experience overall. Every experienced practitioners had insolvency related work experience (average around 3.5 years) establishing these subjects as experienced, but not expert [20]. Experienced practitioners had been involved in an average of 30 cases each, but had been the primary decision-maker on less than 5 of those cases; indicating that they were at a career stage where they were not experts leading insolvency work teams.

On entering the laboratory each subject was supplied with paper based experimental materials and asked to complete the demographics section of the questionnaire contained in Appendix 1. They were shown a demonstration of the DSS, and then asked to complete a short tutorial case. The subjects opened the <sup>fi</sup>rst envelope, considered the <sup>fi</sup>rst case and recorded an unaided decision. Subjects used InsolveDG and then recorded a second (aided) decision. All subjects completed both cases in the same way, recording the perceived usefulness items immediately after <sup>fi</sup>nalizing each of the cases, and the manipulation and face validity questions (discussed in Sections 4.5 and 4.2.4) after completing both cases. The order in which the cases were attempted was varied to account for any order/recency effects [25]. Just over half (52%) of the subjects received the more complex task <sup>fi</sup>rst, and the less complex task second; other subjects received the cases in the reverse order. 12 experimental sessions were conducted over 3 months; all experimental sessions used identical scripts and procedures. Sensitivity analysis revealed that neither the lab session attended nor the order in which cases were attempted signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected any of the variables of interest.

## 5. Results

All hypotheses were tested using ANCOVA/MANCOVA techniques to appropriately identify variations between groups [47]. Separate MANCOVA analyses were conducted to test Hypotheses 1–3. The detailed results contained in Table 7 support all three hypothesized relationships. As expected, <sup>fi</sup>t between the technology and the user (ITeF) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects user attitude towards the technology, after controlling for their unaided performance and decision agreement. Support for Hypothesis1 shows that <sup>fi</sup>tting the technology design to the individual (ITeF) affects user attitude. Support for Hypothesis 2 shows that <sup>fi</sup>tting the task to the individual (TaIF) affects technology performance. Support for Hypothesis 3 shows that <sup>fi</sup>tting the task to the technology (TTF) also affects technology performance. An additional ANCOVA analysis reported in Table 8 shows that user attitude is not affected by either TaIF or TTF, and that task performance is not affected by any form of <sup>fi</sup>t.

The results in Fig. 6 depict whether different dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t affect attitude and performance, the descriptive statistics in Table 7 establish how the <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions affect attitude and performance.

Finding support for Hypothesis 2 and Hypothesis 3 shows that when the individual <sup>fi</sup>ts the task (TaIF); and when the technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the task (TTF), technology performance improves, but not task performance or user attitude. An additional ANCOVA analysis reported in Table 9 also <sup>fi</sup>nds no direct effect on task performance for any of the three forms of <sup>fi</sup>t. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are important and interesting as they speak to a basic DSS dilemma, whether technology should <sup>fi</sup>t the user or the task being performed. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that <sup>fi</sup>tting technology to a task improves technology performance; <sup>fi</sup>tting technology to an individual changes attitude.

An ANCOVA analysis was used to test Hypothesis 4, the results shown in Table 10 support the hypothesis. User attitude and technology performance directly and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect task performance after controlling for the effect of unaided performance and decision agreement. In addition, the interaction between technology performance and user attitude also signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects task performance.

For completeness, I retested Hypothesis 4 while controlling for all three forms of <sup>fi</sup>t. The results obtained showed that the direct effects of attitude and technology performance on task performance were unchanged (p=.004 and p=.000 respectively) and con<sup>fi</sup>rm that no form of <sup>fi</sup>t directly affected task performance. The robustness of the results obtained was further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by a one-way ANOVA which tested the effect of perceptions of usefulness on the unaided decision (no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result), technology performance (p=.009) and task performance (no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant result).

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

## 6.1. Fit and user attitude

When a technology is designed to <sup>fi</sup>t the level of an individual's expertise (ITeF), the individual's attitude towards the technology is directly affected. By contrast, when a technology is designed to <sup>fi</sup>t the complexity of a task, technology performance is directly affected. These results are important as they reinforce the desirability of <sup>fi</sup>tting technology design to a task rather than an individual where a DSS is intended to improve performance rather than attitude.

The descriptive statistics graphed in Fig. 7 demonstrate that perceptions of usefulness are lower when <sup>fi</sup>t is good, and higher when <sup>fi</sup>t is poor. As shown in Table 7 the technology was perceived as most useful where the technology design was poorly <sup>fi</sup>tted to the expertise of the individual (ITeF). This result speaks to the point made earlier in relation to the nature of the relationship between perceptions of usefulness and performance. Poor individual–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (ITeF) occurred when novice users were provided with additional information cues. While they perceived the technology as being more useful, it did not directly create better performance outcomes, indicating that the reality of their performance did not match their perceptions about the DSS. Users thought the DSS was more useful than it actually was. Good ITeF occurred when experienced practitioners were provided with pattern cues, these subjects perceived the technology as being less useful, possibly as they felt that the pattern cues provided replicated pattern knowledge they already possessed.

## 6.2. Fit and technology performance

Task–technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) and task–individual <sup>fi</sup>t (TaIF) both affect the quality of the recommendation generated, providing evidence of a substitution effect occurring in relation to task expertise. A DSS improves performance by <sup>fi</sup>lling the gap between the expertise of the user, and the expertise required to complete the task well. In the case of good TaIF, that expertise is contributed by the individual, while in the case of good TTF that expertise is being contributed by the DSS.

## 6.3. User attitude, technology performance and task performance

## 6.3.1. User attitude and task performance

Task performance was directly and positively affected by the user attitude towards the technology and technology performance. When the technology was perceived as being useful, task performance improved. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is interesting as it highlights the importance of fostering reliance on the technology. Recommendations that are not relied on cannot and do not affect task performance. Improving performance as a result of DSS use requires that a high quality recommendation is produced initially, and that the user relies on the recommendation. When the DSS was perceived as being more useful, reliance on the recommendation increased, and task performance improved. The interaction between the perceived usefulness of the technology and the quality of the recommendation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively affected task performance, indicating strongly the interrelated nature of technology performance and user attitude.

## 6.3.2. Technology performance and task performance

The evidence shows that the quality of the recommendation generated directly and positively improved task performance for most forms of <sup>fi</sup>t (see Section 6.3.3 for a further discussion about <sup>fi</sup>t and declining task performance). Where technology performance was high task performance was also high, providing strong evidence that technology performance translates into task performance. Importantly poor quality recommendations also translated into poor quality decisions, albeit at a lower rate. Within the 144 treatments that achieved a high quality decision, reliance levels were quite high. 90% of subjects had generated a high quality recommendation and relied on it, the remaining 10% had correctly ignored a poor quality recommendation. By comparison, for the 44 treatments where a poor quality decision was generated reliance was lower. 57% of this group had inappropriately relied on a poor quality recommendation and the remaining 43% generated a high quality recommendation but ignored it.

## 6.3.3. Fit and task performance

Fit affects task performance indirectly through user attitude and technology performance. Returning to consider the Table 9 statistics, task performance was best overall where task complexity suited the expertise of the individual (TaIF). This result is driven by the task experience of the user as these subjects recorded the best unaided performance. Interestingly, for this group, use of the DSS harmed performance slightly overall, comparing their unaided to <sup>fi</sup>nal task performance. When TaIF was good the quality of the recommendations generated was lower than unaided decision quality, and some users relied inappropriately on the recommendation resulting in a drop in task performance (as shown in Fig. 8).

A second group where performance declined was where experienced practitioners were supplied with suggestive guidance. In this group, the quality of the recommendations provided was superior to the unaided decisions, but the experienced practitioners were unwilling to accept the support offered and actually moved away from the recommendations provided, to an extent which damaged their overall decision performance, a phenomenon which has been previously described as “shifting away” behavior [8].

Task performance was lowest overall (57%) under TTF when informative guidance was provided for a more complex task. TTF also provoked the worst technology performance (68%), showing that where TTF is poor, both task and technology performance can be affected. Despite these negative outcomes overall the DSS still improved task performance overall, only 53% of this group produced a high quality unaided decision.

Overall, the results demonstrate three important points. First, user attitude is affected by the <sup>fi</sup>t between individual and technology whereas technology performance is affected by the <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology, and task and individual. This highlights the importance of designing technology to <sup>fi</sup>t the task being undertaken. Second, achieving good <sup>fi</sup>t between individual and technology does not necessarily positively affect task performance as users of technology can perceive that technology as being more useful than it actually is. Third, technology performance translates into task performance. Where technology performance was high, task performance was also high.

## 6.4. Limitations

The study has several limitations. Data was collected in a laboratory based experimental setting, which maximizes environmental control but introduces limitations in terms of the richness of the experience for subjects. Because of this, behavior of subjects in a real world setting may differ. The experimental session and data recorded identify only short-term effects; a longitudinal study may result in different outcomes. Given the speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem domain, generalizability of the results may be limited, although these results will generalize to any unstructured, non-normative judgment task. There is also a possibility that the results obtained may relate only to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c task and software artifacts in use.

## 6.5. Conclusion

The paper investigated how <sup>fi</sup>t affect user attitude and performance. The results show that the <sup>fi</sup>t between individual and technology affects user attitude; <sup>fi</sup>t between task and individual, and between task and technology affects technology performance. User attitude and technology performance in turn affect task performance. The richness of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings is made possible by employing three two-way <sup>fi</sup>t dimensions, which make it possible to observe the effects of user variance from differing perspectives.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in relation to <sup>fi</sup>tting the technology to the task and user are of great importance. When a DSS is designed to <sup>fi</sup>t a task technology performance and consequentially task performance is affected. When a DSS artifact is designed to <sup>fi</sup>t an individual attitude towards the technology and consequentially task performance is affected. These results demonstrate that all three dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>t must be considered concurrently to form a comprehensible picture of how DSS use translates into task performance. Task performance outcomes vary based on whether users accept the support offered by the DSS. The thoughtful design of decisional guidance is integral to improving task performance via reliance on technology performance. The practical implications drawn from this study relate to opportunities to improve DSS design. These results show that task performance is a function of how the technology is designed and used; and of whether the user accepts and incorporates recommendation received from the DSS. DSS use is a necessary, but not suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient condition, to improve task performance.
