# PLATFORM COMPATIBILITY AND DEVELOPER MULTIHOMING: A TRADE-OFF PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Jing Tian Center for Digital Innovation, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {jtian9@gsu.edu}

Xia Zhao Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia Athens, GA, U.S.A. {xia.zhao@uga.edu}

Ling Xue Department of Computer Information Systems, Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {lxue5@gsu.edu}

Digital platform ecosystems feature interplatform competition. A potential strategy for a platform with market disadvantage is to make itself compatible with competitors to attract multihoming of developers. However, many questions remain unresolved regarding how a platform’s compatibility strategy impacts developers’ multihoming decisions and subsequent app performance. In this study, we use the context of web browser platforms (i.e., Firefox and Chrome) to investigate how the increase in the technological compatibility of a secondary platform (Firefox) influences developer multihoming from a primary dominant platform (Chrome). We theoretically consider how increased platform compatibility influences the trade-offs in developers’ multihoming decisions and their subsequent app development choices. Based on this trade-off perspective, we posit and empirically investigate the effects of increased platform compatibility on developer multihoming, and the subsequent development choices and app performance resulting from multihoming. Our findings illustrate that developers with larger user bases on the primary platform are more willing to multihome than those with smaller bases, but increased platform compatibility reduces this gap and enables developers with smaller user bases to better embrace multihoming. Increased platform compatibility also encourages multihoming developers to develop exclusive apps for the secondary platform. Moreover, increased platform compatibility facilitates multihoming developers to leverage their primary-platform user bases to enhance their secondaryplatform app performance, and this effect is influenced by the development type (porting or exclusive development). Our study generates unique insights that add to the theoretical understanding of platform compatibility, multihoming, and platform competition

Keywords: Platform compatibility, two-sided markets, network externalities, multihoming, exclusive app development, platform competition

## Introduction

A technological platform is an extensible technological foundation that can be extended or augmented by third-party complements (McIntyre & Srinivasan, 2017; Tiwana, 2008, 2018). As a type of two-sided market, technological platforms create values by facilitating interactions between complement providers and users (Rochet & Tirole, 2006; Song et al., 2018). Platform competition is a crucial issue studied extensively in the extant literature (e.g., Cennamo & Santalo, 2013; Katz & Shapiro, 1985; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005; Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Zhu & Liu, 2018). Competition between technological platforms is influenced by many factors, such as pricing structures (Armstrong, 2006; Caillaud & Jullien, 2003; Hagiu, 2009; Katz & Shapiro, 1985; Lin et al., 2011; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2005; Seamans & Zhu, 2014), platform governance (Rochet & Tirole, 2003), and app exclusivity (Cennamo & Santalo, 2013; Lee, 2013), etc. Platform compatibility has also been recognized as a vital competitive strategy (Adner et al., 2020; Katz & Shapiro, 1985). It is particularly critical for nondominant platforms to compete with market leaders in acquiring users and developers. A platform in a disadvantageous competitive position (referred to as secondary platform hereafter) will be more motivated to make itself technologically compatible with its dominant competitor (referred to as primary platform hereafter) in hopes of attracting third-party complement providers from the latter to multihome and contribute to its own ecosystem.

While the main motivation of the compatibility strategy adopted by a secondary platform is comprehensible (Casadesus-Masanell & Ruiz-Aliseda, 2008; Viecens, 2011), many questions remain unresolved and are deserving of in-depth investigation, especially from an empirical standpoint. In this research, we specifically focus on three questions: (1) How does a secondary platform’s compatibility strategy help attract developers multihoming from a primary platform? (2) How does platform compatibility influence the app development choices of multihoming developers on the secondary platform? (3) How does platform compatibility influence multihoming developers’ cross-platform performance?

In addressing the first research question, our objective is to develop a more granular insight into the use of platform compatibility to attract developers from competing platforms. We take a trade-off perspective and consider developers multihoming choice as weighing market expansion opportunities against multihoming costs (Armstrong & Wright, 2007; Bresnahan et al., 2015; Corts & Lederman, 2009). We consider how a key market factor, i.e., a developer’s user base on the primary platform, and a key technological factor, i.e., platform compatibility, both influence the trade-off in developers’ multihoming decisions. In addition to their individual effects, we also examine how the interaction of these factors affects multihoming decisions.

Our second research question focuses on the development choices of multihoming developers, which have been underinvestigated in the literature. Multihoming developers also face an important trade-off between the market gain from developing exclusive and distinct apps on the secondary platform and the cost burden of such exclusive development. Market factors characterizing the secondary platform typically shape the market gain from app development. Therefore, we focus on examining how key market factors, including multihoming developers’ user bases and the intensity of app competition on the secondary platform, and a key technological factor, i.e., platform compatibility, influence the exclusive app development of multihoming developers on the secondary platform.

The third research question considers the market performance of multihoming developers’ apps on the secondary platform, as better development performance is desirable for both developers and the secondary platform. To ensure the relevance of our consideration to the cross-platform context, we specifically focus on how the market performance of a multihoming developer’s app on the secondary platform is related to the developer’s performance on the primary platform, and how platform compatibility influences this relationship. In addition, we also explore how the impact of platform compatibility is contingent on app development choice (i.e., app porting versus exclusive development).

Our empirical investigation uses the setting of two top web browser platforms―Google Chrome and Mozilla’s Firefox. In March 2016, Firefox (the secondary platform in this context) released a new generation of API, i.e., WebExtensions API, which is technologically more compatible with the app development specifications on Chrome (the primary platform). Such increased platform compatibility was expected to attract Chome developers to develop apps for Firefox. We collected data from the Chrome Web Store and the Firefox Add-ons Store over a period from January 2017 to June 2018 and developed an approach to trace developers and apps back to as early as December 2010. Our analysis sample comprises 42,150 Chrome apps (so-called Chrome Extensions) developed by 27,732 unique developers and 9,706 Firefox apps (so-called Firefox Add-ons) developed by 5,686 unique developers.

Our empirical analysis generates three sets of findings with important theoretical contributions and managerial implications. First, we find that when motivating Chrome developers to multihome onto Firefox, increased platform compatibility has a greater effect on developers with smaller user bases on Chrome than those with larger user bases. This finding confirms that increased platform compatibility has heterogeneous impacts on developers and allows the secondary platform to recruit more diverse developers, rather than just attract well-performed developers from the primary platform. Second, we find that increased platform compatibility motivates multihoming developers with smaller user bases and those facing more intense competition on Firefox to engage in exclusive development for Firefox. The theoretical implication is that increased platform compatibility mitigates the market barriers to developing exclusive apps and therefore encourages more innovations on the secondary platform. Third, we find that increased platform compatibility strengthens the positive relationship between multihoming developers’ overall performance on Chrome and their individual app performance on Firefox. However, this reinforcing effect is weaker for exclusive Firefox apps. A key implication of these findings is that platform compatibility not only allows well-performed developers to leverage their existing market advantages in porting their successful apps, but also enables disadvantaged developers to achieve growth through expansion on the secondary platform.

## Theoretical and Hypothesis Development

## Developer Multihoming and App Multihoming

On technological platforms, the general concept of “multihoming” can unfold in different forms, including user multihoming, developer multihoming, and app multihoming. User multihoming refers to the use of multiple platforms (such as different Web browsers and game consoles) by individual users (Rochet & Tirole, 2003). Developer multihoming refers to the choice of third-party developers to develop complements for multiple different platforms (Corts & Lederman, 2009; Landsman & Stremersch, 2011). App multihoming occurs when an app is available on multiple platforms (Landsman & Stremersch, 2011). It is worth noting that typically an app produced for a given platform cannot be directly installed and run on other platforms; app developers often release distinct versions of the same apps for different platforms. App porting refers to the practice of modifying an existing app on one platform according to the technical specifications of another platform so that the app can run on the latter (Cennamo & Santalo, 2013). In this case, the modified app is a multihoming app, and the corresponding developer is a multihoming developer. However, developer multihoming is not equivalent to app multihoming because a multihoming developer can provide distinct and exclusive apps for different platforms. User multihoming is related to the user side of the platform, and developer multihoming and app multihoming are related to the supply side of the platform. In this study, we focus on supplyside strategies, including both developer multihoming and app multihoming, rather than user multihoming.

## Multihoming Decision: A Trade-off Perspective

The extant platform research has primarily adopted a trade-off perspective to understand the multihoming decision of platform participants (e.g., Armstrong & Wright, 2007; Corts & Lederman, 2009; Landsman & Stremersch, 2011; Rochet & Tirole, 2003). From this perspective, the multihoming decision is a trade-off process between the benefits of market expansion and the technical and operational costs of developing apps for multiple platforms (Cennamo et al., 2018; Corts & Lederman, 2009; Eisenmann et al., 2006). When the benefits of market expansion outweigh the overall cost of porting existing apps or initiating new development beyond their primary platforms, developers will find multihoming to be a desirable strategy.

In this study, we develop a unified framework that incorporates both market factors, i.e., the developer’s user base and app competition, and a key technological factor, i.e., technological compatibility. The primary motivation for focusing on these factors is that they are highly relevant to the decision-making of individual developers in multihoming. Still, they have been underexamined in the extant research. The multihoming literature has largely studied the platformlevel user base to capture network effects (e.g., Bresnahan et al., 2015; Corts & Lederman, 2009; Kaiser & Wright, 2006; Nair et al., 2004; Rysman, 2004), and the consideration of the developer-level user base is relatively limited. App competition, which is an influencing factor for developer decisions, has been primarily examined in the context of intraplatform competition rather than interplatform multihoming (Boudreau, 2012; Boudreau & Jeppesen, 2015; Rietveld & Eggers, 2018; Tiwana, 2015; Yin et al., 2014).

Our theory focuses on how the market and technological factors influence market expansion opportunities and multihoming costs, i.e., the trade-offs of multihoming. We consider both the individual effects of market and technological factors as well as their interaction. In addition to the developers’ multihoming decisions, we examine multihoming developers’ exclusive development choices and their app performance on the secondary platform. It is worth noting that multihoming developers can also contribute exclusive apps to their participating platforms (Landsman & Stremersch, 2011). Therefore, exclusive development can be part of a multihoming strategy, rather than an alternative option to app multihoming as the prior literature has suggested (Corts & Lederman, 2009; Landsman & Stremersch, 2011).

## User Base, Technological Compatibility, and Multihoming Decisions

In multihoming, developers encounter the uncertainty associated with market expansion. First, developers on the primary platform may have insufficient knowledge about user preferences on the secondary platform, and therefore their expected market gains from multihoming may be inaccurate (Chintakananda & McIntyre, 2014; Kim, 2008). Second, the heterogeneity between platforms may prevent developers from effectively exploiting the economics of scale in market expansion across platforms (Eisenmann et al., 2006; Hagiu & Yoffie, 2009).

To cope with the market expansion uncertainty, developers can leverage their existing user bases as an important resource (Kalish et al., 1995; Krijestorac et al., 2020; Putsis et al., 1997). The network effect generated from their existing user bases on the primary platform facilitates developers’ attraction of new users in the new market on the secondary platform (Kalish et al., 1995; Krijestorac et al., 2020). With large user bases on the primary platform, developers can also learn how to deal with various market situations and dynamics. Such a learning effect enables developers to effectively handle possible market complexity on the secondary platform. Therefore, holding the technical costs of multihoming constant, multihoming would be more appealing to developers with larger user bases on the primary platform because their benefits from market expansion are more likely to outweigh the technical costs.

The reduction in technical costs would reshape the trade-off in multihoming decisions. Technical costs in multihoming decrease when the technological compatibility between the primary and the secondary platforms increases (Cennamo et al., 2018; Ham & Park, 2011). First, technological compatibility reduces developers’ learning costs as it provides a more familiar infrastructural environment on the secondary platform for multihoming developers. Second, developers incur lower adjustment costs associated with porting their existing apps from the primary platform to the secondary platform. With more compatible technological specifications, developers can migrate more existing modules to the secondary platform without dramatically modifying the source codes. Third, developers also encounter lower development costs related to building new apps on the secondary platform because increased technological compatibility allows them to more effectively leverage their acquired knowledge on the primary platform. Fourth, by reducing cross-platform heterogeneity, technological compatibility can reduce the disparity in app quality across platforms (Cennamo et al., 2018). Multihoming developers can avoid incurring additional costs to address the app quality deterioration on the secondary platform.

Increased platform compatibility also helps developers cope with market expansion uncertainty. This benefit is particularly prominent for developers with smaller user bases. This is mainly because, as mentioned above, these developers are less capable of leveraging their existing user bases to resolve market expansion uncertainty and cover high technical costs in multihoming. With higher technological compatibility, developers can better understand the market on the secondary platform and use their existing development skills to serve users on the secondary platform. Therefore, the learning effect and the network effect facilitated by large primary-platform user bases would become less crucial in multihoming decisions. In addition, increased platform compatibility lowers technical costs, which helps conserve resources for developers to address market expansion uncertainty. Developers with smaller user bases are more resource constrained and therefore would benefit more from resource availability. As a result, increased technological compatibility would have a higher motivating effect on the multihoming decisions of developers with smaller user bases on the primary platform. In this way, the positive relationship between the primary-platform user base and the multihoming willingness becomes less salient.

Taken together, we expect that developers with larger user bases on their primary platform are more likely to multihome because their user bases assist them in addressing market expansion uncertainty. However, increased platform compatibility weakens this positive effect of user bases on multihoming.

H1: Developers with larger user bases on the primary platform are more likely to multihome onto the secondary platform. Increased platform compatibility weakens the positive relationship between the developer’s primary platform user base and the likelihood of developer multihoming.

## Exclusive App Development Decisions

As part of their multihoming activities, developers may choose to develop exclusive new apps for the secondary platform (i.e., developer multihoming while not app multihoming) (Corts & Lederman, 2009; Landsman & Stremersch, 2011). Since exclusive apps contribute to platform differentiation and help platforms avoid head-tohead competition (Corts & Lederman, 2009; Landsman & Stremersch, 2011), they are desirable from the secondary platform’s standpoint. Prior studies show that entrants in the networked industry leverage exclusive software to gain market positions in competition (Lee, 2013). Because exclusive apps are developed only for the secondary platform, the market environment on the secondary platform is more relevant for this development decision than that on the primary platform. Developers often encounter a trade-off between market gain and development cost (Landsman & Stremersch, 2011). On the one hand, developers can better cater to users on the secondary platform by developing apps that exclusively fit the latter’s demand. On the other hand, developing exclusive apps separately for the secondary platform means that developers would bear the extra burden of development efforts. Such a trade-off is influenced by market factors (such as user bases and app competition on the secondary platform), technology factors (such as technological compatibility), and their interactions.

Multihoming developers’ user bases on the second platform influence their decisions regarding exclusive development. Developers may engage in exclusive development before establishing large user bases on the secondary platform. However, such a strategy is risky because there would be weak network effects and limited word of mouth to leverage on the secondary platform (Corts & Lederman, 2009; Krijestorac et al., 2020; Verhoef & Donkers, 2005) and little local experience to guide the expansion of their markets. In contrast, developers who have already accumulated substantive user bases on the secondary platform would have a better lever in local marketing and more experience to build upon (Corts & Lederman, 2009). In this regard, multihoming developers with larger user bases on the secondary platform are more likely to engage in developing exclusive apps for the secondary platform because the benefits of doing so are more likely to outweigh the development costs.

Platform compatibility can influence the trade-off in the developers’ decision of exclusive development by reducing technical costs (Ham & Park, 2011) and alleviating market uncertainty, which is more of a concern for developers with limited user bases on the secondary platform. Increased platform compatibility enables multihoming developers to readily reuse their expertise acquired from the primary platform to build new apps for the secondary platform. Developers bear a lower cost burden of learning about the secondary platform’s architecture, adjusting their skills, and overcoming the development barriers across platforms. As a result, multihoming developers would not have to wait until they have accumulated sufficient user bases on the secondary platform to make exclusive development worth the costs. Developers with limited user bases on the secondary platform are more likely to pursue exclusive development to expand their markets. The impact of developers’ secondary-platform user bases on the decision of exclusive app development can become weaker. We therefore hypothesize:

H2: Multihoming developers with larger user bases on the secondary platform are more likely to develop exclusive apps for the secondary platform. And increased platform compatibility weakens the positive relationship between the developer’s secondary-platform user base and the likelihood of exclusive app development.

As an important trade-off factor, app competition on the secondary platform discourages exclusive app development for the secondary platform. When multihoming developers encounter many competing apps on the secondary platform, their space for market expansion and revenue gains would be eroded (Church & Gandal, 1992; Corts & Lederman, 2009; Koh & Fichman, 2014; Tiwana, 2015; Tucker & Zhang, 2010). In this case, developers would likely choose a less costly strategy of porting their existing apps to the secondary platform, as this strategy is more justifiable considering the limited expected gains from market expansion (Corts & Lederman, 2009). Therefore, exclusive app development is less likely to occur in app categories with intense competition on the secondary platform.

Platform compatibility can also influence this trade-off in the multihoming developers’ app development choices. By mitigating the technological distinction between platforms, increased compatibility can effectively reduce the overall costs associated with exclusive app development (e.g., costs of learning and skill adaptation) (Ham & Park, 2011). As a result, the strategy of entering a competitive app category on the secondary platform would become more justifiable. Moreover, when increased platform compatibility reduces the cost burden of learning and development adaptation, multihoming developers can devote more effort and resources to promoting their exclusive apps on the secondary platform. Therefore, developers can better cope with app competition and become more willing to enter competitive app categories on the secondary platform. We therefore hypothesize:

H3: Multihoming developers are less likely to develop exclusive apps for the secondary platform when app competition on the secondary platform is more intense. And increased platform compatibility weakens the negative relationship between app competition and the likelihood of exclusive app development.

## App Performance on Secondary Platform

As theorized before, we expect that multihoming developers utilize their primary-platform user bases as a resource to cope with the uncertainty of market expansion on the secondary platform. This logic also suggests that multihoming developers’ market performance is positively correlated across platforms. Developers with more outstanding performance on the primary platform should have relatively more resources and experience to apply to the development on the secondary platform (Moorman & Miner, 1997). For example, they have more modules and code packages to reuse for innovation (Zhou, 2020), and they can also apply successful software development principles and practices to the development of secondary-platform apps. In this regard, for multihoming developers with larger user bases (i.e., better performance) on the primary platform, their apps on the secondary platforms are also likely to win more users.

However, this positive relationship can be constrained by technological barriers between platforms (Anderson et al., 2014; Baldwin & Woodard, 2009). From the trade-off perspective, by lowering cross-platform technical barriers, increased platform compatibility should reinforce the positive relationship between multihoming developers’ market performance across platforms. Compatibility allows app functionalities to be migrated smoothly, which makes successful app features more likely to function well on the secondary platform and avoids quality deterioration (Cennamo et al., 2018). The reduction of technological barriers also facilitates developers to better leverage their effective development expertise to boost app performance on the secondary platform. Therefore, we expect the positive relationship between a multihoming developer’s app users on the secondary platform and the developer’s user base on the primary platform to be stronger with increased platform compatibility.

H4: The user base of a multihoming developer’s app on the secondary platform is positively associated with her overall user base on the primary platform. And increased platform compatibility reinforces this positive relationship.

However, the influence of increased platform compatibility on the cross-platform performance correlation should be subject to the type of apps that a multihoming developer builds for the secondary platform. The implications of platform compatibility should be distinct for building exclusive apps for the secondary platform and migrating existing apps from the primary platform. Exclusive apps are developed more specifically in conformity to the characteristics of the secondary platform (Anderson et al., 2014; Cennamo et al., 2018). While developers can still use their experiences on the primary platform to support exclusive app development on the secondary platform (Moorman & Miner, 1997), exclusive apps on the secondary platform are likely to entail new features and functionalities independent of the primary platform. Therefore, compared to the performance of migrated apps, the performance of exclusive apps on the secondary platform should be influenced less by platform compatibility in correlating with the developer’s overall performance on the primary platform.

H5: The reinforcing effect of platform compatibility on the positive relationship between the user base of a multihoming developer’s app on the secondary platform and the developer’s overall user base on the primary platform is weaker if the app is exclusive to the secondary platform.

## Methodology

## Research Context

Our study focuses on the top two web browser platforms: Google Chrome and Mozilla’s Firefox. Since 2016, these two dominant platforms have jointly represented more than 70% of the global market of web browsers, with Chrome acquiring about 66% and Firefox acquiring about 8% by 2020.<sup>2</sup> Chrome and Firefox are both open-sourced platforms with large bases of third-party developers contributing complements (i.e., “extension” apps on Chrome and “add-on” apps on Firefox). These apps extend the platforms’ core functionalities in various development areas, such as privacy protection, ad blocking, games, and news or weather subscription. Both Chrome and Firefox distribute third-party apps using their online app stores, i.e., Chrome Web Store and Firefox Addons Store.

Given their apparent difference in market dominance, Firefox is under substantial competitive pressure to expand its developer base and market share. A strategic move of Firefox was to make itself more compatible with Chrome so as to attract Chrome developers. In March 2016, Firefox released a new version of the browser, Firefox version 45.0, which supports a new type of application programming interface (API)<sup>3</sup>—WebExtensions API. Firefox’s WebExtensions API is compatible with the APIs provided by Chrome; thus, apps developed using WebExtensions API can run on both Chrome and Firefox, and Chrome apps can be migrated to Firefox with minimum adjustments using WebExtensions API.

We leverage this setting to study the impact of increased platform compatibility on the multihoming decisions of Chrome developers, as well as multihoming (from Chrome to Firefox) developers’ development choices and app performance. In this setting, we consider Firefox to be the secondary platform and Chrome to be the primary platform.

## Data

Our research data consists of third-party developers and apps on Chrome and Firefox. We collected data from both browsers’ official online app stores, i.e., Chrome Web Store for Extensions and Firefox Add-ons Store.<sup>4</sup> The Chrome Web

Store publishes app titles, developer’s names and home pages, number of app users, user reviews and ratings, last update times, and app installation sizes. We crawled weekly information of Chrome Extension apps from January 2017 to June 2018 and recorded 42,150 apps developed by 27,732 developers. Since the Chrome Web Store does not publish historical data, we developed an approach (details in Appendix A, Part 1) to trace back to as early as December 2010 for more complete app information.

The Firefox Add-ons Store publishes app titles, weekly downloads, numbers of active users, user reviews and ratings, release times, updating history (i.e., when and how many times), categories, and developer names. The Firefox site also allows developers to create developer pages, which publish developers’ registration times, home page links, numbers of apps developed, occupations, and locations. From the Firefox Add-ons Store, we crawled app information and developer information over a period from December 2009 to June 2018. In total, we recorded 9,706 Firefox add-on apps developed by 5,686 developers.

The first technical challenge in our study was to identify developers who first contributed to Chrome and later multihomed to Firefox. We identified these developers in the following steps. First, we identified all multihoming developers with apps published on both platforms. We matched developers on the two platforms by their names, emails, and URLs to their home pages and app pages. We found a total of 1,541 unique multihoming developers. All Chome developers who did not multihome were labeled Chrome-singlehoming developers. Second, we identified whether these developers first joined Chrome or Firefox. Firefox publishes the initial release times of apps. We considered a developer’s earliest initial release time as her joining time on Firefox. Identifying a developer’s joining time on Chrome is challenging as Chrome only displays the latest app versions and their release time. An app’s initial release time is available only when the app has never been updated. Leveraging the observations of historical app reviews on the Chrome Web Store, we approximated a developer’s joining time on Chrome using the earlier value of two observable dates: the earliest release date of the developer’s apps and the earliest review date of her apps. If a developer’s joining time on Chrome was earlier than that on Firefox, we considered this developer a multihoming developer, labeled a ChromeFirst developer. Third, we also manually checked the home pages of multihoming developers and used their self-disclosed joining times to verify or correct the results from the above two steps. We finally identified 716 ChromeFirst developers, who contributed 1,648 unique apps on Chrome and 1,138 unique apps on Firefox.

The second challenge is to identify competing apps. The Firefox Add-ons Store groups apps into 15 categories.<sup>5</sup> The size of addons category ranges from 677 (Language Support) to 2,526 (Social Communication). Firefox category can be used as a relatively coarse measure to assess app competition. To more accurately classify apps, we used a text-analysis method to classify apps based on app descriptions published on the Firefox Add-ons Store. Specifically, we used TF-IDF to model app description as vectors and then applied k-means clustering on app description vectors and original app categories. We employed the silhouette score (Kim et al., 2020; Rousseeuw, 1987) and the sum of squared error (SSE) (France & Ghose, 2016) as two criteria to determine the number of clusters (K). Based on these two criteria, we found that the optimal cluster number is K = 20 (details in Appendix A, Part 2). Thus, we used 20 groups of competing apps in our analysis.

The third challenge is to identify exclusive apps on Firefox. We manually labeled whether an app is exclusive or not on Firefox. For each Firefox app of a developer in our data set, we manually reviewed the titles and descriptions of all Chrome apps released by the same developer. If two apps had identical titles or their functions specified in the app descriptions were highly similar, we considered them the same app on different platforms.

The results of Model (2) are shown in Table 3. A 10% increase in a ChromeFirst developer’s Firefox user base was associated with only a 2.11% increase in the odds of exclusive development. Therefore, H2 is supported.

In Column 4, the coefficient of CompetingApp is negative and significant (-0.859, p < 0.05) but that of the interaction term CompetingApp×Compat is positive and significant (1.467, p < 0.05). We also find that a ChromeFirst developer’s user base on Chrome (ChromeUserBase) lowers the odds of exclusive development for Firefox. This result is consistent with prior research, suggesting that the supply of exclusive complements to a platform is negatively related to the developer’s market potential on other competing platforms (Corts & Lederman, 2009).

## Robustness Analysis

We used several robustness tests to verify our findings. First, we conducted placebo tests by randomizing the release time of WebExtensions API for Model (1) and Model (2). For example, we chose March 2015 and March 2017 as the hypothetical time points for the release of WebExtensions

API. We reassigned the values for the After<sub>t</sub> variable in Model (1) and Compat<sub>j</sub> in Model (2) based on the hypothetical release time. The results of Model (1) are shown in Columns 1-2 of Table D1 (Appendix D).<sup>6</sup> The results of Model (2) are shown in Columns 1-4 in Table D2 (Appendix D). The coefficients of the interaction terms in all of these results are not significant, suggesting the lack of moderating effects at these hypothetical release time points and supporting our main findings.

Second, the developers who joined Firefox late might have low personal development capabilities and hence smaller user bases on both Chrome and Firefox. To address this potentially confounding effect, in the main analysis, we control the developer’s Chrome user base at the release time of the Firefox app, i.e., ChromeUserBase. In the robustness test, we added another control variable for the developer’s user base— the developer’s Chrome user base at the time of multihoming, ChromeUserBase\_M. These two variables help better control for the developer’s development capability. The results (Appendix D, Table D3) show that the coefficient of the interaction FirefoxUser×Compat is still negative and statistically significant (-0.102, p < 0.05, Column 4, which further supports the finding that the marginal impact of the Firefox user base on the developer’s exclusive app development is lower after the release of WebExtensions API.

Third, ChromeFirst developers might choose to first release a new app on Firefox for testing purposes and then release it on Chrome. To address this potentially confounding effect, we further checked the ex post statuses of the Firefox exclusive apps at two additional time points after our data collection, i.e., December 2018 and January 2020. We found that among these Firefox exclusive apps, 42 apps and 61 apps were also available on Chrome in December 2018 and January 2020, respectively. We reestimated Model (2) after removing these apps in December 2018 and January 2020, respectively. The results remain consistent (Tables D4 and D5 in Appendix D).

## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

Theoretically, our study echoes the platform research on the relationship between platform architecture and the evolutionary dynamics of ecosystems and complements (Cennamo et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010). From an architecture perspective, while prior studies have focused on cross-platform heterogeneity in technological architecture (e.g., Cennamo et al., 2018), our study explores the compatibility of technological architecture between platforms, which sheds light on the decision-making on platform architecture. From a multihoming perspective, while the existing literature has primarily focused on the implications of multihoming on platform competition, we consider how platforms’ compatibility impacts the trade-offs in developers multihoming decisions and performance outcomes.

We first show that increased platform compatibility makes multihoming more attractive to developers who do not have superior market performance (i.e., the developers with smaller user bases). This finding adds to the theoretical understanding of the trade-offs in developer multihoming decisions. Multihoming barriers arise due to the technical cospecialization requirements as suggested in prior studies (e.g., Cennamo et al., 2018) and the demand and environmental uncertainties associated with entering a new market (Chintakananda & McIntyre, 2014; Kim, 2008). Our results essentially characterize how developers weigh the trade-offs in this process and how increased platform compatibility is factored into decision-making. To be specific, successful developers on the primary platform can leverage their existing user bases and technical expertise to overcome multihoming barriers. The mediocre developers often lack substantive resources (e.g., user bases and technological expertise) to do so. It is therefore more likely for the latter to find it challenging to multihome onto other platforms. Increased platform compatibility not only reduces the technical multihoming costs but also mitigates the market uncertainties in multihoming, which are more critical for developers with smaller user bases. As a result, it has a higher motivating effect on developers with smaller user bases.

Our second set of findings provides indicative evidence of the reinforcing effects of increased platform compatibility on multihoming developers’ exclusive app development for the secondary platform. There are three important theoretical implications of these findings. First, exclusive app development by multihoming developers is an underexamined area. The study highlights the distinction between developer multihoming and app multihoming. Developer multihoming is a much broader concept than app multihoming, which is one of the development choices for multihoming developers. Such development choices essentially reflect multihoming developers’ willingness to conform more specifically to and better leverage the secondary platform’s technological features in their app development.

Second, exclusive app development by multihoming developers has important implications for interplatform competition. Prior studies have suggested that multihoming complements might not reduce differentiation among platforms. The existing insights have primarily focused on the differential quality of multihoming complements across platforms (e.g., Cennamo et al., 2018). Our findings suggest that platform compatibility can enable greater innovations by multihoming developers on the secondary platform. Developers are encouraged to migrate their technological expertise and experience to the secondary platform to spark innovations in new apps, rather than replicate their existing apps. By encouraging and facilitating exclusive app development in multihoming, platforms may achieve new differentiation. Therefore, the theoretical implication of increased platform compatibility for platform competition should be more nuanced.

Third, our findings characterize how exclusive development decisions in multihoming are dependent on the market environment on the secondary platform and how increased platform compatibility weighs in. In particular, market factors such as demand uncertainty and app competition on the secondary platform may both constitute barriers that discourage exclusive development. The theoretical implication is that multihoming barriers are multifaceted and not just limited to technological distinctions between platforms. Our findings suggest that increased platform compatibility can not only directly reduce the technical barriers, but also indirectly weakens the adverse effects of market factors that dampen developers’ incentives for exclusive app development (e.g., app competition). This is essentially an alternative venue in which increased platform compatibility addresses multihoming barriers.

Our third set of findings illustrate how increased platform compatibility may allow multihoming developers to leverage their experiences on the primary platform to enhance their app performance on the secondary platform. A key theoretical implication is that by overcoming multihoming barriers, the secondary platform can facilitate the spillover of developer expertise through increased platform compatibility. Multihoming developers may find it more convenient to extend their accumulated technological experiences on the primary platform to their development for the secondary platform. As a consequence, developers could migrate more quality of their complements. In this regard, a more critical benefit of increased platform compatibility for the secondary platform is to foster the spillover of app quality. Such spillover helps multihoming developers achieve superior market performance on the secondary platform and also facilitates the secondary platform to better serve end-users and cope with platform competition.

Our findings further illustrate that the benefits of app quality spillover can be constrained by development choices in multihoming. More specifically, the facilitating effect of increased platform compatibility for multihoming developers to leverage their primary-platform experiences is weaker if developers choose exclusive development on the secondary platform. From a theoretical perspective, this insight is in line with the view on the trade-off between the fungibility of complements across competing platforms and the platform uniqueness (Cennamo et al., 2018; Schilling, 2000). When the secondary platform aims to attract exclusive development that fits with its unique features, it has to sacrifice some benefits of increased platform compatibility.

## Practical Implications

Our study provides practical implications for both the secondary platform and third-party developers on the strategic use of platform compatibility. Platforms benefit from having a set of heterogeneous complementors to meet diverse and evolving customer needs (Clements & Ohashi, 2005; Wareham et al., 2014). When leveraging increased platform compatibility to attract third-party developers, the secondary platform needs to recognize that multihoming developers may not just port their existing apps. They can engage in exclusive development for the secondary platform, as long as multihoming costs do not hinder it. Therefore, the secondary platform may implement complementary initiatives (e.g., using helpful promotion mechanisms such as recommendation, exposing more platform resources, and offering more favorable licensing agreements) to further mitigate multihoming barriers and support exclusive development (Parker et al., 2017).

For third-party developers, our study suggests that they can leverage increased platform compatibility to expand their development scope. Porting their existing apps to a secondary platform enables developers to extend their app user bases at a manageable cost. More importantly, they can combine their own know-how with compatible platform features and resources to engage in innovations for the platforms that they multihome onto. This is essentially a process for third-party developers to exploit their technological expertise in differential platform environments and leverage the unique advantages of different platforms to expand their app markets. By consolidating the development effort across compatible platforms and exploring the economics of scale, developers have more resources to utilize in customizing their app products for advantageous features of different platforms.

Platforms have distinct incentives to achieve compatibility depending on their competitive positions. Due to the selfenforcing nature of cross-sided network externality, a dominant platform tends to persist (Bonardi & Durand, 2003; Eisenmann et al., 2011; Schilling, 2002), and nondominant platforms may keep losing ground to the dominant ones when competing based on incompatible standards (Rysman, 2009; Zhu & Iansiti, 2012). Therefore, Firefox has a strong incentive to make itself compatible with Chrome because doing so would potentially help it seize resources from the latter and level the competitive ground (Adner et al., 2020). In contrast, Chrome, concerned about losing its dominant position, may have limited motivation to pursue compatibility (Casadesus-Masanell & Ruiz-Aliseda, 2008; Doganoglu & Wright, 2006; Viecens, 2011). Since Firefox unilaterally made its technical specifications compatible with Chrome’s standards, it is difficult for Chrome to impede compatibility. But it is worth noting that platform compatibility potentially has a positive impact on Chrome. With more compatible standards, it is also less costly for Firefox developers to port their apps to or develop new apps for Chrome.<sup>7</sup> Firefox developers may also be encouraged to multihome onto Chrome. However, our extended analysis shows that increased platform compatibility does not strengthen the positive relationship of cross-platform performance for the Chrome apps released by FirefoxFirst developers (Please see Appendix E). The prelimilary finding results essentially suggest that the secondary platform benefits more from increased platform compatibility. Nevertheless, both platforms may benefit from higher cross-platform network externalities as the numbers of multihoming developers and apps increase (Corts & Lederman, 2009). The overall impact on the primary platform is worthy of in-depth consideration.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our specific research setting allowed us to better understand how increased platform compatibility influences developers’ multihoming decisions. However, given the breadth and depth of multihoming issues across competing platforms, there are many questions that our study cannot fully address, which constitute the limitations of our study. First, our study focuses on how increased platform compatibility may benefit a secondary platform by attracting multihoming developers from a primary platform. Since increased platform compatibility may also drive the secondary platform’s developers to join the dominant platform, future research might examine more specifically how the primary platform may cope with increased platform compatibility. Second, multihoming may not just be a developer-side issue, and users of platforms can also multihome (Choi, 2010; Kim & Park, 2017; Koh & Fichman, 2014). User multihoming may influence app development of third-party developers and competition between platforms. Because of data constraints, we were unable to observe multihoming behavior on the user side and examine its implications. Future research could rely on different research settings to address this issue. Third, while we were able to assess the impact of increased platform compatibility on multihoming developers from the primary platform to the secondary platform, our data is still insufficient for examining the optimal choice of platform compatibility that has interested many analytical researchers (Adner et al., 2020; Casadesus-Masanell & Ruiz-Aliseda, 2008; Viecens, 2011). Future research could empirically examine many questions related to platforms’ compatibility choices, e.g., to what extent should a platform revoke or hinder compatibility with a competitor? Under what conditions do platforms jointly adopt technologically compatible standards? What is the overall effect of platform compatibility on platform competition?

## Conclusion

The competition between technological platforms escalates “a battle of devices to a war of ecosystems” (Hyrynsalmi et al., 2016). Platforms strive to attract more third-party contributors into their ecosystems. A potential strategy for a nondominant platform is to make itself more technologically compatible with a dominant platform to attract multihoming developers from the latter. We study the impacts of such platform compatibility increase on developers’ incentives and behaviors. We focus on the trade-offs in developers’ multihoming decisions, their app development choices, and cross-platform app performance. We particularly look at the strategic move of Firefox improving its compatibility with Chrome and the Chrome developers’ responses. We find empirical support that platform compatibility impacts the developers’ multihoming-related decisions and performance. The results suggest that changes in platform architectures, particularly technological compatibility, are consequential for development decisions and outcomes.

## Estimation of Historical Overall Chrome Users

To estimate historical overall Chrome users, we first obtain estimated monthly Chrome market shares from StatCounter.<sup>8</sup> Sites such as StatCounter and W3Counter estimate web browser market shares by keeping track of the proportion of Internet visitors to a group of websites using each specific brand of web browser<sup>9</sup>. Second, we employed the method from Statista<sup>10</sup> and used the estimated Chrome market shares to impute historical overall Chrome user bases. In particular, the overall Chrome user bases were estimated as

$$
C h r o m e U s e r B a s e = C h r o m e M a r k e t S h a r e \times I n t e r n e t U s e r s,
$$

where ChromeMarketShare is the monthly market shares of Chrome as explained above, InternetUsers is the total number of Internet users estimated by Internetworldstats on a half-year basis<sup>11</sup>. Statista also directly reports its estimated yearly user bases of web browsers (including Chrome) from 2014-2017<sup>12</sup>, and we also use these estimates.

$$

$$

where $n _ { i } ( t )$ is the user base of app $I , n ( t )$ is the user base of the whole market (in this case, the overall Chrome user base). $m _ { i }$ is the total market potential for the specific app $i , p _ { i }$ is the online users’ inherent adoption rate for app $i , q _ { i }$ is the increase in adoption rate for app i due to the increase in user base. As explained in Duan et al. (2009), from an estimation perspective, the nonlinear model (A.1) can be approximated using a fixed-effect model as follows,

$$
l g \frac {n _ {i} (t)}{n (t)} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} A g e _ {i t} + \alpha_ {2} A g e _ {i t} ^ {2} + \sum \alpha X _ {i t} + u _ {i} + \lambda_ {i t} \quad (\mathrm{A.2})
$$

where $A g e _ { i t }$ is app age up to time t, and $A g e _ { i t } ^ { 2 }$ is the squared term used to approximate the nonlinear diffusion process over time. $X _ { i t }$ is a set of time-varying control variables. $u _ { i }$ is app fixed-effect. $\lambda _ { i t }$ is the random error term. For control variables, we include the number of apps in the corresponding app categories, average app rating, total number of app reviews, and update time.

We use the sample over our observation window (1/2017 - 6/2018) to estimate the model coefficients in Model (A.2). In the observation window, we can observe the user base of a specific app at time t (which can be used to calculate the app’s market share), and other independent variables. We then use the resulting diffusion model to estimate the historical app market shares. Based on the results from the above steps, we obtained the estimated historical user bases for each app on Chrome.

## Part 2. Competing Apps Identification by K-means Clustering

The K-means clustering algorithm does not determine the optimal number of clusters. Therefore, we searched for the best number of clusters starting with sixteen clusters (i.e., K = 16) to get a more granular classification of apps than the fifteen categories provided by Firefox. We use two measures: the silhouette scores (Rousseeuw, 1987; Kim et al., 2020) and sum of square error (SSE) (France & Ghose, 2016; Steinley, 2006) to compare the performance of different clustering results. The silhouette score captures the ratio of the average distance between the observations in the same cluster to the average distance between clusters (Kim et al., 2020). The score value ranges over [-1, 1], with highe scores representing better clustering results (Rousseeuw, 1987; Kim et al., 2020). Figure A1 shows the silhouette scores given K = 16, $1 7 , . . . . . , 2 5$ . The graph indicates that clustering with K = 20 has the highest silhouette score, i.e., the best performance. SSE captures the sum of the squared distance between each observation and its cluster centroid (Steinley, 2006). Figure A2 presents SSE given K =16, 17,……, 25. The “elbow” of the graph, i.e., a marked flattening of the graph (France & Ghose, 2016; Ketchen & Shook, 1996; Hair et al., 2009; Posey et al., 2013) suggests the appropriate number of clusters. The elbow in Figure A2 appears at K = 20, which is consistent with the performance evaluation based on the silhouette scores. Therefore, we applied 20 groups of competing apps in our analysis.

## Appendix C

We adopted the relative time model (Jayaraman and Wu 2019) to examine the parallel-trends premise, which supports that the release of WebExtensions API is a legitimate shock for causal inference. For each month, we separated the Chrome developers in Model (1) (i.e., Chrome-singlehoming developers and ChromeFirst developers) into two groups: a large-user-base group, i.e., developers whose user bases are larger the 70th percentile, and a small-user-base group, i.e., developers whose user bases are smaller than the 30th percentile.

We used Model C.1 to gauge the differential effects of the release of WebExtensions API on the large-user-base and small-user-base groups:

$$
\text { Multihoming } _ {i t} = \delta_ {0} + \sum_ {\tau = - T} ^ {T} \lambda_ {\tau} D _ {i \tau} + \delta_ {I} \text { Userbase } _ {i t} + \sum \boldsymbol {\delta} \boldsymbol {X} _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{C.1}
$$

where τ indexes a month relative to the release month of WebExtensions API (denoted as Month0), i.e., Month-12<sup>+</sup>, Month-11, Month 10,……, Month-1, Month0, Month1,……, Month10, Month11, Month12<sup>+</sup>. The months that are twelve or more months before or after the release of WebExtensions API are denoted as Month-12<sup>+</sup> or Month12<sup>+</sup>, respectively. $D _ { i \tau }$ is a dummy variable with value 1 indicating developer i is in the small-user-base group in month τ and 0 otherwise. Equation (C.1) uses the same control variables as those in Equation (1). We also included month dummies to control for the time trending effect. The set of coefficients $\lambda _ { \tau }$ helps us identify whether a pretreatment trend exists and how the effect changes over time after the treatment.

Figure C1 plots the coefficient $\lambda _ { \tau } ,$ which represents the multihoming tendency of the small-user-base group (compared to the large-user-base group) in month τ. The graph shows that the multihoming tendency of the small-user-base group was lower than that of the large-user-base group before WebExtensions API was released. The multihoming tendency in the small-user-base group did not appear to be trending up in the pre-treatment period. When WebExtensions API was released, the increase in multihoming tendency in the small-user-base group kicked in. The results suggest that the parallel-trends assumption is not violated.

## Appendix E

We conducted extended analyses on the cross-platform performance for developers who joined Firefox first and then multihomed onto Chrome, and we considered them to be FirefoxFirst developers. We examined the relationship between FirefoxFirst developers’ app performance on Chrome (ChromeAppUser) and their user bases on Firefox (FirefoxUser). We used the data set of all Chrome apps developed by FirefoxFirst developers. We adapted Model (3) and used the following model:

ChromeAppUser<sub>jt</sub>= γ<sub>0</sub>+γ<sub>1</sub>FirefoxUser<sub>jt-1</sub>+γ<sub>2</sub>WebExtension<sub>j</sub>+γ<sub>3</sub>ChromeExclusive

+γ<sub>4</sub>FirefoxUser<sub>jt-1</sub>×WebExtension<sub>j</sub>+γ<sub>5</sub>FirefoxUser<sub>jt-1</sub>×ChromeExclusive<sub>j</sub>

+γ<sub>6</sub>FirefoxUser<sub>jt-1</sub>×WebExtension<sub>j</sub>×ChromeExclusive<sub>j</sub>+∑ γX<sub>jt</sub>+µ+ε<sub>jt</sub> (E.1)

The difference between Model (E.1) and Model (3) in the main analysis is that the variables ChromeAppUser<sub>j</sub>, FirefoxUser<sub>j</sub>, ChromeExclusive<sub>j</sub> and all relevant control variables are calculated based on the data of the FirefoxFirst developers between January 2018 to June 2018. Chrome apps do not use WebExtensions API. We therefore used WebExtension<sub>j</sub> to indicate the release time of app j relative to that of WebExtensions API. A value of 1 represents the release time of app j was after WebExtensions API was released and 0 otherwise. The results are summarized in Table E1. The results show that FirefoxFirst developers have better app performance on Chrome when they have larger user bases on Firefox. This suggests that FirefoxFirst developers can leverage their Firefox experience to benefit their app development on the platform that they multihome onto, which is consistent with the results of our main analysis. However, increased compatibility does not moderate this relationship.
