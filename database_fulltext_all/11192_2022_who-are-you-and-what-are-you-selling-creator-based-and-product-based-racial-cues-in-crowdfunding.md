# WHO ARE YOU AND WHAT ARE YOU SELLING? CREATOR-BASED AND PRODUCT-BASED RACIAL CUES IN CROWDFUNDING<sup>1</sup>

Lauren Rhue and Jessica Clark Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park, MD, U.S.A. {lrhue@umd.edu} {jmclark@umd.edu}

The display of personal information in crowdfunding campaigns is vital for facilitating trust; however, this information often communicates the racial identity of the fundraiser. We study the relationship between racial cues and crowdfunding success. Using data from more than 100,000 projects gathered from Kickstarter.com, we categorized racial cues as creator-based versus product-based. For each category, we derived racial cues in two different mediums: photo vs. textual. We used propensity score matching to estimate the effects of racial identity across racial groups, categories, and mediums. We found that the category of racial cues is associated with crowdfunding success. Projects with either creator-based or product-based cues of African American identity had lower success rates. In contrast, creator-based cues of Asian identity were associated with lower levels of success whereas product-based cues were associated with increased success. Furthermore, when product-based cues and creator-based cues were misaligned, we found that the outcomes more closely followed those associated with productbased cues, suggesting that backers are more attuned to product attributes. Our results also suggest that racial anonymity correlates with higher success rates, as compared to African American and Asian racial cues. Our study contributes to the understanding of racial identity on digital platforms across multiple contexts, mediums, and racial groups.

Keywords: Crowdfunding, empirical analysis, diversity, text mining, matched sample, econometrics, sharing economy, racial bias

## Introduction

Crowdfunding platforms are defined by information asymmetry, or the difficulty in assessing a fundraiser’s true motives (Radoynovska & King, 2019; Sundararajan, 2016). Creators and platforms must encourage potential project backers to trust the project creators, who are thus encouraged to represent their true identity through self-disclosure (Bucher et al., 2018; Duarte et al., 2012; Ellison et al., 2006; Radoynovska & King, 2019; van Dijck, 2013). However, disclosing identity-related information can also reveal racial cues, potentially leading to biased outcomes.

Evidence that some people of color face discrimination on digital platforms is reported in research across contexts such as crowdfunding (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018, 2019), Airbnb (Edelman et al., 2017), eBay (Ayres et al., 2015), and Craigslist (Gaddis & Ghoshal, 2015). Collectively, this literature suggests that either platforms or individuals of color could conceal personal cues of race (Ayres et al., 2015; Edelman et al., 2017) or “whitewash” their profiles (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018) to improve outcomes. Several different aspects of profiles on digital platforms can signal race, and the current understanding of the multifaceted concept of race and racial cues on digital platforms is limited. Furthermore, in order to fully achieve the recommended racial anonymity, creators would have to limit the information shared about themselves and possibly their projects, reducing their ability to build trust among potential backers.

Our research explores this crowdfunding dilemma through an observational study that examines racial signaling and the consequences of self-disclosure on success. We study how the expression of racial identity is related to outcomes in a crowdfunding marketplace. Using more than 100,000 projects gathered from Kickstarter.com, we constructed multiple measures of racial cues and used a matching methodology to address currently unanswered questions about racial identity on online crowdfunding platforms. We extend prior work that has largely examined a single type of racial cue by differentiating the racial identity of creators and of products and analyzing the racial identity of the creator picture, project campaign picture, creator name, and project description.

Our analysis produces three main sets of results. First, depending on whether racial cues are creator-based or product-based, we find that they are associated with contradictory effects. Although the effects’ magnitudes vary, the crowdfunding outcomes are more aligned with expectations from the product-based racial cues. Second, we find that the effect of racial cues varies by group. Consistent with past findings, our results indicate that African American racial cues are associated with lower crowdfunding outcomes. While creator-based Asian racial cues are connected to lower success, product-based Asian racial cues are related to higher success. Third, despite a potential trade-off in information asymmetry, we find evidence that racial anonymity heightens success compared to racial cues from people of color.

This study makes several contributions to the information systems literature. Our findings imply that in order to study racial dynamics on digital platforms, researchers must consider multiple identity cues and multiple racial groups. Our work provides evidence that campaigns often contain multiple identity-related cues, and each racial cue in each medium is related to success to varying degrees. Furthermore, our work expands on past research to consider a multidimensional view of race rather than a simplistic binary perspective. In expanding the view of race, we find that identity-related cues may differ based on whether they are associated with the creator or with the product. The inclusion of Asian racial cues and their conflicting outcomes shed additional light on the mechanism behind racial disparities on digital platforms. This study also quantifies the racial characteristics of creators and projects, enabling a large-scale analysis of the dynamics of race on crowdfunding platforms. Our methodology might interest platforms that want to assess racial cues as a part of a larger bias reduction strategy. Finally, our study highlights the benefits and challenges resulting from contradictions in racial cues and racial anonymity. Future research in this area must consider these trade-offs when making recommendations for how to de-bias digital platforms.

The Hypotheses section motivates our hypotheses about racial cues and mediums. The Data section describes the dataset and methodologies used to construct variables for analysis. The Empirical Analysis section shows the design used to analyze differences in outcomes across racial cues and across racial groups. The Robustness Checks section examines the robustness of our results, and the Discussion section concludes.

## Theory

We start with an introduction to crowdfunding markets and the information asymmetry inherent in them. Next, we examine the concept of identity, particularly focusing on racial identity; summarize relevant literature; and present our conceptualization of social identity cues in the context of information revelation on crowdfunding platforms.

## Crowdfunding

In general, crowd-based marketplaces have three direct participants: consumers, sellers, and the digital platform itself (Hagiu & Wright, 2015). Crowdfunding is a specific form of crowd-based marketplace that enables “individuals and groups … to fund their ventures by drawing on relatively small contributions from a relatively large number of individuals using the internet” (Mollick, 2014). In these markets, consumers are the campaign “backers,” sellers are the project campaign “creators,” and transactions occur on the crowdfunding platform. Crowdfunding has witnessed a sharp acceleration to \$17.2 billion dollars in annual growth (Shepherd, 2020). Of the numerous crowdfunding platforms (Belleflamme et al. 2015), some, such as Kickstarter.com, are general-purpose, while others are more specialized.

Prior research about crowdfunding has investigated both the campaign elements that encourage success and the factors that motivate backers to contribute to a particular project. Buyers in a crowdfunding marketplace, i.e., backers, are motivated to transact not only based on the expectation of financial gains, but also on diverse nonmonetary considerations (Gerber & Hui, 2013; L. Liu et al., 2017; Mollick, 2014). For instance, crowdfunding contributions can be affected by emotions (Allison et al., 2015; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Rhue & Robert, 2018). Backers are motivated by both geographic and cultural similarity to the creator (Burtch et al., 2014; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016). Backers also prefer to transact with friends (Lin et al., 2013) and build community through their contributions (Mollick, 2014); thus, information concealment may harm the likelihood of future contributions on the online crowdfunding platform (Burtch et al., 2015).

As is often also the case in regular capital markets (Healy & Palepu, 2001), crowdfunding markets are characterized by information asymmetry. Although creators provide information about their campaigns, such information is necessarily incomplete. Further, backers often lack prior experience with any given creator. As a result, contribution decisions must be made based on partial information (Mollick 2014). Backers’ inability to observe the creator’s true quality leads to the use of heuristics to make trade-offs among competing contribution opportunities. To the extent that potential backers learn about a project and its creator from all available information, a creator’s choices about information disclosure and presentation become critical.

## Identity Cues in Technology-Mediated Contexts

Our focus in this study is on the expression of racial identity and how it drives outcomes in a crowdfunding marketplace. The construct of identity, rooted in the literature on social psychology, is multifaceted. Social identity theory argues that one’s identity encompasses two dimensions: personal, idiosyncratic elements such as interests, attributes, experiences, and physical attributes (Ma & Agarwal, 2007); and social elements such as gender, race, ethnicity, and class (Howard, 2000; Tajfel & Turner, 2004). Identity perception, or how “others” perceive one’s identity, has been shown to influence user behavior in technology-mediated spaces (Ma & Agarwal, 2007).

Among the different elements of social identity, our focus is on racial social identity.<sup>2</sup> In the United States, race plays a critical role in how users engage with technology (Hanna et al., 2020; Ogbonnaya-Ogburu et al., 2020). A growing body of literature provides evidence for the effects of race on outcomes on a variety of digital platforms including Airbnb (Edelman et al., 2017), Prosper.com (Pope & Sydnor, 2011), Craigslist (Gaddis & Ghoshal, 2015), eBay (Ayres et al., 2015), Facebook (Hebl et al., 2012), and Kickstarter (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018, 2019).

How do users discover and learn about an individual’s social identity in a technology-mediated setting? Identity communication encapsulates the ways in which people express and present themselves (Ma & Agarwal, 2007). This may be through direct utterances: e.g., a post stating “I am a 35-year-old Asian woman,” or indirectly inferable through other means, e.g., a photograph clearly showing gender expression. Importantly, identity communication may occur subtly without the focal user’s direct intent.

We define racial cues as the means by which racial social identity is communicated. Skin color, hair texture and style, eye shape, and language choices are all examples of racial cues. In technology-mediated settings, racial cues can be conveyed through common digital mediums such as pictures, names, and language. Pictures can convey racial cues (Fu et al., 2014; Hosoi et al., 2004), such as the skin color of a hand holding a product (Ayres et al., 2015) and/or other facial attributes such as nose shape and hair (Fu et al., 2014). Names can convey racial cues if the name is distinctive and common for people from a particular racial background (Bertrand & Mullainathan, 2004; Cui et al., 2019; Edelman et al., 2017; Ge et al., 2016). Language can convey racial cues through speech patterns, common words/phrases, or topics (Wardhaugh, 2011; Labov & Harris, 1986; Massey & Lundy, 2001; Preotiuc-Pietro & Ungar, 2018).

Unlike face-to-face exchanges, in which social identity cues are inextricably bundled through visual cues, technologymediated platforms enable user control over whether and which social identity cues to disclose, based on the medium. For instance, in face-to-face settings, a person automatically shares their visual appearance, thus communicating cues about their racial identity. In the technology-mediated setting, however, users are able to control the medium and therefore conceal identity cues. Early research on technology-mediation of human interaction expected that technology-mediated spaces would disconnect online and offline identities (Bruckman, 1993) and function without the social identities that are present in the offline world (Bruckman, 1993). However, modern digital platforms require the disclosure of user and product information using pictures, names, and language (Ellison et al., 2006; H. Liu, 2007; van Dijck, 2013). These elements, which platforms often verify, facilitate trust—and transactions—among the participants (Ert et al., 2016). Each of these elements intended to communicate personal identity also act as mediums to separately communicate cues related to social identity.

## Identity Cues in Crowdfunding

Peer-to-peer digital platforms often require multiple sources of information about the seller as well as the offering being sold that may be important to correctly brand and position it in the marketplace. Sellers have agency and choice regarding whether to divulge or conceal their identity, but the platform requires them to provide information about their product or service offering. Such information is often supplied via a picture and description that are embedded with cues.

Based on the requirements for information disclosure imposed by digital platforms, we conceptualize two distinct types of social identity cues: creator-based cues and product-based cues. Creator-based racial cues are racial signals stemming from information that platform users disclose about themselves, intentionally or inadvertently. For example, if a person’s race is not easily discernible from their picture, their race may still be inferred from their first name or from their educational background, e.g., a degree from a historically black college or university (HBCU). Product-based racial cues are related to information provided about the product, project, or service sold on a platform. The two types of cues need not necessarily be consistent with each other, although they can be. For instance, an Asian creator may decide to launch a crowdfunding campaign and hire a White actor to pose in the campaign picture holding the product. In this instance, the creator-based cues associated with the creator profile page would indicate an Asian social identity whereas the product-based cues would indicate a White identity.<sup>3</sup>

Table 1 summarizes key studies that have explored how creatorbased and product-based racial identity cues influence success on digital platforms. Collectively, these papers affirm the existence of a relationship between racial identity cues and outcomes in a wide range of settings.

Creator-based racial cues have been shown to affect the amount of money raised and the probability of attracting backers in crowdfunding (Cui et al., 2019; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018), even controlling for other user profile attributes such as name and gender. Creator-based racial cues signaled by user names can yield lower acceptance rates for offers on homesharing platforms (Edelman et al., 2017), higher interest rates on peer-to-peer platforms (Pope & Sydnor, 2011), and higher wait times and more cancelations on ride-sharing platforms (Ge et al., 2016; Mejia & Parker, 2019). Product-based racial cues have also been shown to affect outcomes on peer-to-peer platforms. The skin color of the hand holding a product yielded a 20% difference in the final prices on the online auction platform eBay (Ayres et al., 2015) and similarly generated lower offers in online classified ads (Doleac & Stein, 2013).

Our study builds upon previous literature on discrimination and bias on digital platforms and departs from prior work listed in Table 1 in three key ways. First, we jointly examine creatorbased and product-based racial identity cues, theorizing why creator-based racial cues could yield different outcomes from product-based racial cues. Second, prior studies have focused solely on African American as compared to White racial identity. Our study includes Asian racial identity, enabling a more robust examination of creator-based and product-based cues. Finally, we construct machine learning models to estimate latent racial cues, such as speech patterns, that are evident to users but not explicitly stated in the standard profile information. The inclusion of such latent cues supports a deeper and more nuanced analysis of the effects of racial identity signals on outcomes.

## Hypotheses

## Creator-Based Racial Cues

Individuals’ characteristics are difficult to fully decode, even in face-to-face settings, and are even more complex in technologically mediated environments where less information is available (Richey et al., 2018). In situations with limited information where there is a need to make judgments and assessments about a person, research has suggested that individual cognition relies on stereotypes. Stereotypes are the expectations associated with social categories and act as shortcuts to understand and remember things about people in different categories (Ellemers, 2018). Stereotypes facilitate information processing because people can rely on previously stored knowledge about the category, rather than incoming information (Hilton & von Hippel, 1996), and are invoked automatically in numerous cognitive tasks (Devine, 1989; Hilton & von Hippel, 1996).

Stereotypes that are grounded in race are pervasive in American culture and become automatic (Devine, 1989; Dixon & Rosenbaum, 2004; Hilton & von Hippel, 1996). As stereotypes take hold in the culture, they stabilize over time and between generations (Dixon & Rosenbaum, 2004). Racial cues associated with people, such as distinctive names, can activate racial stereotypes and influence economic outcomes in offline interactions (Bertrand & Mullainathan, 2004) as well as on digital platforms (Cui et al., 2019; Edelman et al., 2017; Ge et al., 2016).

On digital platforms, backers often decide among horizontally differentiated offerings, e.g., distinct product offerings that differ according to personal preference. Personal preferences provide alternative explanations for discrimination; thus, such decisions cannot be directly attributable to stereotypes and/or racial discrimination even if backers have those motivations. Because their motivations cannot be ascertained, people are less likely to actively avoid using stereotypes and more likely to unconsciously incorporate stereotypical beliefs in their decisions (Hilton & von Hippel, 1996; Snyder et al., 1979).

Although specific stereotypes vary by racial group, the valence of minority stereotypes is often negative as compared to the majority group, which in the U.S. and our study is White. African American racial cues negatively affect outcomes on digital platforms (Cui et al., 2019; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Stereotypes of African American sellers include that they create lower quality products, and African American sellers experience a financial penalty on digital platforms (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2019). Latinx are subject to similarly negative stereotypes as African Americans, particularly in professional contexts (Chung‐Herrera & Lankau, 2005; King et al., 2006). In contrast, stereotypes of Asian Americans often revolve around being the “model minority” (Lee, 2003) and being excessively and threateningly competent in professional settings (Fiske et al., 2002; King et al., 2006). In addition, Asians’ stereotyped competence is coupled with stereotypes of being unassimilated foreigners, nerds, and noncreative (Zhang, 2010). The “othering” of Asian Americans is corroborated through anecdotes about anti-Asian bias on digital platforms (Abrams, 2019; Martin, 2017; Wang, 2017), as well as the rise in anti-Asian rhetoric and violence in the U.S. during the COVID-19 pandemic (Dionne & Turkmen, 2020). Thus, although racial stereotypes themselves vary across groups, minorities in all groups consistently experience less favorable outcomes compared to the majority (Booth et al., 2012). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1a. Creator-based African American racial cues are associated with lower success outcomes compared to White cues.

H1b. Creator-based Asian racial cues are associated with lower success outcomes compared to White cues.

## Product-Based Racial Cues

In crowdfunding campaigns, creators’ status and objectives for their projects are mediated through the digital platform. Potential backers cannot explore prototypes on their own but must rely on the narrative and visual elements provided by creators. As such, potential backers examine project attributes with limited information to assess their quality and potential success.

From the creator’s perspective, creators consider how to market their projects to generate interest in their projects as well as find financial backers (Agrawal et al., 2015; Mollick, 2014). Creators market their projects using visual images, written text, and other elements that “act as a representational system that produces meaning outside the realm of the … product” (Borgerson & Schroeder, 2002, p. 574). Racial cues can be employed as a marketing tool, relying on “persons and other symbolic and material representations” associated with a particular social and cultural identity (Crockett, 2008). Racial cues in marketing products can range from overt, such as an actor in a commercial (Henderson & Baldasty, 2003), to cultural elements derived from that community, such as common music genres (Crockett, 2008).

Product-based racial cues can also be an unintentional and potentially inevitable consequence of the project’s content and intended consumer. Some projects naturally contain elements with racial and cultural connotations, such as referencing Asian philosophers or promoting hip-hop music (Crockett, 2008; Southworth, 2019). Because these elements are intertwined with the product content, product-based racial cues may imply the intended consumers. Therefore, the effects of product-based racial cues are distinct from creatorbased racial cues.

For brands and products associated with Asia, focusing on Asian cultural aspects in their branding, such as images related to Asia, can enhance their brand positions in the U.S. markets (Southworth, 2019). Branding that incorporates elements consistent with Asian culture is associated with perceptions of higher-quality products and increased purchase likelihood (Southworth, 2019; Steenkamp et al., 2003). Furthermore, Asian consumers are emerging as a desirable target market because, in the United States, Asians’ median income exceeds the U.S. median income (Budiman & Ruiz, 2021). Thus, product-based Asian racial cues would be expected to increase the success of these projects, all else being equal.

In contrast, representations of African American culture in marketing often emphasize its dissimilarity from the dominant culture (Crockett, 2008). Since the dominant culture perceives African American cultural elements as associated with lower quality, product-based African American racial cues are expected to reduce success. Plus, African Americans’ presence in advertisements can trigger discrimination (Qualls & Moore, 1990) or other biases (Whittler, 1991; Whittler & Spira, 2002), leading to smaller market size and lower prices for products associated with African Americans (Ayres et al., 2015; Doleac & Stein, 2013). Plus, African American consumers are often overlooked by corporations because African American households have lower incomes and wealth on average (Chui et al., 2021). Backers may similarly view this as a less attractive market to serve. We hypothesize:

H2a. Product-based African American racial cues are associated with decreased crowdfunding success outcomes.

H2b. Product-based Asian racial cues are associated with increased crowdfunding success outcomes.

## Creator-Based vs. Product-Based Racial Cues

Creator-based and product-based racial cues are present in projects on crowdfunding platforms but they are somewhat independent. Creator-based and product-based racial cues may not be aligned with each other, or they may be altogether absent.

## Misaligned Racial Cues

Crowdfunding platforms rely on backers to assess both the quality of the product but also the creator’s ability to bring the project to fruition. As described above, creator-based racial cues may activate stereotypes about the creator and their ability to execute their project. Product-based racial cues may activate concerns such as project quality and/or consumer market size. A strong belief in the creator may not allay those concerns.

Creator-based racial cues and product-based racial cues are not necessarily aligned. For instance, a creator may choose to market their project by posing the product next to someone with different racial cues, leading to misalignment between the creator-based and product-based racial cues. Project-related attributes, such as the picture and description, are particularly influential for crowdfunding backers, as backers use project-related information to assess quality and eventual product success (Mollick, 2014). In a set of lab studies, product-centric considerations were found to be central to the backer’s decision of whether to contribute to a crowdfunding project or not (Zvilichovsky et al., 2018). Project-based attributes have been found to have the most significant effect on the probability of success on Kickstarter.com (Chan et al., 2018). Moreover, signals of quality about the project can overcome racial disparities in crowdfunding outcomes (Younkin and Kuppuswamy, 2017). Thus, when the creator-based racial cues differ from the product-based racial cues, we expect that the expected outcomes will most closely resemble the expected outcomes of the product-based racial cues.

H3. Success outcomes for projects with misalignment in the racial cues will more closely follow the outcomes of productbased racial cues.

## Anonymity

One recommendation to ameliorate the negative effects of racial cues from marginalized groups is to conceal racial identity on digital platforms (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). This strategy would eliminate the racial cues associated with both the user and the product, reducing the spillovers from related stereotypes and cultures. In order to do so, users and/or platforms must obfuscate the racial cues in names, profile photos, campaign photos, and campaign language. While there is a trade-off, in that removing racial cues reduces the amount of information available, we expect that racially anonymous campaigns, i.e., campaigns without racial cues, will ultimately have higher success than those with African American or Asian cues.

H4. Racially anonymous projects experience higher success than projects with racial cues from African Americans and Asians.

## Data

Kickstarter.com is a popular crowdfunding platform that connects project creators and entrepreneurs to small-scale investors. Fundraisers create campaigns to raise capital and fund their projects. A project campaign is successful if it meets or exceeds a predefined amount of capital by a certain date; otherwise, the creator receives no money. However, even if a project is not ultimately successful, the ability to attract backers can act as advertising for the product. Therefore, we measure the effect of racial indicators on two outcomes: success and number of backers.

Creators start a project campaign page for their project that includes the key elements associated with the project. In addition to the campaign goal and deadline, the standard project profile displays the campaign name, a description of the project, a list of rewards associated with various amounts of monetary support, a video associated with the campaign, a picture associated with the campaign, and the name and picture of the creator. Projects are also assigned categories, with possible categories including arts, comics & illustration, design & tech, film, food & craft, games, music, and publishing. Furthermore, Kickstarter.com designates some projects as a “Project We Love”—meaning that they get extra promotion and a badge.

This section describes our data collection and variable construction process. Our analysis incorporates several categories of variables: campaign outcome metrics, visual and textual racial cues, and other observable campaign characteristics.

## Data Collection

Information about Kickstarter.com campaigns is scraped and stored at WebRobots.io (WebRobots, 2014). We acquired this data for the entire set of projects based in the United States whose completion dates had occurred as of December 2014. From this dataset, we extracted the project outcome metrics (binary success and number of backers) and observable features of campaign pages, including the sources of racial cues shown in Figure 1. We removed any projects with unusually low or high fundraising goals: less than \$100 or more than \$250,000.

One of the goals of this work is to estimate the extent to which creator-based and product-based racial cues affect Kickstarter campaign outcomes. Because racial identity is not explicitly stated on campaign pages, and these cues comprise our focal independent variables, we classified the racial cues using predictive modeling techniques.

## Visual Racial Identifiers

We derived visual creator-based and product-based racial cues from Kickstarter projects’ profile and campaign pictures, as shown in Figure 1. We used the Face++ application (Face++, 2017) to estimate the photo subjects likely races as African American, White, or Asian (not mutually exclusive), then verified the results using Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk).<sup>5</sup> In a further preprocessing step, we introduced conditions to remove noise by excluding campaigns with inaccessible photos or multiple racial cues in either photo (3,992 or about 3.5% of the projects). The final dataset consists of 108,752 records. Table 2 shows summary statistics.

## Textual Racial Identifiers

We derive textual creator-based and product-based racial cues from the fundraiser’s profile name and the campaign text description, respectively. Figure 1 shows the locations of these cues.

## Name-Based Identity

We used a publicly available database of given (first) names matched with ethnicities (Tzioumis, 2018) to estimate the racial association of each creator’s name. A name is assigned a racial classification if the percent of individuals with the name who are of a specific race is greater than the overall percentage of that race in the database. 69% of the names in our dataset correspond with one of the names in the database.<sup>6</sup> We also repeated this analysis using surnames and the 2010 Census data and the results are similar.

## Text Feature Metrics

The description of a crowdfunding project has been found to directly contribute to its probability of success (Gorbatai & Nelson, 2015; Mitra & Gilbert, 2014). Furthermore, differences in success rates across racial groups are mainly driven by a perceived difference in project quality, and linguistic elements have been previously used to control for project quality across racial groups (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Thus, we summarize some linguistic elements of project descriptions in order to both control for the racial association of a project description and investigate the effect of racial association on project outcomes.

Kickstarter campaign pages describe the creators, products, and “rewards,” or gifts that a potential backer will receive in exchange for funding the project, depending on their contribution amount. We scraped the full HTML code of every project’s page in early November 2015 and extracted the description text as well as the reward information. After applying some rudimentary preprocessing techniques, we summarized the rewards via the number of different reward levels available and the minimum contribution required in order to receive a gift, recorded the presence of a video on the page, and computed characteristics related to the sophistication of each project description.

We also found that some words in the project descriptions are correlated with the race of the project creator and subject. We developed three variables that indicate the degree to which the words in a project’s description are correlated with the visual cues of each racial group. These three variables are denoted as the Asian linguistic indicator (ALI), African American linguistic indicator (BLI), and White linguistic indicator (WLI), respectively. For instance, a high ALI indicates that the project description contains more words that are associated with projects having Asian creator and/or project pictures and fewer words indicating non-Asian creator or project pictures.<sup>7</sup> Each project’s LI scores are 1 if their LI score is greater than the mean for that group and 0 otherwise.

## Methodology

We analyzed the relationship between racial cues and crowdfunding success using the variables described in the prior section. The first sets of hypotheses, H1 and H2, cover the creator-based and product-based racial cues for two different groups, African Americans and Asians.

## Figure 2. Success Rates by Category

Our identification strategy was to create matched samples of the projects in order to control for observable differences in project characteristics. The benefit of using a matched sample approach is that it controls for observable differences in projects and addresses possible alternative explanations for the disparities in project outcomes across racial groups.

We used propensity score matching (PSM) techniques that match the projects in the treatment and control groups based on their propensity to be in the treatment group (Austin, 2011; Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983; Sekhon, 2011). The propensity of being in the treatment group was estimated using the observable covariates (other racial cues and other project features). We matched the projects from the treatment and control groups based on their propensity to be in the treatment group. Once the data were divided into treatment and control groups, we estimated a propensity score using a logistic regression model. Equation (1) describes the propensity score model.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {l o g i t \left(\mathrm{P} \big (T r e a t m e n t _ {i j} = 1 \big)\right) = \alpha + \beta_ {1} G o a l _ {i} +} \\ & {\beta_ {2} G e n d e r _ {i} + \sum_ {r} \beta_ {r} R a c e C o v a r i a t e s _ {i} + \sum_ {k} \beta_ {k} T e x t _ {i} +} \\ & {\sum_ {g} \beta_ {g} R e g i o n _ {i} + \sum_ {y} \beta_ {y} Y e a r _ {i} + \sum_ {c} \beta_ {c} C a t e g o r y _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Treatment in Equation (1) refers to the propensity for project i to be in treatment condition j. Goal is the target for the project i. Gender is an indicator variable for the creator’s gender for project i. RaceCovariates are the other racial cues r not associated with the treatment condition j: in the creator photo, creator name, campaign photo, and/or the campaign description. Text is the set of text features k for the campaign description: positive, negative, sentence counter, average sentence length, reading ease, grade level, average syllables. Region denotes the region g of the projects: South Atlantic, Mid-Atlantic, Eastern Central, Western Central, Pacific,

Mountain, New England. Category is the main category c for the project: performance, technology, visual arts, writing. Year is the set of indicators for each value of year y.

We used probability estimates to create a propensity score for each project and employed one-to-one matching to match each campaign in the treatment condition with a campaign in the control condition based on the propensity score differences below 1<sup>-05</sup>. This methodology led to matched datasets in which the treated and control conditions are statistically the same based on observable characteristics, allowing us to isolate the relationship between treatment and success probability.

We examined creator-based and product-based racial cues across two mediums as well as two racial groups. White racial cues served as the control group for both treatments. The unmatched data were restricted to projects in which the racial cue was either African American/Asian or White. In the matched datasets, the treatment was 1 if the racial cue was the minority group, and 0 otherwise. Using the propensity scores, we matched the treatment and control groups for each medium and racial group, leading to eight distinct datasets. The matched datasets were used to evaluate the effect of racial cues on project outcomes. Appendix B shows that the observable characteristics are matched (not statistically different) for each treatment and control group in all eight datasets.

Goal is a numeric variable that controls for the amount that the creator is requesting. Gender is the gender in the creator photo. We also ran a negativebinomial regression and found results consistent with our logtransformed OLS estimates. The OLS specification provided better goodness-of-fit measures, and the results are consistent with our findings for the main success measure.

## H4: Anonymity

Lastly, we analyze H4 to understand projects that are racially anonymous, i.e., devoid of racial cues. ## Summary

Table 10 contains a summary of our hypotheses and whether our findings support them. H1a is supported, suggesting that creator-based racial cues for African Americans are significantly and negatively associated with success. Creatorbased racial cues for Asians are significantly associated with reduced success for the name medium but not the photo medium, partially supporting H1b. Product-based African American cues are negatively associated with success across both mediums, supporting H2a. There is a significant positive relationship for product-based racial cues in the photo medium only, generating partial support for H2b. When creator-based and product-based racial cues are not aligned, success probability is more closely aligned with the product-based racial cues, supporting H3. We also found support for H4, that racial anonymity is associated with higher success rates.

## Robustness

We employed a number of robustness checks for our analyses. We considered same-group preferences as a mechanism for the effect of racial cues on crowdfunding success. Exogenous attributes such as location may be correlated with racial cues but are not as likely to correlate with crowdfunding success; thus, we examined our results through the lens of location. We also note that the inclusion of racial cues may be subject to selection bias, so we employed a selection model. Finally, we include a discussion of the effect of Latinx ethnic identity.

## Same-Group Preferences

A possible alternative explanation of the results we see is that within-group preferences of the majority racial group of backers drive disparate success rates. The phenomenon of preferring similar individuals to oneself is known as homophily (MacPherson et al., 2001). For a subset of successful projects, Kickstarter.com displays a sample of backers’ names on its “Community” tab. We scraped these names for 21,440 successful projects in November 2017. We then estimated the likely race of each backer in the dataset using a name-based classifier.

Figure 4 shows the probability of a backer from each racial group contributing to a project associated with a member of each racial group, based on either the creator-based or product-based visual cue. These probabilities are indexed by the proportion of projects created by members of that group; the reference lines at 1 represent the expected probability of backing. Values of greater than 1 represent higher-thanexpected probabilities of backings. Perfect homophily would be represented by zero values for backings across different racial groups.

For both creator-based and product-based cues, all racial groups appear to have some in-group preference, perhaps reflecting a degree of homophily. Asian and African American backers are more likely to back same-race creators and projects. These results are consistent with our main analysis. Some degree of homophily would be expected, given that potential backers would presumably not rely on stereotypes as much when evaluating members of their own racial group. On the other hand, homophily is unlikely to explain our results completely. White backers are actually less likely than would be expected to back projects with White signals in product pictures and more likely to contribute to projects with Asian cues. African American backers are more likely than would be expected to back projects from Asian creators and projects with Asian racial cues.

## Location and Selection

Race is often correlated with other factors, so we examined location as an exogenous variable that is associated with race but not necessarily tied to success. We choose crowdfunding campaigns from three cities—Portland, Atlanta, and San Francisco—as cities with high populations of each racial group according to the U.S. Census. Further, our linguistic analysis reveals that these cities also emerge as indicative of a particular race in the text description. We reran our analysis, with the treatment variable as these racially indicative locations rather than the creator-based racial cues. Our findings from this analysis are consistent with our main conclusions. Projects tied to Portland raised significantly more money than average, whereas projects associated with Atlanta raised significantly less money than average.

## Latinx Ethnicity

Latinx ethnic identity represents an important area for future work and has complex origins. As noted earlier, U.S. government policy treats Latinx as an ethnic group rather than a racial group due to geopolitical concerns (Cong Li et al., 2013; Hayes-Bautista & Chapa, 1987; Padilla, 1984; U.S. Census Bureau, 2011); however, the multinational Latinx community has not achieved consensus regarding the bounds of Latinx identity as a race, ethnicity, or place of origin (Demby, 2014; Padilla, 1984) or regarding its preferred terminology (Calderón, 1992; Demby, 2014; Hayes-Bautista & Chapa, 1987). Across multiple surveys, Latinx respondents were mixed between those who identified Latinx as a part of their racial identity and those who identified their race as either White or Black (Demby, 2014; López & Gonzalez-Barrera, 2016; Tafoya, 2004). One of the most prevalent racioethnic identities in the U.S. is Afro-Latinx; in a 2016 study, 25% of Latinx respondents self-identified as Afro-Latinx (López & Gonzalez-Barrera, 2016). The intersection between racial and ethnic dynamics provides a compelling opportunity for future work to delve deeper into the effects of ethnic identity.

To that end, we performed a preliminary analysis. Prior research has shown that Latinx ethnic identity is often associated with negative stereotypes about individuals, products, and purchasing power (e.g., Chung & Launkay, 2005; Cong Li et al., 2015; King et al., 2006); therefore, we would ex ante expect that Latinx ethnic identity in crowdfunding cues would be associated with similar outcomes to African American racial identity. Using a namebased classifier, we compared the success rates for projects with Latinx vs. non-Latinx names. We found that projects with name-based Latinx ethnic cues were less likely to be successful across all racial groups. Thus, projects with creatorbased Latinx ethnic cues also appear subject to similar negative stereotypes to African American cues. However, these early findings would need to be confirmed in follow-up work related to the effects of ethnic cues.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Summary and Contributions

This study compared the effects of creator-based and productbased racial cues on peer-to-peer platforms. We employed a multimethod approach combining predictive modeling technologies and a large-scale observational study of more than 100,000 crowdfunding campaigns. We automatically categorized creator-based and product-based racial cues in pictures using facial classification software. We classified creator-based and product-based racial cues in the text medium as well. For product-based racial cues, we employed text analytics techniques that differentiate word choices among racial groups, quantified via the BLI, WLI, and ALI variables. For creator-based racial cues, we automatically classified first and last names based on a dictionary of race and names. With these quantified racial cues, we used propensity score design to understand the influence of racial cues on crowdfunding outcomes. Our results build upon past findings on African American racial cues on crowdfunding and other digital platforms. We found that all racial cues associated with African Americans—creator-based or product-based, photo or text medium—are associated with worse outcomes. Our findings align with prior research that African Americans obtain lower success on peer-to-peer platforms (Cui et al., 2019; Edelman et al., 2017; Mejia & Parker, 2019; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). In comparing the magnitude of the cues, we found that product-based cues are associated with the largest difference in the outcomes for African Americans for attracting backers.

Our results also build upon prior studies by hypothesizing about racial cues associated with Asian identity on digital platforms. We found that product-based Asian cues in the campaign description correlate with an increased probability of success whereas some creator-based Asian racial cues, i.e., creators with more distinctively Asian names, are associated with significantly lower levels of success. We found no evidence that Asian cues in the photo medium affected success for creator-based or product-based racial cues.

Collectively, these findings highlight the multifaceted way in which race influences behavior on digital platforms, that racial groups have varied experiences on digital platforms, and the trade-offs inherent in racial self-disclosure. This study addresses several gaps in the literature and provides avenues for future work.

First, we theorize about the multiple ways that racial identity can be inferred on digital platforms by comparing creatorbased and product-based cues of race. Although we use the term “creator-based” racial cues to be contextually specific to crowdfunding, our results could generalize to any person creating or selling a product on a digital platform. For instance, the host of an Airbnb listing, the fundraiser for a GoFundMe campaign, or even a social media influencer may each experience the same dynamic between creator-based and product-based racial cues on their respective platforms. Each prior study on racial bias on digital platforms has only examined a single set of racial cues and has often focused on a single medium. Our study compares racial cues associated with both creators and products, and we find a nuanced relationship between racial cues and success. Our results indicate that success outcomes can vary within the same racial group for creator-based and product-based cues. The results also highlight the importance of studying more than two racial groups.

Second, the extant literature on racial identity on digital platforms focuses on two racial groups, but this dichotomy does not explain whether all people of color face similar racial bias or provide guidance on how other racial groups could mitigate bias on digital platforms. Our research suggests that distinctly Asian names are associated with lower success outcomes, whereas Asian linguistic indicators are associated with higher success outcomes. Thus, the implications for Asian creators are consistent with the implications for African American creators in that Asian creators should choose less distinctively Asian names on the digital platform (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Likewise, our findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that the same person is perceived more positively with a white name versus a distinctively Asian name (Zhao & Biernat, 2017). Future research could consider the ethical and psychological implications of requiring creators to remove their distinctive names in order to achieve success on digital platforms.

Third, we examine how to quantify racial cues in an observational study on digital platforms. Prior research has applied the cues ex ante, but our study considers how to assess and quantify racial cues to understand their impact. Crowdfunding and other digital platforms may want to automatically identify users’ racial cues to statistically test whether members of each racial group receive similar treatment. Our paper provides several methods to assess race in an automated, large-scale manner. For example, by using observational data, we discovered that the linguistic elements that are most predictive of racial groups are interpretable as cultural or geographic project topics, (i.e., African, gospel or Asian) rather than sociolinguistic differences in speech patterns (Labov & Harris, 1986; Massey & Lundy, 2001).

Fourth, we critically examine the recommended strategy of racial anonymity that suggests the concealment of users’ unique attributes, e.g., their name and face, in favor of nonracial photos and language, e.g., an assimilated name and a cityscape. Although we find that racial anonymity increases success for people of color, this strategy can raise ethical quandaries. Prior research focusing on discrimination against African Americans has suggested that digital platforms could deemphasize sources of racial cues (Edelman et al., 2017) and/or users could conceal their racial identity (Ayres et al., 2015; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Although racial anonymity does dampen disparities, creators of particular types of campaigns, e.g., a hip-hop album, may not be able to eliminate all racial cues. Therefore, even if creators conceal race in their creator profile, other campaign elements may contain implicit or explicit racial cues, concurring with research that private traits are inferable online (Kosinski et al., 2013).

## Limitations and Future Work

There are important limitations to this study. First, our study focuses exclusively on racial cues and does not inspect ethnicity-related cues or the intersection of racial and ethnic identity. This decision was based on prior work and policy. However, Latinx ethnicity is an important social identity with its own particular place and significance in U.S. society. The influence of ethnic cues and the intersection of race and ethnicity would be fascinating questions to study in potential future work.

Second, we did not study the pronunciation effect of names. Easy-to-pronounce names are viewed more positively than names that are difficult to pronounce (Laham et al., 2012), and we did not examine the difference between distinctly Asian names that are easy for English speakers to pronounce, e.g., Jing or Min, and names that are more difficult, e.g., Xiohan or Zhiruo. Future research could examine the intersection of distinctively racial names and pronunciation.

Third, we cannot definitely rule out that African American and Asian creators’ lower success rates for certain racial cues were driven by lower project quality. It is important to note that we did not uncover any evidence of disparities in project quality in any of the observable variables that we measured, including numerous quality proxies such as writing grade level and sentiment (aside from the description length). Additionally, it has already been established that given identical projects, potential backers are more likely to contribute to a White creator than to an African American one (Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018).

Fourth, as mentioned above, we removed projects from our dataset that contained either campaign or profile pictures with multiple racial cues. The sample size of such pictures in our dataset was small and any subsequent analysis was insufficiently robust. However, studying the effects of multiple signals within a single image could provide an interesting avenue for future work.

Finally, we note that there are limitations to our measurement of racial cues. Although we verified our model-based classifiers using Amazon Mechanical Turk, no classifier is 100% accurate. The racial cues did not include individuals’ self-identified race. Since we were interested in the effect of inferred or perceived race, we find this limitation to be acceptable for this study; however, we did not necessarily fully capture the effects of race or racial identity.

## Conclusion

This study refines our understanding of racial dynamics on digital platforms, addresses previously unanswered questions about racial cues, and raises new questions about racial cues on crowdfunding platforms. Because we conducted an observational study, we were able to observe creators’ and backers’ actual behaviors and uncover systematic interactions that we would not have been able to discover otherwise. Furthermore, we provide a method to identify and quantify existing racial cues rather than introducing those cues in a lab environment. Our work contributes to the information systems literature by investigating multifaceted racial cues on crowdfunding platforms, expanding racial groups, and examining racial anonymity. This study adds to a growing discourse on unpacking the antecedents and consequences of race-based bias in technology-enabled settings.

## Technical Details of Racial Identifiers

## MTurk Study of Face++ Accuracy

To verify Face++’s racial classification accuracy, we conducted two tests on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). In the first, we randomly selected 2000 creator photos, and in the second, we selected 1000 creator photos that Face++ scored as having the lowest confidence levels for its racial predictions. Two independent workers were asked to classify the race of the individual in each photo from a list that included African American, White, Asian, Latinx, unknown, no faces, and combinations of two or more races. About 86% of the photos had agreement between the two raters, and among photos where the two raters agreed, Face++’s African American and White classifications were about 93% accurate. Because Face++’s estimates of photo subjects’ races were acceptably accurate, we operationalize the creator and project photo race as the perceived race(s) using the software.

## Constructing Name-Based Identity

We categorized creator name by race using a dataset from the Harvard Dataverse (Tzioumis, 2018). The dataset uses publicly available home mortgage data in the U.S. to match given (first) names with races and ethnicities. Each first name is represented as a vector of normalized frequency count for each particular ethnicity (e.g., White, African American, Asian-Pacific Islander (API), Latinx, Asian, or mixed-race) on the mortgage applications. We focused on African American and Asian names.

In order to analyze the names, we removed special characteristics. In cases where there were two creator names listed in the creator profile (e.g., Vanessa and Denise), only the first name (e.g., Vanessa) was included in the dataset. We assigned each name to an ethnicity according to majority rules, i.e., if the normalized frequency count was greater than or equal to 50 out of 100, then the name was assigned to that particular ethnicity. We assigned a category of “unknown” for creators whose names were absent from the database and a category of ambiguous to names for which ther was no single majority (i.e., each individual race was below 50).

This dataset of first names includes 69% of names in our database. Many of the creators are bands or organizations, and those names do not carry the same name-based racial cues. Of the names in our dataset, the vast majority of names are associated with White (95%). Only 214 names are distinctly African American and only 798 names are distinctly Asian.

As a robustness check, we considered the surname ethnicity according to the 2010 U.S. Census. We found a higher number of African Americans were identified using this methodology (1977) and more Asian (1715). We also found a similar number of unknown names: 28%. We also found fewer White names (about 24,000), likely because 73.7% of surnames are not classified according to the 2010 Census data. As such, our main analysis used the cues associated with first names.

## Linguistic Indicators

We needed to operationalize the construct of linguistic racial cues in Kickstarter projects. There are some words and phrases in Kickstarter project descriptions whose presence is strongly indicative of one of the racial groups that we study. Therefore, we used these words to create three linguistic indicator (LI) scores: African American linguistic indicator (BLI), Asian linguistic indicator (ALI), and White linguistic indicator (WLI). These scores represent the degree of correlation that a project’s description has with the racial group of interest.

At a high level, we found words whose presence was most highly correlated with the presence of an African American (Asian, White) face in either the profile or campaign photo. These words are useful as an interpretable dictionary of mostly geographic and cultural signifiers for racial groups. We had an additional goal of converting these words into scores, which represent the likelihood of a project containing an African American (Asian, White) face, based on the words in the description. These scores are used in our analysis as a measure of racial signal in the description.

We started with a subset of the Kickstarter projects that Face++ was highly confident contained at least one face that is either African American, Asian, or White. We further restricted the data to include projects containing faces of only one race. This subset of data contains approximately 58,000 projects. We then split the data into 80% training and 20% test data for validation.

Let a binary indicator variable $b _ { i }$ be created such that

$$
b _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} 1, \text {   if   project   } i \text {   contains   at   least   one   African   American   (White,   Asian)   face } \\ 0, \text {   otherwise } \end{array} \right.
$$

We trained predictive models to predict $b _ { i }$ given the bigrams in project i’s description. We used L<sub>2</sub>-regularized logistic regression classifiers because they yielded superior performance in a preliminary analysis. We tuned the regularization parameter using 10-fold cross-validation. Figure A1 reports the AUCs for the predicted vs. actual $b _ { i }$ values on the 20% held-out test data. AUC of 1 indicates perfect binary predictions; AUC of 0.5 indicates random performance. The AUCs on held-out projects are higher than 0.5, indicating that the LI scores are correlated with visually indicated race. Although the scores are relatively low, we believe that this makes sense, as not all projects associated with a racial group necessarily contain a racial signal. To further validate our models, we inspected the most predictive words for each racial group. Table A1 contains a list of some of the top words for each racial group.

Unsurprisingly, most of these top words are geographic or cultural indicators. We performed an Amazon Mechanical Turk study to validate that these words are interpretable. Three independent MTurk workers were asked to choose the group which they most identify with each of the most predictive words in our sample. Table A2 presents the results of this study. These results give us confidence that our LI variables are a reasonable operationalization of the racial signal in Kickstarter project descriptions. Finally, the logistic regression models which were trained on the subset of clearly labeled data were used to generate predictions for the entire set of Kickstarter projects. Table A3 contains examples of high LI score projects for each racial group.
