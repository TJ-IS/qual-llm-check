## 1. Introduction

## a b s t r a c t

Research has shown that preexisting individual biases about a product can have negative effects on future purchase behavior or use. While extensively studied in marketing, the role of informational blocking with regard to decision making about information technologies has not been investigated. This research explores the interplay of biases as a form of information blocking and explores these biased-blocking effects in the context of technology. Results show that while different types of experience have a significant effect on the decision to use a technology product, this effect is completely blocked by the preconceived bias of the individual about the technology.

Why are Apple Stores so successful? There are a number of reasons for their success, but clearly Ron Johnson's supposition is that the experiences that these environments offer for potential and existing customers represent an important part of the equation. In many ways, the Apple Store experience is designed to prime a customer to develop a favorable attitude about not only the software and technology in the store, but the environment and brand associated with the product. In other words, the store experience is designed to “bias” the customer in favor of the Apple brand and products. This type of bias in individual preference is also seen in non-tangible technology goods including experience goods such as Internet browsers (Internet Explorer versus Firefox) or even in credence goods (such as IPad repair shops, auto mechanics, or diet plans) [1]. This is important because whether we are discussing a choice between two different brands of cell phones, operating systems, or ERP systems, decision makers will carry into the decision-making process preexisting knowledge and biases that influence their attitudes and purchasing behaviors. As a result, products that have similar or even superior features might be ignored because the existing biases preclude the decision maker from evaluating all of the products equally. Of course, this begs the question of why some individuals will reflexively count out a particular software product and, perhaps more importantly, what the nature of biases and experiences are that influence an individual to block out the full evaluation of other alternatives. For software and technology vendors, brand-related biases are significant and, as suggested by the Apple Store experience, an important question is whether and how biases can be overcome through usage experience or persuasion.

Bias [2] and blocking [3] have each been examined to account for the phenomenon of cognitively configuring or reshaping information about products, and hence attitudes pertaining to products or services [4,5]. Specifically, research on bias has shown that if consumers are presented with a product brand along with quality information (positive or negative) regarding that brand, later introduction of attributes pertaining to the product itself are altered by the earlier brand quality information [5]. We argue that individual actions based on these biases are one type of blocking, which occurs when the subject actually blocks subsequent information based on a bias in favor of (or against) the product.<sup>1</sup> With regard to information systems adoption decisions, this biasedblocking may affect the level of acceptance of a particular alternative by certain individuals. Biased-blocking may also help to explain why certain individuals accept a particular technology based solely on the technology brand. Information systems acceptance has classically been seen as a rational process [6,7], with later studies exploring other determinants outside of the rational domain [8,9], but no studies have explored the influence of biased-blocking in this decision-making process. As a result, a better understanding of the relationship between biases, the blocking process, and resultant behavioral intentions would provide managers and technology marketers with information to assist in designing, building, and deploying information technologies.

Previous research in technology consumption/adoption has investigated some of these non-traditional processes that affect information systems adoption. Yang and Yoo found “considerable influence” of affective attitude on usage intention and called for more attention to the construct [10]. More recently Djamasbi et al. found that positive mood is a significant predictor of technology acceptance [11], while Luo found that positive affective attitude also significantly impacted behavioral intent [12]. Clearly, this work on affect indicates that there are other factors in play beyond the traditional acceptance model, and marketing literature leads us to believe that information bias and information blocking may also have a significant impact on our understanding of technology adopters' decision mechanisms. Since bias is often linked to brand perceptions, we also further extend the IS brand literature [13,14] by investigating this information bias as it pertains to brand and its impact on technology usage intentions.

The purpose of our research is to examine the relationship between bias and blocking in the context of information technology adoption decisions. To examine these issues, we first propose a theoretical framework incorporating the concepts of bias and blocking. The review leading to this model will provide a basis for understanding not only the bias and blocking processes individually, but it will also provide a conceptual framework for linking these two constructs into a process we call biased-blocking. In doing so, we discuss situational conditions that enable biased-blocking to occur as well as define the valence direction of the effects. Additionally, we examine this phenomenon under varying informational assessment scenarios that are defined by the type of exposure that the subject is given to the technology. To evaluate these questions, we use an experimental study to examine how bias and blocking work together (i.e. biased-blocking) to influence intention decisions when subjects are presented with a novel product through either a “hands on” or vicarious-only exposure to the product. The paper concludes with a discussion of the findings, implications for theory and practice, and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Background

Much of what we know about the processes of bias and blocking in relation to product adoption decision-making comes from research in marketing. For example, researchers have explored how both of these processes affect satisfaction within consumer behavior (see [15] for an extensive review of this literature). This literature is relevant to the study of adoption of information systems because the consumer decision-making process for a variety of products parallels that of the processes used to select and acquire various information systems.<sup>2</sup> Nevertheless, the processes by which bias and blocking should influence the acceptance of software also include unique factors relative to how these processes have been shown to operate in marketing. For example, users are often exposed to new software technologies early in development through alpha and beta testing. While a physical product vendor might pre-release prototypes of products or demonstration videos on a crowd funding site, it is unlikely that the scale of distribution or use will be as large as it would be for software because of the higher replication and distribution costs for physical products. For example, eight months before its release, over one million people had downloaded the Windows 10 Technical Preview, which suggests that many people had exposure to the software prior to the release of the final product.<sup>3</sup> Given this, our research is designed to explore the impact of both bias and blocking on attitudes and decisions made by potential technology adopters.

To develop our framework, we review the literature from marketing as well as from information systems to develop a theoretical model detailing the role of bias and blocking on intention decisions with regard to information technologies. In our model (see Fig. 1), four major constructs or events are represented. First, biases, which come from external information sources such as advertising, word-of-mouth, or other sources of information about the specific product [4]. These types of biases contribute to product evaluation, as the subject's biases affect how they will interpret the product's characteristics.

Second, the decision maker experiences the product that is the current focus of his or her evaluation. In practice, if the product is new to the decision maker, he or she will often be given an opportunity to learn about the product through direct experience. This experience can be obtained either through hands-on use of the product or by observing others engaged in use [16]. In the context of information systems evaluations, these two modes of experience are commonly used in retail venues where, for example, an Apple Store sales representative might explain and demonstrate use of the technology and follow this with an invitation to “test drive” information technology and software.

The third component in the model is the potential blocking of secondary product information.<sup>4</sup> This occurs when extant information that the decision maker holds about the product causes him or her to block secondary information about the product [5]. Our model suggests that a decision maker's final product intentions are dependent on how the bias and blocking processes operate to determine the final evaluative information used in their decision-making. As outlined below, theory suggests that the manner by which these factors are brought together will have varied effects on whether and how the decision maker evaluates each in his or her consideration of the product.

Finally, the user will make a decision whether or not they would use the product. While the marketing literature typically looks at buying behavior, use intention has been extensively used as a dependent variable in the IS literature. If the blocking effect holds, then the preliminary bias will mask the effects of experience such that bias will provide the sole influence on use intention.

## 2.1. Bias

Bias refers to the tendency for individuals to perceive information that is consistent with personal attitudes as more valid than information that is inconsistent with personal attitudes [2,4]. Bias has been shown to affect individual attitudes, with those holding stronger attitudes toward a particular product tending to resist attitude change [17–19]. When individuals adhere to an existing, strongly held bias, they tend to resist persuasive efforts such as marketing promotions and other informational campaigns [4]. This relationship between bias and attitude change is important to understand for marketing purposes because individuals who hold a strong attitude about a brand or product type may be harder to convince to adopt or use a different product [4].

Of course, for many products an individual may be entirely unfamiliar with its features or brand. In such a scenario, because the individual has no experiential history with the product, he or she will possess little or no pre-formed attitudes about the product. As a result, all of his or her attitudes will be formed by external sources of bias, such as information from advertising or word of mouth. These biases will, of course, shape the expectations that the individual holds about the product. The Expectancy Disconfirmation Model is useful in understanding how these biasinduced expectations relate to attitudes such as product satisfaction [20,21]. Specifically, this model suggests that attitudes about satisfaction form when consumers compare their personal perceptions of a product's performance to preexisting performance expectations [20,21]. Positive disconfirmation occurs when perceived performance exceeds consumer expectations while negative disconfirmation occurs when performance falls short of consumer expectations [21–23]. Satisfaction with a product derived from disconfirmation develops after 1) an expectation pertaining to a product is established in the individual (e.g. through biased information) and 2) the individual has subsequently experienced or observed the product in action [24].

Research suggests that expectations developed through marketercontrolled messaging (e.g. induced positive or negative bias with regard to a product) can be described as a marketer- supplied, persuasionbased expectation [25–27]. Positively biased messaging leads to higher expectations that are more likely to be negatively disconfirmed. On the other hand, negatively biased messaging leads to lower expectations that are more likely to be positively disconfirmed [23,28]. In other words, by setting the bar too high/low, consumers will experience disconfirmation of their expectations of the product and thereby develop satisfaction levels opposite to the valence of the expectations set.

Product characteristics have been investigated in the context of expectancy disconfirmation as a predictor of satisfaction with software and technology [29–31]. While satisfaction is important in predicting some forms of behavior, we have elected to use behavioral intent as our dependent measure because it is a more widely used outcome variable in research examining adoption decisions pertaining to of information systems. To our knowledge, this use of behavioral intent in the context of expectancy disconfirmation has not been previously used, and we feel this is an important gap that needs to be filled because the nature of the relationship between pre-existing attitudes and intent has not been fully resolved. For example, while the role of affective attitude on behavioral intention was discussed extensively during the development and testing of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), from which TAM was derived, argues that beliefs influence behavior only via their indirect influence on attitudes [32,33]. Nevertheless, some scholars suggest that attitudes represent a co-determinant of behavioral intent [34] while others have suggested that attitudes should be viewed as antecedents of beliefs [35]. While the original articulation of TAM postulates that people form behavioral intentions toward those items for which they have positive affect [7], attitude was later removed from the TAM model. Nevertheless, Davis [6] referred to the attitude-behavior relationship as an open issue, citing scholars who believed that a substantive relationship exists between attitudinal affect and behavioral intent. In summary, the literature suggests that the relationship between affective attitudes and behavioral intention is important and, therefore, we suggest that similar processes that have been shown to influence attitudes such as satisfaction will also influence behavioral intentions. This has been shown in previous research where both perceived usefulness [29,30,36] and perceived ease-of-use [37–39] have both been found to have a significant effect on satisfaction with these same predictor variables of perceived usefulness and perceived ease-of-use predicting intent in countless IS studies. Given this link between attitudinal affect and both satisfaction and behavioral intent, the use of behavioral intent as a dependent variable is warranted and is expected to be driven by similar bias mechanisms as is satisfaction and other attitudes.<sup>5</sup>

In a primary product evaluation, the Expectancy Disconfirmation Model suggests that those individuals presented with biases that are negative in tone would show a higher usage intention toward the technology after having a direct experience with the technology (i.e., either seeing it used or using it). Alternatively, individuals who are presented with biases that are positive in tone would show a lower level of overall behavioral intent after having experience with the technology. Given this, we hypothesize…

H<sub>1</sub>. Bias and individual behavioral intent to use the product will be negatively related.

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 a } } .$ A positive bias toward a particular technology will negatively affect individual behavioral intent to use the product

$\bf { H } _ { 1 b } .$ A negative bias toward a particular technology will positively affect individual behavioral intent to use the product.

## 2.2. Blocking

The concept of blocking was originally described by behavioral psychologists as a process that would occur in the context of classical conditioning. Blocking asserts that the learning of one predictive cue can inhibit or “block” the learning of subsequent predictive cues [5]. More formally, a relationship between a conditioned stimulus (i.e., information about an object) and an unconditioned stimulus (i.e., the object itself) is learned before a later second stimulus (i.e., subsequent information about the object). When blocking occurs, the relationship between the unconditioned stimulus and the second conditioned stimulus will not be “learned” by the subject [3]. Put more simply, when a subject is first conditioned to form an attitude (e.g., through exposure to a bias), blocking will cause subsequent stimuli to be ignored in favor of the preconditioned bias, even if the new information is highly relevant to the subject's decision context [5].

This same type of relationship has been shown to exist with regard to consumer learning. Research has shown that if product brand is initially paired with a description of the quality of the product, subsequent information about product attributes are blocked by the subject [5]. Given this, if the subject initially learns that a particular product brand is of high quality, subsequent exposure to attributes about that product, whether positive or negative, will not impact the subject's initial perception of product quality (as determined by preconditioning). Put another way, this concept suggests that first impressions are influential

and persistent, even when subsequent relevant information is made explicitly available.

Previous research on blocking in the context of classical conditioning [3] and marketing [5] view the introduction of secondary attributes or subsequent information as invariant; in other words, they always use a simple passive exposition (i.e., an advertisement) to disseminate secondary attributes. While research has investigated the use of various types of decision aids prior to the buying decision, these decision aids are all passive in nature [42,43]. Research has shown that subject learning (and thus subject apprehension of new product information) can vary greatly depending on the mode of subject experience. Bandura [16] suggests that the two most effective means of providing experience are: 1) a hands-on enactive mastery experience, where the subject personally interacts with the product or object, or; 2) a vicarious experience, where the subject learns through observation of another's modeling of task behaviors with the product or object.<sup>6</sup> Bandura states that enactive mastery is most effective in transferring information because the individual is highly engaged with the product or object. Vicarious experience also has the potential to transfer information by allowing an individual to learn through observation, but it is expected to be less effective at transferring information in most contexts [44]. Vicarious experience and enactive mastery represent two common types of experience that can each influence attitudes about a product or object, so it is imperative that these different modes be accounted for in tests of blocking.

The extent of prior experience with a technology has been found to influence usage intention decisions [40]. Previous research has operationalized experience with regard to technology acceptance as the amount of time spent utilizing a technology [9,40] as well as the type of experience [45]. This research explores experience type as a contributing factor toward behavioral intent with more interactive experience leading to more positive effects with regard to usage intention. Stated more formally:

H . Individuals receiving training involving vicarious experience coupled with enactive mastery will have a higher behavioral intent compared to those receiving training involving vicarious experience alone.

## 2.3. Biased-blocking

Both bias and blocking have been demonstrated to affect consumer attitudes toward a particular product or service and subsequent buying decisions [4,5,46]. Nevertheless, while these constructs seem related, no research has explicitly linked the two. We contend that there is a theoretical convergence of bias and blocking, such that when bias is present, it causes informational blocking; we refer to this phenomenon as biased-blocking. Bias will actually block information received subsequently, thereby preventing this new information from influencing ongoing evaluations or decisions by blocking the integration of the new information into product schemas [47–49]. Biased-blocking will also have a blocking impact on an individual's usage intention toward a technology. This impact will be tested in the context of new information integration (through user experience with the technology) such that, while new experiential information alone is significant, bias is hypothesized to be more significant and remove the significance of new experiential information. For this hypothesis to hold, new experiential information integration will first need to be found to significantly influence user intention and then the subsequent inclusion of bias as a variable will need to remove this significance.<sup>7</sup> Stated formally:

H . The effect of bias on behavioral intent will block the effect of subsequent experience type (i.e., there will be no difference in behavioral intent between the vicarious alone versus the combined vicarious/ enactive mastery experiences).

## 3. Data collection

Subjects for this research were solicited from three sections of a core undergraduate business course at a large Midwestern university. Subjects were evenly represented from all majors in the college. The course instructor offered students extra credit for participating in the research. The course instructor was the same in all course sections and had not discussed the stimulus – virtual world technologies – during lectures. A virtual world technology was selected as the focal research artifact because we expected that the subjects would have minimal experience with the specific technology used in the research (i.e., Second Life) and therefore little preexisting bias that could confound the experiment.

While some may view Second Life as the product and not the brand (the parent company of Second Life is Linden Labs), other studies have looked at iconic products that are viewed as the brand themselves. In the seminal work by Jennifer Aaker [50], the author develops a measure of brand personality by identifying different brand groups. The study used brands identified by a previous study [51] that used a national audience to identify 131 brands in 39 categories, which Aaker further verified with multiple samples in her study. These items were identified as brands and specifically contrasted with products in a quote from Keller's seminal work saying “In contrast to ‘product-related attributes,’ which tend to serve a utilitarian function for consumers, brand personality tends to serve a symbolic or self-expressive function” [50,52]. Several brands listed in the paper include Crest toothpaste and Oil of Olay lotion (a product of Proctor and Gamble), Advil pain reliever (product of Pfizer), and Cheerios cereal (product of General Mills) just to name a few. In relation to our research, these seminal pieces on brand provide justification for our use of Second Life as a brand given that several products have been identified as brands in previous research. It is further argued that these brand personality traits can be influenced by direct or indirect contact of the consumer with the brand [53] with the American Marketing Association identifying brand as “experiences with the specific product or service” [54]. Therefore, we again feel justified in our identification of Second Life as a brand through the experience of the individual with the product, which is operationalized as one of the independent variables in our study.

Subjects for this experiment were randomly assigned to both levels of each independent variable. Each research session included around 20 students and lasted for approximately 30 min. Previous research has shown that this amount of time is sufficient to show changes in vicarious alone versus combined vicarious/enactive mastery experiences [45]. The sessions were identical in form with the same facilitator present at each. The same presenter (voice) and content (video) were utilized during all sessions where the content was included in the manipulation. The activities were the same for all sessions and the virtual world environment for each user was standardized to look exactly the same.

The lab experiment was a 2 (positive vs. negative brand information) × 2 (vicarious experience vs. enactive mastery) factorial design.

Brand (Second Life) represents the unconditioned stimulus, while presentation of positive or negative views about the product represents the first conditioned stimulus or bias. Subjects' subsequent experiential learning (i.e., second conditioned stimulus) about the product was operationalized by either passively viewing a product demonstration (i.e., vicarious experience) or by watching and actually using the product (i.e., enactive mastery). While enactive mastery, in its purest sense, would involve individuals interacting with a product through trial and error with no facilitation, traditional hands-on IS training often involves instruction in tandem with hands-on experience [55,56]. Therefore, we have operationalized enactive mastery as involving facilitator modeled training and hands-on experience (i.e. vicarious experience plus enactive mastery) and vicarious experience as solely facilitator modeled training (i.e., the subject merely observes the facilitator demonstrating the product), but will use vicarious experience and enactive mastery as the names of the two conditions for brevity.

Second Life specifically represents a primary product evaluation given the subjects' lack of experience with this or similar products (subjects with Second Life experience were screened out, with potential drop-out bias analyzed in the results section), as well as no previously released family of products related to Second Life or the Second Life brand. The random assignment of subjects within the experimental design also negates any preconceived notions/intentions about the product, which is essential for this scenario.

Our research is examining the matched question of one type of train ing (vicarious experience + enactive mastery) versus another (vicarious experience alone); therefore, this type of matched pair design needs no control (i.e., no training) group [57]. Given the nature of comparing training methods, this type of research design has been used previously in IS research. In his classic work, Fred Davis studied training based on evaluating two graphics systems with no control group [6]. Donald and Donna Davis evaluated training using a lecture-based versus self-directed approach [58]. Venkatesh examined traditional training versus game-based training [59], while Sid Davis examined a 2 (direct manipulation interface versus command-based interface) × 2 (exploration versus instruction-based training) matrix where neither level contained a traditional control group [60]. With regard to bias, in their seminal work (off which our research design was based) van Osselaer and Alba [5] ran six separate experimental studies with varying experimental conditions but where each subject always received brand information that portrayed the brand as having either high or low quality. Our choice to compare two experimental conditions (with regard to training and bias) also mimics that of Compeau and Higgins who say that the decision to compare two conditions without a traditional control group provides a “stricter test of behavior modeling” that allows a comparison of effectiveness between techniques [55].

Fig. 2 is a pictorial representation of the procedures utilized during the sessions. At the beginning of the session, the facilitator introduced himself to the students and briefly described virtual worlds and Second Life. Prior to the introduction of the research stimuli, the subjects were asked to fill out a web-based survey to gauge their pre-session exposure to Second Life, prior use of this and related technologies, and various demographic questions. The subjects were then shown a narrated PowerPoint presentation that included either positive or negative information about the Second Life environment. Both presentations utilized Harvard Business Review and Wall Street Journal articles to provide legitimate arguments supporting the positive or negative description of the Second Life technology. While all statements utilized were true and corroborated by the sources, only positive statements were utilized in the positive manipulation and negative statements in the negative manipulation. The presentation was 5 min in duration and was designed to provide a believable story that would establish a bias about the technology.<sup>8</sup>

Following this, the subjects were presented with a video presentation showing them how to use the technology. The theme of the video tutorial involved interior design. The video presentation showed the user how to navigate in Second Life as well as how to manipulate furniture objects to position the furniture in a particular arrangement. Voiceover for the video also provided verbal instructions in tandem with what the avatar was doing in the video. In the vicarious experience condition, subjects watched the video but could not use the software. Alternatively, in the enactive mastery condition, they watched the same video, but were asked to interact with the Second Life product, actually using avatars in the Second Life environment to mimic the tasks modeled in the video. Users could “slip-on” either a male or female avatar body based on their preference, and they worked within a private Second Life workspace identical to the video's to accomplish their tasks. Once the training session was completed, subjects were asked to fill out a questionnaire measuring attitudes toward the Second Life technology (using the user acceptance measures from UTAUT) [40].<sup>9</sup>

## 5. Discussion

This research provides a number of important contributions. First, the research provides a theoretical model linking the separate constructs of bias and blocking into a combined concept of biased-blocking. Research shows that the independent concepts of bias and blocking both affect consumer attitudes toward product(s)/services(s) and buying decisions related thereto [4,5,46]. This biased-blocking effect can have a substantial impact on consumer buying and use decisions, as it has the potential to thwart pertinent product information (even experiential), even if this information is highly relevant to the specific decision context.

Second, this research provides justification for the biased-blocking effect through an experimental study that uses intention decision as the construct of interest. Results show that an induced bias given to individuals has a blocking effect for experiential information the individual subsequently receives regarding the software. This bias presents itself in line with Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory [22], wherein subjects presented with a negative bias are expected to display a higher level of usage intention toward the product as compared to those presented with a positive bias. Furthermore, the level of experiential interactivity with the focal technology artifact, allowing for greater immersion and contact with the technology, has no effect on this usage intent. This is because, as predicted in our hypotheses, bias fully blocks both the main effect and interaction effects of the experience with the technology.

Theoretically, this research also builds on both the bias and blocking literature by providing a true experimental treatment with regard to bias. Both the bias [4] and blocking [5] literatures have used subjectreported, preexisting attitudes toward a particular product/service as a measure of preexisting viewpoints/biases. While this does not diminish this prior work, this methodology can, at best, provide associative proof of a link between bias, blocking, and subsequent attitudes. We experimentally manipulate bias through random assignment of subjects to treatments, providing causal evidence of a blocking effect of bias on subsequent attitudes toward a product. Furthermore, we account for other contributing attitudes by controlling for these effects, providing even stronger proof of a causal relationship.

This research also extends previous work in the area by using multiple experiential levels with regard to subsequent introduction of attributes (i.e., second conditioned stimulus). Previous blocking research has viewed the secondary apprehension of information as an essentially static introduction of information that is taken up identically for all individuals [5]. Research has also shown that experiential introduction of information (e.g., in either the vicarious experience or enactive mastery contexts) differs from passive verbal presentation of information [68], and provides a more impactful experience. This research utilizes two experimentally manipulated experiential treatments (i.e., vicarious experience and enactive mastery) to introduce secondary product information. This provides a powerful stimulus to the subjects, and more closely simulates real technology decision-making contexts [16,44].

This research is especially intriguing given the counterintuitive nature of the results. Bias is shown to have a substantial effect by completely blocking the effect of secondary experiential information about the product. These findings are completely at odds with wellestablished memory theory (serial position effect theory) that postulates when recalling information, individuals preferentially recall the most recently experienced stimuli (recency effect) compared to those items presented first (primacy effect) [69]. The long-term effects of the bias are strong enough to overpower the short term recency effects of experience, which positions our research as offering an important contribution to understanding the nature of primacy and recency effects when biases are an important part of the stimuli [70–73]. Furthermore, other research offers a more conservative recency effect where this subsequent information (adjustment) helps to adjust the individual's decision in accordance with the primary information (anchor). Our research also contradicts this anchoring and adjustment [74,75] effect by showing that only the anchoring has an effect with no adjustment in the face of subsequent information.

While our analysis provides support for the concept of biasedblocking, it also paints an unexpected and disappointing picture with regard to individual behavior decisions in the presence of preconceived biases. While experience has been shown to affect technology acceptance in previous research [9,40,45,76], our findings suggest that this effect can be thwarted when experience is presented to individuals who hold extant bias. Furthermore, different types of experience are equally, fully blocked by this bias during a primary product evaluation. This is important because it helps to explain why individuals might be less likely to accept an otherwise useful or easy to use technology, even when favorable information or hands on experience should suggest that the technology offers value.

As is the case with many types of first encounters, first impressions matter and are often difficult to overcome. This is a well-known phenomenon with regard to human interactions and our research suggests that first impressions related to information systems use decisions similarly persist and are difficult to change.

## 6. Implications and future work

This research demonstrates that bias can have a blocking impact on subsequent informational experience and the results demonstrate that the effects of experience with a technology can be fully blocked by preconceived bias held by the individual. The research provides promise for better understanding individual usage intention in the context of biases toward a particular technology.

This has important ramifications with regard to the introduction of new technologies and significant upgrades to existing technologies; a proper balance of information about the product is essential to its being well-received. Firms developing new technologies must resist the urge to put too positive a spin on their product, as it may actually cause a negative product evaluation by the consumer [77–79]. In other words, if a product is overhyped it is undoubtedly the case that the product with not meet expectations with the result that subsequent attitudes will be negatively affected.

This work also underscores the significant importance of bias on information systems evaluation. The bulk of earlier work on adoption focused on various permutations of utility and ease of use predicting intent to adopt. Our work shows that even when those factors are present, bias for or against the product affects the adoption decision.

This research suggests many avenues for future work. First, different types of secondary information presentation should be investigated to verify that preconceived bias also blocks a broad range of information. While interesting, our research offers a dismal view of consumers' reliance on preexisting bias; thus, work should be done to find ways to overcome biases or use these biases constructively. Given this, our research highlights the need to focus future research on the cognitive processes associated with bias and blocking and to analyze the role of biasinducing characteristics (e.g., brand, posts on social sites, product reviews, etc.) in information systems adoption and purchase decisions. Furthermore, the effect of biased-blocking on group-based, organizational adoption decisions should be investigated to further explore differences with individual decisions.

## 7. Conclusion

The purpose of our research is to examine whether biases can be overcome through usage experience or persuasion. Our research is premised on the notion that when a decision maker is presented with information about a product (either positive or negative) that biases his or her attitudes, subsequent information will be blocked. The result is that the evaluation made of a product will be strongly influenced by the initial bias and subsequent information will have a minimal effect. To examine these issues, we presented our theoretical framework that is based on the concepts of bias and blocking and the Expectancy Disconfirmation Model. Following this, we empirically examined this theory by manipulating the informational assessment scenarios that were presented to subjects. Specifically, we examined how biased-blocking works in conjunction to influence intentions when subjects were exposed to a new software product by either using the software in a “hands on” manner or observing its use through vicarious exposure. The results support our theoretical model by showing that a bias presented to individuals has a blocking effect for subsequently received experiential information. We discuss these findings in light of other adoption research and discuss the implications of our research for theory and practice. These results offer a unique contribution that helps us better understand the adoption decision-making process and suggests that future research should examine the role of bias in software and technology adoption.
