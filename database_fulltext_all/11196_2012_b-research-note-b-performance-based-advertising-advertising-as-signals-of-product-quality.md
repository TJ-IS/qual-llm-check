## 1. Introduction

Performance-based advertising refers to the practice of online advertising whereby advertisers pay the publisher only when the advertisement generates an “action” (e.g., a click-through or a purchase). This practice differs from the traditional impression-based pricing model for banner ads or TV commercials, in which advertisers are charged for the right to expose the audience to their spots, and their payments are not dependent upon how many consumers the advertisement actually attracts. The performancebased advertising scheme has only recently become practically implementable because new technologies provide an opportunity to tie an exposure to an action that can be counted, tracked, and analyzed in ways that exposure in the traditional mass media cannot (Economist 2006). Search engines, for example, now are able to record the click-throughs generated by a certain online advertisement to measure the ad’s performance. Performance-based advertising is becoming increasingly popular and has been adopted by such industry leaders as Yahoo! (Overture), Google (AdWords and Adsense), MSN (AdCenter), eBay (AdContext), Amazon, and Shopping.com. These advertising models generated more than \$10 billion in 2008, constituting 45% of the total Internet advertising revenue,<sup>1</sup> and are widely credited for the revitalization of the search engine business. Some leading practitioners are moving toward more aggressive performance measures, such as the “costper-action” model proposed by Snap.com, where an action refers to a realized purchase, download, registration, subscription, or lead. A number of merchants are following this trend, including Google, and ZiXXo (pay-per-print), Ingenio (pay-per-call), and others (Economist 2006).

This paper explores the impact of this new advertising format on one basic function of advertising: signaling product quality. More specifically, does the switch to performance-based advertising impact the advertisers’ ability to signal product quality? Is it easier to signal under performance-based advertising compared to traditional impression-based advertising? Will high-quality advertisers spend more on advertising? Under what conditions will the advertiser be better/worse off under performance-based advertising?

We identify several unique issues related to the signaling function of performance-based advertising. The first issue concerns the information available to consumers. When switching to performance-based advertising, two levels of information might be unknown to consumers: (a) Consumers might not be aware of the change in the advertising format and, as a result, they might mistakenly perceive performancebased advertising as impression-based; and (b) consumers might not be able to observe the merchant’s advertising expenditure. It is important to note that under traditional impression-based advertising, the total advertising expenditure (a crucial piece of information required for advertising to signal product quality) is determined in advance and is directly related to the total impression exposure (e.g., air time on TV, the number of lines an ad occupies in newspapers) and is thus observable by the advertisement viewers. Under the performance-based advertising model, however, total advertising spending is determined only after viewers take actions, and thus it is not directly available to consumers when they make purchase decisions. These issues raise some crucial questions: Is it still possible to signal product quality under performance-based advertising? What happens if consumers incorrectly perceive performance-based advertising as traditional impression-based advertising because of a lack of information? To address these issues, we study three cases that differ in the amount of information available to consumers. We show that consumers do not need to know the total advertising expenditure of the merchants. Rather, as long as consumers are informed of the advertising scheme (i.e., that it is performance-based) and are aware of the peraction price, it is possible for advertising to resume its signaling function under the performance-based advertising model.

The second issue concerns the measurement of “advertising performance,” the basic building block of performance-based advertising. Such measures as click-through, download, or purchase are often used to represent a valid response or to measure the performance of an advertisement. Because publishers (e.g., Google.com and Snap.com) often not only host advertisements but also provide direct links to the advertisers’ websites, they attract not only new customers but also existing customers who use the links provided in the publisher’s website to reach their favorite sellers repeatedly. Hence, the actions recorded by Internet media could come from two types of customers: (1) new consumers, whose actions represent responses to advertisements posted on the publisher’s website; and (2) repeat consumers, whose actions represent responses to the quality of the product, because they make repurchases only when they feel satisfied with the product. Therefore, recorded actions can in fact measure a mix of two types of performance: advertising performance and product performance. We address this issue by defining an advertising performance overmeasure factor to capture the extent to which an advertiser’s payment, under performance-based pricing, is overly measured, i.e., the proportion of product performance (repeat purchase) that is counted as advertising performance.

The third issue deals with uncertainty. A major attractiveness of performance-based advertising is uncertainty reduction for advertisers, because the total advertising expenditure is determined only after the demand is realized. It is unclear how, under performance-based advertising, the reduced uncertainty affects the signaling behavior of merchants. For example, should merchants advertise more aggressively because of the reduced risk, or spend less in advertising because they do not need to pay for customers who are not interested? We thus define a demand uncertainty factor, which measures advertisers’ uncertainty about their potential market through advertising, to address this question.

We develop a formal model to explore the signaling function of performance-based advertising, incorporating the three issues discussed above. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first review relevant literature in §2; we then set up our model and compare equilibrium outcomes under the two advertising schemes in §3; finally, we discuss the business implications of adopting the performancebased advertising scheme and present our conclusions in §4.

## 2. Literature Review

The performance-based pricing scheme is attracting increased attention from academic researchers. A fastgrowing literature reports on the optimal design of specific performance-based advertising models, such as “keyword auctions” in major search engines such as Weber and Zheng (2007), Balachander et al. (2009), Agarwal et al. (2008), Feng (2008), Feng et al. (2007a, b), Chen et al. (2009), Hosanagar and Cherepanov (2008), and Liu et al. (2010). These papers, however, focus on the design of the auction mechanism and the corresponding bidding behavior, without considering the relationship between a sponsored result’s quality/relevance and its position/ranking in the search results. Animesh et al. (2007, 2010), and Yang and Ghose (2010) fill this gap and examine whether a higherranked advertiser is likely to be more relevant to consumers. These papers, however, do not consider the signaling behavior of advertisers. They model passive advertisers where high-quality advertisers do not take the initiative to differentiate themselves from low-quality advertisers.

Another research stream examines the publisher’s incentive to promote advertising under different pricing schemes. These papers generally focus on the contract design that motivates the publisher to promote advertising based on a “principal-agent” framework (Hu 2004, Zhao 2005, Sundararajan 2003). These papers, however, focus on the publisher’s performance instead of the advertisers’, under different pricing schemes.

An extensive literature exists on the relation between advertising spending and quality of product/ service (e.g., Gerstner 1985, Shoemaker et al. 2000, Zhao 2000, Zhang 2001, Horstmann and Moorthy 2003) or between pricing schemes and merchant qualities (Choudhary 2007). Since Nelson (1974), it is well recognized that when the quality of a product is difficult to observe before purchase, advertising might signal product quality. A higher-quality merchant spends more on advertising because it offers a higher return, compared to that of a lower-quality merchant, because the initial sale of a higher-quality product is more likely to generate a repeat purchase than will the sale of a lower-quality product (Kihlstrom and Riodan 1984, Milgrom and Roberts 1986).<sup>2</sup> Note that signaling instruments can be characterized as sale independent (such as advertising) or sale contingent (such as slotting allowances) (Kirmani and Rao 2000). However, there is little literature that compares these two signaling functions. Advertising, in our paper, can serve as both a sale-independent signal (under the impression-based pricing) and a sale-contingent signal (under performance-based pricing); it thus offers a unique perspective to compare these two different types of signals. We adopt the framework of Milgrom and Roberts (1986) to study the impression-based advertising. To model the performance-based pricing scheme, we make the following extensions compared to Milgrom and Roberts (1986): (1) we introduce a demand uncertainty factor to model risk-averse advertisers, while Milgrom and Roberts (1986) consider only risk-neutral bidders; and (2) we differentiate the performance of advertising (which is represented by the first-period demand) from that of the product (which is represented by the repeat purchase or second-period demand). More specifically, we introduce a performance over-measure factor to reflect one unique feature of performancebased advertising: because the publisher usually cannot effectively separate an initial purchase from a repeat purchase, advertisers often need to pay for every action generated from the advertisement under this pricing scheme

## 3. The Model

To focus on the fundamental economic forces behind performance-based advertising, in this section we present a basic model in which advertising serves as a quality signal. We derive the equilibrium results for both impression-based and performance-based advertising, respectively, and derive our main insights by comparing these two advertising schemes.

## 3.1. Model Setup

We adopt the modeling framework used in the literature on advertising signaling (see Milgrom and Roberts 1986, Kihlstrom and Riordan 1984). Specifically, following the literature, we consider a merchant offering a new product with an unknown quality level, q, which can take only two values, H and L, where $0 < L < H < 1$ . The product could be either H or L with equal probability. The product’s actual quality is known to the merchant but unknown to consumers. Consumers decide whether to make their initial purchase in the first period after observing the merchant’s advertising expenditure, A, from which they might or might not be able to infer product quality. If they make an initial purchase, they gain information about the quality through their use of the product. In the second period, they decide whether or not to purchase again. Let the quality of the product be operationalized as the probability that a customer finds the product satisfactory, so that he/she will repurchase. Assume that customers who do not purchase in the first period will never purchase it. Thus, if the demand in the first period is D, the second-period demand is proportional to the first-period demand qD.

Specifically, let demand in the first period be D = $\tilde { R } - \cal { P } / Q ,$ , where $\tilde { R }$ is market potential and Q is the perceived quality in the first period. The existing advertising literature has treated R<sup>˜</sup> as a constant (e.g., Milgrom and Roberts 1986, Kihlstrom and Riordan 1984). We relax this assumption by assuming it to be a random variable, which allows us to model the merchant’s attitude toward uncertainty in its market demand. Specifically, we assume $\tilde { R } = \mathrm { \bar { { R } } } + \varepsilon ,$ , where  follows a normal distribution with a mean of zero and variance $\sigma ^ { 2 } ,$ representing the estimation $\mathrm { e r r o r } . ^ { 3 }$ Let $c _ { q }$ denote the cost of producing a product with quality q. ## 3.3. Performance-Based Advertising

Under performance-based advertising, the merchant decides its per-response, or per-unit, advertising expenditure, a. The merchant’s final advertising expenditure, ${ \tilde { A } } ,$ instead of being determined before the demand is realized, depends on the actual responses generated by the advertisement. Depending on the context, the responses could be “purchases,” “clicksthroughs,” or “downloads.” As mentioned earlier, such responses can be made by both first-time and repeat buyers, implying that under the performancebased advertising scheme, the payment that a merchant owes the publisher might depend on both the performance of the advertisement (which determines the number of responses by first-time customers) and the performance of the product (which determines the number of responses by repeat consumers). To capture this observation, we define an over-measure factor $\beta \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . Note that $\beta$ represents the degree to which the merchant is charged a higher amount than if the advertising payment is based solely on consumer responses generated by the advertisement, in other words, the proportion of repeat purchase (product performance) that is counted as the advertising performance. The case of $\beta = 0$ represents an ideal situation, where the advertising payment depends purely on the advertisement’s performance. The case of $\beta = 1$ refers to the other extreme, where all responses from repeat customers are counted in the advertising payments. The more general case is when $0 < \beta < \bar { 1 }$ . In reality, even though consumer responses can be accurately monitored, it may still be difficult to identify which response is the result of the advertisement effectiveness and which is caused by the product quality/reputation. For example, when customers repurchase, they might still use the same search engine they used before and click on the same link they previously followed. Technically, repeat purchases can also be hard to track if consumers visit the same website using different computers (e.g., from home or office computers).

It is important to note that, unlike traditional impression-based advertising where consumers can infer advertising expenditure directly from observing the advertisement’s exposure, some unique issues arise when switching to the performance-based advertising scheme. First, consumers might not be aware of the change in advertising scheme, i.e., they might still use advertising exposure to infer advertising expenditure, even though the former is no longer linked to the latter. Second, even if consumers are aware of the change in advertising scheme, they might be unable to infer advertising expenditure by simply observing the advertisement’s exposure, because an advertisement is displayed before the advertising is paid for. These issues imply that the observed exposure might not be an accurate measure of advertising expenditure, which suggests that to ensure the signaling function under performance-based advertising, it is necessary to provide additional information to consumers. In general, there are two levels of information related to performance-based advertising: (a) the advertising scheme, and (b) the per-action advertising fee. Information about the per-action advertising fee can be revealed directly. For example, some publishers, such as Taobao.com (the largest consumer-toconsumer auction market in China), publishes the perclick advertising expenditure for each keyword; search engines such as Overture.com (previously Goto.com) and Snap.com also used to reveal such information explicitly. The information can also be revealed indirectly. Google and other major search engines, for example, use so-called “generalized second-price auctions” to sell advertising slots, which ensure that a higher-ranked merchant pays more than the nexthighest-ranked merchant pays<sup>7</sup> (Varian 2007, Edelman et al. 2007). In this section, we specify three cases that differ in the information provided to consumers: (1) full information (both levels of information are provided to consumers), (2) partial information (only the first level is provided), and (3) no information/misinformation (not even the first level is pro vided). We will discuss later that this also represents a “misinformation” scenario.

(1) Full information. In this case, consumers are aware of both the change in the advertising scheme and the per-unit advertising fee. Thus, advertising can serve as a quality signal when the low-quality merchant finds it unprofitable to mimic the advertising behavior of the high-quality merchant; that is, ∃a $> 0$ such that

$$

Neither the per-unit advertising fee nor the actual response to the advertising is observable, however. We thus have that $x ( a ) = x ( { \hat { a } } )$ , for all $a > 0 , { \hat { a } } > 0$ . Therefore, a low-quality merchant is able to mimic the high-quality merchant by any positive advertising.

Such information asymmetry often occurs in the early stages of a transition, when a majority of consumers are not well educated about the new practice/phenomenon. Consequently, they still use the observed exposure to infer a merchant’s advertising expenditure, while the advertising expenditure is not related to advertising exposure. Proposition 2 reveals an interesting finding: The over-measure factor, $\beta ,$ impacts the high-quality merchant’s per-unit advertising expenditure and its total advertising expenditure in different directions $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ $\partial a ^ { * } / \partial \beta < 0$ but $\partial E [ A ^ { * } ] / \partial \beta > 0 )$ . This is because when $\beta$ increases, advertising becomes more costly, and this effect is stronger for the high-quality merchant, who attracts more repeat purchases than the lowquality merchant. Consequently, the overall effect of $\bar { \beta }$ on the high-quality merchant’s total advertising expenditure is positive. The uncertainty factor, $r \sigma ^ { 2 } ,$ however, affects both the per-unit expenditure and the total advertising expenditure of the high-quality merchant in the same direction (i.e., $\partial a ^ { * } / \overset { \smile } { \partial } ( r \overset { \cdot } { \sigma ^ { 2 } } ) < \overset { \cdot } { 0 }$ and $\partial E [ A ^ { * } ] / \partial ( r \sigma ^ { 2 } ) < 0 )$ . A higher $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$ implies greater uncertainty in advertising performance and thus a lower expected gain from advertising, which reduces the incentive of the low-quality merchant to mimic and leads to a smaller $a ^ { * }$ . However, $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$ has no effect on the total number of actions to be charged. As a result, the total advertising expenditure of the highquality merchant also decreases with $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$

## 3.4. The Impact of Performance-Based Scheme on the Signaling Function of Advertising

We have derived the separating equilibrium under the two advertising schemes (see Lemma 1 and Proposition 1). We now examine how switching from the traditional impression-based advertising to performance-based advertising can affect the signaling function of advertising by comparing the conditions required for a separating equilibrium under the two schemes. Proposition 3 summarizes our findings.

Proposition 3 (The Impact on the Conditions Required for the Signaling Function).

1. Switching from impression-based to performancebased advertising will disable the signaling function of advertising $i f$ consumers are informed about neither the advertising scheme nor the per-unit advertising cost, or if the over-measure effect is too strong, i.e., $\beta \geq { \bar { \beta } } .$

## 2. Otherwise,

(a) The two advertising schemes require the same conditions for advertising to signal in the absence of both factors, that is, if both $\overset { \vartriangle } { \boldsymbol { \beta } } = 0$ and $r \sigma ^ { 2 } = 0 ;$

(b) Performance-based advertising requires a stronger condition for advertising to retain its signaling ability if the over-measure factor is strong $( i . e . , \beta > \beta ^ { \ast } )$ but a weaker condition otherwise, where $\partial \beta ^ { * } / \partial ( r \sigma ^ { 2 } ) \geq 0 , \ \partial \bar { \beta } / \partial ( r \sigma ^ { 2 } ) <$ 0 $( \bar { \beta }$ and $\beta ^ { * }$ are defined in the appendix).

Proposition 3 underscores three important issues determining the impact of performance-based advertising on the signaling function of advertising: (1) information known to consumers, (2) the performance over-measure factor, $\beta ,$ and (3) the demand uncertainty factor, $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$ . It shows that although performance-based advertising differs in its fundamental contract design (i.e., payment is based on actual consumer actions rather than being a predetermined lump sum) from traditional impressionbased advertising, it requires the same conditions for advertising to signal when both the advertising performance over-measure factor and the demand uncertainty factor are insignificant $( r \sigma ^ { 2 } = 0$ and $\beta = 0 ) . ^ { 8 }$

When these two factors cannot be ignored $( \beta > 0$ and $r \sigma ^ { 2 } > 0 )$ , they impact the conditions required for signaling in opposite directions. Specifically, it is more difficult to satisfy the conditions required for advertising to signal under performance-based scheme when $\beta$ is sufficiently large $\left( \beta > \beta ^ { * } \right)$ . However, an increase in $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$ makes the performance-based scheme a more efficient signaling mechanism than the impressionbased scheme, because the merchant needs to pay only for each realized action, thereby reducing uncertainty. As a result, $\beta < \beta ^ { * }$ is more likely to be satisfied when $r \sigma ^ { 2 }$ increases $( \partial \beta ^ { * } / \partial ( r \sigma ^ { 2 } ) \geq 0 )$

Finally, because $\partial \bar { \beta } / \partial ( r \sigma ^ { 2 } ) < 0 ,$ , even though firms that face higher demand uncertainty are naturally more attracted to performance-based advertising, performance-based advertising might fail to signal quality in markets with very high demand uncertainty or for firms that have a very strong concern about the uncertainty.

## 4. Conclusions

Recent developments in information technology have enabled new market practices that have significantly affected traditional advertising functions and raise new questions (Granados et al. 2010, Mookerjee and Tan 2005). This paper focuses on one emerging practice, performance-based advertising, and examines how this advertising scheme affects one fundamental function of advertising—signaling product quality. Our analysis leads to some important insights. First, performance-based advertising can signal product quality if the publisher is able to communicate to consumers both the advertising pricing scheme and merchant’s per-unit advertising expenditure. Second, although performance-based and impressionbased advertising differ profoundly in their contract design, they might lead to the same equilibrium outcomes in the absence of two factors: the demand uncertainty factor (which facilitates the signaling function of performance-based advertising) and the advertising performance over-measure factor (which impedes the signaling function of performance-based advertising). Third, it is commonly believed that performancebased advertising reduces the uncertainty associated with advertising performance and thus merchants should have a stronger incentive to advertise. We find, however, that this is true only when advertising performance is not significantly over-measured. To encourage high-quality merchants to signal quality through advertising, the publisher might need to make an effort to separate the performance of the advertisement from that of the product. For example, it can offer a volume discount to the high-quality merchant, or it can adopt technologies that distinguish the actions created by new buyers from those of repeat customers, and collect advertising payment based on the former only. In addition, merchants can benefit from attracting more repurchases directly to their own stores or websites. The practice of following up, via email or by some other means of reminder, on consumers who have made previous purchases can also serve this purpose.

This paper is subject to limitations. First, we assume a monopolist merchant making signaling decisions. One straightforward extension is to allow competition among merchants (Fluet and Garella 2002) and study the impact of this competition on the signaling function under performance-based advertising. Second, future research should also include an empirical study to compare the pricing and advertising signaling behavior of merchants under these two pricing schemes. The magnitude of the two critical factors, $\beta$ and $r \sigma ^ { 2 } .$ , might depend on different product categories, such as durable goods, entertainment goods (Liu and Chen 2006), or information goods (Sundararajan 2004). Third, in this paper we focus only on the signaling function of advertising. In reality, advertising might also serve as an exposure method, and it is possible for the publisher to grant different exposure to differentiate advertisements based on their advertising spending. It will be interesting to study the impact of such practice on advertising when it serves both signaling and exposure purpose. Last, as with many other advertising signaling models, our model does not capture the impact of consumer dynamics. In reality, consumers are exposed to an advertisement at different times, and some might purchase the product earlier than others, and some repeat customers might coexist with new customers. It would be interesting to study such a dynamic framework in the future, for example, by revealing a merchant’s historical advertising expenditure for later consumers to observe.<sup>9</sup>
