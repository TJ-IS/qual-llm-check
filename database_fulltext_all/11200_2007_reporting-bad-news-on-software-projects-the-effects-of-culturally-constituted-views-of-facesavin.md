# Reporting bad news on software projects: the effects of culturally constituted views of face-saving

Mark Keil,\* Ghi Paul Im<sup>†</sup> & Magnus Mähring<sup>‡</sup>

\*Department of Computer Information Systems, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303, USA, email: mkeil@gsu.edu, <sup>†</sup>Department of Decision Science, School of Business Administration, Clark Atlanta University, 223 James P. Brawley Dr SW, Atlanta, GA 30314, USA, email: gim@cau.edu, and <sup>‡</sup>Department of Marketing and Strategy, Stockholm School of Economics, PO Box 6501, SE-113 83 Stockholm, Sweden, email: magnus.mahring@hhs.se

Abstract. The reluctance to report bad news about a project and its status is a known problem in software project management that can contribute to project failure. The reluctance to report bad news is heightened when it bears personal risks. Oftentimes, those who report bad news end up losing face. In extreme cases, they not only lose face, but may end up on the unemployment line. The need to preserve face is a powerful influence on social behaviour. While universal, it manifests itself differently in different cultures. To date, there have been no empirical studies of the extent to which culturally constituted views of face-saving affect reporting of bad news on software projects. This is a particularly important topic given the increased prevalence of global, dispersed software development teams and offshore outsourcing of software development. In this study, we conducted a roleplaying experiment in the USA and in South Korea, to investigate the effect of culturally constituted views of face-saving on the willingness to report bad news regarding a software development project. A blame-shifting opportunity was chosen as the means to operationalize face-saving in a culturally sensitive fashion. The two countries were chosen because they differ markedly in their views of facesaving and the relative importance ascribed to two important aspects of face: lian and mianzi. Results reveal that the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity had a significant effect on US subjects’ willingness to report bad news, but the effect on Korean subjects was not found to be statistically significant. In the absence of a blame-shifting opportunity, we did not observe any significant differences between US and Korean subjects in willingness to report bad news. The implications of these findings are discussed.

Keywords: software project management, whistle-blowing, mum effect, facesaving, cross-cultural, experiment

## INTRODUCTION

Software project management continues to be a challenging area for practitioners: more than half of all software projects experience severe difficulties and/or failure (Standish Group, 2004, p. 2). In recent years, researchers have begun to focus attention on the importance of accurate status reporting, so that management is aware of problems facing a project and can take corrective action. A key area of interest to both research and practice is understanding the reasons why status reports are often inaccurate. One reason is the reluctance of people to report bad news about a project and its status (Tan et al., 2003). When software projects go awry, it often takes weeks, months, and sometimes even years, before senior management becomes fully aware of the situation. Evidence of impending failure may be apparent to those who are closely involved in a project, yet this information may not be communicated up the hierarchy (Keil & Robey, 1999) or may be substantially distorted in the communication process (Snow & Keil, 2002). Fears of negative personal consequences frequently contribute to a reluctance to report bad news on failing software projects (Keil & Robey, 1999). Recently, several studies have pointed to the particular challenges of software project control in cross-cultural settings (Carmel, 1999; Choi & Choi, 2003; Evaristo, 2003). Cultural differences in attitudes and values have been observed between information systems (IS) professionals from Confucian (Korean, Chinese) and Western (US, Western European) cultures (Choi & Choi, 2003; Peterson & Kim, 2003; Peterson et al., 2003), although studies that focus on the impact of cultural differences on IS professionals’ views regarding issues specific to IS development are scarce (Carmel, 1999; Peterson et al., 2003).

In this study, a role-playing experiment was conducted in the USA and South Korea in order to investigate the effect of culturally constituted views of face-saving on the willingness to report bad news on a software development project. The need to preserve face has a powerful impact on social behaviour (Goffman, 1967). While this need is present in all cultures, it differs in how is it constituted and manifested in different cultural contexts (Earley, 1997). In this study, a blame-shifting opportunity was chosen as the means to operationalize face-saving in a culturally sensitive fashion. In today’s software project environment, firms are increasingly turning to outside vendors to help them develop and implement software systems. When such projects go awry, the vendor often becomes a ready scapegoat (Bulkeley, 1996; Stein, 1998). Thus, we operationalized the blame-shifting opportunity through the presence of a vendor, which provided a realistic target for channelling blame away from the subject. We chose to conduct matching experiments in the USA and South Korea because these two countries differ markedly in their views of face-saving and the relative importance ascribed to two important aspects of face: lian and mianzi, explained later (Earley, 1997).

The remainder of the paper is organized into four sections. First, we briefly review the relevant literature, specifically focusing on status reporting and the reluctance to report bad news, and the concept of face-saving. Second, we introduce our research approach (laboratory experiment) and hypotheses. Third, we present the results of the experiment, including both a quantitative and a qualitative analysis. Finally, we conclude the paper with a discussion of the results and their implications for IS research and practice.

## BACKGROUND

## Status reporting on software projects

Software development is a complex task, with low task observability and where decisionmakers exercising oversight often have limited knowledge of the work process (Kirsch, 1996; Kirsch et al., 2002). Software projects are also extended in time and non-repetitive, which further stack the deck against decision-makers charged with exercising project control (Mähring, 2002). Status reporting, however, remains one of the key ‘mechanisms’ of software project control (Kirsch et al., 2002; Kirsch, 2004) and the difficult task of controlling software projects becomes even more challenging in the absence of accurate information concerning project status.

The importance of accurate status reporting was further underscored in a recent study by Iacovou et al. (2005), which suggested that reporting quality is positively associated with project outcomes. Unfortunately, executives are often faced with a quandary as the project managers who report to them do not always know the true status of the projects they are reporting on, and, moreover, may purposely bias their project status reports. Snow & Keil (2002) suggest that distortion in project reporting (i.e. reported status being different than true status) results from the combined effect of error (i.e. misperceiving true status) and bias (i.e. reporting status differently than one actually perceives it).

Partly as a consequence of the intangibility of the work process and its output, assessments of project progress as well as status reporting often have an optimistic bias (Brooks, 1975; Snow et al., 2003). Prior literature suggests that the reporting errors (i.e. misperceiving true status) made by software project managers can be significant (Brooks, 1975; Zmud, 1980; DeMarco, 1982), and that reporting bias (i.e. intentionally altering perceived project status) may be even more important than reporting error in contributing to overall reporting distortion (Snow & Keil, 2002). Reluctance to transmit bad news offers one explanation for why project managers may choose to withhold negative information or optimistically bias their reports to management.

## Reluctance to report bad news

The reluctance to transmit bad news has been researched from several different perspectives since the early 1970s. Work in this area began with studies of the so-called ‘mum effect’ which focuses on an individual’s reluctance to transmit bad news (Conlee & Tesser, 1973; Tesser & Rosen, 1975; O’Neal et al., 1979). Closely related to the mum effect concept is a broader body of more recent literature on whistle-blowing which seeks to explain why individuals choose to report or not to report events that are immoral, illegal or illegitimate (Miceli & Near, 1992). More recently still, researchers have studied what has been labelled ‘organizational silence’ (Morrison & Milliken, 2000). While there are subtle distinctions in these three streams, they all deal in some way with reluctance to transmit bad news. In the IS domain, Smith and others (Smith et al., 2001; Smith & Keil, 2003; Tan et al., 2003; Keil et al., 2004) have embraced the whistle-blowing stream in order to develop and test a theoretical model that explains the reluctance to report bad news. While Smith et al. have investigated such factors as organizational climate, information asymmetry, perceived wrongdoing and perceived impact, there has been no published research investigating the relationship between face-saving and willingness to report bad news.

The whistle-blowing literature suggests that fear of being blamed can inhibit an individual’s willingness to report bad news (Conlee & Tesser, 1973; Tesser & Rosen, 1975). Therefore, it seems reasonable that the opportunity to shift blame away from oneself might allow for facesaving, while effectively removing one of the major factors that inhibit bad news reporting.

## Reluctance to report bad news on troubled software projects

There have been several publicized incidents of software project failure in which non-reporting or distorted reporting of project status appeared to contribute to the failure (Frantz, 1989; Oz, 1994; Tomsho, 1994). Keil & Robey (1999) report that even IS auditors are frequently reluctant to report bad news about project status.

Smith & Keil (2003) review the literature on whistle-blowing and the mum effect, showing how it can be used to build theory in the area of bad news reporting on troubled software projects. Three empirical papers have been published in this area, all based on role-playing experiments. Smith et al. (2001) investigated the effects of perceived wrongdoing and perceived impact of failure and found that both variables affected subjects’ willingness to report bad news. Keil et al. (2004) examined the role of organizational climate and information asymmetry on bad news reporting. Both variables were found to have a significant effect. Tan et al. (2003) extended this work by examining bad news reporting in a cross-cultural study. Again, both variables were found to have a significant effect and the authors concluded that there were differences across cultures that could be attributed to differences in the individualism– collectivism dimension.

With the exception of the study by Tan et al. (2003), there appears to be no empirical research on the cross-cultural factors that may affect an individual’s willingness to transmit bad news on software projects. In this paper, we focus on the culturally constituted notion of facesaving, an important cross-cultural construct that has not been examined in the context of bad news reporting. This shortcoming of the present literature is particularly important to remedy because of the increasingly popular practices of software development in globally dispersed development teams (Carmel, 1999; Evaristo, 2003) and offshore outsourcing of software development (Carmel & Nicholson, 2005). Cultural differences do pose a major problem in controlling and coordinating global software development teams (Gurung & Prater, 2006) and for offshore outsourcing of software development, control is complicated because of the need to have control ‘beyond the political, economic, social and technological boundaries of the country’ (Carmel & Nicholson, 2005, p. 39).

Since accurate status reporting is crucial for both coordination and control of these kinds of projects, understanding how face and face-saving impacts the propensity of individuals from different cultures to report bad news may contribute to improved control and reporting practices for cross-cultural software development projects.

## Face-saving

The concept of face is Chinese in origin (Ervin-Tripp et al., 1995) and refers to the respectability and/or deference which a person can claim for himself from others, by virtue of the relative position he occupies in his social network and the degree to which he is judged to have functioned adequately in that position as well as acceptably in his or her general conduct (Ho, 1976). Face is lost when an individual is not successful in meeting the requirements placed upon him or her in relation to his or her social position (Ho, 1976). Although culturally sensitive, face regards a universal form of social interaction among individuals concerning how we present ourselves to others and how we evaluate ourselves (Goffman, 1967; Earley, 1997; Yu, 2003). Specifically, face-saving can be seen as a behavioural strategy for avoiding blame and retaining social standing in the face of a potentially damaging event or situation. The concept of face-saving as a culturally constituted social phenomenon has, to our knowledge, not previously been studied in the context of bad news reporting.

In this paper, we suggest that because face-saving is culturally constituted, individuals in one culture will perceive blame-shifting as an attractive option for face-saving, while individuals in another culture will not. This differential attractiveness of blame-shifting as a mechanism for saving face will, we believe, affect the willingness to report bad news.

Considering face in investigating an individual’s willingness to report bad news makes sense for two reasons. First, face helps shape the behaviour of individuals within any given social context (Earley, 1997). The concept of face has been used to explain diverse social phenomena, including politeness discourse, compliance-gaining, impression management, negotiation and conflict management (Haugh & Hinze, 2003). Because bad news reporting is a social phenomenon, applying the concept of face is expected to bring new insights. Second, an individual’s willingness to report bad news is, to a large extent, a cultural phenomenon in that culture and society influence organizations, and organizations and employees reciprocally influence one another (Erez & Earley, 1993). The social context in which an individual’s face-related behaviour is expressed is determined through one of its salient determinants, i.e. culture.

Different cultures are known to bring different implications to the conceptualization of face, and emphasize different aspects of face-related behaviours of individuals (Earley, 1997; Hwang et al., 2003). Specifically, Western cultures and South-East Asian cultures have repeatedly been identified as significantly different in how face is constituted (Redding & Ng, 1982; Earley, 1997; Yu, 2003). The differences are related to other cultural attributes such as the function of shame (Redding & Ng, 1982) and the individualism–collectivism dimension of culture (Redding & Ng, 1982; Hwang et al., 2003). On a deeper level, the conception of face in the cultures of South-East Asia can be related to Confucianism and its emphasis on subordination of group members to the group and to society at large as an important means towards cultivating homogeneity (Yu, 2003).

The Confucian cultures of South-East Asia (including South Korea) are generally seen as highly collectivist, with high sensitivity to group belonging, interdependence, peer opinion and a view of relationships as communal and thus important to sustain even in the event of personal disadvantages (e.g. Hofstede, 1980; Hwang et al., 2003). In contrast, Western individualist cultures (including the USA) are characterized by personal independence, importance of personal rather than group goals, social behaviour determined by attitudes, personal rights and contracts and a view of relationships as rational exchanges. Behaviour in individualist cultures is determined by attitudes, personal rights and contracts, whereas in collectivist cultures, behaviours are rooted in norms, obligations and duties (Hofstede, 1980; Hwang et al., 2003). According to several scholars, face has greater salience for South-East Asian cultures than for Western cultures (Redding & Ng, 1982; Yu, 2003). An important means for better understanding these differences is offered by the concepts of lian and mianzi.

## Lian and Mianz

The Chinese conceptualization of face distinguishes between two forms of face known as lian and mianzi (Hu, 1944). Drawing on this notion, Earley makes a distinction between ‘face tied to rules of conduct’ and ‘face as a position in a social hierarchy’ (Earley, 1997, p. 55). Following Earley, we define lian ‘as a set of rules for moral conduct’ (Earley, 1997, p. 56) and mianzi ‘as a person’s position within a social structure’ (Earley, 1997, p. 56). Lian refers to the extent to which a person adheres to moral or evaluative rules of conduct, whereas mianzi refers to a person’s standing in a social hierarchy. Lian is ‘most directly tied to internal referents’, while mianzi is ‘most obviously tied to the actions and reactions of others’ (i.e. external referents) (Earley, 1997, p. 59). Lian reflects the rules of behavioural conduct that are internalized as an individua grows up and is socialized within a particular culture. Mianzi, in contrast, ‘reflects an evaluation by self and others concerning a person’s possession of resources’ within a given social structure (Earley, 1997, p. 59).

In cultures influenced by Confucianism, a person’s absence of lian equals absence of integrity and carries with it social stigma (Hu, 1944; Redding & Ng, 1982). While mianzi is also very important in these cultures, a relative lack of mianzi will not lead to a person being ostracized from society. In business settings, however, mianzi has been shown to be highly important for sealing business deals as well as in everyday workplace interaction, conflict resolution, etc. (Hu, 1944; Redding & Ng, 1982; Hwang et al., 2003). Individualist Western cultures, on the other hand, generally put greater emphasis on mianzi than on lian. This means that in countries such as the USA, personal reputation, prestige or standing (in relation to and in the eyes of others) carries more weight in the constitution of face than internalized communal norms and values.

Consistent with this line of argument, Earley (1997) developed a two-by-two grid using two of Hofstede’s (1980) cultural dimensions: power distance and individualism–collectivism. In this model, the relative influences of lian and mianzi are hypothesized to vary according to which quadrant of the grid a specific country is located (Figure 1).

Figure 1 allows us to map cultures into one of four quadrants. In quadrant 1, which represents cultures in which power distance is high and individualism is low, LIAN and MIANZI are in allcaps format, signifying that both aspects of face are salient in such cultures and LIAN is listed before MIANZI, signifying that it is the more important aspect of face. In quadrant 2, MIANZ is both more important and more salient than lian. In quadrant 3, LIAN is both more important and more salient than mianzi. In quadrant 4, mianzi is more important than lian, but neither aspect of face is as salient in this type of culture as compared with other types of cultures.

## RESEARCH APPROACH AND HYPOTHESES

## Experimental design and hypotheses

A role-playing experiment was conducted in the USA and South Korea in order to investigate the effect of culturally constituted views of face-saving on the willingness to report bad news. Specifically, we used matching laboratory experiments to compare the willingness to report bad news across these two cultures, which differ markedly in their views of face-saving and the relative importance ascribed to lian and mianzi (Figure 1). A blame-shifting opportunity was chosen as the means to operationalize face-saving in a culturally sensitive fashion. Through this, we could explore whether individuals from a culture in which the lian aspect of face is salient (South Korea) would respond differently to a blame-shifting opportunity than individuals from a culture in which the mianzi aspect of face is dominant (USA).

In order to manipulate the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity while controlling extraneous sources of variance, a laboratory experiment was deemed to be the most appropriate research approach. The usefulness of experimental research has been demonstrated in a number of prior research studies concerning decision-making (Harrison & Harrell, 1993; Sitkin & Weingart, 1995; Tan et al., 2003) and strategic decision-making (Thomas & McDaniel, 1990; Sutcliffe & Zaheer, 1998).

Results were pooled to obtain a two-by-two factorial design with two independent variables: blame-shifting opportunity (present or absent) and national culture (mianzi-dominant culture, culture in which lian aspect of face is salient). The dependent variable was willingness to report bad news.

As mentioned before, based on Hofstede’s (1980) index values, South Korea can be classified as a collectivistic culture (11 on individualism–collectivism index), whereas the USA can be classified as an individualistic culture (50 on individualism–collectivism index). For the dimension of power distance, the differences between South Korea and the USA are less pronounced: the USA has an index value of 40, South Korea has 60. Using Hofstede’s values, we can locate the USA and South Korea within the grid in Figure 1. Power distance is reasonably high in both cultures, placing both countries in the top half of the grid. South Korea’s low individualism score places it squarely in quadrant 1, whereas the USA’s high individualism score places it in quadrant 2.

Figure 2 depicts the two-by-two experimental design and forces at play in each of the treatment conditions. In all treatment conditions, a negative reporting climate was established in order to control for this variable and provide some level of reluctance for reporting bad news.

In the top half of Figure 2, treatment conditions A and B were structured so as to include a blame-shifting opportunity. In the bottom half of Figure 2, treatment conditions C and D did not include a blame-shifting opportunity. The presence of a blame-shifting opportunity in quadrants A and B (see Figure 2) should exert a positive influence on bad news reporting, as it provides a mechanism for reporting the bad news without fear of being blamed (and the associated risk of losing face) or any of the associated consequences of being found to be at fault (such as losing one’s job). This leads to our first hypothesis:

H1 Controlling for culture, a blame-shifting opportunity will be associated with a greater willingness to report bad news than if no such blame-shifting opportunity is presented.

Returning to Figure 2, we now consider the effects of culture. When a blame-shifting opportunity exists, we hypothesized that individuals in a ‘quadrant 1 culture’ (Korean subjects – cf. Figure 1) would be significantly influenced by both lian and mianzi aspects of face. From a lian perspective, we see that there are two opposing forces that come into play. On the one hand, the lian aspect of face-saving (‘doing the right thing’) favours reporting the bad news. On the other hand, if reporting the bad news requires blame-shifting, this might be seen as ‘doing the wrong thing’ because blame-shifting may have negative connotations in Korean culture in which harmony is considered a virtue (Doney et al., 1998). In this context, exercising the blame-shifting opportunity by reporting can be seen as a form of opportunistic behaviour and prior research suggests that quadrant 1 cultures are less likely to behave opportunistically (Doney et al., 1998). On balance, we hypothesize that the two opposing forces associated with the lian aspect of face will cancel one another.

From a mianzi perspective, saving face in this context would mean reporting the bad news so as to avoid negative consequences to one’s social status in the workplace hierarchy, and the blame-shifting opportunity provides a means of achieving this. According to Earley (1997), both lian and mianzi are salient in quadrant 1, but lian is the more dominant aspect of face. This suggests that Korean subjects will be somewhat restrained in their willingness to report bad news even when a blame-shifting opportunity exists.

In quadrant 2 of Figure 1, Earley (1997) suggests that mianzi is both more salient and of greater importance than the lian aspect of face. Thus, when a blame-shifting opportunity exists, we hypothesized that individuals in a ‘quadrant 2 culture’ (US subjects) would operate from a mianzi perspective. Thus, they would see reporting the bad news in this context as a means of avoiding negative consequences to one’s social status in the workplace hierarchy, because the blame could be shifted to another party. While the lian aspect of face might exert some influence towards reporting, we reasoned that this force would be small in comparison with the force exerted by the mianzi aspect of face that is so dominant in quadrant 2. Consideration of the relative influence of lian and mianzi in quadrants 1 and 2 gives rise to the following hypothesis:

H2 When a blame-shifting opportunity is present, US subjects will exhibit greater willingness to report bad news than Korean subjects.

In the absence of a blame-shifting opportunity, individuals in a quadrant 1 culture (Korean subjects) will be significantly influenced by both lian and mianzi aspects of face. Under these circumstances, however, the lian aspect of face which involves ‘doing the right thing’ will favour reporting, because there is nobody else who will be blamed and reporting the bad news is the right thing to do. From a mianzi perspective, however, there may be serious repercussions associated with reporting bad news, especially if one is found to be at fault for the problem which is the situation in treatment condition C. Thus, in this treatment condition, the lian and mianzi aspects of face represent opposing forces from the standpoint of bad news reporting.

From a lian perspective, saving face in this context would mean ‘doing the right thing’ and while bad news reporting might normally be viewed as ‘doing the right thing’, blame-shifting might be seen as ‘doing the wrong thing’. According to Earley (1997), both lian and mianzi are salient in quadrant 1, but lian is the more dominant aspect of face. This suggests that Korean subjects will be somewhat inclined to report bad news.

In quadrant 2, Earley (1997) suggests that mianzi is both more salient and of greater importance than the lian aspect of face. Thus, when no blame-shifting opportunity is present, we hypothesized that individuals in a quadrant 2 culture (US subjects) would operate from a mianzi perspective. Thus, reporting the bad news in this context would be seen to carry negative consequences in terms of one’s social status in the workplace hierarchy, especially if one is found to be at fault for the problem which is the situation in treatment condition D. While the lian aspect of face might exert some influence in the opposite direction, we reasoned that this force would be small in comparison with the force exerted by the mianzi aspect of face that is so dominant in quadrant 2. Consideration of the relative influence of lian and mianzi in quadrants 1 and 2 gives rise to the following hypothesis:

H3 In the absence of a blame-shifting opportunity, Korean subjects will exhibit greater willingness to report bad news than US subjects.

## Scenario and procedure

In the scenario given to the subjects (see Appendix), each subject was asked to play the role of a software project leader in Software Solutions Corporation (SSC), a company that develops systems designed to meet specific customer needs. As project leader for the CAPS project which consists of two software modules, the subject is told that a serious problem has been noted in one of the modules. In the treatment condition, there is an external vendor who has been contracted to supply the problematic module. In the control condition, there is no external vendor and both modules are being developed by SSC. If the problem is not resolved soon, it will delay the ability of SSC to deliver the CAPS system to the customer. In each case, the subject is told that he or she must decide whether or not to report the bad news to a senior manager. The scenario used was developed specifically for this experiment and was subjected to an iterative series of pilot tests and refinements.

To ensure that the meaning of the scenario and the questions would be the same for subjects in the two countries in the study, we followed an iterative approach where the materials were translated into Korean and back-translated into English. One of the authors performed the Korean translation and an independent translator back-translated the Korean version into English. Original and back-translated items were then compared and another round of translation and back-translation was conducted whereupon both translators agreed that the meaning had been conserved in the two versions.

Subjects in each treatment condition were told that this was an experiment on business decision-making and that their answers would remain anonymous. The subjects participated in the study voluntarily and did not receive any credit for their participation. Subjects in each country were randomly assigned to one of the two treatment conditions (obtained by varying the presence or absence of a blame-shifting opportunity). During the experiment, subjects received a copy of the scenario corresponding to their respective treatment condition. They were asked to read the scenario and complete a questionnaire that measured their likelihood of reporting the bad news about their project. Willingness to report bad news (the dependent variable) was measured using a single-item measure that was anchored on a seven-point scale ranging from ‘very unlikely’ (1) to ‘very likely’ (7).

## Subjects

The subjects were business students who were taking an introductory business course at a large university in the USA and South Korea. Table 1 provides descriptive statistics for the subjects who participated in the experiment.

While the experimental approach is widely accepted in social science research, the appropriateness of using college students has long been a subject of debate (e.g. McNemar, 1946; Peterson, 2001). A standard argument against student subjects concerns external validity. Some studies addressing the question have found significant differences between student subjects and other subject groups (e.g. Cunningham, 1974, studying consumer behaviour). Other studies have found no significant differences between student and non-student subjects (e.g. Remus, 1986, studying decision-making).

However, a closer examination of this debate reveals that the choice between student and ‘non-student’ subjects is not as straightforward as one might at first think. In fact, non-student samples are often drawn from narrowly defined, homogeneous groups (Dipboye & Flanagan, 1979; Greenberg, 1987), which impedes generalizability. Furthermore, findings from one particular field setting experiment are per se not more likely to generalize to another field setting than is a laboratory experiment (Dipboye & Flanagan, 1979; Lynch, 1999).

Neither is the use of non-student subjects directly related to increased realism of the experiment: in fact, field experiments using non-student subjects may have high mundane realism (experiment procedures resemble real world events) but low experimental realism (the perception of realism of the study by subjects) (Dobbins et al., 1988). In theory-building studies particularly, it becomes important to safeguard internal validity by controlling experiment settings, which is better done in a non-field setting. Furthermore, some scholars argue that external validity can only be extended through successive experiments with different, homogeneous samples whereby generalizability of the theory is established and moderating variables that refine the theory are identified (Greenberg, 1987; Lynch, 1999). External validity is a function of theory, not a function of method (Lynch, 1999).

The importance of the distinction between mundane and experimental realism also extends to the question of what research results tell us: if we are interested in building theory, experimental designs should be evaluated on whether they are likely to increase our understanding of work behaviour, not on whether they mimic organizational settings (Dobbins et al., 1988). In line with this, Calder et al. (1981) argue that two distinctive types of external validity should be considered. One regards ‘effects application’ research, which aims to obtain findings that can be applied directly to the real world situation of interest. The other regards ‘theory application’, which focuses on scientific theory that provides a general understanding of the real world. In the latter case, data from student subjects are acceptable, even if they would differ from nonstudent responses (Calder et al., 1981; 1982; Calder & Tybout, 1999).

The above argument suggests that the issue of student subjects should not be assessed absolutely and universally, but rather on a case-by-case basis, in terms of appropriateness for the specific purpose and research approach. This position is further supported by the continued publication of research based on experiments with student subjects in widely regarded IS journals (e.g. Jessup et al., 1990; Dennis & Carte, 1998; Lee & Truex, 2000). Since this study aims to increase our knowledge of work behaviour through the conducted experiment, rather than deliver statistical facts about a certain response group, the use of student subjects is appropriate (Calder et al., 1981; Lynch, 1999).

However, it is still relevant to ask whether student subjects are likely to be competent participating in a specific experiment. In this experiment, we focus on culturally constituted behaviour in work settings under particular circumstances (bad news reporting). We argue that business students can be used to gain knowledge about these behaviours. Each student group in our experiment is immersed in a national culture that influences the conception of face and related reporting behaviour. Cultural belonging has been shown to carry with it intersubjective expectations about appropriate behaviours and their social meanings in different contexts (Goffman, 1955; Blum-Kulka, 1987; Yu, 2003). Thus, subjects are highly likely to behave in accordance with the culturally constituted conception of face when confronted with a situation that requires face work (Goffman, 1967; Yu, 2003). In other words, they are likely to be competent in understanding face and face work within their culture. In this particular case, the relatively larger cultural homogeneity of an undergraduate business student group compared with, say, a group of more experienced project managers – some of whom may have worked abroad or for foreign companies – might actually benefit the experiment (Greenberg, 1987; Lynch, 1999).

In line with this argument, the use of student subjects is widely accepted in cross-cultural studies (Wagner, 1995; Triandis & Gelfand, 1998; Chiou, 2001). There is also ample precedent in the literature for the use of student subjects when the focus is on decision-making tasks in general (e.g. Sitkin & Weingart, 1995), and this approach has been followed by others who have studied bad news reporting on software projects (Smith et al., 2001). In addition, Remus’s (1986) study found no significant differences in decision-making behaviour between managers and students without managerial experience and concluded business students to be adequate surrogates for managers in decision-making situations.

Thus, while knowledge about organizations and project management most likely helps in interpreting the experiment scenario, we argue that the main key to success in this experiment lies in whether the subjects are likely to be competent in assessing the impact of face of a specific behaviour in their culture. The organizational behaviours relevant to our experiment are considerably circumscribed (Gordon et al., 1987) and in-depth knowledge of software technology or project management is therefore not required. Furthermore, ex post, the qualitative data we collected on subject motivations and explanations of their decisions indicated that subjects had a high level of understanding of the scenario as an organizational situation with face implications.

In sum, we argue that the use of student subjects is highly appropriate for this study. All the same, we do acknowledge that there are limitations associated with using student subjects – as with other homogeneous subjects groups – and we address these when discussing the limitations of the study.

## RESULTS

## Manipulation checks for blame-shifting opportunity

Three questions were included as manipulation checks to determine if subjects responded to the presence or absence of a blame-shifting opportunity. These items were combined to produce a reliable measure (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.75) of whether or not subjects perceived that they could escape from being personally blamed for the project’s problems. In a one-way ANOVA, significant differences were observed between the treatment and control groups on the manipulation check (F = 44, P < 0.001). The treatment group (blame-shifting opportunity present) exhibited a mean of 5.28, while the control group (no blame-shifting opportunity) exhibited a mean of 3.93. In other words, subjects in the treatment group tended to believe that they could avoid personal blame for the software project’s problems, while subjects in the control group did not share this belief. This difference shows that the blame-shifting manipulation was effective.

## Test of hypotheses

A two-way factorial design ANOVA with interaction was performed to determine if there was any significant interaction effect prior to an analysis of main effects. Since we hypothesized that culturally constituted views of face-saving would impact bad news reporting, checking for an interaction effect between country and the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity was reasonable. An interaction effect can be said to exist whenever the effect of one factor depends upon the levels of another. Table 2 shows the results of the two-way ANOVA with interaction.

Table 2 shows that the interaction between country and the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity was significant $( F = 7 . 1 0 ; P < 0 . 0 0 9 )$ . There is clearly an interaction between country and blame-shifting. This can be seen graphically in Figure 3.

US subjects’ reporting behaviour showed a much greater sensitivity to the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity than that of Korean subjects. Table 2 also reveals main effects associated with both country $( F = 6 . 0 9 , P < 0 . 0 1 5 )$ and the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity $( F = 1 3 . 3 8 , P < 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . However, when there is an interaction along with main effects, we must re-examine the main effects to see if they are actually important, as an interaction can override any main effects. The main effect associated with blame-shifting says that subjects presented with a blame-shifting opportunity were more willing to report bad news than subjects who were not presented with a blame-shifting opportunity. Even though the interaction was significant (which says that the blame-shifting opportunity has a greater effect on US subjects than Korean subjects), the main effect is also worth paying attention to because it holds true for both countries. This main effect suggests that mechanisms that allow face-saving can be an effective means of encouraging bad news reporting. Figure 2 shows that the other main effect associated with country is not interesting, as the interaction is what is important here.

## Qualitative data analysis

To gain more insight into the response patterns that were observed, subjects were asked to explain the major reason(s) behind their decision. These responses were subjected to content analysis following the grounded theory approach described by Strauss & Corbin (1998), which involves three coding procedures: open coding, axial coding and selective coding. In open coding, concepts are identified and categories are developed with regard to their properties and dimensions. One of the authors created categories that captured the various factors that subjects indicated to be influential in their decision to report or not to report. All subjects responses were coded in this manner and approximately 30 unique categories were created in order to capture a rich and fine-grained set of factors that were expressed as being influential in the decision. All of the authors then reviewed the results of the open coding which included a summary of how many times a particular category was applied. Where categories appeared to represent different shades of the same concept (for example, truth and honesty), we collapsed them into a single category. In this manner, we discussed the categories that emerged from the open coding and reached a consensus on the coding.

Table 4 shows the most frequently mentioned factors viewed as influential in the decision to report or not to report the project status. The frequency counts are presented in the form of a two-by-two matrix corresponding to the experimental manipulation (i.e. blame-shifting opportunity present or no blame-shifting opportunity present) and whether or not the subject was in the US or the Korean sample. For the purpose of this analysis, a likelihood of reporting of ‘slightly likely’, ‘likely’ or ‘very likely’ was defined as ‘willing to report’ and a likeliness of reporting of ‘slightly unlikely’, ‘unlikely’ or ‘very unlikely’ was defined as ‘unwilling to report’. Straddlers (subjects who chose a likelihood of reporting of ‘neutral or unsure’) were excluded from this analysis. Nearly all of the categories in Table 4 concern factors that relate to ‘reporting the project’. The only exception was ‘desire to try fixing problem before reporting’ which was given as a reason for ‘not reporting the project’ and is shown in bold.

The second stage of the qualitative analysis involved axial coding which is a process of systematically relating categories in terms of their properties and dimensions. Our aim was to work towards developing a qualitative model consisting of concepts identified in the study and the causal chains between them (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2001). One author did the initial part of axial coding based on the categories in Table 4 for both the US and Korean data. Then, all authors discussed the relationships between the categories. We revisited some of the categories developed in the open coding to clearly identify differences between them. After the axial coding was completed, it was validated with existing data.

The developed model was further refined with the selective coding. Selective coding is the process of integrating and refining the model by deciding on a central category, and relating major categories to it through relationships. We identified ‘willingness to report’ as the central category of the model. It has relationships with all other major categories, appears frequently in the data, and provides logical and consistent explanations in relation to other categories, thus meeting the criteria for choosing a central category (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

## The model that emerged from the qualitative data analysis

Figure 5 shows the model that emerged from the qualitative data analysis. It depicts 12 concepts identified in the study and the causal chains between them. The concepts and their interrelationships are described as follows, with examples of how subjects used these concepts to help make decisions about whether or not to report the project’s status.

## Vendor’s fault

‘Vendor’s fault’ is recognized as the leading cause of the troubled software project among US and Korean subjects offered in the treatment condition where a vendor is present to be blamed. This explanation does not appear in the control condition in which there is no vendor present. This provides additional evidence of the effectiveness of our manipulation. The following remark from a Korean subject was typical:

This critical error is found in IN-TECH’s module. And the contract clearly specified that IN-TECH is responsible for any kinds of faults and project delays pertaining to its modules. So I have no reason to get blamed on reporting in this situation.

Our qualitative data suggest that Korean subjects were only slightly less likely to offer ‘vendor’s fault’ as a reason for reporting. However, our quantitative data clearly show that the US subjects were significantly more likely to make use of this opportunity to blame the vendor.

## Small chance of success, severe consequences and responsibility as project leader

Three concepts – ‘a small chance of success’, ‘severity of project consequences’ and ‘responsibility as project leader’ – were all frequently associated with the troubled software project and common to all four quadrants of Table 4. It is noteworthy that none of them are explicitly related to blame-shifting, yet they all promote ‘willingness to report’. ‘Severity of project consequences encompasses the following three subcategories: disaster prevention, project criticality and great consequences. The following remark from a US subject illustrates how the small chance of success affected his willingness to report:

I will report this to Mr A because there is only a 10% chance to finish the project. So he needs to be informed about the problem with the project.

Severity of project consequences was expressed by numerous subjects, but the following remark from a Korean subject was typical:

If we don’t reveal the problem at hand, we may have greater consequences afterward.

Responsibility as a project leader was given by many subjects as a reason for reporting the project’s status. The following remark from a Korean subject was typical:

First of all, I am the leader of this project. Since I am responsible for the results out of this project, I should report. It may be better to go right now than [allowing] a bigger problem to occur by hiding it.

## Remedial action

When a blame-shifting opportunity was present, many US subjects expressed a belief that it was best to report the bad news because their boss would help them to rescue the project by exercising remedial actions. The following explanation, for example, was offered by a US subject:

. . . Mr A should know what is going on. . . . Mr A needs to know if the deadline will be made so he can take immediate action with the key customer.

This type of explanation was offered much less frequently by Korean subjects. Differences in power distance, along with differences in the constitution of face between the two groups of subjects, may account for this. US subjects, coming from a culture where there is less power distance, were more willing to report the bad news as long as they knew that they would not be held responsible, expecting that their boss would come to their rescue in order to help avoid a project failure. Koreans, on the other hand, may feel greater power distance with their boss and thus are more reluctant to approach him for a resolution, and less optimistic about actually getting help if they would do so. Furthermore, going forwards to ask a superior for help is also more likely to result in a loss of face. Thus, in the presence of the vendor, Koreans may be less willing to report to Mr A because of power distance as well as a higher risk of losing face.

## Concern for company reputation

In the ‘vendor not present’ condition, ‘concern for company reputation’ was frequently mentioned by Korean subjects as a reason for reporting the project’s status. The following remark was typical:

[I] should report with some risks in the present situation, considering the possibility of having a massive layoff that occurs because of the lowered company image.

It is interesting to note that ‘concern for company reputation’ was rarely mentioned by US subjects. Concern for the company’s reputation may emerge as significant for Koreans because they belong to a more collectivistic culture in which the company is viewed as part of the collective and where face work pertains also to this collective.

## Honesty and concern for personal reputation

Among Korean subjects, ‘honesty’ and ‘personal reputation’ frequently emerged as explanations for reporting regardless of whether or not a vendor was present. A Korean subject explained the importance of honesty:

. . . in all, it is important to build trust with customers by being honest and not cheating myself. In that regard I should report in spite of an expected disadvantage.

Another Korean subject described why concern over personal reputation motivated him to report:

I may get around an immediate crisis by pressing the team. However it eventually lowers confidence on me and a bigger problem can occur.

Interestingly, among US subjects ‘honesty’ was frequently mentioned only in the condition where there was no vendor present and ‘personal reputation’ did not emerge as a frequent explanation at all. This is a reflection of the greater emphasis placed on the lian aspect of face in Korean culture. In the USA, the importance of honesty appears to become salient as a reason for reporting bad news only when there is no blame-shifting opportunity. This is consistent with the mianzi aspect of face being more dominant for US subjects.

## Desire to avoid surprising

Among US subjects, ‘desire to avoid surprising’ senior management was frequently mentioned as a reason for reporting, but only in the absence of vendor to blame. A US subject described his thinking:

In case my group misses the promised delivery date, it won’t be a surprise to the senior vice president.

## Desire to try fixing

The only consistent reason given for not reporting was a ‘desire to try fixing the problem before reporting’. Some subjects related it to risk taking while others just described it broadly without connection to personal risk propensity. A Korean subject explained:

It is not likely for me to report to Mr A because I know I will be responsible for it. First of all, it is best to make efforts in the current situation to try to fix the problem.

What is highly interesting is that US subjects express a desire to try fixing the problem rather than reporting it only in the absence of an opportunity to shift blame. In contrast, Korean subjects express a desire to try fixing the problem rather than reporting it only in the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity. This is consistent with our earlier observation that Koreans identify the opportunity to blame the vendor but are reluctant to exercise that opportunity. One reason for their reluctance may be that they are concerned about the vendor’s face as well. Another interpretation is that the lian aspect of face drives Koreans to try fixing the problem even when they have opportunity to shift the blame because blame-shifting would reflect negatively on their moral character (i.e. lian). This will be discussed further below.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

On the surface, it may appear paradoxical that the availability of a blame-shifting opportunity did not significantly affect reporting behaviour in a culture known for assigning high importance to face-saving (i.e. Korean culture). One possibility is that the loyalty towards the group or community characterizing collectivist cultures extends to the software vendor with which the company cooperates, and where face work pertains also to this collective. Another interpretation is that the lian aspect of face drives Koreans to try fixing the problem even when they have opportunity to shift the blame because blame-shifting would reflect negatively on their moral character (i.e. lian). Further support for the importance of lian for Korean subjects can be found in such explanations for reporting as ‘honesty’ and ‘personal reputation’. Thus, our findings suggest that channelling blame away from oneself and placing it on an outside vendor is a culturally adequate strategy for face-saving in the USA, but not in South Korea.

In cultures where the mianzi aspect of face dominates lian, the dilemma of reporting bad news revolves around how to protect yourself in the process. In this context, a vendor represents a suitable target for shifting blame away from one’s self. Because this would be a culturally shared understanding of the individual’s dilemma, it would follow that the subject’s environment will expect and accept a certain amount of blame-shifting (because, after all, he or she is trying to save himself or herself). Correspondingly, the individual would be relatively less concerned about exercising this option, because of it being accepted and because of the lesser attention given to lian.

In cultures where the lian aspect of face is more salient and more important than mianzi, reporting of bad news encompasses the dilemma of safeguarding other members of a socially (and thus often ambiguously) defined group or community. This would indicate that blaming others is much less acceptable in cultures that ascribe greater importance to the lian aspect of face, because it would be at odds with the individual’s internal referents for adequate behaviour. Furthermore, an individual prone to blaming others will be conceived of as engaging in activities that are generally seen as not socially acceptable (because, after all, next time he or she might blame us in order to save himself or herself). If future studies confirm this explanation, it will have consequences for how face-saving is conceived and operationalized in future studies. This also has implications for the challenge of how to encourage bad news reporting on troubled software projects in different cultures.

Before discussing the implications of the study, it is important to understand both the strengths and limitations of the research approach that was taken. Since the results are based on a laboratory experiment, internal validity is high. In addition, a culturally competent and relatively culturally homogeneous subject group such as students provides an advantage for internal validity. However, the study should be replicated across different populations to examine its generalizability and to refine the findings.

The experimental setting of the study also raises the question of generalizability to the actions of managers operating in actual organizations. Clearly, not only face but a number of factors and circumstances influence the reporting behaviour of managers in a real-life organizational setting. In addition to studies using samples from other populations, we would therefore also welcome studies using other methods, in particular intensive methods, which can help understand more complex patterns of face work in the context of software project control within different national cultures. In spite of these limitations, the study holds interesting theoretical and practical implications and lays the groundwork for future research aimed at understanding the extent to which these results can be generalized.

## Implications for research

This study goes beyond a surface view of face as something that is just ‘more’ or ‘less’ important in different cultures. Employment of the concepts of lian and mianzi provides a much richer view of face and face-saving. The study clearly shows that in mianzi-dominated culture such as the USA, the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity has a significant influence on subjects’ willingness to report bad news about a troubled software project. It is notable that the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity had a significant effect even though the reporting climate was portrayed as being non-conducive. The presence of a blame-shifting opportunity, however, did not seem to have as strong an effect in a culture such as South Korea that puts comparatively more emphasis on the lian aspect of face. Although the means were in the expected direction, they were not found to be significantly different. This supports the idea that face-saving behaviour is culturally sensitive and strongly suggests that this in turn affects reporting behaviour in software projects.

These results contribute to IS theory by adding to our present understanding of bad news reporting in software project contexts. While the IS literature contains studies that have examined such factors as impact, wrongdoing, organizational climate and information asymmetry on individuals’ willingness to report bad news, there have been no studies that have examined culturally constituted views of face and how this impacts bad news reporting for software projects.

Furthermore, cross-cultural IS studies have identified the challenge of project control and coordination across cultural boundaries, both in the context of offshore software development outsourcing and in the context of globally dispersed software development teams. In both of these contexts, effective project control becomes even more difficult than for IS development in mono-cultural settings. Previous cross-cultural IS studies have also called for more research on the impact of culture on issues specific to IS. Few such studies exist and consequently, this study, as the first study of the impact of culturally constituted views of face on bad news reporting on software projects, addresses an important, identified knowledge gap in the crosscultural IS literature.

## Implications for practice

Accurate status reporting should be an issue of paramount concern to practitioners, because it is a key control mechanism for software projects and because recent research has linked poor reporting quality to negative project outcomes. Obtaining accurate status reports is particularly challenging in the context of software development and it appears that one of the most important factors in securing reporting quality is to overcome the human tendency to withhold bad news. With the growing interest and activity in both global software teams and the offshoring of software development work, managers would be well advised to consider the consequences of these trends with respect to reporting accuracy.

One practical implication of this research is that software projects in some cultures bear a greater risk for non-reporting and thus escalation. In particular, firms operating in cultures in which the lian aspect of face is salient might be more prone to escalation because blameshifting is not a helpful strategy for increasing bad news reporting. For firms normally operating in mianzi-dominated cultures, differences in reporting propensity is arguably an important factor to consider in offshore outsourcing of software development.

In mianzi-dominated cultures, where a blame-shifting opportunity appears to have a significant effect on bad news reporting, we would expect that software projects involving known problems with outside contractors would be less prone to the mum effect than, say, in-house software development projects, where there is no obvious party to blame for problems that may arise. This is a testable hypothesis that bears further investigation.

In the contexts of global software teams and off-shoring of software development outsourcing work, managers should be aware that software development teams located in different parts of the world may behave quite differently with respect to their willingness to report bad news. Thus, managers must remain attuned to both situational and cultural factors that may inhibit accurate software project status reporting.

For global software development teams, an important practical challenge highlighted by the results of this study is to design and enact effective control and reporting arrangements across borders and cultures. One option is to design and use a common project control system for the whole team, but to enact this control system (in relation to groups at different locations) with sensitivity towards cultural differences in reporting behaviour. If this is done, it is of paramount importance for reporting accuracy that project managers and executives who exercise control understand not only cultural differences in general, but also specifically how these cultural differences, such as pertaining to face, impact bad news reporting behaviour. Another option, depending on the specifics of the task, might be to institute a loosely coupled control system that is implemented in a culturally sensitive fashion for work groups belonging to different cultures. This allows for more culturally determined variation in control and reporting procedures and their enactment than a uniform control system. However, if this is to work, interdependencies between sub-teams probably need to be minimized and the coordination of the overall project deftly managed, for example, by a project (or programme) office.

In offshore outsourcing of software development, contractual agreements need to be monitored through control arrangements, including status reporting. Here, it becomes important to consider, first, whether the outsourcing partner is capable of ensuring internal accuracy in status reporting (since face may be more likely to inhibit bad news reporting in some cultures than in others) and second, whether issues of face inhibit the outsourcing partner from reporting project status accurately to the client, particularly in circumstances where bad news need to be communicated. In other words, distortion of project reporting related to face-saving may occur both internally within the subcontracting firm and externally in relation to the client firm.

In both of these cross-cultural software project settings, it is important to consider and conceive of culturally attuned ways to facilitate face-saving in order to facilitate bad news reporting.

## CONCLUDING REMARKS

While this study has contributed to our understanding of bad news reporting in the context of troubled software projects, there is clearly much work to be done. Helping members of organizations to save face when they come forwards to report problems appears to be a viable strategy for reducing the reluctance to report bad news. A key area for future research is to find ways to facilitate face-saving that do not necessitate the presence of a party onto which blame can be shifted. This appears to be particularly important in cultures where the lian aspect of face is salient and the presence of a blame-shifting opportunity appears to have little, if any, effect on bad news reporting.

## Biographies

Mark Keil is the Board of Advisors Professor and Department Chair of Computer Information Systems in the J. Mack Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University. His research focuses on software project management, with particular emphasis on understanding and preventing software project escalation – cases in which projects seem to take on lives of their own, continuing to absorb valuable resources without ever reaching their objectives. Keil’s research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Sloan Management Review, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Information Systems Journal, Decision Support Systems, and other journals. He currently serves on the editorial boards of IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Decision Sciences, and the Journal of Management Information Systems. He has also served as an Associate Editor for MIS Quarterly, and as Co-Editor of The DATA BASE for Advances in Information Systems. He earned his bachelor’s degree from Princeton University, his master’s degree from MIT’s Sloan School of Management, and his doctorate in management information systems from the Harvard Business School.

Ghi Paul Im is an Assistant Professor in the Decision Science Department at Clark Atlanta University. His interests centre on organizational learning and knowledge management enabled by information technology, and their implications for firm strategy, the competitiveness of nations, and solutions to social problems. His current research interests are in knowledge management in software development and interorganizational learning facilitated by information technology. His research has been published in The DATA BASE for Advances in Information Systems and Expert Systems with Applications. He received his PhD from Georgia State University and an MS from NYU Stern School of Business.

Magnus Mähring is Assistant Professor in the Department of Marketing and Strategy at the Stockholm School of Economics. During 2002–2003, he was Visiting Research Scholar in the Computer Information Systems Department, J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University. His current research focuses on governance of large IT projects, project escalation and deescalation processes, executive and board involvement in IT, and the dynamics and limitations of organizational control in these contexts. He has coedited two research anthologies, of which one, Exploring Patterns in Information Management, is the first offering of the Association for Information Systems Virtual Bookstore. He has extensive teaching and consulting experience and has consulted with organizations including Bang & Olufsen, Microsoft, Scandinavian Airlines Systems, The Swedish Red Cross and municipal public organizations.
