# Bidding Patterns, Experience, and Avoiding the Winner’s Curse in Online Auctions

Ro ber t F. Eas le y, Char les A. Wod , and Shar ad Bar kataki

Rober t F. Easley is an associate professor of information technology management in the Management Department of the Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame. He holds a Ph.D. in decision and information systems from Indiana University, Bloomington, and an MBA from Pennsylvania State University. His current research focuses on economic modeling of Internet auctions, privacy and piracy in e-commerce, recommendation systems, and collaborative technologies.

Char les A. Wood is an assistant professor in the management information systems area at the Palumbo-Donahue School of Business at Duquesne University in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota’s Carlson School of Management. His research interests are e-commerce, electronic auctions, buyer and seller reputations, and pricing in Internet-based selling. His research appears in Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Information Technology and Management, Electronic Commerce Research and Applications, Managerial Decisions and Economics, Information Systems Frontiers, and International Journal of Intelligent Systems in Accounting, Finance and Management. He has authored multiple books on software development, database management, and computer systems development.

Shar ad Bar kataki is an assistant professor at the American University in Dubai. He received his Ph.D. in management from Purdue University. His research interests lie at the intersection of information systems, economics, and operations management.

Abstr ac t: The design and implementation of online auctions has given rise to a unique set of bidding strategies that has stimulated a growing body of research. We make use of a theoretically grounded, well-understood, and empirically observable bidder behavior—the winner’s curse adjustment for the expected number of bidders in an auction—to examine the relationships between bidder experience, bidding patterns, and the winner’s curse adjustment in rare coin online auctions. We also examine the impact of uncertainty on the winner’s curse adjustment, both by using precise measures of uncertainty and by considering seller and bidder strategies for reducing that uncertainty. We analyze a complete record of all auctions in a three-month period for rare U.S. coins, examining 284,681 bids from 62,625 auctions hosted by eBay, the market leader in online auctions. One of the main contributions of this paper is to demonstrate that the bidding patterns associated with different bidders are strongly related to whether they calculate their bids to take into account the number of competing bidders, as predicted for common-value auctions. This is a substantial extension and empirical confirmation of prior work that has explored the implications of different observed patterns of bidding. We also explore new territory by examining the relationships between bidder experience, bidding patterns observed, and the economic outcomes for bidders. We are able to show that bidders with more domain-specific experience (rather than general auction experience) make better adjustments for the winner’s curse, that experience has an effect on the type of bidding strategy, and that the type of bidding strategy has a significant effect on the economic outcomes for the bidders.

Key w or ds and phr ases: bidder experience, bidder strategies, bidding patterns, online auctions, winner’s curse.

In r ec ent year s, Inter net auc tions have bec ome increasingly important outlets for a wide variety of products. In 2009, eBay, the market leader in online auctions, assisted buyers and sellers in \$57.2 billion worth of transactions, and by the second quarter in 2010, eBay boasted approximately 89.5 million active users.<sup>1</sup> Existing retailers such as Disney, Goebel, and Enesco have started to utilize online auctions to reach collectible customers. In fact, in an eBay pilot program, these three companies alone have sold over \$2.6 million over eBay, according to eBay officials [11]. Online auctions have become such a potentially profitable channel that major retailers such as Disney and Dell have started their own online auction Web sites to sell their products. Thus, online auctions have become markets of considerable size and importance, and factors that affect bidding behavior and strategies can literally affect billions of transactions.

Online consumer auctions have a number of characteristics that distinguish them from traditional auctions. These include low barriers to entry, low fixed cost of participation, simple search and monitoring mechanisms, and a primarily Web-based presentation of product, quality, and delivery information. The underlying Internet technologies also support a wide range of online auction types, including hybrids of traditional auctions, by encoding auction rules in a series of Web forms and related services. For example, eBay’s mechanism combines features of both traditional ascending and sealed-bid second-price auctions: a bidder at eBay can enter a single bid that will not be revealed if it remains the highest bid at the fixed closing of the auction and pay the amount of the second-highest bid. On the other hand, if the bid is exceeded at some point, the bid is revealed and becomes the price to be paid by the bidder placing the new higher bid. An eBay bidder who is outbid in this manner can either exit the auction or enter any number of additional ascending bids, attempting to surpass existing bids or responding to new bids when they are entered. Online auctions thus have their own particular strategy space, as noted by Bapna et al. [3, 4], who identified several different bidding strategies that bidders appear to follow in online auctions. There is a growing body of literature examining bidder patterns in both consumer (e.g., [15, 31]) and business-to-business (e.g., [13]) Internet auctions.

The same advances in Internet technology that permit easy public access and participation in online auctions have also permitted researchers to capture large detailed data sets for analysis. Comprehensive data sets of this type are especially suitable to examining and identifying some of the more nuanced predictions of recent theories addressing bidder behavior in online auctions. They enable clear detection of theoretically predicted phenomena such as the shading of bids with respect to the expected number of bidders. They also enable the identification of relationships between bidding patterns, experience, and online bidding strategies, which have not been examined in prior literature.

We use a comprehensive data set for online auctions of collectible rare coins. The size of the data set enables us to confirm the existence of the predicted winner’s curse adjustment <sup>2</sup> in the expected number of bidders; we find generally that bidders lower their bids in anticipation of greater competition. We then examine the relationship of bidding patterns to the bidder’s implementation of this predicted adjustment and show that different bidding patterns are associated with significantly different adjustments. We also provide clear evidence supporting the predicted winner’s curse adjustment in response to increased uncertainty concerning item valuation.

The three-month time period covered in our data collection permits us to examine two measures of bidder experience—overall experience with eBay auctions and specific experience with coin auctions—as well as their relation to both the bidder’s implementation of the winner’s curse adjustment and choice of bidding strategy. We show that bidders with higher levels of domain-specific experience make stronger winner’s curse adjustments, and that both types of experience are related to the bidding patterns observed. Next, we outline the related literature and our empirical predictions; then, we present our data and analysis. In the fourth section we present our results. The paper closes with our discussion and conclusions.

## Prior Research and Hypothesis Development

In this sec tion w e r eview pr ior r esearc h r esults and develop four hypotheses. In the first subsection, we summarize well-established theoretical predictions concerning the adjustments bidders are predicted to make in common-value auctions to avoid succumbing to the winner’s curse. The classic example of these adjustments involves bidding on oil leases, which arguably have the same—though at the time of bidding, unknown—value to whoever wins the lease. Bidders perform due diligence in attempting to estimate the value as best they can, but they also realize that if they win, this is likely to reflect the fact that their estimate was the most optimistic [5]. Moreover, the more bidders who participate, the more overly optimistic the winner’s estimate of the value is likely to be conditional on winning; thus any bid adjustment grows in the number of bidders. This bid adjustment, described in the next subsection, has been shown with eBay data in other literature, so our confirmation of this finding is a preliminary step that underscores the common-value nature of the auctions we examine. The hypotheses presented below establish the manner in which the predicted adjustment for uncertainty would be expected to play out in the context of the online auctions we examine. In the oil lease example, the greater the uncertainty surrounding a bidder’s estimate of the oil lease value, the more likely the bidder is to adjust the bid downward. The level of uncertainty for an auctioned item is generally much more difficult to estimate than the number of bidders, but since a published book value is either clearly available or not in our context, we are able to address this prediction with an objective measure.

We move on to further novel contributions with our final three hypotheses, first by examining the relationship of both bidder behavior (as evidenced in the pattern of the bids) and experience level with the predicted bid adjustment in the expected number of bidders and then by examining the interrelationship of observed bidding patterns and bidder experience levels. Then, we consider the Evaluator and Participator strategies identified in Bapna et al. [3] as they relate, given other recent theoretical developments, to the strength of the expected winner’s curse adjustment. We then further examine how the winner’s curse adjustment is moderated by the experience level of the bidder. Finally, we explore the possibility of predicting a bidder’s bidding pattern given the bidder’s overall level of experience in online auctions and the bidder’s specific level of activity within the online rare coin auction market.

## Prior Research on the Winner’s Curse Adjustment in the Number of Bidders

A winner’s curse is said to occur when a bidder wins an auction for an item of unknown but common value to all bidders and then determines that the final bid exceeded the item’s value. The winner’s curse results from a failure to reduce bid levels based on the likelihood that the winning bid will result from excessive valuation. It occurs because the winning bidder generally has the highest draw from a distribution of value signals, as noted by Capen [5], who first described the phenomenon. When the item up for auction has a common but unknown value for bidders, the bidding strategy for avoiding the winner’s curse involves discounting from the individual bidder’s value signal, and the implicit value of the item conditional upon winning (i.e., having one’s signal be the highest-value signal), by an amount that is increasing in both the number of bidders and the level of uncertainty about the item’s value.

The implications for the seller are that the winning bid will converge to the item’s true value as the number of bidders increases, and that revealing information that reduces uncertainty about valuation increases the winning bid amount. These basic results were obtained by Rothkopf [27] for items of equal (to all bidders) but uncertain value for a two-parameter Weibull value distribution. A Nash equilibrium solution to the bidder problem is developed in Wilson [33], and the conditions required for “nonaggressive bidding” strategies are described in Smith [28], who provides an alternate description of the somewhat counterintuitive response of bidding less as competition increases. A general affiliated values model that encompasses both the common- and private-value cases is developed in Milgrom and Weber [22]. A summary and overview of these developments is provided in Klemperer [18].

Paarsch [24] found indirect support for the predicted winner’s curse adjustment in the number of bidders for a sample of 144 sealed-bid timber auctions in British Columbia. He found that the Rothkopf model [27] best fit the data, followed by the Levin-Smith model [20], while no private-value models (wherein no winner’s curse occurs and thus no adjustment is predicted) that were tested fit the data. Oh [23] discusses the winner’s curse adjustment in online auctions, comparing predicted and observed behavior regarding the adjustment in both business-to-business and business-to-consumer online auctions. The eBay coin market is a market that has been shown by Bajari and Hortaçsu [2] to reflect a common valuation for rare coins. They studied a sample of 1,248 eBay coin auctions and found “suggestive, but not conclusive” evidence of the predicted winner’s curse adjustment in the number of bidders based on simple regression analysis. They also used a variant of Paarsch’s [24] method to estimate a bidding model based on common-value assumptions. Using the Bajari–Hortaçsu approach, we confirm the evidence for the predicted winner’s curse adjustment.

## The Winner’s Curse and Uncertainty

The predicted winner’s curse adjustment for uncertainty has received less attention from empirical researchers, in part due to the difficulty in measuring uncertainty, especially with respect to valuation. In the oil lease setting, uncertainty may vary by bidder depending on, for example, the quality of seismological data and expertise [5], and it may be impossible to ascertain the uncertainly levels of competitors. Online auctions arguably introduce other sources of uncertainty that may be more easily measured, at least at the auction level. Clemons [6], for example, examines how uncertainty of valuation and of seller intent results in reduced prices in online auctions. The ability to quantify uncertainty in online auctions permits us to test a series of hypotheses based on the classical prediction that bidders will lower their bids in response to greater valuation uncertainty.

We are able to address this issue in a precise manner because the coins in our data set have two distinct levels of availability of market value information, known as the book value, for rare coins. For about 90 percent of the coins auctioned in the market, there is a book value available for the exact grade of the coin offered. For the other 10 percent of the coin auctions, book values must be interpolated using two other grades that bound the coin’s grade. This distinction in levels of uncertainty, precisely focused on the book value of the coin being auctioned, permits us to test the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1a: The final bid placed by each bidder will decrease for items with no readily available book value, relative to items for which there is a posted book value.

There are two other proxies often used for valuation uncertainty that have appeared in other studies of online auctions. One involves uncertainty about the seller’s fulfillment of his or her obligations as captured by the seller score, a ratio of the number of positive to negative ratings he or she has received from other bidders, which is widely interpreted as a reputation score.<sup>3</sup> This score is not directly controllable by the seller, though it is clearly designed to reflect his or her behavior. This is reported as a percentage score, with a score nearing 100 percent representing a seller who has a very good reputation. This is generally assumed to reduce uncertainty related to value of the item conditional on winning because it affects, to a degree, the probability of an accurate description and of actually obtaining the item if it is won.

Hypothesis 1b: The final bid placed by each bidder will increase in the ratio of a seller’s positive comments to total comments.

The other proxy involves the inclusion of a photograph of the item for sale. This is something the seller can decide to include or not, and it has the potential to reduce uncertainty about whether the item is in fact as described. It is thus also indirectly related to item value. We predict that increased item information, such as that provided by a picture, will indirectly reduce valuation uncertainty:

Hypothesis 1c: The final bid placed by each bidder will increase for items that have a picture available.

## Bidder Behavior and the Winner’s Curse Adjustment

Bapna et al. [3] identify three different types of bidders in Internet auctions: Evaluators, with a clear idea of their valuation, bid once earlier in the auction; Participators, who derive some utility from participating in the auction itself, bid multiple times in the auction; and Opportunists, who are risk-seeking bargain hunters, bid at the end of the auction. In this study, we focus on distinguishing the effect of the first two types: Evaluators, who enter a single bid and use the automatic proxy bidding services provided by eBay, and Participators, who seem to derive utility from discovering other bidders’ valuations despite the time and monitoring costs associated with entering multiple values to the proxy bidding agent.<sup>4</sup>

Bajari and Hortaçsu [2] develop a model that builds from an assumption of costly bidding in which there is a single cost for entering and bidding in an auction, consistent with long-standing assumptions about the cost of entering and participating in live auctions. This contrasts with the approach taken by Easley and Tenorio [10], who argue that a model that associates a cost with each bid placed is more appropriate for online auctions, where bidders must typically incur an opportunity cost of time, as well as possible connection and other actual costs, for each bid placed. This leads to differing assumptions about the signaling value of bids, with Bajari and Hortaçsu assuming that bidders may costlessly return at a later time to bid again, thus reducing the value of the information revealed when they are outbid. Easley and Tenorio, by contrast, find that when each bid is costly, it is an equilibrium strategy for bidders to bid only once at a value sufficient to deter lower-valuing bidders from entry. They find empirical support for this prediction of a single bid per bidder even in firstprice auctions without bid agents, where bidders risk leaving money on the table by entering signaling bids. In an eBay auction, we would expect even more Evaluators because this risk is removed by programmed bidding agents that automatically bid for those entering a single bid, thus ensuring the winning bidder will only pay the second-highest bid amount.

But if Easley and Tenorio’s [10] approach is correct, why do we see Participators, or bidders, placing more than one bid to the agent in eBay auctions? A possible explanation is that these are bidders uncertain of their own value estimates who thus want to observe other bidders’ valuations to help them arrive at their own. Rasmusen [25] points out that there is a cost involved in estimating one’s own valuations, and Milgrom and Weber discuss this as well in connection with the predictions of their general model, saying,

when bidders are uncertain about their valuations, they can acquire useful information by scrutinizing the bidding behavior of their competitors during the course of an English auction. That extra information weakens the winner’s curse and leads to more aggressive bidding in the English auction. [22, p. 1095]

In an eBay auction, we assign the labels Evaluator or Participator based on the pattern of bidding that is visible to any competing bidder and captured in our data. Because all bidders at eBay use the same proxy bidding agent mechanism, it is important to understand that they all enter to that agent a value representing the maximum they are willing to pay (MWP) and let the agent “bid” for them. No bidder’s MWP is revealed except when the bidder is outbid, at which point the MWP determines the price at which the auction is being won at a particular point in time. Each time a new bidder enters the auction, or an existing bidder revises his or her MWP, a new entry is made on the bidding history list associated with the auction. Consider a bidder, Jane, who has entered her MWP and is shown to be currently winning the auction at the minimum bid. If a new bidder, Dick, enters the auction and happens to enter the highest MWP to his agent, he will be shown to be the winning bidder at that point, and the price at which Dick is winning is Jane’s MWP. If Dick enters and happens to enter a MWP to his agent that is not as high as Jane’s MWP, she will remain the current leader with a new price equivalent to his MWP. Dick may then decide that he will raise his MWP and try again. If his new MWP is still lower than Jane’s, his new bid will increase the price at which she is still winning. Note that in this case, Jane has not entered a new MWP, her agent is simply calculating that she is still the leader, but at a higher price.

## Bidder Experience and the Winner’s Curse Adjustment

In discussing his groundbreaking work describing the winner’s curse in the petroleum industry, Capen et al. [5] make it clear that experience, in particular, bad experiences of overpaying for oil leases, led initially to a pressure to shade bids downward, and ultimately to an understanding of the underlying winner’s curse phenomenon. Capen recounts initial adjustment efforts that went too far, resulting in “our bids [being] so low we didn’t buy anything” [30, p. 1], as well as resistance to the strategy of systematically lowering bids based on the expected number of bidders: “Many managers do not like this strategy because the reduced bids also lower the chance of winning” [30, p. 1]. Capen was eventually allowed by his company to go public with his findings because the whole industry was better off if all bids were lowered, in effect amounting to a kind of legal collusion in bidding.

Dyer and Kagel [9] describe construction industry–specific mechanisms that enable experienced contractors to make the winner’s curse adjustment in the field, even when they fail to make the adjustment in laboratory settings. This finding underscores the importance of industry-specific auction experience, as opposed to general auction experience, in making the necessary adjustments. Kagel and Richard [14] find experimental support for the idea that inexperienced bidders in laboratory settings make a winner’s curse adjustment, but do not adjust sufficiently, and that bidders asymptotically increase their adjustment as their experience increases before leveling out at higher levels of experience. This suggests that with rare coin auctions, for example, the winner’s curse adjustment should be moderated by rare coin auction experience, and the moderations should be most pronounced with less-experienced bidders. Because overall online auction experience is a superset containing rare coin auction experience, we expect that the former will also have a moderating effect on the winner’s curse adjustment, although to a lesser degree than the latter. We state a general hypothesis here and test this contention with both general and rare coin auction–specific experience measures, as described in the third section:

Hypothesis 3: The reduction of the final bid placed by each bidder in the expected number of bidders will be moderated so that bidders with greater auction experience will decrease their bid amount to a greater extent.

## Bidder Experience and Observed Bidding Patterns

Given that we expect both bidder experience and bidding patterns to be related to the strength of the observed winner’s curse adjustment, it seems reasonable to expect that as bidder experience increases, bidders will adopt behaviors that most profitably leverage their experience. Roth and Ockenfels [26] compare the hard close of an eBay auction to the soft close of an Amazon.com auction and find evidence that due to the hard close of the eBay auction, experienced bidders are more likely to bid late rather than early. Stafford and Stern [29] assesses the impact of related factors, such as familiarity with computers and acceptance of technology, on bidding participation in online auctions. Wilcox [32] finds empirical support for the hypothesis that more-experienced bidders tend to behave closer to Nash equilibrium predictions than less-experienced bidders. This suggests that bidders with different bidding experience may choose different bidding strategies. Given the work of Bajari and Hortaçsu [2], Bapna et al. [4], and Easley and Tenorio [10], we would expect that bidders would move to the single-bid Evaluator pattern as they gain experience.

As noted in the previous subsection, we expect rare coin auction experience to have a stronger moderating effect on the winner’s curse adjustment than general auction experience. However, the strategic advantages of the Evaluator approach may become apparent in any type of online auction. Thus, we state a general hypothesis here and test this contention with both general and rare coin auction–specific experience measures, as we did for Hypothesis 3:

Hypothesis 4: Bidders with greater auction experience are more likely to adopt a single-bid Evaluator approach.

## Data and Empirical Model

In this section, we give an overview of the bidding process on eBay and then describe the data, hypotheses, and empirical model used in this research.

## Data

We used a data-collecting agent to collect online auction data for three months on every rare coin auction in eBay from April 24, 2002, to September 11, 2002. Our data set includes auction information (e.g., ending time, selling price, number of bids, item description, comments left for an item, etc.), seller information (e.g., seller rating, previous ratings left for seller, seller reputation score, number of auctions seller hosted), and bidder information (auctions bid on, previous bidder ratings). We developed an expert system that identifies coins using pattern recognition from the item text and the item description. Using this technique, we are able to identify specific coins by deriving (1) the mint year (e.g., 1802, 1798), (2) the grade of the coin (from 3 to 70, e.g., Very Good 8, Extra Fine 40), and (3) the denomination (e.g., ½-cent piece, 20-cent piece, penny) and special mint marks and coin features (e.g., D (Denver mint), DDO (doubled die obverse)). Our resulting data set contains 284,681 distinct bidder/auction combinations. This data set describes 62,625 identified single-coin rare coin auctions hosted by 4,606 distinct sellers with 21,147 distinct bidders (of which 13,514 bidders won at least one auction in the data set) on 4,408 distinct coins (i.e., different denomination/mint year/mint mark/condition combinations). We only included distinct coins sold in at least five auctions so that a common value for these coins could be estimated.

For this study, we examine only the final bid placed by each bidder, recording as well whether that bidder has entered multiple bids for the same item. Thus, if a bidder bids three times in an auction, we consider only that bidder’s highest bid as a signal of that bidder’s valuation. Note also that we consider every bidder’s high bid, not just the winning bid. We capture two measures of bidder experience levels, one general and one coin specific, as discussed above. The BidderScore measure is the number of comments left for both buying and selling experience in all eBay auctions, not just coin auctions. The scope and duration of our data collection also allow us to capture a count of the total number of rare coin auctions that a bidder has participated in during the period we study, which we label BidderActivity. Since this is a count of rare coin auctions that bidders participate in until their final auction in the data set, we can only use BidderActivity in the analysis of a data subset that contains only the bidder’s final bid in the bidder’s final auction during the period of study.

Coin types and current book values for the coins in our data set were obtained from Coin World Online (www.coinworld.com), which mirrors the listing in coin-collecting books [12] and in periodicals. Coin World Online’s book values are typically the prices charged by dealers at stores. They represent market prices for collectors with a small dealer margin. Coin World Online is responsible for providing bidders at eBay with a list of coin values through an easily accessible link on the rare coin bidding site, and most coin-collecting literature mirrors Coin World in the coins and grades that are listed.

Approximately 10 percent of the coins in our sample were at grades whose book value is not reported in the mainstream coin literature. Because valuations do not have a linear relationship to grade, to interpolate missing coin values, we took the average percentage of valuation for all coins where some valuation is reported for the coin grade as well as a bounding grade both above and below. For example, one coin in our study is a 1903 Indian Head Penny, graded at AU-55 (almost uncirculated, rated 55 on a 70-point scale). Most coin listings and magazines, including Coin World and the online Coin World listing easily accessible on eBay, do not report grades at every level, and AU-55 is not reported for this particular coin, although valuations are reported for this coin for the AU-50 grade at \$15.00 and the BU-60 (brilliantly uncirculated, rated 60 on a 70-point scale) grade at \$22.50. However, some other coins do list all three grades (AU-50, AU-55, and BU-60). When all three grades are listed, on average, the valuation for an AU-55 coin is 31.48 percent between an AU-50 and a BU-60 ranking. Thus, the valuation we assign for this coin would be calculated at $0 . 3 1 4 8 ^ { * }$ (\$22.50 – \$15.00) + \$15.00, or \$17.361. This is a convex function, so bidders comfortable with simple linear interpolation would obtain a higher estimate of book value than ours, providing us with a conservative measure of any detected adjustment.

The rare coin online auction market has been shown in Bajari and Hortaçsu [2] to reflect a common valuation for rare coins.<sup>7</sup> The existence of a published book value for most of the coins may seem to suggest that the common value could easily be determined relative to, or perhaps even equivalent to, this posted price. Such a fixed and known common value would remove the threat of the winner’s curse, but would also obviate the need for an auction. However, in practice, the published book value is something akin to a suggested retail price, which most bidders seem to treat as an upper bound to the market value. To illustrate this, we plot the distribution of winning bids in our data relative to the published book value of the coin in Figure 4, which shows an approximately normal distribution of winning bids with a mean of around 60 percent of the book value. Note that the right tail is longer than shown but the entire tail above the 100 percent of Book Value mark contains only 1.6 percent of all winning bids.

## Heteroskedasticity, and Weights

Our initial OLS analysis revealed that we had a heteroskedasticity problem in our data set.<sup>8</sup> We identified the activity level of the bidder to be the biggest source of heteroskedasticity in our data set—that is, the most active bidders tend to skew the results. To resolve this problem with heteroskedasticity, we use a weighted least squares procedure described in Wooldridge [34]. In our data set, we weight each observation by the multiplicative inverse of the number of auctions in this study in which that bidder participated. Thus, each observation of an active buyer is reduced in weight so that every buyer contributes at the same level, restoring the homoskedasticity of the model while ensuring that every observation adds information to the regression analysis.<sup>9</sup>

## Endogeneity and Independence

The predicted winner’s curse adjustment is inversely related in magnitude to the number of bidders. However, the actual number of participating bidders remains uncertain until the end of an eBay auction. Thus, bidders must predict the number of bidders in an auction before placing their bids so that they may shade their bids appropriately. If we wish to identify evidence of a winner’s curse adjustment in these auctions, we must be able to endogenously predict the expected number of bidders in an auction at the time the bidder makes his or her final bid.

To address possible instability of coefficient estimates due to endogeneity, we use a two-stage nonlinear method where an instrument variable is developed using the information available to the bidder at the time of the bid. At the time bidders place their bids, they observe several auction characteristics, such as the number of other bidders in the auction at that time, the current bid in the auction, the book value of the coin, the time remaining until the auction closes, whether the auction is closing on a weekend, and whether the auction is a featured auction. We use this information to develop and test an instrument variable that estimates, at the time a bid is placed, the expected final number of bidders in an auction. To develop this instrument variable, we note that the prior literature (in particular, [2]) has assumed that the number of bidders in an auction follows a Poisson process. This is a reasonable assumption given that the Poisson model is often used to model count data (including the number of bidders in an auction) and that the number of bidders in an auction will always be nonnegative.

Zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) models are commonly used to model count data with a large number of zeros. The assumptions behind the ZIP model are that with probability $p ,$ we observe zero, and with probability $1 - p ,$ we observe a Poisson (λ) random variable. In our setting, this corresponds to instances when a bidder arriving at an auction will expect no additional bidders for the item with probability $p .$ However, in other instances with probability $1 - p ,$ the bidder will expect that the additional bidders will enter the auction according to a Poisson distribution. Note that the ZIP model allows both $p$ and $\lambda$ to be able to depend on covariates. It may be that the availability of a picture is attractive to those who are less sure of their valuation estimates, and thus are also more likely to bid multiple times, and, as we saw earlier, are less likely to make the winner’s curse adjustment.

Although both types of experience have a significant effect in moving a bidder toward a single-bid Evaluator approach, it remains the case that a great majority of bidders continue as Participators. This is shown clearly in Figure 6, which presents an approximate quartile analysis (with about 65,000 to 78,000 bidders per quartile, depending on cutoffs) for both BidderScore and BidderActivity . For both measures, the probability of acting as an Evaluator increases as experience increases, although an individual bidder level analysis would be needed to draw any firm conclusions. This is a question we hope to address in future research.

In this connection, it is natural to examine how the bidding pattern observed relates to the final price paid. If coin auction experience leads bidders to act as Evaluators, one might expect that this strategy should lead to better results. Yet Bapna et al. propose and find just the opposite: “An increase in the number of bidders in the winning set who adopt a nonparticipatory bidding strategy has a nondecreasing effect on the auctioneer’s revenue” [4, p. 92].

Looking at all auctions together in our data set and examining only the winning bids in these auctions, Evaluators tend to pay slightly more than Participators (69 percent versus 68 percent of book value), which is consistent with Bapna et al.’s [4] findings. Yet when we partition the data by book value of the coins, as shown in Figure 7, we find that Evaluators, on average, tend to pay less, as a percentage of published book value, than Participators across all book value ranges. This is explained by the fact that Evaluators have a stronger presence in lower-value auctions (dashed line) and Participators have a stronger presence in higher-value auctions (solid line). This means that the higher apparent overpayments associated with lower-value coins are more heavily weighted in the overall average for Evaluators, even though they consistently do better than Participants in all book value ranges. We would argue then that by partitioning the data into reasonable value ranges, it becomes clear that the Evaluators have an advantage in terms of price paid conditional on winning.

## Discussion and Conclusions

In this paper , w e lay the gr oundw or k f or our c ontr ibutions by extending a wellestablished theoretical prediction concerning the efforts of bidders to avoid the winner’s curse in common-value auctions—the strategic lowering of bids in response to an increased expected number of bidders. We confirm a related theoretical prediction of a lowering of bids in response to increased uncertainty about an item’s value. This effect has also been found in prior studies using various proxies for uncertainty, some of which we also consider, but we provide more robust confirmation using a measure that captures two precisely defined and clearly differentiated levels of publicly available valuation information.

Our main contributions involve further examination of bidder behaviors in online auctions, as described by Bapna et al. [3, 4], paying particular attention to the apparent advantages of the single-bid Evaluator strategy over the multiple-bid Participator pattern as discussed in Bajari and Hortaçsu [2] and Easley and Tenorio [10]. We find strong support for our prediction that Evaluators will make a stronger winner’s curse adjustment by showing that Participators in fact make a significant positive adjustment in the expected number of bidders. Studies based on data sets as large as the one we use are sometimes criticized for the apparent ease with which highly significant results are obtained. The fact that we find not only a significant difference in the adjustment but an actual reversal of direction (–24.5 percent to +15.2 percent) may help set such concerns to rest.

The positive adjustment observed for Participators also lends support to the interpretation that bidding can be used in a discovery process to determine another bidder’s estimate of an item’s valuation, especially for uninformed bidders [22, 26]. Thus, inexperienced bidders may rely on other bids to develop their own estimate of an item’s valuation, and are thus more likely to be Participators, bidding several times for an item to try to gather information about other bidders’ estimated valuation of the item being auctioned.

Because our data span three months and we capture data on many bidders who bid in multiple auctions, we are able to distinguish between two experience measures, one that is often used to capture overall online auction experience and the other, which is a proxy for domain-specific (in this case, coin auction–specific) experience, which is more likely to reflect domain-specific valuation abilities. We show that although overall experience has no significant moderating effect on the ability of bidders to make the predicted winner’s curse adjustment, domain-specific experience does have a moderating effect. This is consistent with the interpretation, discussed above, that Participators may be willing to increase their risk of incurring the winner’s curse as a form of payment for valuation information, and thus tend to raise their bids in response to increased competition.

We are also able to demonstrate a relationship between experience and bidder behavior, with greater experience of both types seen to increase the likelihood that a bidder will use the single-bid Evaluator approach. As discussed in a number of recent publications (e.g., [2, 10, 26]), there are good reasons to adopt the Evaluator approach beyond simply having the valuation expertise to use it effectively, so it is not surprising that even general auction experience leads over time to increased appearance of the Evaluator pattern. Taken as a whole, however, our results do suggest some possible limitations on the effect of overall online auction experience: although it may lead to better bidding strategy choice over time, it may not have much impact on domain-specific valuation abilities.
