# Using Information Systems to Sense Opportunities for Innovation: Integrating Postadoptive Use Behaviors with the Dynamic Managerial Capability Perspective

NICHOLAS ROBERTS, DAMON E. CAMPBELL, AND LEO R. VIJAYASARATHY

NICHOLAS ROBERTS (nick.roberts@colostate.edu; corresponding author) is an assistant professor of computer information systems in the College of Business at Colorado State University. He received his Ph.D. from Clemson University. His research interests include the determination of the business value of information technologies and organizational learning.

DAMON E. CAMPBELL (damon.campbell@millsaps.edu) is an associate professor of management information systems and Kelley Gene Cook, Sr. Chair of Business Administration at the Else School of Management at Millsaps College. He holds a Ph.D. from Washington State University. His research interests include e-commerce, human–computer interaction, and interface design. He has published in Information Systems Research, Decision Sciences, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and other journals and conference proceedings.

LEO R. VIJAYASARATHY (leo.vijayasarathy@colostate.edu) is an associate professor of computer information systems in the College of Business at Colorado State University. He earned his Ph.D. from Florida International University. His research on the development, use, and consequences of information systems has been published in Electronic Markets, European Journal of Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Professional Communications, Information and Management, and Journal of Management Information Systems. He serves on the editorial advisory board of Internet Research.

ABSTRACT: Fast-paced environmental changes require that managers quickly sense opportunities for organizational innovation. Information systems (IS) that support business intelligence and analytics help managers access and analyze data from various sources, thereby providing insight into potential opportunities. Building on the dynamic managerial capability perspective, we investigate the extent to which two managerial IS use behaviors—routine use and innovative use—influence a manager’s volume and diversity of ideas for organizational innovation. We also examine the moderating role of three organization-level entrepreneurial orientation characteristics—autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking. We test our research model with survey data collected from 248 managers. Our results show that routine IS use is not related to volume or diversity of ideas for organizational innovation. However, innovative IS use is positively related to idea volume and idea diversity. Furthermore, organizational autonomy and innovativeness positively moderate the aforementioned innovative use/idea relationships. Our study contributes to the literature by linking postadoptive IS use behaviors to managerial sensing ability, an important dynamic managerial capability. We also further the understanding of how organizational factors such as entrepreneurial orientation play a key role in determining whether, when, and how managers use IS to develop ideas for organizational innovation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business analytics, decision support systems, dynamic managerial capability, idea set, innovative use of IS, IS use, IT business value, sensing ability.

As global markets evolve and new forms of technology and competition arise, contemporary firms must sense opportunities and threats, effectively seize those opportunities, and continually reconfigure their intangible and tangible assets [84]. To endure and thrive under conditions of fast-paced change, firms should develop the “dynamic capabilities” that enable them to create, extend, or modify their resource base [39]. The ability to sense opportunities is one key dynamic capability. To effectively sense opportunities, organizations must frequently scan and explore “local” and “distant” technologies and markets [69]. Information technology (IT) can readily facilitate an organization’s sensing capability [76]. For example, organizations can leverage information networks [94], IT infrastructure capabilities [35], interorganizational interpretation systems [62], and virtual customer communities [74] to help identify market opportunities.

Research on the relationship between IT and sensing has been largely conducted at the organizational level of analysis. This is not surprising given the fact that dynamic capability research is rooted in organizational-level perspectives such as evolutionary economics and the resource-based view [39]. However, recent scholarship stresses that managers play important roles in recognizing opportunities [8]. Specifically, a manager’s capacity to sense opportunities before they fully materialize is a critical dynamic managerial capability [41]. Potential antecedents to managerial sensing ability include researching target market segments, directing internal research and development efforts, and using information systems (IS) to access and manipulate information [84]. Thus, similar to IT’s impact at the organizational level, IT can likely facilitate a manager’s sensing ability. Specifically, managers could use IS to retrieve and analyze data that help them gain insight into potential opportunities for innovation and competitive advantage. Yet despite a wellestablished stream of research in this area [20, 43], we do not have a clear picture of the ways in which managers might use IS to sense opportunities. Thus, our first research objective is to determine if a manager’s IS use is related to a manager’s sensing ability.

Dynamic capability research suggests that organizational factors can influence a manager’s sensing ability [39]. An organization’s entrepreneurial orientation is one such factor. Entrepreneurial orientation refers to an organizational-level disposition to engage in a set of five distinct but related behaviors (autonomy, competitive aggressiveness, innovativeness, proactiveness, and risk taking) that lead to change in the organization or marketplace [60]. For example, organizations that encourage autonomy among their members are less likely to be blindsided by market and technological developments [85]. At the organizational level, theory suggests that entrepreneurial orientation moderates the relationship between IT capabilities and sensing ability [76]. In the same way, we propose that entrepreneurial orientation will influence the relationship between a manager’s IS use and his/her ability to sense opportunities for innovation. Hence, our second objective is to determine whether an organization’s entrepreneurial orientation affects the relationship between IS use and sensing ability.

## Theoretical Foundation

Figure 1 depicts our conceptual framework. Our primary theoretical base is dynamic managerial capabilities. This theory argues that “firms whose managers have superior dynamic managerial capabilities can adapt and change more successfully than firms whose managers have less effective or no dynamic managerial capabilities” [40, p. 24]. Dynamic managerial capabilities include the ability to sense opportunities, seize opportunities, and reconfigure organizational assets. Individual characteristics such as managerial IS use, cognition, and social capital influence the creation and development of dynamic managerial capabilities [40]. Furthermore, organizational characteristics such as the organization’s identity, culture, entrepreneurial orientation, and governance structure may influence the relationship between individual characteristics and dynamic managerial capabilities.

With this framework in mind, we look at one dynamic managerial capability— sensing. We also view IS use as a behavior that falls under the individual characteristics umbrella. Finally, we consider the role of entrepreneurial orientation—a key organizational characteristic—in our study. We elaborate on these ideas as we build our theoretical foundation. Dynamic managerial capabilities are an outgrowth of the dynamic capability perspective [1]; thus, we begin this section by discussing dynamic capabilities.

## The Dynamic Capability Perspective

The dynamic capability perspective is grounded in the evolutionary theory of the firm [69]. Since managers make decisions under uncertainty and are boundedly rational, they satisfice rather than optimize when searching for and selecting solutions to problems [64]. The implication is that firms should develop and maintain dynamic capabilities that can be leveraged to continuously create, extend, upgrade, and protect their unique resource base. Dynamic capability is formally defined as “the [organization’s] ability to sense and then seize new opportunities, and to reconfigure and protect knowledge assets, competencies, and complementary assets with the aim of achieving a sustained competitive advantage” [8, p. 412, emphasis in orginal]. The essence of the dynamic capability approach is that competitive success arises from the continuous creation, extension, and reconfiguration of firm-specific assets [39, 85]. Broadly speaking, dynamic capability can be broken down into three types—the capability to sense opportunities, the capacity to seize opportunities, and the ability to manage threats by combining, recombining, and reconfiguring assets inside and outside the firm’s boundaries [84].

Given its roots in the evolutionary theory of the firm and the emphasis on firmlevel competitive advantage, researchers have often investigated dynamic capabilities at the organizational level [39, 51]. However, recent studies point to the important role of dynamic managerial capabilities [8, 40, 41]. Managers play critical roles in recognizing opportunities, making investment choices, designing appropriate business models, and orchestrating assets to improve efficiencies and appropriate returns from innovation [8]. Empirical studies provide evidence of systematic managerial impact on firm performance across a diverse range of firm types and industries [40]. Moreover, organizational success increasingly requires that managers behave in an entrepreneurial manner and build into their organization the ability to sense and seize opportunities [39]. Furthermore, IT has the potential to play a key role in enhancing dynamic managerial capabilities [84].

We focus on one key dynamic managerial capability, namely, a manager’s ability to sense new opportunities. We define managerial sensing ability as the capacity of an individual manager to identify opportunities for organizational innovation. We note three important aspects of this definition. First, our definition is consistent with the term “capability” in dynamic capability research, where capability refers to the capacity to perform an activity in a generally reliable manner when called upon to do so [3]. Capabilities develop in part through experience and practice. As a manager gains experience sensing opportunities, the capacity to perform this activity again in the future tends to improve [6]. Second, opportunities for organizational innovation include—but are not limited to—ideas for growing the business, for improving its competitive position, for making systems or processes work more efficiently, and for improving customer service. Finally, our definition shifts the traditional dynamic capability unit of analysis from the firm to the manager. Put another way, we investigate sensing ability at the individual (manager) level, not the organizational level.

Broadly speaking, two factors influence a manager’s ability to sense opportunities [84]. First, managers can have differential access to existing information [52]. Second, new information and new knowledge can help managers recognize opportunities [78]. Thus, opportunity recognition requires both access to information and the ability to sense developments. Furthermore, managers must scan both “local” and “distant” technologies and markets [56, 69]. This involves understanding customer needs, technological possibilities, industry evolution, and likely supplier and competitor responses. Dynamic capabilities also encompass creating change as well as reacting to it [32]; hence, sensing includes alertness and a discovery process. In summary, the ability to sense opportunities before they fully materialize is an important dynamic managerial capability.

Despite its central importance to the dynamic managerial capability perspective, empirical studies on the individual recognition of opportunities are sparse [48]. However, recent advances in construct development provide a relatively comprehensive profile of (potentially multiple) opportunities identified and considered by individuals. Namely, we use the concept of “idea sets” [42] to help us conceptualize and operationalize managerial sensing ability. Idea set is defined as “the complete stock of entrepreneurial ideas an individual has accessible within his or her memory at any given time” [42, p. 88]. We apply this concept in a corporate context (i.e., corporate entrepreneurial ideas, not situations where an individual is considering ideas for starting his or her own business). In our study, an entrepreneurial idea refers to the application of new knowledge, or applying knowledge in a novel manner, to one or more of the dimensions of a business offering (e.g., product/ service, production methods, business model) such that a new or improved source of economic gain may be realized.

There are several reasons why idea set is an appropriate operationalization of managerial sensing ability. First, the idea set construct captures an individual’s capacity to identify opportunities for organizational innovation. This feature aligns with our definition of managerial sensing ability. Second, the idea set construct constitutes a person-centered approach to measurement; thus, it is consistent with our focus on the individual (manager) level of analysis. Finally, idea set and sensing ability are both grounded in a knowledge-based perspective on innovation. Regarding idea sets, the elements informing entrepreneurial ideas may be viewed as residing in diverse fields of knowledge concerning (in broad) products, markets, technologies, production methods, and business models [42]. Similarly, managerial sensing ability requires differential access to various knowledge sources [52].

We use two established idea set dimensions—idea set volume and idea set diversity [42]. Idea set volume refers to the quantity of ideas for organizational innovation contained within a manager’s idea set in a given period of time. For instance, a manager could have thought about two, five, ten, or twenty ideas over a six-month period. Idea set diversity is defined as the variety of the content of ideas within a manager’s idea set in a given period of time. For example, a manager’s idea set could involve many areas (products, markets, technologies), or it could be focused on a single content area. These two idea set dimensions allow us to capture multiple aspects of a manager’s sensing ability.

## The Role of IT in Sensing Opportunities

At the organizational level, IT plays an important role in helping organizations sense opportunities [35, 73, 76, 94]. In a similar way, IT may influence a manager’s ability to sense opportunities. Specifically, conceptual research on dynamic capabilities suggests that a manager’s use of IT may affect opportunity recognition [8, 84]. This perspective is similar to arguments that IS use is an important variable in explaining the impact of IT on performance-based outcomes [29]. Following this line of work, we posit that the extent to which managers use information systems that support business intelligence, analytics, and simulation is related to managerial sensing ability. IT innovation in organizations usually involves four stages—comprehension, adoption, implementation, and assimilation [34, 83]. We view IS use as occurring in the assimilation stage of IT innovation. After gaining firsthand experience with a system during the implementation stage and early parts of the assimilation stage, individuals develop a certain level of knowledge about the system. This knowledge helps them use the system to achieve work objectives in the later parts of the assimilation stage [49]. Moreover, individuals may use IS in routine and/or novel ways to support their work during the assimilation stage [22, 45, 57]. Following this, we use two established assimilation-stage use constructs—routine use and innovative use. Routine IS use is defined as managers’ using IS in a routine and standardized way to support their work; innovative IS use is defined as managers’ using IS in novel ways to support their work [57].

In our context, routine use and innovative use represent two different IS usage behaviors performed by a manager to support his or her work. The agent (i.e., the manager), the system, and the purpose (i.e., to support his or her work) of these two usage behaviors are the same. The primary difference lies in how the manager uses the IS. For example, a manager’s work responsibilities might include identifying new products and services, formulating new sales approaches, and seeking out customer needs and wants. To complete her assigned work, the manager might use a business intelligence system to analyze and manipulate product, service, sales, and customer data stored in an integrated data warehouse. When using the IS in a routine manner, the manager can learn about current customers and product/service performance and subsequently recommend changes in sales approaches. In addition to routine IS use, the manager could use the IS in innovative ways, such as extracting other variables from the data warehouse, combining variables across several data sets to develop unique views of sales-related information across product/service portfolios and customer segments, or splitting the analysis across various time spans. In short, using the system in an innovative manner involves creatively analyzing data to identify new products and services, develop new sales approaches, and seek out latent customer needs. Hence, although routine use and innovative use constitute different ways of using the IS, both usage behaviors can help a manager accomplish his or her assigned work [57].

## Entrepreneurial Orientation

Scholars posit that organizational and managerial factors—and their interaction—are important to understanding managerial sensing ability [1, 8, 40, 84]. For example, an organization’s orientation toward specific technologies influences the relationship between managerial attention and the decision to enter a new product market [31]. Other organizational factors include culture, identity, governance structure, and entrepreneurial orientation. We include this latter concept—entrepreneurial orientation—in our model. Entrepreneurial orientation is defined as a firm-level disposition to engage in a set of distinct but related behaviors that lead to change in the organization or marketplace [60]. Based on a strategic-choice perspective [19], entrepreneurial orientation involves the intentions and actions of key actors working in a dynamic generative process aimed at new venture creation [67]. Simply put, an entrepreneurial orientation essentially refers to the processes, practices, and decisionmaking activities that lead to new entry.

There is some debate over what constitutes entrepreneurial orientation [24]; however, many scholars agree that entrepreneurial orientation is a multidimensional construct consisting of five dimensions—autonomy, competitive aggressiveness, innovativeness, proactiveness, and risk taking [25]. Although all five dimensions may be present when an organization participates in new entry, successful new entry may also be accomplished when only some of these factors are present [36]. In other words, the dimensions of entrepreneurial orientation may vary independently of each other [25, 60]. Moreover, several studies have shown that the individual dimensions have different relationships with various constructs such as product performance [9] and sales growth [23]. Thus, while we recognize the multidimensionality of the entrepreneurial orientation construct, we treat it as a disaggregated set of constructs. Doing so allows us to study the independent effects of entrepreneurial orientation’s subdimensions on the relationship between IS use and sensing ability. Furthermore, treating a multidimensional construct as a disaggregated set of single constructs (dimensions) is not uncommon in IS research (when theoretically appropriate) [72].

The inclusion of any entrepreneurial orientation subdimension(s) in a particular study should be strongly supported on theoretical grounds [24, 25]. Scholars suggest a number of organizational characteristics that inform our understanding of entrepreneurial orientation’s role in the nomological network surrounding managers’ IS use and sensing ability. Drawing on this line of research [27, 90, 92], we suggest that three entrepreneurial orientation dimensions—autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking—are relevant to our context. We further elaborate on these three dimensions as we develop our research model. We do not include competitive aggressiveness and proactiveness because these dimensions of entrepreneurial orientation are more related to the actions a firm takes rather than opportunities a manager may sense. For example, competitive aggressiveness—a firm’s propensity to directly challenge its competitors—is “characterized by responsiveness” [60, p. 148]. Likewise, proactiveness refers to a firm’s disposition to initiate actions [89]. Put another way, competitive aggressiveness and proactiveness appear to be more relevant to seizing opportunities than to sensing opportunities. As such, we do not include them in our research model.<sup>2</sup>

## Research Model

Figure 2 depicts our research model. We propose that IS use is positively related to sensing ability. Specifically, routine IS use is related to idea set volume, and innovative IS use is related to idea set volume and idea set diversity. We also hypothesize that three dimensions of an organization’s entrepreneurial orientation autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking—positively moderate these relationships. Finally, we include relevant control variables for sensing ability (further described in the research method section).

## IS Use and Managerial Sensing Ability

We first provide arguments for a relationship between routine IS use and idea set volume. As noted earlier, information systems that support business intelligence and analytics enable managers to retrieve, filter, manipulate, and analyze data from various sources. When incorporated into their regular work activities, managers can use these systems to generate insight and ideas for improvement or competitive advantage on a consistent basis [82]. Routine use can help highlight what is important, thereby helping managers recognize valuable opportunities [84]. For example, managers at Western Digital routinely used dashboard applications to develop ideas for cost savings and competitive advantage [44]. Furthermore, managers who access and analyze data from various data repositories are better positioned to sense market opportunities [8]. Managers at Continental Airlines incorporated decision support applications into several regular work practices,<sup>3</sup> such as customer segmentation, target marketing, retention management, customer acquisition, and channel optimization, thereby assisting them to better detect opportunities [4]. Routine IS use in these practices helps managers identify areas for organizational innovation on a regular basis.

However, managers must also be able to analyze information to sense the right opportunities [41]. For example, managers at Norfolk Southern used IS to determine where to move special multilevel cars, optimal changes in railcar distribution, where to locate service offices, and how to optimize crew scheduling [93]. Individuals who use IS as part of their normal work routines have most likely acquired the requisite experience and knowledge to retrieve and analyze data that are useful for sensing opportunities. Hence, we suggest that managers who use IS on a routine basis will generate more ideas than managers who do not use IS on a routine basis.

## Hypothesis 1: Routine IS use is positively related to idea set volume.

While routine use helps managers generate more ideas than they normally would (ceteris paribus), we do not believe that routine use will be related to a manager’s diversity of ideas. Our rationale is that, generally speaking, managers use IS in routine ways when they face routine problems or situations. For example, a manager might generate a standard report from an IS to monitor productivity levels in a certain production area. Routine use in this setting will not likely trigger ideas outside that context. Thus, we do not expect routine use to be related to greater diversity of ideas.

We do suggest that innovative IS use is related to both idea set volume and idea set diversity. As noted earlier, opportunity recognition involves scanning both “local” and “distant” markets and technologies. In other words, managers must effectively exploit their current products and services and explore new avenues for future growth [56, 63]. A manager might use a system in innovative ways to creatively combine various data sets to identify opportunities for exploration and exploitation. For example, managers at United Parcel Service (UPS) used business intelligence tools in novel ways to develop analyses showing that it would be cheaper to outsource the delivery of packages to addresses on some less-traveled rural routes to the United States Postal Service [53]. UPS managers may not have noticed this cost-saving opportunity if they had not used the IS in a nonroutine fashion. Similarly, managers at the UK grocery chain Tesco used data analytics in experimental ways to analyze customer-reward card redemption rates in great detail, in turn adjusting their processes to get a better response from customers [65].

In addition to evidence from practice, theory suggests that innovative use will facilitate idea volume and idea diversity. The dynamic capability perspective assumes bounded rationality on the part of organizational actors [95]. Bounded rationality constrains an individual’s capacity to receive, store, retrieve, and transmit information without error [80]. As a result, bounded rationality places a limit on an individual’s ability to identify opportunities for organizational innovation. Nevertheless, information systems can positively influence the information processing abilities of individuals [12], thus expanding the limits of rationality and, in turn, the limits of idea volume and idea diversity. This is especially true when individuals use IS in innovative ways to expand their information processing abilities.

Hypothesis 2a: Innovative IS use is positively related to idea set volume.

Hypothesis 2b: Innovative IS use is positively related to idea set diversity.

## The Moderating Role of Entrepreneurial Orientation

As noted earlier, three entrepreneurial orientation dimensions—autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking—are salient to our context. Autonomy is defined as the extent to which an organization encourages individuals to act independently in bringing forth an idea or a vision and carrying it through to completion [60]. The freedom of action necessary to develop and implement emerging opportunities is a driving force of entrepreneurial value creation [15]. Autonomy affords managers the freedom and flexibility to identify and develop opportunities for organizational innovation. Simply put, autonomy is an essential ingredient in the process of identifying opportunities that are beyond the organization’s current offerings [50].

In an organization that promotes autonomy, there are several reasons why managers may be more likely to identify opportunities when using IS in routine and/or innovative ways. First, managers in this context are usually allowed to act and think without interference [47]. Less interference from others allows managers to consider a greater number of alternatives and options when working with an IS. As a result, managers will be more likely to identify opportunities for organizational innovation. Although some organizations with a strong analytics culture may require their employees to base decisions on hard facts [27], autonomy still provides a context in which managers can consider the possibility of identifying opportunities to improve their organizations. Managers using IS in a low autonomy context may be distracted or discouraged by greater interference and restrictions from others, thereby leading to lower levels of opportunity recognition.

In a high autonomy organization, top management often believes that the best results occur when individuals and/or teams decide for themselves what business opportunities to pursue [59]. This bottom-up approach should encourage managers to use IS to identify opportunities for organizational improvement. By contrast, managers working in a top-down decision-making environment would have less incentive to use IS for opportunity recognition purposes. These arguments lead to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3a: The greater an organization’s autonomy, the stronger the positive association between routine IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 3b: The greater an organization’s autonomy, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 3c: The greater an organization’s autonomy, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set diversity.

Innovativeness is defined as the extent to which an organization supports new ideas, novelty, experimentation, and creativity that may result in new products, services, or processes [60]. Although innovations can vary in their degree of “radicalness” [30], innovativeness represents a basic willingness to abandon existing technologies or routines and move beyond the current state of the art. As such, innovativeness is characterized by an organization’s propensity to continuously seek improvements in its products, services, and processes.

Organizations that promote innovativeness are often creative and forward-looking [60]. By promoting creativity, these organizations encourage their managers and other members to produce novel and useful ideas for organizational improvement or competitive advantage [2]. In a similar way, we suggest that managers are more likely to use IS to help them produce unusual, useful ideas if their organization supports creativity and innovation. Furthermore, innovative organizations encourage experimentation, and experimentation is an often-mentioned “desirable” organizational characteristic in contemporary analytics research [26, 27]. For example, managers at Harrah’s Entertainment use analytics to run thousands of experiments each year, and the findings from these experiments are used to help identify opportunities for improvement and competitive advantage [90]. When working in an organization that supports active experimentation, managers will be more likely to use IS for experimental purposes. The findings from such experiments will help managers sense promising business opportunities. Managers who use IS in organizations that promote little to no experimentation will be less likely to identify opportunities for organizational innovation.

Hypothesis 4a: The greater an organization’s innovativeness, the stronger the positive association between routine IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 4b: The greater an organization’s innovativeness, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 4c: The greater an organization’s innovativeness, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set diversity.

Our final dimension of entrepreneurial orientation is risk taking, defined as the extent to which an organization is willing to commit resources to projects, activities, and solutions that contain a high level of uncertainty regarding their likely outcomes [60]. Risk-oriented firms combine opportunity seeking behaviors with constructive risk taking to develop a bias toward exploration and experimentation [11]. As such, these firms often encourage their managers to seek opportunities for organizational innovation that may involve large and risky resource commitments, that is, opportunities that have a reasonable chance of costly failure.

In an organization that promotes risk taking, there are a number of reasons why managers may be more likely to identify opportunities for innovation when using IS in routine and/or innovative ways. First, managers in these environments are encouraged to break away from the tried-and-true and venture into the unknown. Such a tolerance for uncertainty invites managers to develop ideas that may be “outside the box” in which the organization normally operates and competes. Managers using IS in such settings are more likely to identify “risky” opportunities than managers using IS in a risk-averse organization. Second, risk-oriented organizations provide an environment in which managers can explore markets and technologies far removed from the organization’s existing state [66]. Such exploration activities may lead to radical innovations that make prevailing product/service designs and technologies obsolete [30]. When working in an organization that encourages high-risk exploratory activity, managers will be more likely to use IS to identify ways in which their organization could radically innovate.

Hypothesis 5a: The greater an organization’s risk taking, the stronger the positive association between routine IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 5b: The greater an organization’s risk taking, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set volume.

Hypothesis 5c: The greater an organization’s risk taking, the stronger the positive association between innovative IS use and idea set diversity.

## Research Method

We developed and administered a survey that collected data from managers to test our research model. We selected decision support systems (DSS) as the target system. We define DSS as a set of systems that retrieve stored data and enable the manipulation of these data by individuals in creating ad hoc queries, analyses, and reports supporting work activities [20]. We view DSS as an umbrella term for systems that support business intelligence, analytics, dashboards, visualization, simulation, and modeling [43]. We focus on DSS because literature suggests that DSS enable managers to access and analyze data from various sources [13, 20, 28, 33], thereby providing insight into potential opportunities. Although managers may use a wide variety of information systems, DSS are arguably the most relevant types of systems for sensing opportunities.

## Measures

We adapted existing scales for idea volume/diversity [42], routine/innovative use [57], and autonomy/innovativeness/risk taking [47]. We also included respondent characteristics (age, level of managerial authority, education level, and organizational tenure) and organizational characteristics (age and size) as control variables for idea set volume and idea set diversity. Although we measured individual-level constructs (e.g., routine IS use) and organizational-level constructs (e.g., autonomy), our unit of analysis is an individual. Furthermore, we surveyed one respondent per organization (i.e., our data are not nested).

Consistent with prior research [42], we treat our dependent variables—idea set volume and idea set diversity—as formative measures. Overall idea set volume is determined from the totality of ideas reported across all developmental stages (e.g., ideas one is thinking about, ideas one is trying to get the company interested in supporting, ideas that are currently being implemented by the company). A change in one of these seven “idea set volume” indicators is not necessarily associated with a change in all the other indicators. Moreover, we would not expect a high correspondence between, for example, the number of ideas individuals generate and the number of those implemented by the firm. Consistent with previous work [42], we calculated idea set volume as the sum of the scores for the seven “volume” indicators (see the appendix for the seven indicators).

While idea set volume is based on stages of idea development, idea set diversity is based on the content area of ideas. For example, an individual could have many ideas related to customers/marketing and few ideas related to manufacturing/distribution. A change in one of these nine “idea set diversity” indicators is not necessarily associated with a change in all the other indicators. Likewise, we would not expect a strong association between the idea set diversity indicators. We calculated idea set diversity as the sum of the scores for the nine “diversity” indicators (see the appendix for the nine indicators). We treated all other multi-item measures as reflective.

It is important to note that our three entrepreneurial orientation dimensions are measured as our survey respondents’ perceptions of autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking in their organizations. In other words, we are treating our survey respondents as those who are the most informed to assess the extent to which their organization promotes autonomy, innovativeness, and risk taking [46]. Researchers have used a single respondent approach to measure entrepreneurial orientation [47]. While it would have been ideal to measure these organizational-level constructs with another source or method (e.g., another member of the organization, secondary data), we acknowledge this as a limitation of our study. However, we do not believe it threatens the overall validity of our results.

Since we used measures from existing literature, we followed standard scale development recommendations [61] to adapt previously validated measures to our study. First, several academic experts reviewed the survey. Based on their suggestions, we addressed the ambiguity, sequencing, and flow of the survey. Second, we conducted a pretest with seventy-eight managers who use decision support systems on a regular basis. We conducted reliability analysis and exploratory factor analysis for each set of items. Our results placed sufficient confidence in the scales to proceed with the full-scale survey administration of the target sample frame. The appendix contains all survey items.

## Survey Administration and Sample Characteristics

We contracted with Qualtrics to help us collect survey data from managers who use DSS in for-profit U.S.-based organizations.<sup>4</sup> We included two screening questions at the beginning of our survey: (1) A manager is a person who has decision making authority and responsibility in his/her organization. Are you currently a manager in your organization? and (2) Decision support systems are defined as a set of systems that retrieve stored data and enable the manipulation of these data by individuals in creating ad hoc queries, analyses. and reports supporting work activities. This includes systems that support business intelligence, analytics, dashboards, visualization, simulation, and modeling. Do you use a decision support system on a regular basis? Respondents had to answer “yes” to both screening questions to participate in the survey. Respondents were paid \$25 each for participating in the study. A total of 266 respondents completed the survey hosted on the third-party website. After excluding incomplete responses, we used a data set of 248 responses in all analyses.

Sample demographics reveal that 54 percent were male, had an average age of forty-two years, and tended to be well-educated (82 percent with at least a bachelors degree). Our respondents were very active in the formulation of organizational decisions and policies for their firms when the survey was administered (mean = 4.36 on a five-point scale, 5 representing “very active”). Respondents’ average organizational tenure was 8.8 years, with an average of 5.8 years in their current position. The average firm age was twenty-three years, and the average firm size was 1,311 employees.

We used wave analysis [7] to assess potential nonresponse bias. Respondents were grouped into early and late respondents, and comparisons were made along respondent characteristics (respondent age, level of authority, education level, organizational tenure) and organizational characteristics (firm age, firm size). Our analysis showed no significant differences between early respondents and late respondents. Based on these findings, nonresponse bias does not appear to impact the results of this study.

We employed various techniques to diagnose and control for common method bias (CMB). First, we conducted a Harman [38] one-factor test. Our results extracted seven factors from the data which corresponded to the latent constructs in our study. The factors accounted for 68 percent of the variance with the first factor accounting for 26 percent. No single factor accounted for a majority of the covariance, suggesting that CMB might not pose a severe threat to the validity of our study. Second, we controlled for the effects of a single unmeasured latent method factor in our measurement model [71]. The change in comparative fit index of 0.006 between the original model and the model with the unmeasured latent method factor is less than the recommended value of 0.01 [18]. Finally, we performed a partial correlation test using a marker variable to partial out the influence of CMB. Following recommended guidelines [58], we used both the smallest positive correlation and the second-smallest positive correlation as proxies in computing the CMB-adjusted correlations. Our analysis showed that none of the significant correlations became nonsignificant when adjusted for CMB. Taken together, these results provide support that CMB is not a significant threat to the validity of our study.

## Measurement Properties and Hypothesis Testing

We used confirmatory factor analysis techniques in EQS to evaluate measurement properties of our latent constructs. Model fit indices suggest that the data fit the measurement model well $( { \chi } ^ { 2 } = 4 0 2 . 7 5 , \ \mathrm { d . f . } = 1 8 2 ; \ C F \mathrm { I } = 0 . 9 4 ; \ \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 6 8 ;$ $\operatorname { S R M R } = 0 . 0 5 2 )$ [17]. Standardized factor loadings of measurement items on their respective factors were all highly significant $( p \ < 0 . 0 1 )$ and greater than 0.70, providing support for convergent validity. Chi-square difference tests are significant at the $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ level, providing support for discriminant validity. Cronbach’s alpha values support construct reliability (see Table 1). These analytic results lead us to conclude that our measures were valid and internally consistent [61]. Table 1 depicts construct correlations, descriptive statistics, and reliabilities.

We used hierarchical ordinary least squares regression to test our hypotheses. For each dependent variable we used a three-step procedure. We included our control variables in step one. In step two, we added main effects. We added interaction terms in step three. We mean-centered main effect variables to reduce the potential for multicollinearity [21]. Table 2 summarizes our results.

Our results show that routine IS use is not related to idea set volume. Thus, H1 is not supported. We do find that innovative IS use is positively related to idea set volume $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 ; p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and idea set diversity $( \beta = 0 . 2 6 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . These results support H2a and H2b, respectively. We assessed the regression coefficient and the partial F associated with the change in $R ^ { 2 }$ that resulted from adding the interaction terms to test whether an interaction effect existed [21]. We also conducted simple slope analyses to examine the forms of the interactions and to test our moderator hypotheses. We did so by calculating simple slopes at one standard deviation below and above the moderator’s mean and assessed the coefficients at both levels [21]. We report unstandardized coefficients for our simple slope analyses.

There are no significant interaction effects for relationships that involve routine IS use. Hence, H3a, H4a, and H5a are not supported. We also find that risk taking does not moderate relationships that involve innovative IS use. Thus, H5b and H5c are not supported. We do find that autonomy positively moderates the relationships between innovative IS use and idea set volume $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and idea set diversity $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting H3b and H3c, respectively. Our simple slope analysis shows that innovative IS use is significantly related to idea set volume when autonomy is high (β = $0 . 3 5 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ but not when autonomy is low (see Figure 3a). Likewise, innovative IS use is significantly related to idea set diversity when autonomy is high $( \beta = 0 . 3 3 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ but not when autonomy is low (see Figure 3b). These results further support H3b and H3c.

## Discussion

We set out to achieve two objectives in this study. First, we wanted to determine if a manager’s IS use is related to his/her ability to sense opportunities for organizational innovation. Second, we wanted to discover whether an organization’s entrepreneurial orientation influenced the aforementioned relationship. Using a dynamic managerial capability perspective, we conceptualized and empirically tested a model that integrated these constructs. Our study has implications for research and practice in the areas of IS use, IT business value, and dynamic capabilities.

## Implications for Research

The “IS use” construct is central to the study of information systems in organizations [16, 79, 88]. We further our understanding of IS use in two ways. First, we confirm the previous conceptualization and operationalization of two IS use behaviors— routine IS use and innovative IS use [57]. While there are many types of IS use behaviors [49], routine use and innovative use represent two theoretically grounded, distinct IS use behaviors. Second, we link these two IS use constructs to two important outcome variables—idea set volume and idea set diversity. Our results provide key insights into the relationship between IS use and managerial sensing ability. Namely, managers who use IS in novel and new ways are more likely to identify a greater number and diversity of ideas than managers who do not use IS in innovative ways.

Surprisingly, routine IS use is not related to idea set volume. However, we consider our results from the broader perspective of organizational learning [37, 55, 63]. On the one hand, managers use IS in routine ways to exploit their organization’s current products and services. As such, managers may not be looking for opportunities for organizational innovation when working in such a routine manner. On the other hand, managers use IS in innovative ways to explore ways in which they could move their organization beyond its current offerings, perhaps into new product markets, new customer segments, or new business models. In this way, innovative IS use is more clearly linked to managerial sensing ability. Future research should investigate the link between other IS use behaviors and dynamic managerial capabilities.

Much of the research on decision support systems has focused on the relationship between IS use and decision quality [4, 77, 86]. Findings in this area have been mixed [20]. One major difficulty in defining and measuring decision quality lies in the challenge of understanding human and organizational decision-making processes [80]. We provide some insight in this area by looking at what might be considered part of a typical decision-making process. Research on organizational agility and dynamic capability stresses that managers must identify opportunities before they (or the proper authority) can decide what opportunities the organization should respond to [70, 84]. As such, it is important to take a closer look at how IS use may influence a specific part of the sense–respond process. Thus, we build on and extend prior IS research by linking IS use to an alternative outcome variable, namely, sensing ability.

Our study also has implications for dynamic capability and IT business value research. Although researchers have started paying attention to the importance of dynamic managerial capabilities [8, 40], empirical research in this area is limited (especially in the IS field). We further our understanding in this area and in IT business value research in three ways. First, we leveraged the concept of “idea sets” to operationalize managerial sensing ability. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few operationalizations of managerial sensing ability. Second, we provide evidence that IS use is positively related to managerial sensing ability. This result supports conjectures that managers use IS to identify opportunities for organizational innovation [8, 84] and arguments that IS use is an important variable in explaining the impact of IT on organizational performance [29]. Finally, our inclusion of entrepreneurial orientation helps provide support for arguments that organizational factors play a role in a manager’s ability to sense opportunities [41]. Specifically, organizational autonomy and innovativeness are instrumental in facilitating the relationship between IS use and sensing ability. Our findings mirror firm-level theories that posit entrepreneurial orientation moderates the relationship between IT capabilities and organizational sensing capabilities [76].

## Implications for Practice

In addition to lending empirical support for numerous case studies and practitionerbased research in areas such as business intelligence, our work has direct implications for managers. Managers who use decisions support systems (e.g., business intelligence, analytics) on a regular basis would do well to understand that they most likely need to use the system in new and novel ways if they wish to consistently identify opportunities for organizational innovation. Furthermore, while routine IS use may influence task performance in other ways, our study shows that managers should not necessarily expect routine IS use to be associated with a greater frequency or diversity of ideas for organizational innovation.

Our results also suggest that entrepreneurial orientation is important. One reason organizations may not be getting the benefits they desire from their IT investments is because their employees feel constrained in their work environment. Our study suggests that organizations that promote organizational members’ perceptions of autonomy and innovativeness are more likely to reap benefits from innovative IS use than organizations that do not promote these entrepreneurial behaviors. Put another way, entrepreneurially driven firms are better positioned to tap the power of managerial IS use.<sup>5</sup>

## Limitations and Conclusion

Our study is not without limitations. We used the same respondent for both independent and dependent variables. Statistically, common method bias does not appear to threaten the validity of our findings. However, we note that using the same respondent might have upwardly biased our results. Furthermore, we used a thirdparty market research firm to help us collect data. While these types of services have been used in IS research [5, 10, 74], researchers should be aware of the potential limitations (e.g., demographics, psychometrics, generalizability) these services may introduce in a research study [81]. Finally, we focused on one type of IT (decision support systems) to control for extraneous effects. There may certainly be other types of information systems that managers use to identify opportunities for organizational innovation.

Future research could build on our work in several ways. First, one could take a closer look at the relationship between routine IS use and sensing ability. Theories of mindfulness in organizations suggest that rote behavior may desensitize one’s ability to sense opportunities for organizational innovation [91]. Thus, there may be a curvilinear effect wherein—at a certain point—routine use is negatively related to sensing ability. Another interesting avenue would be to look at how IS use shapes the types of opportunities a manager senses. For example, is IS use related to a greater number of exploratory ideas or exploitative ideas (or both) [55]? Is IS use related to a manager’s ability to identify ways to create new sources of economic value (value creation) and/or ways to effectively appropriate existing sources of competitive value (value appropriation) in their organization [68, 87]? This avenue would essentially look at how IS use is related to alternative perspectives on managerial sensing ability. Another potentially fruitful option is to apply a multilevel lens. How does individual-level IS use shape organizational routines and capabilities? How does managerial IS use at different levels in the organization influence the evolution of organizational capabilities? Recent advances in multilevel research make it possible to address these and other exciting questions [75].

Much of the IT business value research to date has been conducted at the organizational or industry level [54]. Our study complements this line of work by investigating the nomological network surrounding managers’ IS use, managers’ sensing ability, and an organization’s entrepreneurial orientation. We believe there is substantial opportunity to understand how managers’ IS use can facilitate or hinder value creation processes in organizations. We hope our work provides a stepping-stone for future research in this important area.
