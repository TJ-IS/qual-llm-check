# Impact of GDSS: Opening the black box

Moez Limayem <sup>\*</sup>, Probir Banerjee <sup>1</sup>, Louis Ma <sup>2</sup>

Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Kowloon, Hong Kong

Received 20 October 2004; received in revised form 16 August 2005; accepted 17 August 2005 Available online 20 December 2005

## Abstract

Previous research on Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) and its impacts have predominantly adopted a <sup>b</sup>black box<sup>Q</sup> approach in which GDSS design or decision settings have been manipulated and the variation in their effects on decision quality, efficiency, or decision makers’ attitudes have been measured. A decade of research in this area has failed to provide convergence of findings; results are mixed and to date, there is limited understanding of the phenomena. This study leans on the Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) and uses the <sup>b</sup>Faithfulness of Appropriation<sup>Q</sup> (FOA) concept in the theory to explain <sup>b</sup>why<sup>Q</sup> GDSS usage has resulted in such equivocal outcomes and perceptions. A process-oriented approach is taken to examine decisional outcomes in an experimental setting that uses a GDSS enriched with embedded decisional guidance. The study contributes to theory by providing better insight into the process of GDSS usage. It also indicates the need to tailor the training and decisional guidance in a manner that promotes faithful appropriation of the structure of the GDSS, thereby leading to better decision outcomes and perceptions of the decision process. © 2005 F1gevicn P V A1 riαh

Keywords: GDSS; Groupware; Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST); Faithfulness of Appropriation (FOA)

## 1. Introduction

Important decisions in organizational settings are normally taken by a group of people whose collective decision making ability is considered to be more pragmatic than individual opinions. One widely recognized method to improve the effectiveness of group decisions is the use of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS). These systems have been used in many organizations to aid groups in their decision-making tasks. They combine computer, communication, and decision technologies to support group activities. Advancements in information and communication technologies have enabled the development and implementation of some classes of applications that are broadly classified as GroupWare, that include GDSS [16].

With the advent of the Internet, GDSS, as a tool for enhancing group decisions, assumes a new dimension in which dispersed group meetings can be conducted over an Internet-based GDSS. Barriers of space and time are being overcome, facilitating dispersed collaborative efforts across several geographical locations. Meetings no longer involve face-to-face interactions but screen-to-face interactions [4]. Collaborators can now work from different places and at different times, using technology to mediate their communication. In dispersed GDSS systems, network-linked computers allow members to type in their ideas and read others’ ideas without traditional talk time restrictions of face-to-face meetings. This computer-mediated communication encourages individual group members to focus on getting an idea out to the group without immediately responding to other members’ ideas, thereby allowing parallel communication [17].

The potential of networked GDSS is evident from its use in the fields of strategic planning, collaborative engineering design and product development, medical diagnostics and surgery, etc. For example, a system called CAIRO (Collaborative Agent Interaction and synchROnization) uses a distributed conferencing architecture for managing designers and engineers in distributed design meetings by supporting multi-media interactions over computer networks [34,59]. CAIRO emphasizes the role of the computer as a mediator and conference control mechanism and thus aids the concurrent engineering effort by relaxing participants from the task of mediating meetings. By using this system, designers and engineers can work together in virtual teams without the physical, temporal and organizational constraints experienced in traditional design meeting environments.

In another instance, the US Air Force used GDSS with facilitators for their strategic planning process. Hundreds of participants interacted in structured decision-making groups using a GDSS to develop coordinated plans designed to guide the United States Air Force into the future. The Citrix WinFrame technology was incorporated with video and audio conference capability for distributed interaction. The results of the investigation of strategic planning in the Air Force indicate that there are significant rewards for pursuing distributed collaboration efforts [1].

The importance of GDSS in collaborative work is further reinforced by the fact that researchers feel the need for further in-depth research in this area. For example, Costa et al. [16] called for the need to study the functional aspects of computer supported cooperative work provided by distributed GDSS. They suggested that while some impressive approaches have already been proposed, further research is required to fully understand the synchronous and asynchronous usage of GDSS.

Thus, it can be seen that advancements in telecommunication and technology, particularly the Internet, position GDSS as an important technological instrument that could benefit both business and society with better decisional outcomes through collaborative efforts.

## 1.1. Motivation for the research

During the last two decades, GDSS research studies have been conducted in different contexts and perspectives. For example, Kim et al. [41] have studied the impact of system restrictiveness of coordination structures in an asynchronous distributed GDSS environment. In another study, Pollard [61] has explored the factors that determine continued use and discontinuance of GDSS in organizations. The ways in which Western and Asian cultures deal with group conflict generation and management have been studied and compared [63]. On the other hand, GDSS is also finding increased usage in educational settings. Increasingly, studies are now being dedicated to understanding how GDSS can be applied in the educational sector, how it facilitates learning, and its effect on knowledge management [40,43,46,65,80]. In another distributed environment, Lee et al. [45] investigated the potential of using an agent-based approach for developing a location-based asynchronous group decision-support system for mobile teams.

While such studies show diversity of areas investigated in the context of administration of GDSS technology, they have, for the most part, assumed a <sup>b</sup>black box<sup>Q</sup> approach in that the process of GDSS has received little or no attention as a factor that impacts the outcomes from GDSS administration and usage. Other than that, in most prior research on GDSS usage, variations in the GDSS design or decision setting have been manipulated, and the effects on decision quality, efficiency, or decision-makers’ attitudes have been observed, leaving the <sup>b</sup>process<sup>Q</sup> unexplored. Thus, a decade of research has yielded mixed results on the effects of GDSS, and the real impacts that these systems have on group decision making is still not known with certainty. To answer the call to <sup>b</sup>open the black box<sup>Q</sup>, Dennis and Garfield [19] adopted the Faithfulness of Appropriation (FOA) concept in the Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) [22] to investigate factors that impact participative processes and outcomes that reflect the participative processes.

Specifically, we identify the following gaps from our review of past research on impact of GDSS in group decision settings: (1) There is little knowledge on how groups use a GDSS (the process) and thus, little is known about <sup>b</sup>how<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>why<sup>Q</sup> facilitation impacts decision outcomes in group decisional settings with decisional guidance. (2) Due to the lack of understanding of the process of GDSS use, the appropriateness of the process used to arrive at decisions is not known. Thus, the objective of this research is to address these two gaps. The premise is that meaningful and effective facilitating interventions for optimal decisional outcomes in group decision settings can only be made with improved, better, or more accurate knowledge of the process of GDSS usage.

Some advances have been made in this direction. For example, Debreceny et al. [18] deplored the fact that few studies have attempted to synthesize and consolidate the findings into logical themes that could be easily understood, and they call for the need to focus on the <sup>b</sup>process<sup>Q</sup> box between the input and output in order to understand group information use and exchange and group conflict processes. For example, a recent study by Barkhi et al. [6] investigated the impact of two different incentive structures, and two different communication modes on several objective and measurable outcome and process variables for GDSS groups. In another study, Dennis and Wixom [20] examined five moderators (task, GDSS tools, the type of group, the size of the group and facilitation) and their potential effects on GDSS use. Martz and Shepherd [52] have used an <sup>b</sup>influence level of information approach<sup>Q</sup> to explain the non-consensus process loss.

Dennis and Garfield [19] found that GDSS helps in more participative processes and their outcomes. However, they also found disconfirmation of their belief that the facilitator helps in fostering the spirit of GDSS leading to appropriation of the GDSS structures of parallelism, anonymity, and meeting memory in participative ways. They suggested that this issue should be investigated in future research.

In this research, we delve deeper into this line of investigation and attempt to open the black box further in order to better understand the impacts of GDSS use with decisional guidance and how the facilitating process impacts specific decisional outcomes and group perceptions of the outcomes. We use the AST to explain the process of technology appropriation. We focus on <sup>b</sup>how<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>why<sup>Q</sup> facilitation impacts GDSS impact on decisional outcomes. In addition, this research refines the AST by examining as a process the determinant of decisional outcomes in a GDSS setting. By doing so, we move GDSS and group research beyond simple cause-effect studies to the area of group processes and try to explain how members appropriate the technology in a decision making situation with facilitation, as well as why they do so.

In the next section we provide a summary of prior research on GDSS and the theoretical background embodied in past research. This is followed by a section that covers the description of the research model and provides justification for the hypotheses. The research methodology follows thereafter and the paper concludes with a discussion of the findings and a concluding note.

## 2. Prior research and theoretical background

Past research studies on GDSS use have yielded mixed results in respect of its effectiveness. For instance, Lewis [48] and Gallupe et al. [29] found that GDSS groups generated a higher number of ideas than unsupported groups. Similarly, Watson et al. [81] found that GDSS allowed group members to achieve a better understanding of each other’s point of view. In contradiction, Beauclair [7], Easton et al. [26], and Jarvenpaa et al. [36] found that GDSS did not have any significant effects. In fact, Watson et al. [81] and Gallupe et al. [29] found that GDSS actually lowered groups satisfaction. Some recent GDSS laboratory experiments and field studies reflect these contradictory findings [10,28,33,60,83,86]. The inconsistent findings have led to a growing appreciation of the need to understand the processes that intervene between technology and the outcomes of its use; this motivates us to re-investigate and re-think the nature of groups, group work, and the type of support that a GDSS can or should provide [14,15,22,28,66,82].

Technologists argue that the sets of rules and resources that are embedded in technology by designers during development will change dynamically as users interact with the technology. It is therefore a source of social structure [44]. Technology and user behavior coevolve as a structurational process during the process of human–computer interaction [57]. Thus, the manner in which a group uses technology determines the social structure of the group, which in turn impacts the decisional process and the outcomes.

## 2.1. Use of AST in GDSS research

Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) [30] captures the appropriation process in which a social entity adopts a technology. It emphasizes the importance of social interaction in appropriating technology structures and the adaptation process of such structures by the group to achieve satisfactory outcomes. The structure provided by a technology might be used differently across groups, and the effectiveness or ineffectiveness of a GDSS can be explained by the mode of appropriation of the structure by the group members.

DeSanctis and Poole [22] proposed the use of AST to explain the effects of GDSS on the group decision making process. They used AST to explain that a group will actively appropriate technology (GDSS) to fit its needs by re-structuring technology until it interlocks with the interaction system [41]. In other words, this adaptation process of new technology is an evolution of social practice over time [12]. It implies the possibility of failure of the group to adapt the structure to its own needs, if the group faithfully lacks appropriate decision structures [41]. Findings of prior research also advocate this inference that groups with faithfully appropriate decision structures reach better outcomes [3]. In this regard, Anson et al. [3] found that the guidance of a facilitator in a group can help the group in successful appropriation of the technology (GDSS).

## 2.2. Decisional guidance and GDSS appropriation

Facilitation is <sup>b</sup>a set of activities that the facilitator carries out before, during, and after a meeting with the objective of helping the group achieve its own outcomes<sup>Q</sup> [8]. It is a key to successful usage of the GDSS. Previous studies found that group performance is improved when individuals participate in the facilitated discussion and receive cognitive feedback [64]. It is because the facilitation, either from a human or a software agent, supports the group’s social and cognitive processes, allowing group members to focus on more substantive issues in the decision making process [68] and ultimately arrive at the most appropriate solution to a problem [40]. In the GDSS environment, the facilitator directs the group members on what GDSS features to use and when to use them [24]. In fact, human facilitation is a series of reflections on the facilitator’s personal and professional experience [85], which makes training human facilitators a complex and difficult task [84]. It therefore may be logical to assume that, because of individual characteristics or skills, one person might be a better facilitator than another.

In this regard, decision guidance (i.e., software agent facilitator) is a better choice than a human facilitator. Automated decisional guidance is the enrichment of a GDSS with cues that guide decision makers towards successful structuring and execution of the decision making process [50]. The incorporation of decisional guidance into decision support systems has been studied extensively [35,51,55,82]. Some researchers have specifically investigated the incorporation of cognitive feedback into computerized systems [70,73]. According to Mahoney et al. [51], decisional guidance is <sup>b</sup>the degree to which and the manner in which a DSS guides users in constructing and executing decision-making processes to a subset of all possible processes.<sup>Q</sup> It enables the users to appropriately choose and use the system’s functional capabilities in their decision-making processes [72]. An intelligent guidance system monitors users’ behavior and provides cues and customized explanations accordingly [32].

The importance of facilitation/guidance is supported by the finding by Kwok et al. [43]. They found that facilitation in GDSS-supported sessions contributed to a higher level of group process gain on quality of feedback than do non-GDSS-supported sessions. Likewise, Dennis and Garfield [19] found that facilitation plays a key role in introducing the GDSS’s participative spirit and that this participative conception of the GDSS was sufficient to shape the appropriation. The initial understanding of a technology plays a key role in how it is appropriated [37,56].

## 2.3. Faithfulness of Appropriation (FOA)

In explaining GDSS appropriation, DeSanctis and Poole [22] argued that the extent of faithfulness with which a group appropriates the technology (GDSS) determines the group outcomes. In an extension of this argument, Wheeler and Valacich [82] introduced the concept of process restrictiveness and argued that it moderates the appropriation of GDSS, leading to the development of the Process Restricted AST (PRAST). They explained how GDSS and facilitation might act as appropriation mediators through the forces of guidance and restrictiveness to influence specific procedural dimensions of the social interaction process, and ultimately, decision outcomes. Group members need to have their activities structured so that they are able to focus on the task at hand more effectively [40]. Dennis and Garfield [19] used the FOA concept in AST to investigate the nature of the participative process and the factors that impact participative process and outcomes from GDSS usage.

The concept of FOA is best illustrated by the two dimensions that capture the essence of GDSS: the structural features of the GDSS and the spirit of the GDSS technology. The structural features are the specific types of rules and resources, or capabilities offered by the GDSS. For example, parallelism is a structural feature enabled by electronic communication in multi-user GDSS. It is the ability of group members using the GDSS to simultaneously exchange information by typing suggestions concurrently with other members without the need to wait for others [19]. Similarly, anonymity (remaining unidentified in the decision process) and meeting memory (recording of suggestions) are other structural features of a GDSS [19]. The spirit represents the general goals and attitudes an intervention technology aims to promote. It reflects the adaptation of the technology to the context, in keeping with its original intended purpose. For example, most multi-user GDSS aim at promoting a meeting process that is fair and one that fosters equal participation from the members using the GDSS.

Individual group members can appropriate the structure of the technology <sup>b</sup>faithfully<sup>Q</sup> or <sup>b</sup>ironically<sup>Q</sup> [62]. The former represents that they act in a way consistent with the structure of the system while the later implies that they violate the structure. The reproduction of structures occurs with interactions within the group. Interaction coding procedures help in identifying the interactions and assessing the degree of faithful or ironic appropriation of the structure.

There is no one single generalized decision-making architecture that is suitable for all group decision support situations and thus considerable variation in decision outcomes and quality are inevitable [21]. We believe that by explicitly differentiating the <sup>b</sup>spirit<sup>Q</sup> from the <sup>b</sup>structural features<sup>Q</sup>, AST captures not only the design strategy of GDSS (structural features) but also the socio-psychological behavior (spirit) of the appropriation process. Hence, AST is thus uniquely positioned with its theoretical tenets in explaining <sup>b</sup>why<sup>Q</sup> GDSS usage has resulted in equivocal outcomes and perceptions. However, the spirit and the structural features in themselves are not adequate to determine decisional outcomes. It is the interaction between the two and the cognitive perceptions of the user on the facilitation process that leads to the FOA through emergent structures created by the group. In developing emergent structures, users may use an externally defined <sup>b</sup>objective<sup>Q</sup> spirit that comes from the perception of the <sup>b</sup>correct<sup>Q</sup> approach as presented by experts or facilitators indicating how the specific rules and resources of the technology (GDSS) ought to be used. However, a subjective spirit also comes into play, in that the actual observed user behavior may be an adaptation of the correct <sup>b</sup>objective<sup>Q</sup> spirit. There is thus an interplay of the spirit and the technology features in the appropriation process of the group.

Our review and analysis of past research indicates that decisional guidance affects group functioning in a group decisional situation. The review also indicates that the decisional outcomes from a GDSS with decisional guidance are a function of how effectively the decisional guidance has been used by the group towards faithful appropriation of the structure of the GDSS. The outcomes of the decisional process are actually a function of the FOA of the structure of the GDSS. The higher the FOA, the better the decisional outcomes. We now proceed to investigate the issue in depth with enunciation of the hypothesis and development of a research model to explain the cause-effect relationship.

## 3. Research model

Our research model is based on the premise that decisional guidance by way of cognitive feedback and feedforward at decisional breakpoints leads to higher FOA of the GDSS and consequently leads to better decisional outcomes and perceptions of the decisional process used to arrive at the outcomes. We now proceed to enunciate our hypotheses and provide our justification for each hypothesis.

The quality of support received during the decision making processes is the key to reaching optimal decisions. Decisional guidance mechanism provides the decision makers with step-by-step guidance throughout the decision-making process and allows them to evaluate more alternatives. As a result, DSS users with decisional guidance can easily come up with better decisions than those with no decisional guidance. [58].

Mahoney et al. [51] pointed out that when faced with complexities in a decision situation, decisional guidance helps users to choose among and interact with a system’s capabilities. They argued that in less structured tasks that deal with uncertainty and risk, users need more guidance to choose among competing solution techniques or among alternative methods of processing information to structure an appropriate decision-making process using the GDSS.

Another important component of good decisions in a GDSS setting is the knowledge of the GDSS and its appropriate usage. Past research studies [9,11,38,42] have found that decision makers rely on very simple decision heuristics or rules because complex rules are difficult to implement in a time-pressured, changing environment [25,39,75,76]. These researchers attribute the difficulty to limitations of working memory and information processing ability, and they caution that to perform well, these simple decision rules must capture the essential attributes of the environmental system and be supported by timely and accurate information about the state of the system [42].

Lerch et al. [47] further found that while simple decision rules were generally used for computer mediated decision making, novices’ rules were simpler and less effective than those of experts. The experts’ decision-making process incorporated information variables that were critically important in the operation of the system, and the rules combined these variables in more complex ways to select the decision from a choice of alternatives. Complex decision rules of the experts required a better structural knowledge of the system that helped them to focus on salient information and combine it more effectively. The rules also required scanning for more information variables and involved more complex calculations with the variables. Therefore, while the experts’ rules were still very simple when compared to most analytic techniques, the experts performed well and, importantly, much better than the novices. Thus, novices, due to limited knowledge of the system may avoid a comparatively complex decision heuristic (one that may be used by experts) because it requires too much effort; thus, they may settle for a compromise with a simpler rule that supports adequate performance. Experts, however, may be able to rely on more complex rules because their deeper knowledge of the system allows them to execute these rules with no more effort expenditure than the simple rules required of novices.

Thus, it follows that the constraints on implementation of complex decision rules lead to a compromise, resulting in the use of a simple decision rule that may be adequate but that may not yield the best optimal solution. In this research, it is posited that the knowledge of the system can be enhanced with computer supported decisional guidance, enabling the novices in a group to perform at par with the experts in the group engaged in the decisional setting. A facilitator who understands the way decision-making groups communicate and knows how GDSS structure affects communication can strategically create interventions to positively influence group outcome [69]. Dennis and Garfield [19] pointed out that the facilitator fosters participative spirit leading to more equal participation by members using the GDSS in a decisional situation. Equal participation by members, we argue, leads to higher FOA of the GDSS through enhanced knowledge of the system and ability to formulate and implement complex decision rules, culminating in optimal decisional outcomes and better perceptions of the decision process.

Thus, we develop our first hypothesis as follows:

Hypothesis 1. Groups receiving decision guidance will have greater faithfulness of appropriation of the GDSS than groups receiving no decisional guidance.

Groups’ appropriation of the technology may improve if meeting participants are trained and are provided with an orientation on the features and purpose of GDSS. Familiarity with GDSS use can also inhibit unproductive structures such as distributive conflict management [54]. We argue that conflict between individual and group goals leads to lack of consensus on decisional outcome. We propose that decisional guidance embedded in the technology leads to higher FOA of the structure of the GDSS by directing the participants to focus on group goals, thus leading to higher consensus on the decisional outcome. In this context, we refer to Limayem and DeSanctis [49] who concluded that <sup>b</sup>the (decision) guidance provided by the system. . . enhanced group consensus.<sup>Q</sup>

Barkhi [5] found that GDSS used along with a problem modeling tool led to more truthful sharing of information by group members. We draw a corollary that decisional guidance in GDSS leads to higher FOA of the structure of the GDSS through truthful sharing of information amongst group members, thus leading to higher consensus on decision solution. A recent meta-analysis [20] found that GDSS teams with facilitators produced better decisions and were more satisfied with the processes than those without facilitators, supporting our argument. This forms the basis of our next hypothesis (2a):

Hypothesis 2a. Groups with higher faithfulness of appropriation will achieve higher consensus on their decisions than groups with lower faithfulness of appropriation.

According to the Time, Interaction and Performance Theory advanced by Mcgrath [53], three group functions are central in the group decision setting. They are production, member support and group well-being. The member support and group well-being functions are mentioned as process losses and are considered to be inefficiencies of the decision process. The limited media richness of GDSS and the reduced communication of information is said to increase the process losses, thereby increasing inefficiencies and longer time to converge to a decisional outcome. Again, decisional guidance in GDSS allows scanning of information from a larger search space, leading to a larger set of alternatives, thereby increasing the time to process and integrate the information for decisional outcome. However, we argue that the cues from decisional guidance in a GDSS lead to higher FOA of the structure of the GDSS through systematic (as opposed to arbitrary or unorganized) scanning of the search space, as well as filtering and categorization of the scanned information, thus enabling faster information integration and processing, consequently leading to a reduced time for arriving at a decision solution. Categorization alleviates the information overload by arranging smaller pieces of information together for each decision alternative [71], thus reducing the time to arrive at a decisional outcome. In this context Van Groenendaal [79] suggested that frameworks formulated to identify different forms of (group) decision support have mainly focused on the hardware and software, rather than on the kind of decisions to be taken and their time frame. He also pointed out that the process of strategic decision making have received little attention in research on decision support.

In our research model, we therefore incorporate the time dimension as an important decisional outcome and hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2b. Groups with higher faithfulness of appropriation will take less time to reach at a decision than groups with lower faithfulness of appropriation.

Barkhi [4] analyzed decision making with problem modeling tool embedded in GDSS and observed that in a GDSS without a problem modeling tool, there is unsystematic scanning of information in the search space, leading to sub-optimal decisional outcome. Thus the quality of the decision suffers. As argued before, decisional guidance in GDSS allows scanning of information from a larger search space, leading to a larger set of decision alternatives to choose from and contributing to the quality of the decision. Drawing a corollary, we argue that decisional guidance embedded in GDSS leads to cues that enable higher FOA of the GDSS through systematic scanning of the search space (as compared to a GDSS that provides no decisional guidance), thus leading to better decision quality.

Wheeler and Valacich [82] found <sup>b</sup>process facilitation to be the strongest mediator of faithful appropriation of the prescribed heuristic, in turn leading to betterquality decisions.<sup>Q</sup>

This leads us to our third hypothesis (3a):

Hypothesis 3a. Groups with higher faithfulness of appropriation will have more positive perceptions of the decision quality than groups with lower faithfulness of appropriation.

Content facilitation in the electronic medium may be expected to stimulate thought and increase participation from group members, thereby improving satisfaction and product quality [54]. We argue here that the effect of the decisional guidance is not direct but a function of the FOA of the GDSS. Decisional guidance that leads to ironic appropriation could well lead to increase in conflict and random or non-systematic search for information, thus resulting in sub-optimal decisional outcomes. Also drawing from Barkhi’s [5] findings on truthful sharing of information in GDSS with a problem modeling tool, we argue as before that decisional guidance in GDSS similarly leads to higher FOA of the structure of the GDSS through truthful sharing of information amongst group members, thereby creating more positive perceptions of the confidence in the decision solution. Tan et al. [77] also reached a similar conclusion in their study. They found in their research that GDSS with facilitation would have higher decision confidence than GDSS groups without facilitation. A facilitator who carries out task interventions appears to raise decision confidence in a group [77]. Decisional guidance should lead to the same result.

This leads us to our next hypothesis (3b):

Hypothesis 3b. Groups with higher faithfulness of appropriation will have more positive perceptions of decision confidence than groups with lower faithfulness of appropriation.

Our research model (Fig. 1) depicts the hypothesized relationships.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research design

A laboratory experiment was conducted to test the impact of decision guidance on FOA of the GDSS and decision outcomes. This methodology is appropriate to achieve this purpose for the following reasons: (1) this research method allows testing of the effects of decision guidance, while controlling for the effects of other factors that may otherwise confound the findings. Some examples of these factors are group history, nature of the task, and organizational environment; and (2) the methodology enables greater control over random events and more precise measurement.

Fig. 1. Research model.

The experimental design consisted of two treatment conditions: (1) control groups using the GDSS with no decision guidance; (2) experimental groups using the same GDSS enhanced with the decision guidance mechanisms described in the previous section. Groups were randomly assigned to the treatment conditions.

A total of 40 groups of 5 members each (200 upper level undergraduate students from the business school of a university in Canada) were randomly assigned to one of the two treatment conditions (20 control groups and 20 experimental groups). In order to avoid the drawbacks usually associated with zero-history groups, we selected groups for this study that had been working together on a series of class projects. All subjects participated in the study voluntarily. In order to raise the participation rate, each participant successfully completing the task was granted a CAN\$20 supermarket cash coupon.

## 4.2. The experimental task

<sup>b</sup>The Foundation Task,<sup>Q</sup> developed by Watson et al. [81] was used in this study. This task required group members to allocate a sum of \$500,000 among six competing projects requesting funds from a philanthropic foundation. The six projects were based upon the six personality components, namely: theoretical, economic, aesthetic, social, political, and religious. The task was validated against the study of values instrument [2]. The task is considered to be difficult because of its low analyzability and the ambiguity in the cause-effect relationships [78]. Specifically, the background information provided to the subjects about the task did not allow them to choose an evident best solution. An announcement was made to all participants emphasizing that there were no absolutely right or wrong answers. This was done to ensure that the decisions taken were affected by the facilitation they received and not by peer pressure or their own psychological qualities. The task was also found to be highly motivating to groups and at the same time not requiring special knowledge or skills.

## 4.3. The GDSS

The Software Aided Meeting Management (SAMM) developed at the University of Minnesota was used in this study. SAMM is composed of two main programs, one running on the public screen and the other on each individual group member’s terminal. These two programs communicate with one another using the UNIX message passing facility. The private program, running at each member’s terminal, gathers the keyboard inputs (in the form of ideas, messages, or evaluations) from that member, and sends them to the public program. Depending upon the member’s request, the public program stores the inputs, displays group information on the public screen, or transmits information to a group member. Further, some of the information displayed on the public screen can be transferred to the individual private terminals for additional modification by group members. SAMM is a user-driven GDSS, meaning that group members have full access to all system features. Thus, members can use it in any way and in any order they choose GDSS provides the following functionalities:

n agenda setting;

n idea gathering/brainstorming (idea generation about problems/issues, alternatives, criteria, strategies, etc.);

n idea evaluation (weighting, rating, ranking, and various voting options);

n decision aids (stakeholder analysis, allocate/multicriteria model, problem formulation, and contingency model);

n meeting thoughts (i.e., several commenting features); and

n utility functions (various note taking, meeting minutes saving, retrieving, and printing).

## 4.4. Experimental setting and procedure

The research setting consisted of a specially designed and equipped group decision support system laboratory at a Canadian University. The lab contained tables arranged in a U-shaped pattern, swivel chairs with a private terminal in front of each chair, and a large screen projection facility at the front of the tables, constituting the public screen. In order to ensure that all experimental sessions followed the same set of steps, the administrator was provided with a checklist and was asked to adhere to its content. The same administrator conducted all the experiment sessions to ensure consistency in both treatments (i.e., with or without guidance). The experimental procedures consisted of the following steps:

1. Preparation for the session—the administrator started up the system so that it would be ready upon the group’s arrival.

2. Preliminary introduction—the administrator read an introductory script explaining the overall purpose of

Table 1

the experiment and its relationship to the course assignment. Then, subjects were asked to sign a consent form and fill out a background questionnaire.

3. Background to the study—group members read a background statement for the Personal Trust Foundation task.

4. Training—subjects were given training in the use of the GDSS with guidance or without guidance depending on their treatment condition. Efforts were made to provide equivalent training for groups in both treatment conditions. On average, training took 45 minutes for groups using the GDSS with decision guidance, whereas it took 30 minutes for control groups. For each treatment condition, training was aimed at facilitating the groups’ understanding of the GDSS and its features.

5. Group decision—group members worked together using the GDSS to allocate funds to the six projects requesting support. A chauffeur assisted the group with the technology by performing functions such as view, modify, and delete. Dickson et al. [23] argued that if a group were to use a GDSS on only one occasion, it would be beneficial to have an external agent, the chauffeur, <sup>b</sup>drive<sup>Q</sup> the technology for the group. The chauffeur was not part of the group and did not participate in the group discussion. The chauffeur did not answer any questions related to the task. When a decision was reached, the group submitted a form reflecting its final allocation of funds.

6. Administration of post-meeting questionnaire—at the end of the meeting, group members filled out a post-meeting questionnaire assessing their perceived outcomes. They again individually allocated funds to the six projects. These allocations were used to compute post-meeting consensus.

7. Decisional guidance—in this research, decisional guidance was made up of two components. One was a feedforward component that suggested a judgmental recommendation (what to do) to decision makers before the decision or process occurs. Then the subjects received suggestions about what decision to make next based on the current state of the environment. These suggestions were provided at delay and disruption breakpoints. The second component was a cognitive feedback at normal and disruption breakpoints.

## 4.5. Measurement

Decisional guidance—was the independent variable manipulated in this study. Groups performing a multicriteria task using the GDSS without formal decision guidance were compared to groups undertaking the same task but using the GDSS with embedded decision guidance as described in the previous section. Except for the addition of the decision guidance cues, all other features in the GDSS were the same, so that any differences between treatments could be considered as the result of the implementation of decision guidance.

FOA was measured by the 5-item scale developed by Chin et al. [14]. The 5 items used in measuring this construct are shown in Table 1 above.

Decisional outcomes—group consensus was measured using the formula by Spillman et al. [74] as described by Watson et al. [81]. The elapsed time in minutes between the start of task and the announcement of reaching group decision was a measure of the decision time.

Perceptions of the decision outcomes—measured as self-reported scales: (1) Perceived decision quality was based on an 8-item scale developed by Gouran et al. ## 5. Discussion and conclusion

We used a Partial Least Squares procedure to estimate the path relationships among the major constructs in our research model (Fig. 1). PLS-Graph version 3.0 [13] was used to perform the analysis. Tests of significance for all paths were conducted using the bootstrap resampling procedure [27]. The estimated path effects and associated t-values are shown in Fig. 2. All significant paths ( p <sup>b</sup> 0.01) are indicated with an asterisk. The findings confirmed all our hypotheses that decision guidance should lead to more faithful appropriation of structures which, in turn, will result in better decisional outcomes and better perceptions of decision process and outcomes. The path between decision guidance and FOA was significant with an explained variance of $R ^ { 2 } { = } . 6 6 2$ . The paths linking model understanding and the remaining outcome variables are also all significant.

The study supports the main tenet of AST that the way groups use the GDSS determines how much they benefit from it. As can be seen from Fig. 2, the path between decision guidance and FOA, with an $R ^ { 2 }$ of .662 significantly illustrates that decisional guidance leads to higher FOA of the spirit and structure of the GDSS. The paths linking FOA to the decisional outcome and perceptions of the decisional process and outcome are all significant, providing support for our hypotheses that higher FOA leads to better decisional outcomes and better perceptions of the decisional process and decision outcomes. It follows therefore that the way groups use the technology makes a difference in the outcomes.

To a large extent, this research removes the tension that arises from conflicting findings in this area of GDSS research. Several studies have found that GDSS did not have any positive impact on decisional outcomes. Based on the findings from our study, we can now say that such results could be attributed to ironic appropriation of the GDSS, that is, appropriation in a manner inconsistent with or in violation of the purported structure of the GDSS. Furthermore, we can say that conflicting findings regarding GDSS impacts could be attributed to the different ways groups appropriate the GDSS.

In addition to the above theoretical contribution towards understanding the <sup>b</sup>process<sup>Q</sup> of GDSS usage, the study has several practical implications. It points to the need to tailor the training and decisional guidance in a manner that promotes the faithful appropriation of the structure of the GDSS, consequently leading to better decisional outcomes and better perceptions of the decision process and outcomes. The study also points to the need for designers to properly communicate the spirit of the technology to the users in order to facilitate faithful appropriation.

As with any experimental study, we may not be able to generalize our findings over a broad range of tasks, systems, and populations, etc. The limitation of using students as subjects for the experiment is acknowledged, but the fact that the students were upper level undergraduate students with significant experience of working on group projects provides some room for generalizability. Limitation is also acknowledged in respect of the possible impact of cultural traits in the study. Given these limitations, this study, however, makes several valuable contributions. It goes beyond the simple causal effect of technology on behavior. By leveraging a reliable theory, we explain <sup>b</sup>how<sup>Q</sup> a GDSS affects group interactions and outcomes and <sup>b</sup>why<sup>Q</sup> groups have different outcomes and perceptions of the outcomes and decision process. This study is the first of its kind in this respect in that it opens the black box of the <sup>b</sup>process<sup>Q</sup> and enhances our understanding of the impact of embedded decisional guidance in a GDSS on the decision process of the group and its consequent effect on the decisional outcomes and perceptions of the decision process and decision outcomes.

Our research augments the research of Dennis and Garfield [19] who found disconfirmation of their belief that the facilitator helps in fostering the spirit of GDSS leading to appropriation of the GDSS structures of parallelism, anonymity, and meeting memory in participative ways. They suggested that this issue should be investigated in future research. In our research, we investigate the role of the facilitator in depth and provide theoretical background of when and how facilitation provides higher FOA. Also, while Dennis and Garfield [19] provided an illuminating theoretical extension to the DeSanctis and Poole [22] of GDSS appropriation that explains the interaction of the GDSS structures and the spirit with team interactions and creation of emergent structures to produce participative outcomes, our research provides a much needed insight into the manner in which FOA contributes to individual components of the participative outcomes, such as group consensus, decision confidence, decision time and decision quality. In a manner, our research serves to extend the findings of Dennis and Garfield by opening up another layer of the process black box of GDSS administration.
