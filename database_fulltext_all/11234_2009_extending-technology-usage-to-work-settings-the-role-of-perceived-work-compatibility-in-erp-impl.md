# Extending technology usage to work settings: The role of perceived work compatibility in ERP implementation

Yuan Sun <sup>a,</sup>\*, Anol Bhattacherjee <sup>b</sup>, Qingguo Ma <sup>a</sup>

## A B S T R A C T

Spanning over three decades and hundreds of studies, researchers have generated models, that examined user perceptions of technological attributes presumed to influence intention to use IT and consequent usage behavior. While these theories have provided reasonable explanations of IT usage across a broad range of technological and task settings, they did not address the role of IT in organizations, though they have been tested in organizational contexts, the models have not been modified to fit the context of organizational work. Current models are good at explaining IT usage in personal-settings, but they have not adequately examined the role of IT in organizations and thus have limited explanatory power in such settings. Furthermore, organizations deploy IT both to maximize its usage and to derive performance benefits from it. However, current IT usage models generally only consider IT usage as the dependent variable, without examining its performance impact. We extended IT usage models to include the role of IT’s perceived work compatibility in shaping users’ IT usage intention, usage, and performance in work settings. The model was empirically validated using a field survey of 138 users of ERP systems in 62 firms in China.

## 1. Introduction

Firms spend millions of dollars on IT implementation initiatives, such as ERP, workflow, and knowledge management (KM) systems, expecting significant operational or performance gains from them. The worldwide market for ERP software licensing, implementation, and maintenance was \$28.8 billion in 2006, and the number was projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 11% until 2011 [4]. However, many large IT system projects fail due to poor response to the implementation by the organization’s users and inability to realize expected benefits. It is therefore important to understand why many organizational IT systems are not enthusiastically accepted by users, and how IT usage could translate into tangible benefits.

We believed that it was important to contextualize IT usage within work settings in order to maximize its power in organizations. This should provide organizational managers with a broader set of options to increase IT usage within their organizations and assess the impact of such usage on outcomes (such as productivity or performance gains). Our objective was therefore to extend IT usage models to (1) incorporate the role and impact of organizational work, and (2) assess the impact of IT usage on organizational outcomes. Toward these ends, we synthesized prior IT usage research with task–technology fit (TTF) to postulate relationships linking the work context to IT usage, and then, to link IT usage back to individual performance in organizations. The hypothesized model was empirically tested using data collected from a survey of 138 ERP users in 62 firms across ten provinces in China and analyzed using PLS.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. Prior research

Much prior IT usage research was based on Ajzen’s theory of planned behavior (TPB), shaped by three perceptions: attitude, subjective norm (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC). Though it did not identify specific beliefs or other perceptions salient to IT usage, TAM added perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) as attitudinal beliefs salient to IT usage. The resulting relationships, synthesized in UTAUT and shown in Fig. 1, suggested that a person’s intention to use IT was influenced by four user perceptions: PU, PEOU, SN, and PBC. Because these associations are well known and have been extensively validated, we did not include them as formal hypotheses. UTAUT also proposed additional moderating effects of gender, age, experience, and voluntariness of use, but these associations were based on empirically observed correlations rather than on theory, we did not include them as formal hypotheses.

## 2.2. Work context and IT usage

TAM was originally conceptualized in the context of personal use. Though it has demonstrated reasonable explanation of IT usage within organizations, such studies have generally ignored the role of organizational work on IT usage or its predictors, and its performance impact. Clearly, organizational users use IT to perform specific tasks and speed organizational work; hence it is important to consider the role of organizational work in IT usage.

Dishaw and Strong [3] noted that a major weakness of TAM was its absence of task focus and recommended consideration of (TTF), which refers to the degree of correspondence between task requirements, individual abilities, and the functionality of the technology. In their technology-to-performance chain (TPC) model, Goodhue and Thompson argued that IT was more likely to be used in organizational settings and would have a positive impact on individual performance if the capabilities of the IT matched the tasks that the user had to perform. They observed that TTF, in conjunction with IT usage, was a significant predictor of improved job performance and conceptualized TTF as a multidimensional construct tapping into different facets of fit between IT and organizational work. With a sample of 662 people using 25 different IT systems and working in 26 departments at two firms, they empirically validated eight of their original 16 TTF dimensions: quality (of data), locatability (of data), authorization (for access to data), compatibility (between data and work), ease of use/training, production timeliness, systems reliability, and user relationship. Most of these are attributes of data, rather than those related to organizational work, with compatibility the only exception. Staples and Seddon shortened the original TTF scale to four dimensions: work compatibility, ease of use, ease of learning, and information quality. Because work compatibility is the only TTF dimension directly related to organizational work, we selected it as the core construct of interest in our study.

It should be noted that we view work compatibility strictly as the fit of IT to organizational work, and not to personal preferences or work habits. Organizations deploy IT to facilitate organizational work rather than to match users’ personal preferences or habits. Nevertheless, like PU and PEOU, work compatibility is very much a perceptual construct as it is the perception of fit between IT and work that motivates employees to use the system, irrespective of the actual extent of fit. To capture the perceptual nature of this construct and to maintain consistency with PU and PEOU, we refer to this construct hereafter as perceived work compatibility (PWC).

How does PWC influence TAM constructs? Goodhue and Thompson’s TPC model suggested that TTF influenced work performance, both directly and indirectly, via effects on IT utilization and/or the precursors of IT utilization. These precursors may include users’ cognitive beliefs (PU and PEOU) of IT usage. The stronger the fit between IT and the portfolio of organizational tasks that it is intended to simplify, the greater the likelihood that users will realize performance gains from its use. The direct effect of TTF on performance is even more pertinent when IT usage is less voluntary, as in many organizational settings. In such circumstances, performance impacts will depend on TTF rather than on IT usage. Since our study focused specifically on TTF’s work compatibility dimension, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1. Users’ perceived work compatibility between their organizational tasks and a given IT is positively related to their individual work performance.

In circumstances where IT usage is within one’s volitional control, it is normally the extent of IT usage that determines performance. This expectation has been supported by DeLone and McLean’s IS success model, empirically validated by Petter and McLean [7]. Organizational users cannot realize significant productivity or performance gains if they do not use IT adequately and appropriately. On the other hand, IT usage may not always translate into improved performance, suggesting that IT usage is necessary though not sufficient condition for performance improvement. However, in view of the dominant assumption in the literature that IT usage is a necessary condition for workplace performance, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 2. Users’ organizational IT usage is positively related to their individual work performance.

In addition to its direct effect on performance, TTF impacts performance indirectly through IT usage. Though Goodhue and Thompson found this effect to be very small indirect effect, explaining 2% of the IT usage variance, it is possible that the lack of a strong direct effect may be related to the aggregation of multiple TTF dimensions, rather than focusing on PWC along. Further, the lack of a direct effect of TTF on IT usage may be indicative of a mediated effect, where this effect is mediated by users’ intention to use IT.

Theoretical support for a mediated association comes from models of IT usage such as TRA and UTAUT, which suggested that users’ cognitive beliefs of IT usage was manifested on their actual use via their intention to use IT. Hence, PU and PEOU were modeled as influencing intention to use IT, rather than directly linked to usage behavior. Likewise, we should expect intention to use to mediate the effects of other perceptual beliefs, such as PWC, on actual IT usage. Therefore, we proposed:

Hypothesis 3. Users’ perceived work compatibility between their organizational tasks and a given IT is positively related to their intention to use that IT.

Finally, the TPC model indicated that TTF was related to the precursors of IT utilization, such as PU. IT usage studies have long contended that cognitive beliefs related to IT usage are interrelated and may influence each other in intricate ways (e.g., [13]). Empirical evidence for the association between TTF and PU is however quite tenuous. This association was tested but not supported in Dishaw and Strong’s study, but this lack of support may have been due to the small sample size and consequently low statistical power. Given our focus on TTF’s work compatibility dimension, to empirically test for a potential relationship between PWC and PU, we proposed:

Hypothesis 4. Users’ perceived work compatibility between their organizational tasks and a given IT is positively related to their perceived usefulness of IT usage.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Subject sample

Our hypotheses were tested empirically using a field survey of ERP users in Chinese firms during the year 2006. The sampling frame was taken from the China Business Directory available from http://china.alibaba.com. Firms were selected using two criteria: (1) the firms must have implemented one of the four most popular ERP packages in China: SAP, Oracle, Kingdee, or Ufida (the last two are Chinese products) and (2) to ensure comparability of ERP modules and experience among respondents, the firms must not have used the ERP system for more than 3 years at the time of the study. Initial phone calls were made to each firm to verify if they matched our selection criteria, to explain the purpose of the study, and to solicit their participation. Firms who agreed to participate were asked to identify a contact person who could distribute the survey questionnaires (in paper form) to 3–8 end-users (depending on the size of the firm); all respondents were required to have used ERP systems in their daily work. End-users were asked to complete a survey questionnaire and mail it directly back to the researchers in self-addressed stamped envelopes.

Overall, 62 firms, spread across ten provinces of China, agreed to participate in our study. A total of 280 questionnaires were distributed in these firms, of which, 147 questionnaires were returned for a response rate of 52.5%. Nine of the responses were incomplete and were eliminated from the sample, resulting in a total of 138 usable responses. Survey respondents represented a diverse group of industries, including manufacturing (29.0%), transportation (18.1%), chemicals (15.2%), telecommunications (11.6%), information technology (9.4%), energy (7.2%), consumer staples (5.8%), and others (3.6%). Respondents were 59.4% male, their average age was 28.9 years, and over 92% had a college degree or more. This demographic is typical of the professional Chinese workforce at large.

The most widely used ERP functional module among our survey respondents were finance and accounting (31.2%), followed by manufacturing and production (20.3%), and inventory and materials planning (15.2%). Over 79% of respondents used ERP systems every day for their work. However, most (89.9%) of these had used ERP systems for less than 2 years, suggesting that, unlike many Western countries, ERP usage was still a novelty in Chinese firms at the time of our study. The relative lack of ERP usage experience among our respondent sample was not a problem, given that we were studying early-stage IT adoption and usage behavior and that models such as TPB and TPC are better-suited for studying such behavior.

To test whether their relative lack of ERP usage experience may have introduced any systematic error in subjects’ responses, we segmented our responses into two groups based on a median split of ERP use experience, and compared item means and standard deviations between ‘‘expert users’’ (those with higher than median ERP usage experience) and ‘‘novice users’’ (those with lower than median experience). This data, shown in Table 1, did not show any significant difference in perceptions between expert and novice users, suggesting that lack of ERP usage experience did not bias our survey.

Finally, since survey responses are often subject to nonresponse bias, we tested for this bias by comparing early responses with late responses. Comparison of means on several demographic variables, such as age, tenure in firm, and firm size, and chi-square tests on industry distribution revealed no significant difference between the two groups. This suggested that our sample was probably not influenced by non-response bias.

## 3.2. Construct operationalization

The eight constructs of interest to our study were individual performance, IT usage, intention, PWC, PU, PEOU, SN, and PBC. Each construct was measured using multiple-item, seven-point, Likert scales taken from prior research and adapted to relate specifically to the context of ERP usage. The original questionnaire was designed in English and translated to Chinese for administration to subjects. To check for translation bias, a back-translation technique was employed, whereby a different translator changed the Chinese questionnaire back into English. A high degree of correspondence between the original English questionnaire and its back-translated version assured us that the translation process was adequate. Individual items were randomly sequenced in the questionnaire to reduce the potential ceiling (or floor) effect that can bias subjects’ responses. ## 4. Data analysis and results

The empirical data was analyzed in two stages involving a partial least squares technique, using the Graph Version 3.00 software. In the first stage, all measurement scales were examined for their psychometric properties, while the second stage focused on hypothesis testing and model analysis. We choose the variancebased PLS approach over covariance-based SEM approaches such as LISREL, because PLS does not require the data to have a multivariate normal distribution and is less demanding of sample size. Raw data was used as input to the PLS program, and path significances were estimated using a bootstrapping resampling technique with 100 sub-samples.

## 4.1. Measurement model

Assessment of the measurement model included evaluation of reliability, convergent, and discriminant validity of each of the measurement scales. We examined two measures of reliability: Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. As shown in Table 2, each of our six scales had Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.80 and composite reliability exceeding 0.90 for our sample of 138 observations, assuring adequate reliability for our measurement scales.

For convergent validity, we adopted Fornell and Larcker’s assessment criteria: all item factor loadings should be significant and exceed 0.70, and the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50. Table 1 lists item factor loadings, all of which were significant at $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ and were higher than recommended level of 0.70. Furthermore, the square of the principal diagonal elements in Table 2 (which are square roots of AVE) exceeded 0.50. Hence, each of our measurement scales showed strong evidence for convergent validity.

Discriminant validity between constructs was assessed following Fornell and Larcker’s recommendation that the square root of AVE for each construct should exceed the bivariate correlations between that construct and all other constructs. The interconstruct correlation matrix shows that the principal diagonal elements (square roots of AVE) exceeded non-diagonal elements in that same row or column (bivariate correlations), demonstrating that the discriminant validity of all scales was also adequate.

## 4.2. Structural model

The next step in our analysis was to examine the path significance and magnitude of each of our hypothesized effects and the overall explanatory power of the proposed model. Results of this analysis are shown in Fig. 2.

All of our four hypothesized relationships were validated at a $p < 0 . 0 5$ significance level. PWC had a strong and significant positive effect on individual performance $( \beta = 0 . 5 6 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ supporting Hypothesis 1. IT usage had a relatively weaker but significant positive effect on individual performance $( \beta = 0 . 2 2$ $p < 0 . 0 1 $ , providing support for Hypothesis 2. PWC and IT usage jointly explained 49% of the variance in individual performance, with PWC explaining most of that variance. This is consistent with expectations from the TPC model that TTF is more crucial than IT usage for realizing performance impacts in organizations.

PWC also had significant positive effects on intention to use IT $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and on PU $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 ) ,$ , supporting Hypotheses 3 and 4 respectively. PWC explained almost the same proportion of variance in intention to use as PU. The effects of PWC and PU were both almost twice as large as that of PEOU $( \beta = 0 . 1 7$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , SN $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , and PBC $( \beta = 0 . 1 5 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ though the latter three effects were also significant. This pattern of effect size attested to the importance of including the PWC construct in studies of IT usage, especially within organizational settings. Further, our findings support expectations from the TPC literature that TTF not only influences IT usage, but also biases other perceptions driving IT usage perceptions such as PU.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Limitations of the study

First, the context was ERP usage among workers in Chinese firms; this makes our findings less generalizable to Western firms. Unlike most Western countries, China is still a developing country, given that about 90% of our respondents had used the system for less than 2 years when data collection was conducted in 2006. Furthermore, the Chinese workforce is younger than the Western workforce. While this sample may not generalize well to Western firms that have used ERP systems for decades or to Western ERP users, our sample should generalize to developing countries such as India, Thailand, and Philippines, that employ a large IT workforce sharing demographics similar to that of China.

Second, since all of our items were measured at the same time using a single self-reported questionnaire, our findings may be susceptible to common method bias [8]. We tested for this using Lindell and Whitney’s [5] marker-variable technique, with respondents’ tenure at their firm as the marker variable. The common method adjusted correlations between the variables of interest, after partialing out the effects of their correlations with the marker variable, were not significantly different from the unadjusted correlations at p < 0.05, suggesting that common method bias was probably not significant in our study. However, our findings may still be subject to a halo effect—an increased likelihood of statistically significant correlations if responses were influenced by the respondents’ overall impression of ERP usage. Such effects are unavoidable in cross-sectional surveys, and longitudinal designs are needed to overcome halo effects entirely.

## 5.2. Implications for research

Our study has several important research implications. First, it demonstrates the importance of incorporating PWC, or the TTF construct, within contemporary models of IT usage. Current IT usage models have been criticized for being overly simplistic [3]. Furthermore, these models explain IT usage well in circumstances where usage is voluntary, such as in personal usage settings, but less so in organizational contexts where organizational members’ use of IT are less voluntary. By contextualizing the role of IT in work settings, PWC can not only provide an improved understanding of the factors influencing organizational IT usage, but may also help extend the boundaries of current IT usage models to less voluntary settings.

Our study illustrated the complex nomological paths by which PWC influences IT usage. Like other cognitive beliefs PWC impacts intention to use IT, and thereby indirectly influences IT usage. But PWC also has a strong indirect effect on intention via its effect on PU. Combining the direct and indirect effects, we believe that PWC has a stronger overall effect on IT usage intention than PU, which has been viewed as the most dominant predictor of intention, at least in organizational settings.

Finally, IT usage models have been criticized for viewing usage as an end in itself, rather than as a means of improving worker performance. Organizational managers invest in IT because it helps workers be more productive, efficient, and effective in the workplace. Current IT usage models do not venture into the outcomes of usage, but without studying outcomes, we can never know if IT investments are successful. Drawing on TPC theory, our model of organizational IT usage connected IT usage to worker performance, by postulating IT usage and PWC as dual predictors of individual performance.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

Our study suggested that PWC was a potential ‘‘lever’’ that organizational managers could use to maximize IT usage in their organizations. Unlike other user beliefs, PWC is more directly under the control of managers, since IT can be customized to the specific needs of organizational work. This is particularly the case with ERP systems, which are customized to suit idiosyncratic organizational business processes such as production planning and sales. Less customizable organizational systems that do not fit users’ unique work requirements but attempt to unilaterally change organizational work often meet with user resistance and consequent failure.

Because ERP systems are generally based on ‘‘best practices’’ in industry, PWC of ERP systems can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool for firms to assess the extent to which their internal operations and processes are consistent with the rest of the industry. If a firm is already using industry norms in its internal operations, the system will be perceived as being more compatible to users’ work, resulting in higher levels of IT usage and better worker performance. In contrast, if the firm’s processes are out of sync with the industry, it will experience less PWC, lower IT usage, and lower performance.

For IT vendors, our study provides an argument why IT designers should build customizable systems. IT should adapt itself to fit, and not change, organizational work. Complex organizational systems should be designed to be customizable so that parts can be modified as needed to suit specific needs. This is particularly relevant given recent reports citing increasing dissatisfaction among user organizations with ERP systems.

## 6. Conclusions

We proposed and validated a model of organizational IT usage, by integrating TPB with TPC, in an attempt to extend contemporary IT usage theories to organizational settings. We employed PWC (a dimension of TTF) as a theoretical construct that contextualizes the role of IT in organizational work, and hypothesized its associations with IT usage, the performance outcomes of such usage, and other user perceptions driving usage. Our proposed model captured the role of organizational work on IT usage (via PWC) as well as the corresponding effect of IT usage on organizational outcomes (via individual performance), and was empirically validated using a field survey of 138 users of ERP systems in China.
