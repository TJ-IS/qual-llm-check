# Impact of Chatbot Conversational Skill on Engagement and Perceived Humanness

Ryan M. Schuetzler<sup>a</sup>, G. Mark Grimes<sup>b</sup>, and Justin Scott Giboney<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Brigham Young University, Provo, UT; <sup>b</sup>Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, Houston, TX

## ABSTRACT

Conversational agents (CAs)—frequently operationalized as chatbots— are computer systems that leverage natural language processing to engage in conversations with human users. CAs are often operationalized as chatbots which are used for many applications including technical support, customer service, and digital personal assistants. Despite their widespread use, little research to date has investigated how improving the conversational skill of a CA impacts user perceptions of the agent. To elucidate this relationship, this research uses Social Presence Theory to describe how conversational skill influences perceived social presence and ultimately anthropomorphism of a chatbot. We conducted a series of studies in which 450 participants interacted with CAs exhibiting varying levels of conversational skill. We show that people perceive a more skilled CA to be more socially present and anthropomorphic than a less skilled CA. This research advances the knowledge of computer-human interface in information systems, as CA research to date has largely focused on the technical challenges rather than the behavioral questions of how users interact with CAs.

## KEYWORDS

Conversational agents; system humanness; anthropomorphism; user engagement; social presence; chatbots

## Introduction

The idea of interacting with a computer as naturally as one would interact with another human has long fascinated researchers, users, and developers of information systems. As far back as the 1945 writings of Isaac Asimov, natural language conversation with computer systems has been common fodder for science fiction. What was once in the realm science fiction is rapidly becoming a staple of modern life as conversational agents (CAs)—frequently operationalized as chatbots—are becoming increasingly ubiquitous in both personal and business interactions. This is evident in the widespread adoption of “digital assistants” such as Siri and Alexa, the proliferation of bots on messaging platforms such as Facebook and Slack, and the integration of voice control into a wide range of consumer electronics. Similarly, many organizations are deploying chatbots to provide customer service [77], manage stress [51], provide technical support [69], conduct medical interviews [7, 36], provide student counseling services [39], and disseminate emergency response information [64]. Systems such as these are engendering an industry forecasted to grow to \$1.25 billion by 2025 [22].

Despite the importance of this topic for a wide range of personal and business applications, there is a scarcity of research that has examined behavioral aspects of human-CA interactions. Most research to date on CAs has been conducted in the domain of computer science with a focus on the technical challenges of improving the ability for computers to engage in conversations. A long-time goal of this research has been to develop chatbots that are indistinguishable from humans [73]. This has resulted in significant advancement in the technical capabilities of CAs over the last several decades. However, this rapid proliferation of change makes it dificult to discern what features improve interactions with a CA, how these features improve interactions with a CA, and—most importantly from a theory perspective— why these elements improve interactions with a CA. While it is readily apparent that substantial advancements have been made in this arena, the pursuit of making CAs that are “better” has seemingly outpaced our understanding of how CAs and humans interact and how a CA’s characteristics influence user perceptions and behaviors.

The goal of the current work is to better understand how diferent design choices impact perceptions and the efectiveness of CAs, and to extend existing human-human and humancomputer interaction theories to examine the human side of the interaction. Due to the increase in businesses using CAs to interact with customers, this is an important topic to bring into mainstream IS research. Improving our understanding of human-CA interactions will provide significant benefit to practitioners using CAs, as it helps developers better understand when more sophisticated CAs are beneficial, and when more sophistication is unnecessary or may even make the CA less efective for its intended purpose (see [61]).

This work also raises interesting theory-related questions for IS researchers. As users interact with systems such as these, they tend to attribute human traits to them—a phenomenon known as anthropomorphism [42, 71]. As systems act in more humanlike ways, people respond to their humanness by applying conversational norms and increasing their expectations of the system’s capabilities [49, 56]. Accordingly, as the conversational skills of CAs continue to be enhanced, the diferentiation between norms and behaviors in human-human and human-computer conversations are increasingly blurred. This invites questions not only of how anthropomorphic individuals perceive CAs to be, but also if distinct theories for human-computer conversation are necessary, or if our understanding of human–human interactions may be directly applicable as interactions with computers become increasingly humanlike. In this work, we argue that perceptions of anthropomorphism in a CA interaction can be explained by the social presence felt during the interaction. We build on Social Presence Theory [67] to explain the impact of conversational skill on users’ anthropomorphism of a CA. To this end, this research seeks to answer the following research question:

Research Question 1: How does the conversational skill of a CA afect perceptions of the CA’s social presence and anthropomorphism?

To answer this question, we conducted a pilot study and two laboratory experiments. In the pilot study, we investigate the basic premise that CAs that exhibit higher conversational skill via tailored responses—the ability of a communicator to create responses that are contingent on the messages they have received—are perceived as more anthropomorphic. The subsequent studies build on the results of the pilot by incorporating social presence to explain these relationships, and provide a more nuanced exploration of the efect of conversational skill by adding additional levels of conversational skill to the CA. We close the paper with a discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of this research.

## Literature review and theory development

While the majority of CA research to date has been conducted in the domain of computer science [60], our program of research is informed not only by computer science, but also human communication and information systems literature that aid in understanding not only the technical aspects of CAs, but also the behavioral aspects of the interaction. As chatbots have become more pervasive, CAs have evolved from novelties to important realworld IT artifacts. Accordingly, CA research is rapidly becoming of greater relevance for IS, with great opportunity for IS researchers to make significant contributions in this space [14, 20, 60, 66]. In this section, we bring together the rich history of conversational agents and anthropomorphism, conversational capabilities, social presence theory, and humancomputer interactions, thereby bridging the technical and the behavioral. In this, we create a work that informs practical and theoretical applications in IS, and brings about a better understanding of this phenomena.

## Anthropomorphism of computer systems

Anthropomorphism is the application of humanlike attributes and characteristics such as personality [50], verbal and nonverbal behaviors [17, 38], politeness [45], and embodiment [61] to non-human objects [42, 71]. These qualities make CAs seem more humanlike, and can influence how efectively humans interact with them [57]. Anthropomorphism can be the result of users attributing humanlike qualities to a system [50], or from computer systems intentionally impersonating humans, resulting in users mistaking them for human [73]. Anthropomorphism of computer systems is a common phenomenon as humans are known to perceive computers as social actors and interact with them as such [49]. The anthropomorphism of computers can occur in either a conscious or unconscious manner [34, 49, 78]. Kim and Sundar distinguish the two forms of anthropomorphism with the terms mindful and mindless anthropomorphism [34].

Mindful anthropomorphism is the conscious application of humanness and human expectations to the way a system behaves. When users consciously assess the humanness of a system, they form expectations of humanlike behavior that are congruent with their assessment of the system’s humanness. They then make thoughtful assessments of how humanlike the agent is, akin to the expectations imposed by the Turing Test of artificial intelligence. In a chatbot interaction, this mindful anthropomorphism may manifest to such an extent that users believe they are in fact interacting with a human [53]—that is, they experience high perceived humanness of the system.

Mindless anthropomorphism manifests itself in unconscious perceptions of a system and the consequent expectations of how the system should behave [34]. Mindless anthropomorphism has long been studied in the context of the Computers are Social Actors paradigm [49, 50], and is of particular interest because of its distinct nature from perceived humanness. With CA interactions, mindless anthropomorphism manifests in users’ unconsciously ascribing human traits such as thoughtfulness or politeness to an artificial entity. These expectations can develop over the course of an interaction, and they influence how people interpret the actions of their partner [1]. Even when users know they are interacting with a nonhuman entity—for example, Siri or Alexa—they ascribe humanlike attributes and traits to the system [48, 49, 78]. When users experience this type of unconscious connection, they experience more humanlike connection to the system [17]. This is important for social communication as it can lead to a number of behavioral outcomes including difering degrees of trust [11] and disclosure [61].

As a result of the anthropomorphizing of computer systems by users—whether conscious or subconscious—and the rapidly increasing humanlikeness of computer systems, one must consider whether human-human interaction theories can be applied in the context of human-CA interactions. Marakas et al. [42] have suggested that “computers are commonly associated with cognitive processes that often seem, at least superficially, analogous to those which go on in people” (p. 772). Zahedi and Song [79]go as far as to say “there is little controversy regarding the applicability of cognitively-based constructs of interpersonal relationships to human–information technology (IT) artifact relationships” (p. 227). Furthermore, interpersonal interaction theories have been shown to apply to human-computer interactions in a variety of contexts. For example, people can form trusting beliefs with a computer [74].

## Conversational capabilities of conversational agents

Since the mid 1900s, scientists have worked to develop computer systems capable of engaging in humanlike conversations [75]. Approaches have ranged from simple keyword matching to sophisticated systems that leverage machine learning and artificial intelligence [65]. Recently there has been rapid growth in the development of natural language processing (NLP) techniques that have facilitated more natural human-computer interactions [65], and frameworks to readily apply these techniques into social media and communication platforms [16, 46] to answer questions and provide information in a conversational tone similar to a human support agent. Despite these advances, there are no shortage of reports of CA interactions that exhibit very non-humanlike behavior [15, 44]—that is, they lack conversational skill.

The level of conversational skill a communicator exhibits is made up of many diferent behaviors. People who are skilled in conversation give responses that are relevant to the conversation at hand, exhibit variety in their speech, respond within an appropriate timeframe (i.e., neither too fast nor to slow), and exhibit appropriate nonverbal cues [8, 24]. Improving conversational skills such as these improves how others perceive the speaker during an interaction [68]. Feine et al. [17] characterize verbal social cues of CAs in two categories: content and style. Content cues relate to the main intent expressed in a message, while style cues relate to the manner in which the intent is expressed. In this manuscript, we operationalize conversational skill with one cue of each type. Tailored responses are a verbal content cue, while response variety represents a style cue. These specific components of conversational skill were chosen because of their importance in the creation of a highquality conversational agent.

Tailored responses are responses that are crafted in direct response to a message, and signal understanding of the message that was sent. Grice [24] refers to this conversational norm as the maxim of relation, which describes the expectation that conversation partners will respond to each other during the conversation. A partner that gives tailored responses provides on-topic follow-up questions or comments based on the previous statements from the user, while a partner that does not provide tailored responses responds with generic questions or comments, thus potentially violating the maxim of relation. Tailoring of responses forms a content cue that suggests the social capabilities of a conversational agent [17]. When users interact with a chatbot, receiving responses that indicate the chatbot understood them makes them feel heard. This perception activates a social cognition [18], where people feel that the chatbot is perceiving and responding to them. This not only engenders a connection to the agent, but also makes them more likely to continue engaging with the chatbot [58].

Response variety means that over the course of an interaction there will be some variety in the words used to convey a message, even when the same meaning is intended. For example, in typical human-human communication, when one participant in a conversation agrees with his or her partner, they may use a variety of terms such as “Yes,” “Sure,” “OK,” “Uh huh,” “Yup,” or “Alright.” Answering repeatedly with the same exact response would be a violation of conversational norms. Computers, however, typically respond to a given input with the same output every time. While this is efective for programmatic interactions, it gives an unnatural feel to conversation. In a conversational interaction, when computers exhibit variety in responses, it gives the conversation a more humanlike feel [21]. While this variety is natural in human conversations, CA developers must consciously create response variety in the CA’s programming.

Altogether, the features of tailoring and variety result in four possible types of response:

(1) Generic/Non-varied: A response that does not signal understanding of the message, and does not exhibit the variety typically present in human–human communication.

(2) Generic/Varied: A response that does not signal understanding of the message, but does exhibit the variety typically present in human–human communication.

(3) Tailored/Non-varied: A response that signals understanding of the message, but does not exhibit the variety typically present in human–human communication.

(4) Tailored/Varied: A response that signals understanding of the message, and exhibits the variety typically present in human–human communication.

These categories are general descriptions of how a CA responds over several interactions. In a single interaction, however, violations of these definitions may go unnoticed. For example, it is possible for a CA to give a generic response that is considered relevant by the person being conversed with. Similarly, it is not clear in a single interaction that the conversation is lacking in response variety, as the lack of variety would not be noticeable without repetition. Over multiple interactions, however, the lack of response tailoring and variety would become evident. Table 1 provides an example framework of responses that fit into these four categories.

The example is simply meant to illustrate, at a high level, response tailoring and variety.

As illustrated in these simple examples, the introduction of these social cues—verbal cues of content and style—creates a more humanlike interaction [17, 35]. We expect this to be apparent in ratings of both mindful and mindless anthropomorphism. While increasing perceived humanness is an interesting goal—and one of the oldest goals of artificial intelligence—its value may be limited [27]. Complementary to the concept of mindful anthropomorphism, we further hypothesize a link between conversational skill and mindless anthropomorphism. We operationalize mindless anthropomorphism in this work as the level of engagement a user feels with the CA.

Engagement refers to the perceived ability of the CA to communicate well by acting and responding to user input thoughtfully [29]. Engagement is conceptually separate from humanness [13], as engagement is not targeted specifically at convincing people the CA is human, but is rather focused on how well the CA is able to respond to users. Even for systems that do not attempt to masquerade as human, facilitating conversations that are engaging can improve user satisfaction with CA systems [54]. For many applications, it is not necessary that the CA be perceived as human. For example, there is no expectation that Siri or Alexa, two popular digital personal assistants, are human. Nevertheless, this does not diminish the utility of those systems.

When a CA is able to maintain a natural flow of conversation, the interaction is deemed more engaging [37]. CAs that appear to understand user input and respond appropriately are perceived as better communicators [35] and make people feel that their input has been heard and matters [70]. Conversely, when a communication partner gives inappropriate responses and is not able to maintain flow of the conversation, people become distracted and feel less engaged [33], leading to less efective interactions with the system [32]. Based on this understanding, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Increased conversational skill, as demonstrated by (a) tailored responses and (b) response variety, results in higher perceived partner engagement.

## Social Presence Theory

Social Presence Theory (SPT) is a communication theory that refers to the number and quality of verbal and nonverbal channels in communication medium [67]. In the context of computer-mediated or fully digital interactions, prior information systems research defines social presence as the eficacy of communication media to facilitate a sense of connection with another individual through the medium [19, 40, 41, 63]. The ability of the medium to facilitate a feeling of social connection is irrespective of the actual presence of another person and varies depending on the communication medium [19] and the way in which the medium is used [6]. For example, when the medium is used to transmit additional nonverbal cues, such as the addition of response variety and tailoring, the social presence of the medium is improved.

While early social presence media studies focused on the ability of the medium to connect two individuals, modern research has expanded to include creating a sense of human connection with companies and products [19]. Even more recent studies have begun to look at the facilitation of social connection with virtual entities like conversational agents [3]. Prior research has sought to understand how the social cues presented by a conversational agent afect the sense of shared connection felt by users of the system [2, 6, 28, 55] and has found that more skilled virtual agents create a greater sense of social presence than unskilled agents, even when both agents are using the same medium [25]. As demonstrated in this prior work, social cues exhibited by a CA, such as its ability to engage in humanlike conversation, should increase the social presence of the agent as perceived by users. Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Increased conversational skill, as demonstrated by (a) tailored responses and (b) response variety, results in higher perceived social presence.

We use social presence as the theoretical lens through which we interpret the efect of a CA’s ability to project social cues on participants’ perceptions of the agent. Prior research has found that as social presence increases, users feel an enhanced appreciation of the relationship through the medium and therefore are more likely to ascribe social attributes such as trust [19], self-eficacy [4], and expertise [9]. Similarly, in the context of human-CA conversation, we suggest the transmission of social cues provided by the CA facilitates a perception of anthropomorphism in users. Thus, we suggest that social presence mediates the relationship between conversational skill and anthropomorphism.

## Method and results

To create a scenario in which users would interact with a conversational agent, we constructed a custom chat application (Figure 2) using the ChatScript language [76]. In general, the application showed users a series of images, and a conversational agent asked the user to describe the images. CAs were developed with the aforementioned levels of conversational skill: Generic/Non-varied, Generic/Varied, Tailored/Non-varied, and Tailored/Varied.

The CAs for the experiment were constructed as such:

● Generic/Non-varied (Pilot, Study 1, and Study 2): The Generic/Non-Varied condition provides a baseline condition in which the system does not provide any indication of understanding—the CA does not vary its responses in any way, but rather gives the exact same responses for every image and user message. For example, after the participant described each image, regardless of the description the user gave, the CA would always follow up with the same response: “Please describe one more detail about the image.”

● Generic/Varied (Study 1 and Study 2): The Generic/Varied CA provided a variety of responses for the follow-up questions, but the responses were not contingent on the text provided by the participant. For example, the response might be “Okay, now describe more detail about the photo,” or “Next describe another detail about the picture,” or some other similar phrase randomly selected from the corpus of responses. In all cases the intent of the follow-up question remains the same, but the wording is varied from one image to the next.

● Tailored/Non-varied (Study 2 only<sup>1</sup>): The Tailored/Non-varied CA gave responses that were tailored to the message provided by the participant, but were invariant in the language used to phrase the response. For example, if the participant mentioned any type of animal in their response (i.e., “It is a picture of a snake” or “There are three puppies playing”), the CA would always respond with the exact same response asking for more details about the animal that was mentioned (i.e., “Tell me more about the snake” or “Tell me more about the puppies”).

● Tailored/Varied (Pilot, Study 1, and Study 2): The Tailored/Varied CA used natural language processing to give responses that were relevant to the message provided by the participant. For example, if the participant included the word “dog” when describing the image, the CA, cuing of the word dog, might ask if the user knows what type of dog it is. If they mentioned a particular type of dog (Labrador, beagle, golden retriever, etc.), the CA might ask how they know it is that particular type of dog.

## Study procedure

To begin each experiment, participants gave informed consent and completed an online survey that captured demographic information. After completing the survey, participants were told that they would be placed in an online chatroom and asked to describe and answer questions about a series of images which their chat partner could not see. The system randomly assigned participants to one of the CA conditions. Participants were then shown the series of images one at a time and asked questions about the images by their chat partner—one of the CAs. After answering all questions about an image, participants were shown the next image. Examples of the Tailored/Varied and Generic/ Non-varied conversations are shown in Figure 3. In each chat window, the text from the CA is presented in the left-justified dark-gray boxes, while the text from the participant is in the right-justified light-gray boxes.

After the chat session was complete, participants answered a series of survey questions about their perceptions of the humanness, engagement, and social presence. Their belief about the humanness of their chat partner was captured by answering the following question using a six-point scale: “My chat partner was: ‘definitely computer, ‘probably computer,’ ‘not sure, but guess computer,’ ‘not sure, but guess human, ‘probably human,’ and ‘definitely human’” [31]. Partner engagement was rated on a 7-point Likert scale using a six-item rating of their chat partner, including skill, politeness, engagement, responsiveness, thoughtfulness, and friendliness [29]. For the measurement of social presence, participants were given these instructions: “Think about the interaction you just had and rate it on the following scales.” Social presence was then measured with four variables—sociability, warmth, personalness, and sensitivity—on a 5-point bipolar scale [52].

## Pilot study results

Despite the technical issues that invalidated responses from three participants, the chat application overall worked well. The technical problems were resolved, and instructions modified to address the issue that two users encountered with the system. To test the basic premise that individuals interacting with the CA with higher conversational skill would rate their partner as more humanlike than those in the baseline condition, we conducted a two-sample t-test. The test revealed a statistically significant diference of perceived humanness rating between the Tailored/Varied CA (M = 2.03, SD = 1.14) and the Generic/ Non-varied CA (M = 1.25, SD = 0.53), t(44.63) = 3.38, p = .002, d = 1.01 (Figure 4). Participants that interacted with the Tailored/Varied CA also reported significantly higher partner engagement (M = 5.02, SD = 1.01) than those that interacted with the Generic/ Non-varied CA (M = 3.70, SD = 1.41), t(40.09) = 3.88, p < .001, d = 1.07 (Figure 5). Based on the guidance of Cohen [12], these values represent a large efect size.

The results of the pilot suggest there is an influence of conversational skill on perceptions of the CA. With this in mind, we developed an enhanced version of the experiment to investigate why this relationship exists, and to investigate additional levels of conversational skill.

## Study 1

One hundred and fifty students (53% male) from a large public university in the United States participated in study 1 in exchange for class credit. The technical issues identified in the pilot study were corrected and instructions were clarified. Study 1 replicated the image description task from the pilot study with three key changes.

The first change is that users in all conditions received follow-up questions from their CA chat partner. All CAs asked two identical base questions for each of twelve images shown to participants—“Please describe the contents of the image,” and “How does the image make you feel?”—and two follow-up questions that varied based on the condition the participant was assigned to. The second change is that we added a Generic/Varied condition in which the CA gave follow-up questions that varied (i.e., were not identical), but were not contingent upon the message given by the participants. This condition falls between the Generic/Non-Varied and Tailored/Varied CAs in its ability to mimic human conversational skill. The third major change is the addition of social presence as a mediator to provide a theoretical explanation for the relationships between conversational skill, engagement, and perceived humanness.

## Study 1 results

Both partner engagement $( \alpha = 0 . 9 1 )$ and social presence $( \alpha = 0 . 8 0 )$ showed high levels of convergent validity. We also tested discriminant validity to ensure that scale items for partner engagement and social presence measure distinct constructs. We then performed an exploratory factor analysis to show the loadings on each of the constructs (see Table 1.1 in Appendix 1). The scale items were averaged together to create scores for social presence and partner engagement.

To test our hypotheses, we used structural equation modeling (SEM) to create and compare two models to investigate the direct efects of conversational ability on partner engagement and perceived humanness and the mediating efect of social presence on these relationships using lavaan package [59] in R [72]. We explicitly tested the mediation efect by first confirming the presence of direct efects without the mediating factor, then creating a model that includes the mediating factor, comparing the two models.

In the unmediated model, the path estimate from tailored responses to perceived humanness (H1a) was $\mathsf { b } = 0 . 6 1 0 \ ( \mathsf { p } = . 0 0 2 )$ and the path estimate from tailored responses to partner engagement (H2a) was $\mathsf { b } = 0 . 5 5 9 \ \mathrm { ( p } = . 0 1 6 )$ . Thus, both H1a and H2a were supported—tailored responses lead to higher perceived humanness and partner engagement. The paths from response variety to both perceived humanness and partner engagement were not significant, b = 0.277, p = .171 and b = 0.196, p = .403, respectively, thus not supporting for H1b or H2b—response variety was not found to have a direct efect on partner engagement or perceived humanness. The fit indices of the unmediated model are $\mathrm { A I C } = 9 0 4$ $\mathrm { R M S E A } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , indicating an appropriate fit, however we find relatively low $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values of .058 for perceived humanness and .038 for partner engagement.

A second model using social presence as a mediator was used to test the mediation hypotheses (H3 and H4), which examine social presence as the mechanism by which conversational skill influences perceptions of the CA’s humanness and engagement. In the mediated model, we see that tailored responses have a significant main efect on perceived humanness (H1a) and social presence (H3a), and social presence has a significant efect on perceived humanness (H4a), thus social presence partially mediates the relationship between tailored responses and perceived humanness. With the addition of social presence, there is no longer a significant main efect of tailored responses on partner engagement (H2a), however, there is a significant relationship between social presence and partner engagement (H4b), indicating a fully mediated relationship between tailored responses and partner engagement. We find no efect of response variety on any of the constructs measured. The mediated model exhibits greater explanatory power with $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values of .133 for perceived humanness and .235 for partner engagement, and the fit indices of the mediated model $( \mathrm { A I C } = 1 , 2 5 0 , \mathrm { R M S E A } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ are improved compared to the non-mediated model, thus indicating the addition of social presence is useful for explaining the relationship. Full model testing results are presented in Figure 6.

## Study 2

Study two was intended to replicate the results of study one while adding a fourth CA condition—Tailored/Non-varied—to the study. This addition gives us a fully-crossed manipulation of tailoring and variety, improving our ability to distinguish these factors. We also further increased the variety of the Varied conditions by varying the way the initial questions were asked. In Study 1, the base questions (“Please describe the contents of the image,” and “How does the image make you feel?”) were static for each image. For the Varied conditions in Study 2, we varied the language of those questions (e.g., “Now tell me about this photo,” or “Tell me what is in this picture”). The meaning of the statement stayed the same, but the wording changed for each image. This variation was intended to further diferentiate the Varied and Non-varied conditions, and to better reflect the way a person might ask the repeated questions.

Two-hundred and forty-five participants (50.6% male) were recruited from Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). The AMT user population has been found to be representative of the general public and to respond to experimental stimuli in ways consistent with other subject pools [5]. The average age of participants was 40.7 years (SD = 10.5, min = 24, max = 72). Participants were compensated with \$2.00 for their participation, which took, on average 12 minutes. This is equivalent to \$10 per hour.

## Study 2 post hoc analysis

Several post hoc tests were conducted in an efort to more fully understand how diferent aspects of the CA’s conversational capability impacted user perception. During this efort, we were intrigued to find what appears to be a significant interaction efect on perceived humanness with the fully-crossed manipulation of response tailoring and variety (Figure 9). In this figure we see that the rating of perceived humanness appears to be significantly diferent only for the participants in the Tailored/Varied condition, with all the others staying approximately the same. This finding led us to investigate whether an interaction exists for the mediating factor of social presence.

We conducted a post hoc analysis to see if there was a significant interaction efect of response tailoring and variety on the mediating factor of social presence. An interaction plot depicting the diferent efects is shown in Figure 10. A linear regression model with social presence as the DV with no interaction efect shows a significant main efect of response tailoring $\left( \mathbf { b } = 0 . 2 7 , \ \mathbf { p } = . 0 3 \right)$ and response variety $( \mathbf { b } = 0 . 6 0 , \ \mathbf { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ , on social presence $( \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = . 0 \dot { 9 } 5 )$ . With the interaction efect added, the main efects of tailoring (b $= - 0 . 2 5 , \mathrm { p } = . 1 4 )$ and variety $( \mathbf { b } = 0 . 0 3 , \mathbf { p } = . 8 7 )$ are nonsignificant, but the interaction efect of tailored response and response variety is statistically significant $( \mathsf { b } = 1 . 0 7 , \mathsf { p } < . 0 0 1 )$ , and the $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ for social presence improves to .16. With these findings in mind, we ran an alternative SEM model which included the interaction efect. As with the linear regression, the interaction efect in the SEM was significant and the introduction of the interaction resulted in the direct efects of variety and tailored on social presence becoming nonsignificant. The $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ of social presence in the post hoc analysis SEM was .168 as opposed to .102 in the model that did not include the interaction.

## Discussion

The findings of this manuscript illustrate that as conversational agents exhibit signs of humanness via conversational skill, people form mindful and mindless anthropomorphism of the agent. This efect is due to the sense of social presence, or a connection through the medium, that the CA engenders. Our use of social presence as a mediator between conversational skill and anthropomorphism shows that it is how the medium is used that influences the connection people feel. While all our CAs used the same text-based communication, through the use of conversational social cues, the most skilled CA was able to create a connection with users more efectively.

One interesting point to note is that the enhancements made to the chatbot were rather rudimentary in nature—even the chatbot with the highest level of conversational skill (Tailored/Varied) did not engage in truly deep or complex conversations, but simply exhibited some limited indications of understanding. The significant diferences in partner engagement and perceived humanness from this small improvement in capability illustrate the major impact of minor improvements. In addition to the significant statistical findings, there is a great deal of importance to be gleaned from the level of the enhancements in conversational capability and the corresponding increase in social presence and mindful anthropomorphism. Across all three studies we find that the CAs that exhibited both response tailoring and response variety were much less likely to be regarded as “definitely computer” (Table 6). We also find that CAs with higher conversational skill are considerably more likely to be regarded as potentially being human—that is, they were rated as either “not sure, but guess human,” “probably human,” or “definitely human” (Table 7).

While mindful anthropomorphism and perceptions of humanness are interesting with regard to advances in artificial intelligence, potentially more important for business applications is the efect of conversational skill on mindless anthropomorphism, operationalized in our studies as partner engagement. Improving engagement leads to meaningful changes in the interaction, even when people do not believe they are interacting with a human. As with perceived humanness, we found significantly higher levels of partner engagement with the Tailored/Varied chatbot compared to the other bots. This is of great practical importance as engaging chatbots are considered more useful and easier to use [58], more enjoyable to interact with, and build rapport more successfully [23, 30]. Ultimately, this leads to increased satisfaction [41], enjoyment and perceived usefulness [26], and use of the system [55]. The studies reported here show that improving a CA’s conversational skill significantly improves perceptions of how engaging the CA is, which should in turn lead to increased adoption.

The results of our post hoc analysis show an intriguing finding when observing the combined efect of response tailoring and variety. Figure 10 illustrates that it is the combination of both features, and not either one individually, that creates the increase in social presence. To this end, we find stronger explanatory power for social presence in the post hoc SEM model that incorporates the interaction of tailoring and variety. Interestingly, we found that when tailoring and variety were the most incongruent (the Tailored/Non-varied condition), the agent appears to perform slightly worse than even the least capable bot (the Generic/Non-varied condition). These observations suggest not only that the addition of a single social cue may not be suficient to engender social presence, but also that it is important that the potential interaction of cues must be carefully considered. This is congruent with past research investigating efects such as the uncanny valley when the face and voice of an embodied CA do not match [47].

## Implications for practice

We have two major findings from our studies that are relevant for practitioners. The first is that conversational skill in the form of providing responses that are tailored and varied significantly increases how engaging the user finds the conversation. Relatedly, we found that while simply providing variety in CA responses provides some value over repeated questions, it does not rise to the same level of efectiveness as providing tailored responses. Organizations that wish to instill a sense of engagement in their customers during CA interactions—whether it be through online text chats, or audio interactions—will benefit from using tailored responses to create follow-up questions and responses for their CAs. Repetitive questions from a CA result in less engaging interactions and fail to create a feeling of connection in the interaction.

The second major finding is that conversational skill increases the social presence users feel. This means that the conversational skill of providing tailored responses not only improves engagement, but also increases the sense of connection users feel with the agent. Social presence is a valuable construct as developers seek to create connections with users. As users form impressions of social presence with a CA, those feelings can extend to an emotional connection with the organization represented by the CA [3]. An emotional connection can improve peoples’ perceptions of a company’s integrity and benevolence [19].

## Implications for research

Conversational agents, while historically investigated primarily in computer science, are garnering increased interest in IS research. As many of the basic technical challenges dealing with NLP have been overcome, behavioral aspects of CAs are going to become increasingly important over the next decade, as conversational agents take on larger roles within companies and begin to act like employees. Research is still needed to understand the individual and organizational impacts of this rapidly growing technology [60, 66]. We hope this research helps to bring CA research into the mainstream of IS research and encourages others to engage in this fascinating area.

This research also raises the question of the nature of incumbent human-human interaction theories and their relevance in human-computer interactions. In the current work, we demonstrate the usefulness of Social Presence Theory for understanding human-CA interactions. Prior work has shown that other traditionally human-human theories such as Interpersonal Deception Theory [62] and Expectancy Violations Theory [10] have potential eficacy for describing human-computer interactions. We suggest that as computers become more humanlike, theories that have traditionally been used for humanhuman communications may be more readily applied to human-computer interactions. This opens the door for additional investigation and theory development to more clearly understand this emerging paradigm of communication.

## Limitations and future research

The current work is only scratching the surface of potential manipulations of humanness for CAs. There are a wide range of verbal, visual, auditory, and invisible social cues that researchers and developers could use to make CAs more humanlike [17]. For example, future work might consider other social cues such as a chatbot that uses humor, engages in various levels of selfdisclosure, matches or opposes the user’s opinions, or is polite or rude. Other cues to consider include nonverbal traits such as embodiment and auditory cues. In addition to studying individual cues, our post hoc analysis suggests that the efect of these cues should not be studied in isolation. Our bot achieved greater levels of social presence through the combination of multiple cues; however, it is possible that combined cues may serve to decrease the social presence of the bot if they are not in alignment with one another. Future research on social cues must take that into account. Additionally, due to the design of the current study, it is not possible to definitively speak to the temporality and causal relationship between social presence and engagement as both are measured at the same time. While our theoretical explanation suggests the direction of the causal relationship, future research should attempt to more clearly understand the causality of this relationship.

Even without improving the social cues of the agent, other design features such as the context and backstory of a chatbot may also influence perceptions of humanness. For example, a recently developed chatbot, dubbed Eugene Goostman, was able to convince 33% of interviewers that it was human in a five-minute interaction. However, this success was not purely the result of superior natural language capabilities, but was also influenced by the bot’s backstory that described Eugene as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy. This backstory created a plausible reason for poor grammar and a lack of knowledge of certain topics [43]. Similarly, it is likely our Tailored/Varied CA benefitted from the highly controlled scenario and directed conversation found in the experiments, in addition to its conversational skill.

There is also the possibility that the Generic/Non-varied or Generic/Varied CA would occasionally give a response that could be perceived as being directly relevant to the message the user provided. For example, in Figure 3, “Why does it make you feel that way” is a very reasonable response and could be perceived as being relevant, even though it is not a tailored response. Overall our observation of the communications makes it clear that these types of good-but-not-tailored responses were infrequent. From a practical standpoint, however, this type of incidental relevance would serve only to weaken our results since the CAs with lower conversational skill could have occasionally been perceived as giving tailored responses even though it was not. Thus, it is plausible that the real-world diferences are more significant that what is shown in our analysis.

## Conclusion

This research focused on answering the general research question, how does the conversational skill of a CA afect perceptions of the CA’s social presence and anthropomorphism? We proposed and tested a model that describes how conversational skill and social presence afect partner engagement and perceptions of humanness. We operationalized CA conversational skill using tailored responses and response variety to create varying levels of conversational skill. Using social presence as our theoretical lens, we show that conversational skill increases the sense of social presence users feel through a chat medium, and thereby anthropomorphism of the CA. Specifically, we find that people perceive a chatbot with higher conversational skill to be both more humanlike and more engaging than a less skilled one. We also show that exhibiting conversational skill requires more than just adding a variety of text in the conversation, but rather CAs must be able to respond in a manner that is contingent on the text provided to it. Our results have been replicated across multiple studies, lending strong support for our findings. These findings demonstrate the ability of small improvements in chatbot conversational skill to positively influence perceptions of CAs and change the way people interact with computer systems and paves the way for future research to investigate the incremental improvements of additional features to increase humanness and engagement.

## About the Authors

Ryan M. Schuetzler (ryan.schuetzler@byu.edu; corresponding author) is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at Brigham Young University. He received his Ph.D. from University of Arizona. His research focuses on conversational agents, collaboration, and how users behave when interacting with artificial intelligence. Dr. Schuetzler’s research has been published in Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, The Accounting Review, Computers in Human Behavior, and numerous other journals and conferences. He serves as president of the Midwest USA chapter of the Association for Information Systems.

G. Mark Grimes (gmgrimes@bauer.uh.edu) is Assistant Professor of Decision and Information Sciences in the Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Arizona. His research focuses on conversational agents, information systems security, and analysis of human-computer interaction behaviors to detect changes in emotional and cognitive states. Dr. Grimes’s research has been published in Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Computers in Human Behavior, and other journals and conference proceedings, and has been presented to various industry and government organizations.

Justin Scott Giboney (justin\_giboney@byu.edu) is an Assistant Professor of Information Technology & Cybersecurity at Brigham Young University. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Arizona. His research focuses on behavioral information security, deception detection, and knowledge-based systems. Dr. Giboney has been an investigator on eight NSF-funded grants on information security, deception and forensicsrelated technologies and has published 37 papers related to information security, deception detection, and decision support in such journals as MIS Quarterly, Computers & Security, Decision Support Systems, and Computers in Human Behavior, and others.
