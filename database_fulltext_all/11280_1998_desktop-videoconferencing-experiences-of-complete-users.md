## Abstract

This longitudinal case study examines the use of desktop videoconferencing in one organization. Three theoretical perspectives, communication media choice, systems analysis and design, and privacy, help to inform the findings. The paper concludes by drawing implications for future research and practice.

Keywords: Desktop videoconferencing, media choice, implementation, privacy

## Introduction

Videoconferencing technologies have diffused dramatically in organizations since the late 1980s (Hart, et al. 1995; Kydd and Ferry 1994) and are used for such applications as training, interviewing, and meetings. However, organizations are just beginning to experiment with desktop systems, and none has made them widely available to their employees (Halhed 1996).

Desktop videoconferencing allows for audio and video communication between multiple locations through users' personal computers. Although it has been heralded as an emerging technology, its roots go back to the beginning of the century. The first video telephone call took place in 1927, a prototype was demonstrated at the 1934 World's Fair, and the first commercial system, the picturephone, became available in 1964 (Kraut and Fish 1995). It is argued that without an understanding of why the picturephone failed, present desktop videoconferencing systems will follow in the same path:

The development of picturephone service is estimated to have cost AT&T \$500 million. Its market failure in the early 1970s was a complete surprise to AT&T. . . . [I]f the true reasons for picturephone's failure in the 1970s involved a lack of need and even a basic dislike by most consumers and potential users for face-to-face visual telecommunication, then any reintroduction of picturephone service would be doomed to repeat the market failure of the past (Noll 1992, p. 307-308).

Kraut and Fish argued that Noll overstated the grounds for failure. The present case study re-examines these issues by studying the use of desktop videoconferencing in one organization.

Although some research has explored the use of desktop video in organizations, most of it has examined experimental systems, many of which were of low bandwidth, used temporarily by users in research and development labs or universities, and implemented in few workspaces (Kraut and Fish 1995; Whittaker 1995). For instance, Olson et al. (1995) studied 36 groups of MBA students using an inhouse experimental system; Fish et al. (1993) examined 23 permanent and temporary employees of a research lab taking part in a four-week field trial; Kraut et al. (1998) studied an 18-month trial of two in-house competing desktop systems in an R&D technical organization; and Pagani and MacKay (1993) investigated lower-bandwidth links (between two planners for six days, and between an engineer and the shop floor for two weeks) at a manufacturing firm. This has lead researchers to call for more extensive research on the applications of video technology for interpersonal communications (e.g., Whittaker 1995). (See Kraut and Fish [1995] and Whittaker [1995] for reviews of past research, and Rosen [1996] and Trowt-Bayard [1994] for examples of organizational applications.) In contrast to previous research, this longitudinal case study examines a production system incorporating full-motion video, used by regular employees, and installed at 1,000 employees' desks. This organization provided a unique opportunity to study a large-scale implementation of an emerging communication technology.

The overall research question guiding this study was: what influences usage of desktop videoconferencing? The underlying assumption in conducting this research was that multiple theoretical perspectives can help to inform media choices; rather than theories being competing, it was assumed that they would be complementary (Kraut et al. 1998; Sitkin et al. 1992; Webster and Trevino 1995). The study began with one theoretical perspective: communication media choice. However, organizational concerns and study results widened the theoretical perspectives to three broad ones: communication media choice, systems analysis and design, and privacy.

## Theoretical Background

## Media choice

Given the importance of communication to the employee's job (Mintzberg 1973) and the multiple communication media that are now available to accomplish that job (such as telephone, letters, meetings, electronic mail, and videoconferencing), it is important to understand employees' media attitudes and behaviors. The media choice literature suggests that a combination of factors drawn from multiple perspectives affect media choices. As outlined in Table 1, these factors are critical mass, medium richness, situational influences, social influences, media symbolism, and the individual characteristic of medium experience.

As the case study unfolded, it became apparent that other theoretical perspectives were needed to help explain the findings. Therefore, theories from the systems analysis and design and privacy literatures helped inform the results.

## Systems analysis and design

Research in systems analysis and design helps highlight those factors that are important in ensuring the acceptance of new systems (Laudon and Laudon 1996). Within this theoretical perspective, two competing theories, innovation characteristics theory and implementation process theory, have helped to explain the adoption of new technologies. Innovation characteristics theory assumes that individuals adopt technologies to perform individual tasks and therefore that technologies need to be designed with the correct characteristics for these individuals, while implementation process theory assumes that groups perform interdependent tasks and therefore that managerial effort needs to focus on the process of implementation (Yetten et al. 1997). That research argued that these two theories are not competing and combined them into an integrated theoretical framework by extending model of innovation adoption (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps 1988). This integrated theoretical framework includes factors from each theory (e.g., “task relevance” from innovation and “training and documentation” from implementation) and helped inform the present study’s findings. Factors that emerged in the present study were user needs, training and documentation, system reliability, ease of connection, management support, and the individual characteristic of self-efficacy (see Table 1).

## Privacy

Privacy is defined as a condition in which the individual has the capacity to (a) control the release and possible subsequent dissemination of information about him or herself, (b) regulate both the amount and nature of social interaction, (c) exclude or isolate him or herself from unwanted (auditory, visual, etc.) stimuli in an environment, and, as a consequence, can (d) behave autonomously (i.e., free from the control of others) (Stone and Stone 1990, p. 358).

Employees regulate their privacy in organizations through such means as restricting visual access (Sundstrom et al. 1980). For example, they may control their physical environments by closing office doors (Archea 1977) or their videoconferencing environments by denying others' access during the discussion of sensitive topics (Kraut et al. 1998).

Privacy has become more salient in organizations because of organizations' increased capacity to utilize computers to collect, store, and use information about their employees (Stone and Stone 1990). Research on privacy becomes important for designing systems in such ubiquitous computing environments (Bellotti and Sellen 1993), and particularly meaningful in explaining employees' reactions to systems in which their images are displayed. This has lead researchers to call for studies addressing privacy issues around videoconferencing (e.g., Whittaker 1995).

With the specific videoconferencing system used in the case study described below, privacy may have been even more salient because of the system's "awareness" feature. This awareness feature could provide periodic snapshots (or "freeze-frame images"; Kraut and Fish 1995) of coworkers' work areas (Whittaker 1995). Researchers in human-computer interaction have argued that awareness features are advantageous because they provide users with the ability to initiate opportunistic connections (Whittaker 1995), or as it has been termed, "benign surveillance" (Kraut and Fish 1995). These opportunistic connections allow users to determine when coworkers are available or busy, save time in tracking down and traveling to their coworkers' offices, and foster closer working relationships (Adler and Henderson 1994).

More specifically, concerns around personal privacy, perceptions of monitoring, employee control over privacy features, discomfort with being viewed, and the individual characteristic of introversion arose as important constructs.

## Method

The case study approach was chosen for this study of desktop videoconferencing because it is appropriate in the early stages of research when little is known about a phenomenon (Eisenhardt 1989). One proposal was that case studies may be used to provide description, test theory, or generate theory (Eisenhardt 1989), while another suggested that case studies can be positivist, interpretive, or critical (Myers 1997). Aspects of both positivist (the “testing” of traditional media choice theories) and interpretive (a revised understanding of privacy issues surrounding communication media) approaches were utilized in the conduct of the present study (Lee 1991).

The methodology for this research draws on the roadmap for building theories from case study research (Eisenhardt 1989) and techniques for analyzing qualitative data (Miles and Huberman 1994), both of which extend suggestions for the design of case study research (Yin 1984). This methodology is outlined here and elaborated further below. First, Miles and Huberman (1994) suggested that case study research begin with a priori specification of constructs. As proposed earlier (Eisenhardt 1989), this can be used to help shape the initial design of the research; however, it is important to ensure that preordained theoretical perspectives not bias or limit the findings. Second, the selection of the case(s) should be theoretical—that is, to fill a conceptual deficiency. Third, employing multiple data collection methods, both qualitative and quantitative, will help to triangulate on the evidence (Eisenhardt 1989; Myers 1997). The use of multiple investigators, such as one researcher posing the questions and the other taking notes and observations, allows the case to be viewed from different perspectives (Eisenhardt 1989). Fourth, the selection of participants to include in the case study should reflect multiple perspectives; in contrast, in many IS case studies “the only people who have been interviewed are from management” (Myers 1995). Fifth, data collection and analysis should overlap. Many researchers place codes on the data and use tables to summarize the evidence (Miles and Huberman 1994). An important feature of this process is a grounded-theory approach (Glaser and Straus 1967) to compare emerging concepts and hypotheses with past literature (Eisenhardt 1989). An assessment of inter-rater reliability at this point helps ensure that idiosyncratic coding is minimized (Miles and Huberman 1994). Sixth, a search for patterns (that is, for within-group similarities and intergroup differences) takes place. Finally, the process reaches closure when marginal improvements take place (Eisenhardt 1989).

## Case description

JEWEL $^{2}$ is a North American high-technology organization that developed and markets JVideo, a desktop videoconferencing product that can be used in-house or remotely. Although it would have been preferable to study a client site, the developer site was chosen because it is the only known large-scale implementation of desktop video anywhere in the world.

JEWEL possesses little hierarchy: it is a relatively flat, non-hierarchical, high-tech organization. JEWEL is a “survival” organization; that is, it possesses entrepreneurial, risk-taking leadership and focuses on its external linkages to its environment (Cooper 1994). JEWEL employs 1,000 people, the majority of whom are technical-professionals. Before the end of the study, JVideo had been implemented for all 1,000 employees, who were located in one building consisting of eight floors. The floors had open-concept plans, with most employee offices separated by dividers.

## Desktop Video Technology

JVideo contains capabilities for full-motion video, application sharing, awareness of others, and broadcasting. It also contains telephone-like features such as call waiting and forwarding, and features similar to e-mail (electronic mail) such as messaging and call logging.

To initiate JVideo calls, the employee must first launch JVideo on his/her personal computer (if it is not already running in the background), and then click on the message recipient's picture or name in an employee directory. To receive JVideo calls, JVideo does not need to be running on the message recipient's computer; it is automatically launched for incoming calls.

The in-house version of JVideo is analog (full-motion video), and therefore does not have the video quality limitations (low bandwidth) inherent in many systems. $^{3}$ Employees had separate TV monitors for the video image (although with the option of placing the video image on their PC screens). Most liked the separate TV monitor as it freed up their PCs and provided large, high-quality images of their communication partners. Therefore, unlike many systems using lower bandwidths, such as ISDN lines or the Internet, the high quality of the video could support non-verbal communications.

As has been argued (Whittaker 1995), video supports not only non-verbal communication, but two other important functions: sharing physical objects or workspaces and initiating opportunistic connections. JVideo was designed to support both of these functions. To share physical objects, JVideo's camera could be moved or removed easily by the user to point at and demonstrate objects in the workspace. JVideo also contained a facility for whiteboarding and applications that could be shared on employees' PCs during their video calls.

JVideo was designed for opportunistic connections, specifically “awareness” (Whittaker 1995). $^{4}$ That is, employees could see periodic snapshots of others’ workspaces to determine others’ availability. Employees had complete control over who and if others could view their periodic snapshots. Further, they could see views of public areas (such as JEWEL’s main waiting room) in the building at any time; some kept a public view displayed on their TV monitors throughout the day.

One-way communication is another potential use for videoconferencing systems (Kraut and Fish 1995; Whittaker 1995). To this end, JVideo also contained a facility for broadcasts (used for training and JEWEL announcements).

JEWEL management was very concerned about privacy and ethical issues around desktop videoconferencing and raised this issue at the beginning of the study. During the development of the product, they had worked with an outside organization to help design a system in which users' privacy would be protected.

Consequently, a series of alert, customization, and user controls were built into the product, such as a physical shutter on the camera, user configuration of others' access privileges to snapshots of their work areas, video-only (i.e., no audio) snapshots of offices, audio clues to alert users that a snapshot was to be taken, facilities for users to check who had viewed their snapshots and who had called them, the option of using a speakerphone or a private mouthpiece (a headset or handset) for any call, the capability for auto-answer or user pickup of incoming calls, a "do not disturb" setting, and so on. Further, JEWEL was working with other manufacturers to create an alliance to help ensure privacy controls would be built into desktop videoconferencing products.

## Implementation and Diffusion of the Technology

Before implementing the production version of JVideo in-house, JEWEL had conducted a multiple-month test between shipping (on the first floor) and inventory (on the fourth floor), and beta tests at outside organizations. The purpose for implementing JVideo at JEWEL was to support day-to-day in-house communication, to test the large-scale (1,000-employee) connection, and to provide a showcase for potential buyers. Management invested a large amount of money in the system (e.g., for microphones, cameras, TV monitors, and software) and its installation for 1,000 employees, and the CEO was a high-visibility user of the system. Thus, high-level management support existed for the technology.

Implementation of JVideo on employees' desktops proceeded on a floor-by-floor basis. At month one, installations of JVideo began on employees' desktops. At this time, management set up an Intranet page describing JVideo and sent out a series of e-mails to employees concerning JVideo, how to use it, the privacy controls built into it, and JEWEL's ethical principles for the use of JVideo. At month seven, over 80% of the employees had JVideo implemented on their desktops. All 1,000 employees in the organization had

JVideo implemented on their desktops before month 12.

## Data collection methodologies

The contact person in the organization was the product manager for JVideo, who met with the author face-to-face, and through e-mail, telephone, facsimile, and videoconference. He obtained company approvals for the research, provided printed documentation on the product, gave a demonstration of the product, and provided internal company e-mails concerning the product.

Data were collected during the implementation and diffusion of JVideo over a one-year period, at three points in time:

Period 1 (month one). Interviews with management concerning their vision for the product, and their concerns about the product (leading to the theoretical perspective of privacy) were conducted. Semi-structured (open-ended) interviews took place with new users (who had JVideo available on their desktops) relating to their expectations for JVideo. Interview questions were based on the media choice literature, but issues relating to the other theoretical perspectives also arose during these interviews.

Period 2 (month seven). Semi-structured (open-ended) interviews with users and nonusers concerning their current usage and attitudes toward JVideo took place. Interview questions were based on the media choice, systems analysis and design, and privacy literature.

Period 3 (month 12). A one-page questionnaire (again concerning current usage and attitudes toward JVideo, and containing a pre-addressed stamped envelope to the author) was mailed to all employees interviewed in Period 2; one follow-up reminder was sent via e-mail from the author's university account.

Most interviews in Periods 1 and 2 lasted between 30 minutes and one hour, and were conducted in employees' work areas in order to observe their work spaces and how they had positioned JVideo in these spaces. Notes were taken in all interviews, and the majority of interviews were audio taped for later transcription; typists transcribed all audio tapes. Interviews were conducted solely by the author in Period 1, and by the author with the help of a graduate research assistant in Period 2 (in Period 2, the author posed the questions, while the research assistant recorded the employees' responses).

In addition to information provided by the product manager and from interviews and questionnaires with employees, other types of information and experiences helped to shed light on the results: newspaper and magazine articles on the CEO, the organization, and JVideo; observations (by the author and a research assistant) of employees in their workspaces and in public areas; materials copied from the outside of employees' dividers (such as cartoons relating to desktop videoconferencing); the author's hands-on use of JVideo while at JEWEL; and feedback to and reactions from human factors and development personnel on the interview results (while the interviews were occurring). These multiple data collection methods help to provide triangulation on the results.

## Participants

The level of analysis was the individual one. The selection scheme for employees to be interviewed in Period 1 was based solely on whether JVideo was installed at their desks. All employees who were part of the initial installation were telephoned by the author, from within the organization, to request participation (10 of these new users were developers or marketers of JVideo; 11 were regular employees who were not involved with JVideo). Five of the participants were females. Fourteen of 21 employees were taped (two declined to be taped, and the tape recorder did not work for five interviews).

At Period 2, interviews took place with a sample from the over 800 employees who had

JVideo implemented in their work areas (both users and non-users of JVideo who were not developers or marketers of the product). Non-users were employees who had JVideo hooked up on their desks, who had used JVideo to send messages when it was initially available to them, but who had chosen not to use it to initiate messages at the time they were interviewed. Six new users from Period 1 (regular employees who were not JVideo developers or marketers) were contacted again and 43 other employees were randomly chosen from the organization's telephone list; participation was requested by telephone. Near the end of the interview period, a comparison was made of those sampled to the organization chart (based on department and organizational level). A few departments and management levels were under-represented slightly; thus several individuals were added by randomly sampling from these areas on the organization chart. Therefore, this sample should be representative of JEWEL employees' views and should reflect multiple perspectives. Sixteen of the participants were females. Of the 49 employees interviewed, 44 were audio taped (three declined to be taped, and the tape recorder did not work for two others). The data from Period 2 make up the bulk of the results. At Period 3, 29 (of the 49 employees from Period 2) returned the questionnaire (seven had left the organization, and 13 did not return the questionnaire).

## Themes explored

As suggested, a priori specification of constructs can be used to help shape the initial design of the research (Eisenhardt 1989). Thus, past research helped inform the questions. In Periods 1 and 2, although working from a set of interview questions, these questions were open-ended, employees were encouraged to explain their answers and were probed for further explanations.

Period 1 interview questions were drawn from theories of media choice (e.g., medium richness, social influences) and concerned new users' expectations for JVideo and any initial experiences with using it. Before conducting interviews in Period 2, data from Period 1 were analyzed, helping to inform Period 2 questions (and therefore resulting in an overlap of data collection and analysis). At this point, it became apparent that theories from media choice would provide an incomplete explanation for employees' use of JVideo. For example, as described in the results for Period 1 below, issues concerning remote use and sharing of periodic snapshots arose. Further, JEWEL's concern with privacy issues influenced Period 2 questions. Therefore, Period 2 interview questions again drew from media choice theories, but were supplemented by questions drawn from systems analysis and design (e.g., job fit issues) and from privacy (e.g., user control of periodic snapshots). Period 2 interview questions were pretested twice: first with a graduate student who was a user of another desktop videoconferencing system; based on her feedback, the questions were revised and pretested on a second graduate student (another desktop videoconferencing user) who suggested further refinements.

Users in Period 2 were asked about the average number of JVideo calls they sent and received per week. Next, they were asked to describe the last video call that they had initiated and were then asked further questions concerning that call (e.g., "Why did you choose JVideo [rather than another medium]?). Questions concerning the last video call help to minimize retrospective rationalizations (that may occur when asking employees how they generally used JVideo). Next, general questions concerning JVideo and their communication in general were asked (e.g., "What do your coworkers think about JVideo?", "What media do you use most frequently to communicate within this building?"). Care was taken not to ask any questions that could relate to privacy issues (e.g., "To how many employees do you provide access to your periodic snapshots?") until the end of the interview (so as not to lead or influence their responses to the questions). For non-users, a subset of the user interview questions was utilized, as well as several unique ones (such as "What are the reasons that you do not use JVideo?").

The questionnaire (containing closed- and open-ended questions) distributed in Period 3 re-examined some of the same issues raised at Period 2 (such as the number of JVideo messages sent and received per week, reasons for choosing JVideo for the last video call, comfort level with JVideo, and the use of periodic snapshots).

## Analyses

To shape hypotheses, tables summarize and tabulate the evidence (Miles and Huberman 1994). As part of this process, emerging concepts were compared with past literature (Eisenhardt 1989).

In Period 1, new users' views were examined: the views of developers and marketers of the product were contrasted with regular users' views. In Periods 2 and 3, findings were summarized by theoretical perspective, and then a search for patterns in the data was conducted.

To obtain a broad understanding of Period 2 interview data, the author first read every set of interview notes and then listened to every audio tape (comparing the tapes to their transcriptions: minor transcription errors were flagged, and the transcripts were corrected by a typist). No coding took place at this stage. Next, steps for analyzing qualitative data (Miles and Huberman 1994) were followed. First, likely constructs, along with their definitions, were placed in a table, and coding of the transcripts began. This was an iterative process. Approximately half of the transcriptions from Period 2 were coded by the author to determine emergent categories. Next, the literature was revisited, and categories were modified and added based on the emergent categories and the literature. The remaining transcriptions were coded using the modified coding scheme. Then the literature was revisited again to finalize the codes. The author then went through all transcriptions again using the final coding scheme (columns 1 and 3 of Table 1). All constructs but one were coded from the transcriptions: for the individual characteristic of introversion, the research assistant coded the employee's level of introversion based on the interview and the research assistant's observations of their behavior during the interview (see Table 1).

To provide an indication of inter-rater reliability, a second graduate research assistant who was unfamiliar with the research literature was hired from another department. She was provided with a table (containing columns 1 and 3 of Table 1) and the typed transcriptions of the interviews. She coded a random sample (10%) of transcriptions; inter-rater reliability was 80%.

To search for patterns—that is, for within-group similarities and intergroup differences (Eisenhardt 1989; Kirsch and Beath 1996), or, as it has been termed, “alignment of themes with relevant social groups” (Robey and Sahay 1996, p. 98)—data from Period 2 initially were split into a comparison of users and non-users. However, three groups emerged as described further in the results: complete users, wary users, and non-users. Period 3 results were used to complement and extend those found in Period 2.

## Results

Results are summarized here by period, with more detailed results presented below in the explanation of results.

## Overview of findings by period

## Period 1—Initial Use and Expectations

New users viewed JVideo as a replacement for the telephone and expected it to be particularly useful for messages to communication partners outside their own department and for messages requiring physical demonstrations. They viewed it as least useful for communication in which personal contact would be important or for confidential communications.

In contrast to developers and marketers of the product, regular employees saw a much greater need for communication outside of the building and for sharing of applications during video calls. Regular employees also were more likely to state that they would be uncomfortable displaying their periodic snapshots and that they were concerned about privacy issues. Developers and marketers had higher expectations than regular employees for JVideo due to its higher richness as compared with the telephone (they expected more amiable and personal communications, better understanding due to body language, more focused attention, and more open participation).

## Period 2—Accounting for Usage Differences

Employees interviewed had JVideo on their desktops for an average of 4.3 months and used JVideo less than other media available to them within the organization (face-to-face meetings, telephone, voice mail, and e-mail). Both users and non-users viewed the product as useful, and no differences were found between management and non-management use. Many described JVideo as a novelty, indicating that it was early in its adoption stage. As one employee stated: "It's still a novelty though. You know, I certainly haven't used it, and people in the company haven't used it, to its full potential."

Users: Of the 49 employees interviewed, 27 used JVideo on a regular basis; if these findings can be extrapolated to other employees in the organization, it would be expected that 55% of the over 800 employees could be classified as users. The users were asked to describe the last JVideo call that they had initiated. The average call was about four minutes in length, video only, unscheduled, for a routine communication task, and to one other person (who was well-known to them, located on another floor, of similar organizational status, and with whom they had had a previous videoconference). JVideo was chosen for such reasons as to test the system, to save travel and time to other areas of the building, to physically demonstrate something, or the enthusiasm for JVideo of one of the communication participants.

Users' overall attitudes toward the product were very positive. Some mentioned that they especially liked the facilities to set up personal directories, to view public areas, and to receive broadcasts. On average, users initiated 10.6 video calls per week. Of the 27 users, 17 allowed others access to their periodic snapshots; of these, eight gave access to all employees, and the rest gave access to an average of 12.6 employees. About half of the users viewed others' snapshots, usually to determine their availability. Nine of the users sometimes or always physically closed their cameras' shutters.

Most incoming and outgoing JVideo calls were accepted, some said because of the novelty of the system. When their outgoing JVideo calls were refused (and they followed up with their communication partner), they generally went outside of JVideo to leave a message (for instance, through voicemail).

Users viewed JVideo as most suitable for one or more of the following applications: off-site communications, physical demonstrations, communication to a distant work area within the building, broadcasts, and group meetings. They perceived JVideo as least appropriate for private or confidential communications (this finding is consistent with the findings at Period 1 for new users). Further, most users treated JVideo on speakerphone like a telephone speakerphone—they would not use it for private or confidential issues, or if they did, they would ask the other individual to use their hand/head set. Most mentioned that JEWEL has open offices, and that they take the possibility of others overhearing or seeing them into account, and thus they tend to “act professionally.”

Non-users: Twenty-two employees had access to JVideo at their desks, but were classified as non-users. They reported trying it initially with several co-workers (some stating for the novelty factor), but did not use it at Period

2 (although they chose not to initiate calls, they might receive calls from others). For instance, one non-user interviewed had JVideo installed at his desk for five months. In the beginning, he initiated about 20 calls a week and received about 12. When he was interviewed, these had both dropped to zero. As he said:

I guess when you get it, you always want to try it, so you call up just about anybody just to try to see if it works. I did get other people so they started calling me.... It kind of toned down after a while. People say, well if I really want to talk to him, I'll use the phone; if it's not an emergency or they want to waste a bit of time, then they'll use the video phone.

Non-users reported that they did not use JVideo for one or more of the following reasons: JVideo did not fit with their jobs (e.g., most people with whom they communicate were in their immediate work area or were located off-site), the telephone was more efficient, or they identified a lack of training or awareness of JVideo's features. Only two of the 22 non-users allowed others access to their periodic snapshots, and over half also physically closed their cameras' shutters all of the time.

## Period 3—The Influence of Time on Usage

Usage of JVideo should have increased at Period 3 because: (1) $100\%$ of the 1,000 employees had JVideo installed before this time period, (2) conducting the study could have highlighted management's commitment to the product, (3) the interview questions at Period 2 may have introduced employees to useful features that they did not know about, (4) management had instituted a weekly speech through the broadcast feature, and (5) more employees had access to the remote version of JVideo.

Some indicators of usage increased. For the 16 (of 22 interviewed in Period 2) non-users who returned the questionnaire in Period 3, (1) four had become users at Period 3 and (2) eight of those who had previously given no access to their periodic snapshots did so at

Period 3 (while one took away others' access privileges). Further, eight of the 29 employees (representing five complete users, one wary user, and two non-users from Period 2) who returned the questionnaire reported that they felt more comfortable when using JVideo. However, for the users interviewed in Period 2, the number of calls initiated per week dropped from 10.6 to 7.5 at Period 3. This may have been due to the novelty effect at Period 2; that is, some employees' use at Period 2 was for the purpose of trying out JVideo, and Period 3 may more accurately represent their routine usage levels. Open-ended comments for Period 3 differed little from Period 2 comments discussed below.

## Explanation of results: theoretical perspectives

Why wasn't JVideo used more, and why were employees hesitant about allowing access to their periodic snapshots? This was a large-scale organizational test of JVideo, and employees "should" have wanted the product to succeed because the organization's future success would relate to the success of JVideo. Thus, one would expect employees to take part enthusiastically in JVideo's implementation.

In a search for why JVideo was not used more, the analysis of Period 2 data was extended from a comparison of users and non-users to: "complete" users, "wary" users, and non-users, for the following reason. The new users in Period 1 suggested that periodic snapshots would be an important time-saver for tracking down others. However, some of the regular users indicated that they would be uncomfortable displaying their periodic snapshots or would display them only to a small subset of employees (while none of the developers or marketers mentioned any hesitation about their snapshots, suggesting that user needs were not completely identified). Therefore, JVideo could make personal privacy salient in some employees' minds.

As described earlier, employees had complete control over if and when others could view their periodic snapshots. Although employees could see the usefulness of periodic snapshots, some were still wary about using them. For instance, one employee stated:

It's good for me to have it [others' snapshots] because then I can check to see if someone's at their desk if they are on another floor, you know, to see if they are there and go catch them, but, by the same token I don't think I really want anyone checking to see if I'm sitting at my desk, you know what I mean.

In the analyses, complete users were defined as employees at Period 2 who not only used JVideo to initiate messages, but allowed access to their periodic snapshots (for some or all of JEWEL's employees). Complete users initiated the most calls per week and kept their cameras' shutters closed least often. In contrast, wary users were defined as employees at Period 2 who used JVideo to initiate messages, but did not allow any access to their periodic snapshots. Wary users initiated fewer calls than complete users, and half kept their cameras' shutters closed some or all of the time. Non-users did not initiate any calls. Only a few non-users allowed access to their periodic snapshots, and over half kept their cameras' shutters closed some or all of the time.

What distinguishes these three types of employees? ## Media Choice

Critical Mass: Some employees suggested that access was an issue. Although over 80% of employees had JVideo on their desks at Period 2, almost half of the interviewees under-estimated the percentage of users who were accessible via JVideo. Thus, consistent with past research, perceptions of critical mass affected employees' choices. This was more likely for wary users, who were more prone (than the other two categories of employees) to mention critical mass as an issue (see Table 2).

Perceived Medium Richness: Most employees viewed JVideo between the phone and face-to-face communication in terms of medium richness. In terms of comparisons with face-to-face meetings, users viewed JVideo as less personal and more informal. One would expect JVideo to be used for more ambiguous tasks if it were viewed as a substitute for face-to-face communications. Nevertheless (as reported above), it generally was used for more routine tasks. Further, a few employees mentioned that they do not use JVideo as a substitute for face-to-face communications because they like the exercise or change in routine that a face-to-face meeting provides.

In terms of comparisons with the telephone, some mentioned the advantage of eye contact and body language, the personal touch, and the increased attention of participants. Some used JVideo for demonstrations, or sharing of physical objects. For instance, one user stated:

My last call actually was a call to purchasing and it was very useful because he didn't know what I was talking about; I just held this up and he could see it, so it was very useful.

Thus, unlike some research demonstrating few differences between the telephone and video, employees viewed videoconferencing as richer than the telephone. This may be due to the full-motion video studied here, rather than the low bandwidth video studied in most previous research.

Perceptions of medium richness, however, did not distinguish between categories of employees: for the last JVideo message initiated, both complete and wary users perceived no differences in medium richness (Table 2).

Situation: The distance between the communication partners related to JVideo use. As expected, employees were more likely to communicate using JVideo with others who were further away (such as on another floor) than with those closer to them. However, distance did not distinguish between categories of users (Table 2).

Social Influences: Supporting previous research on newer technologies, social influences affected employees' use of JVideo. For instance, one user, when describing his last JVideo communication partner, stated: "Every time I go to his desk, he says: 'What's the matter, is your video broken?'" Complete users were more likely than other employees to mention social influences (Table 2).

Symbolism: Similar to other research on emerging technologies, there was no commonly accepted understanding of what sending a message through JVideo symbolized. The most frequently cited symbolic reasons were: (1) "their desire for teamwork, participation, involvement, or cooperation," (2) "that the message was informal or routine," (3) "that they were technologically innovative," or (4) the employee could not describe what JVideo symbolizes. The first symbolic reason is consistent with the symbolic meaning of face-to-face meetings, the second is consistent with e-mail, and the third with emerging technologies, such as e-mail when first introduced. No differences between categories of employees emerged (Table 2).

Individual Characteristic—Medium Experience: Some employees attributed their lack of JVideo usage to a lack of JVideo experience. However, as shown in Table 2, lack of medium experience did not appear to be an important influence on usage for most employees (and complete users were less likely to mention this lack).

## Systems Analysis and Design

User Needs: As reported earlier, both users and non-users viewed the technology as useful; however, favorable attitudes toward a technology do not necessarily result in use of the technology (Saga and Zmud 1994). Some employees indicated that their job tasks did not fit well with the technology. Some indicated that JVideo is a great product for others, rather than for themselves. For instance, one employee noted:

I know of some organizations that deal with offices overseas and they frequently have to set up phone conferences and on occasion lease satellite time for videoconferences, and in a situation like that, the JVideo hardware would be useful.

Lack of job fit was an important issue for most of the non-users, but only for a quarter of the complete users (see Table 2).

Users mentioned remote communication as one of the most suitable applications of JVideo and others suggested this as a potential source for productivity enhancements. This is consistent with the results in Period 1 for new users, in which off-site communication was indicated as a key application of JVideo. As described above, the external version of JVideo was available, but not implemented for most employees; an internal user needs analysis would have highlighted this requirement for off-site connections. Thus, there may not have been a match between JEWEL's external focus as an organization and JVideo as it was implemented. If users had access to the remote version of JVideo, it may have been used much more. About half of the employees indicated the need for remote use, with somewhat more non-users than complete users highlighting this need (Table 2).

Training and Documentation: A lack of training and documentation was demonstrated by employees' paucity of knowledge concerning the full range of JVideo's features, such as application sharing and features related to privacy (for instance, the feature to determine who had viewed their periodic snapshots). The technical-professional backgrounds of the employees and the ease of use of JVideo may have been working against them. Employees considered JVideo very easy to use; for example, one user stated:

It's very intuitive, very user friendly, it's built with the user in mind, the user interface is definitely one of the highlights of JVideo technology, so I'm very comfortable with it, so I didn't need, I don't think that anybody needs, training more than like two minutes, a quick overview of the system. So I don't need training, like I said.

JVideo included online help (of course, one needs to know a product's features before one can ask for help with them). No manual of documentation was given to employees, but a card describing JVideo's features was made available for employee pickup at one point (but very few employees had obtained it).

Although many employees were unaware of the application sharing feature, new users in Period 1 had indicated shared applications as a key feature of JVideo. None of the employees reporting using the application sharing feature in their last JVideo communication. Further, some non-users in Period 2 said that they did not use JVideo because they often had to show something on their computer screens to someone else (indicating their lack of JVideo product knowledge). However, these types of employees would be the ones that would find application sharing most useful. Employees became aware of this feature through the interview questions, and at Period 3 one user wrote: "Have since learned how to use the application sharing. Very cool!"

Employees expressed a need for training or other ways of becoming more familiar with JVideo's features. About half of the employees reporting receiving a two minute introduction to JVideo when the technician installed it in their workspaces; for most of the remaining employees, they received no training (because they were away from their desks when the installation took place). A lack of training and documentation was perceived to be more important to non-users than to the other categories of employees (Table 2).

Reliability: Some employees were concerned about JVideo's reliability. Employees had to reinstall JVideo from time to time: (1) as user-suggested changes or improvements were programmed and implemented and (2) as new floors of users were added. At Period 3, some employees still mentioned reliability issues. For instance, one non-user wrote:

I think the problem is that JVideo is not stable yet. After my interview with you, I was very encouraged to use JVideo. However, JVideo was down many times, and automatically, I used the telephone instead. I hope that it becomes stable like the telephone and we will be able to use it regularly.

However, perceptions of JVideo's reliability did not distinguish between usage for the three categories of employees (Table 2).

Ease of Connections: In Period 1, developers and marketers primarily viewed JVideo as a substitute for telephone communication within the building. However, some employees in Period 2 noted that using the telephone to call someone in the building was faster and easier than calling through JVideo (if JVideo was not already launched on the sender's PC. $^{6}$ Surprisingly, complete users were more likely to mention a lack of ease of connection than the other categories of employees (Table 2).

Management Support: Few employees mentioned top management support for JVideo (see Table 2), although JVideo did have top management support. As described earlier, the CEO was a high-visibility user of his system, and management sent out e-mail messages about the system at Period 1. However, employees reported receiving many e-mails per day (some said 100), so e-mails may not have been the most effective means of communicating management's support to employees. Further, at Period 3, the contact was asked whether anything had changed regarding JVideo, and he replied: "Senior management still encourages the use of this product."

Individual Characteristic—Self-Efficacy: Employees differed in terms of their JVideo self-efficacy, and this related to their use of the system. $^{7}$ As Table 2 demonstrates, complete users were higher than the other two categories of employees in self-efficacy.

## Privacy

Privacy Concerns: Some employees brought up issues concerning privacy early in the interview (before any questions were asked that might relate to privacy, such as others' access to their periodic snapshots) indicating their real concerns around desktop videoconferencing privacy. In some cases, this was due to concerns about the possibility of others videotaping or listening in on their conversations (highlighting their perception of lower user control over privacy). In other cases, JVideo's "awareness features" (that is, its periodic snapshots and views of public areas) set the stage for privacy concerns with employees. Wary users expressed more concerns about privacy than the other employees (Table 2). $^{8}$

Perceptions of Monitoring: Most employees did not report that their supervisors had set any expectations for their use of JVideo or for allowing others' access to their periodic snapshots. However, the possibility for monitoring was still a concern for them. For instance, one user stated:

I don't have my boss or director, anybody that's in a high position, I don't let them check on my periodic snapshots. I don't want it to be used as a tool for seeing whether or not I am actively working every minute of every day.

Some employees perceived that their personal actions could be monitored through JVideo's periodic snapshots or through cameras in JEWEL's public areas. For example, one employee described this perception:

I do know that people have raised concerns that now they think that more people can see them going for smoke breaks because they have to pass one of these cameras. That's utterly ridiculous, but I do think there is a feeling out there that Big Brother is watching.

More wary users had perceptions of monitoring than other employees (Table 2).

Perceived Employee Control Over Privacy Features: Many employees felt that they had control over their privacy with JVideo. As one employee stated:

As long as the employee has the ability to control it, I think that pretty much takes care of a lot of the privacy. But then if the end user is forced to use it in a particular way, then that takes privacy way down. I think it's very private. If I couldn't be in control of it, that would affect me. Actually if I couldn't be in control of it, I'd be somewhere else.

However, as indicated earlier, some employees were not aware of all the privacy controls built into JVideo. A few thought that their periodic snapshots could be viewed by anyone (without their express permission). For example, the following employee indicated his lack of knowledge of privacy controls, and his resulting misperceptions of being monitored:

I didn't even know for the longest time you could like spy on someone. That your JVideo was spying on you while you were doing your work, like taking that picture every so often of you. I didn't know that. [Interviewer: "Was yours set to take the picture?"] I don't even know, I mean after a while I just shut my shutter over because then I began to get paranoid.

This employee was unaware that unless he had personally gone into JVideo and given permission for specific others to see his snapshots, none were being taken. Some employees also were unaware that they could determine who had viewed their snapshots.

A few employees felt that others could override their control. For instance, one employee reflected on a coworker's fears:

I do have somebody else on my team that I could use the periodic snapshots to see if that person's actually at his desk or not, but he has a phobia of anybody else seeing him work, so he always turns it off. He wants to disconnect it as much as possible. That one person, he's afraid that I'll figure out how to turn on his periodic snapshots without him even noticing. Not only does he close his shutter, he closes the shutters of people around him.

When asked if JEWEL had any ethical principles for the use of JVideo, the vast majority said something like: "They must have, but I've never heard of any." Only a few recalled the initial e-mails about ethical principles (but, as reported above, employees reported receiving many e-mails per day).

Wary users perceived that they had less control over the privacy features than the other employees (Table 2).

Discomfort With Being Viewed: Some employees described their discomfort when participating in video calls. As one employee stated:

A lot of people are self-conscious about talking via camera, they find it awkward. It's a different medium, I find, and a lot of people just have a hard time accepting it.

A perception of monitoring also may change employees' work habits. As one user stated:

Privacy is to a certain extent taken away a little because people can view you at all times unless you choose to block it. So sometimes, because it's on, I'm actually conscious that someone may be looking at me, so it inhibits me because I am not necessarily 100% myself.

In other words, desktop videoconferencing may increase employees' self-attention and decrease their attention to work. More wary, than complete, users reported discomfort with being viewed (Table 2).

Individual Characteristic—Introversion: Employees who were lower in the individual characteristic of introversion tended to use JVideo more. Non-users were more likely to be rated as high on introversion than were wary users; no complete users were rated as high on introversion (Table 2).

## Discussion and Implications

This study utilized multiple theoretical perspectives to develop an inductive understanding of desktop videoconferencing use, bringing together constructs addressed in previous research and others that emerged from this study. None of the theoretical perspectives alone explained the findings for the three types of employees: constructs from media choice and systems analysis and design represented important influences on employee usage of desktop video, and constructs from the perspective of privacy were most useful for describing wary users.

As compared to the other two categories of employees, complete users' levels of desktop videoconferencing usage seem to be explained by social influences, higher medium experience, higher job fit, higher desktop video self-efficacy, and lower introversion. Complete users were most likely to mention ease of connection as a problem (perhaps because of their greater experience with the technology—consistent with Goodhue and Thompson's findings for reliability outlined in Table 1), but this did not hinder their use of desktop videoconferencing as compared with other employees. Compared to the other employees, non-users' levels of desktop videoconferencing usage seem to be explained by a lack of job fit, a lack of training and documentation, lower desktop video self-efficacy, and higher introversion.

In contrast to other employees, wary users' levels of desktop videoconferencing usage seem to be explained by lack of critical mass and lower job fit, and, most importantly, by all of the privacy issues that emerged from the interviews. One would not have expected that privacy would be a significant issue at JEWEL (Bellotti and Sellen 1993): JVideo was designed based on the principles of employee control and feedback. Further, most employees at JEWEL worked in open offices and thus would have lower expectations for privacy than those working in private offices (Stone and Stone 1990). However as described above, not all employees were aware of JVideo's privacy controls or JEWEL's ethical principles—and privacy was an issue for some employees.

## Limitations and contributions

As has been suggested (Eisenhardt 1989), theories from cases tend to be overly complex and more idiosyncratic than sweeping theories. However, this case study represented a unique opportunity to study a very large-scale implementation of desktop videoconferencing—and no other organization has made desktop videoconferencing widely available to its employees (Halhed 1996). Further, the desktop video product was of full-motion quality, whereas most other systems are of lower bandwidths. Unlike other research, any results found in this case cannot be attributed to the poor quality of the video; other explanations must be found.

In this study, ratings of some individual characteristics were made by observing the employees. Future research may want to use validated individual difference measures (such as Goldberg's (1992) measure of introversion-extroversion). Further, although multiple data collection methods were utilized, interviews and questionnaires were used sequentially, rather than at the same time.

This research was conducted in one organization, and the organization itself was the developer of the desktop videoconferencing product, reducing the external validity of the findings. This limitation was reduced somewhat by excluding developers and marketers of the product from the analyses of employees' usage. However, the organization and its employees had a vested interest in the success of the product, and most employees were technical-professionals and were skilled with computers. Thus, we would expect that study results will represent an upper bound on desktop video usage for local communication in other organizations

This research examined desktop videoconferencing solely for local communication within one organization. As suggested (Whittaker 1995), one limitation of studying local systems is that the benefits of video will be reduced as employees have the option of meeting face-to-face. Although JEWEL did have 1,000 employees spread over eight floors, future research will need to extend these results for communication to remote locations.

This study responds to calls for more research and theoretical development on privacy issues in organizations (Stone and Stone 1990). Other studies in information systems have examined such issues as ethical concerns of computer users (Conger et al. 1995), organizational codes of ethics (e.g., Harrington 1996), computer-based performance monitoring (e.g., George 1996; Grant and Higgins 1991), and consumers' information privacy (e.g., Smith 1994; Smith et al. 1996). As previous research has argued:

The greatest concerns for the workplace, however, will be those regarding surveillance of employees in their day-to-day jobs. . . . Increasingly, monitoring is being tied to computerized tasks. . . . Since the law—not anticipating such technological capabilities—is virtually silent on the appropriateness of such monitoring, this activity falls among those that are legal but often attacked as intrusive. As newer technologies . . . are unveiled and diffused across the corporate world in the next few years, additional questions about ownership and norms will continue to spring up (Smith 1994, pp. 201–202).

## Implications for practice and research

Results of this research will interest managers considering the implementation of desktop videoconferencing. Because large-scale installations of desktop videoconferencing systems are costly, managers will want to know more about communication tasks for which these systems are appropriate before then invest in them. The results of this research have highlighted these tasks (such as physical demonstrations). Once systems are acquired, managers will want to exploit their potential. This research helps to pinpoint those factors that encourage and discourage employees from using these systems. The implications for practice are clear. Organizations should implement desktop video for employees who need it in the accomplishment of their work, provide training and documentation on the system, clearly communicate strong management support for the system, provide opportunities for employees to gain experience and higher efficacy with the system, communicate the advantages over telephone communication (such as physical demonstrations and application sharing), and ensure that employees understand any privacy controls built into the system. Further, designers need to understand the importance of shared tools in their design of distance communication systems.

Managers need to be cognizant that awareness features (designed into videoconferencing systems for the purpose of saving employees' time and fostering closer working relationships) may actually have the unintended consequence of making privacy more salient to users, with the result that they are less likely to accept the product. That is, desktop videoconferencing may affect employees in ways that other media may not. As pointed out:

How does one record (observe) the world without affecting it? Sounds a little like a non-subatomic version of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, which states that you cannot observe any phenomenon without affecting it (Bushnell 1996, p. 37).

Designers of systems also need to be aware of this possible unintended consequence and design safeguards into systems to take this into account. A useful framework for designers concerning these safeguards, and the suggestion that “privacy should be a central design issue in its own right,” was recently presented (Bellotti and Sellen 1993, p. 78). However, if employees are not made aware of these safeguards—through the design of the system, its documentation, and user training—then these safeguards do not, in fact, exist for employees.

The findings of the present study also provide more general implications for those conducting beta-test evaluations of software. In beta testing, software firms make their prerelease software available for use and testing by selected customers. Few articles have been written on beta testing, and management information systems texts generally are silent on the topic. $^{9}$ However, some estimate than more than half of Fortune 1000 companies have beta-tested software (Daly 1994). Beta testing can consume many resources of these companies, yet they still may participate because of the opportunity to explore new technologies and the possibility of outsmarting their competition (Daly 1994).

The implications of this study for beta testing relate to: (1) the breadth of issues examined, (2) the time frame for data collection, (3) the types of employees studied, and (4) the richness of data obtained. First, articles concerned with beta testing concentrate on a narrow range of technical and usability issues (e.g., Delphi Research Group 1997). However, the present study went beyond traditional beta testing by building in room for surprise from a wider viewpoint than technical and usability issues (such as the privacy issues that surfaced in this study). This wider viewpoint allowed for the discovery of many more issues that will be of use in the marketing and future installations of the product. Therefore, although a preliminary list of issues to study needs to be developed (e.g., around technical and usability issues), beta tests need to be designed in such a way that other issues can surface. Second, data collection with the same employees at multiple points in time (rather than one-shot designs) helped to show employees' changing views on the product over time. For instance, although new users at Period 1 expected that videoconferencing would act as a replacement for the telephone, this did not occur in Periods 2 and 3. Third, this study concentrated on employees who were not involved in the development or marketing of the product. In contrast, "vendor IS staffs are often pressured to act as guinea pigs for their companies' products" (Ambrosio et al. 1993). This may lead to a focus on technical issues, at the expense of other, equally important, issues, and may lead to differing conclusions about the product. For instance, our study demonstrated dissimilar expectations for use of desktop videoconferencing: regular employees were less likely (than developers or marketers) to suggest that they would use others' snapshots to check on their availability. Finally, customer feedback by beta testers generally takes the form of short questionnaires completed by users (e.g., see Remer and Dunaway 1995), yet the present study's rich data, provided by interviews and observations of users in their workplaces, allowed for the discovery of new findings. In summary, these implications can help inform software developers of issues and tradeoffs that they should consider when designing beta tests for any type of software, and should also provide an impetus for educators and researchers to develop a more formal evaluation methodology for beta testing.

Many opportunities for future research exist, such as the issues that could not be investigated in this case study (for instance, a comparison of employees with differing computer skills and communication to remote locations). Other constructs that have emerged in past research, such as personal innovativeness (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps 1988), also should be investigated.

Researchers of communication systems, and desktop videoconferencing in particular, will need to widen their theoretical bases to include multiple theoretical perspectives (Kraut et al. 1998). Other researchers have developed models to describe the media choice (e.g., Fulk 1993) and systems analysis and design (e.g.,

Saga and Zmud 1994) perspectives. However, no comprehensive models that include privacy issues around desktop videoconferencing systems have been proposed. Research on procedural justice, or the process by which employee outcomes are determined (Ambrose and Adler 1996), will be key to developing this model. More specifically, procedural justice relates to employees' focus on how decisions are made; the procedures used to arrive at decisions can have serious effects on fairness judgments (Tyler and Lind 1992). Procedural justice is affected by a variety of antecedents, two of which are particularly appropriate here: (1) safeguards, or input to the process (Thibaut and Walker 1975), and (2) the types of tasks (such as personal activities) monitored (Aiello 1993). Past research has demonstrated that procedural justice relates to employees' organizational attitudes, such as job satisfaction, evaluation of leaders, and organizational commitment (Lind and Tyler 1988), and it is proposed that higher procedural justice will lead to higher perceptions of fairness (Ambrose and Alder 1996), and thus to more likely acceptance and satisfaction with a system. In support of this, higher procedural justice judgments of users participating in systems development were found to result in higher satisfaction and performance (Hunton 1996).

Based on the findings from the present case study and previous research, it is proposed that a comprehensive model would need to incorporate the following propositions concerning privacy: Employees will exhibit more concerns for their personal privacy in communication systems with objective monitoring, the display of users' images, more "awareness" features, more introverted employees, and less user control over privacy. Further, when employees have higher concerns for personal privacy, they will be more likely to utilize any privacy controls available, such as denying others' access to their periodic snapshots. Those who have higher concerns for personal privacy also will be more likely to perceive that their personal activities are being monitored. This perception of monitoring will result in increased self-attention, and decreased attention to work, and therefore may result in lower productivity. Finally, employees will believe that they are being treated in a more procedurally just way with higher user controls over privacy, less objective monitoring, and lower perceptions of monitoring of personal activities. Higher procedural justice in turn will lead to higher perceptions of fairness, and thus more use of the communication system.

Future research on desktop videoconferencing also will benefit from incorporating more general theories of information redundancy, diversity, and overload. Although users of JVideo generally utilized it as a single medium (video only), it can be used as multimedia (e.g., a video window open at the same time as an application sharing window). Thus, differing (related or unrelated) messages may be communicated at the same time. It has been suggested that multimedia will support rapid task-cycling (Galegher and Kraut 1994), and further proposed that multimedia may provide redundancy, reduce biases, and reinforce message clarity (Sitkin et al. 1992), thus positively influencing the communication. Other theories point to possible negative influences on the communication: the number of media making up the multimedia system and the diversity of information communicated could result in information overload, rather than clarity, to the employee. Information overload and diversity can increase decision time and decrease performance (Iselin 1989); however, little research has addressed this (Evaristo and Adams 1995). More generally, any effects of desktop videoconferencing on productivity need to be explored. A better understanding of these issues will lay the groundwork for future research relating media choices to managerial and organizational effectiveness.

Jane Webster is an associate professor in the Management Sciences Department at the University of Waterloo in Canada. She received her Ph.D. degree in management and information systems from New York University. Her current research interests focus on communication media choices, multimedia communication, and playfulness in human-computer interactions.
