# IT value creation in public sector: how IT-enabled capabilities mitigate tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in public organisations

Jie Mein Goh<sup>a,b</sup> and Alvaro E. Arenas<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Beedie School of Business, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, British Columbia, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems and Technology, IE Business School, Madrid, Spain; <sup>c</sup>IE Business School, Information Systems and Technology, Madrid, Spain

## ABSTRACT

Governments today are striving to improve services in the public sector through digital transformation programs but face tremendous pressures from multiple fronts (economy, national security, healthcare, education, etc.). Even when worldwide enterprise IT spending for the government and education markets has been increasing and is expected to surpass \$652 billion in 2023 to cater to such transformation programs, 80% of the government transformation e<sup>f</sup>orts failed to achieve expected results. A plausible reason for this lacklustre performance could be the presence of tradeo<sup>f</sup>s or con<sup>fl</sup>icts that is particularly salient in public organisations. To better understand the mechanisms by which IT enables or inhibits capabilities of the public organisations in attaining public value, we adopt a con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution lens to study how information technology (IT) enabled capabilities to mitigate these tradeo<sup>f</sup>s. Using a dataset collected from public organisations in a European country unreeling from a <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis, we examine the processes by which IT enables public organisations to manage the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s arising from con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. We identify three mitigation strategies facilitated via IT-enabled organisational capabilities – bias, tunnelling and hybridisation. This paper contributes to the understanding of how IT mitigates value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in public organisations to achieve public value.

## 1. Introduction

Worldwide investment in IT for government and education sectors is estimated to increase to more than \$652 billion in 2023 (Gartner, 2019). This spending is expected to increase especially in countries (e.g., Japan, Germany, Singapore, etc.) with IT as one of the key sources of their economic growth. The capacity of IT to transform the public sector has materialised into positive impacts of IT such as cost savings, improved data access, productivity gain, time e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, improved decision processes, etc., and it is doubtless that IT can make governments more e<sup>fi</sup>cient and e<sup>f</sup>ective in delivering public services to citizens through public organisations (Díaz-Díaz, Muñoz, & Pérez-González, 2017)<sup>1</sup>. However, despite the large amounts of IT investments in every level of government, a recent report suggests that most public sector IT investments do not translate to value for citizens (Shevory, 2015). Further, despite the increase in government IT spending, 80% of the government transformation e<sup>f</sup>orts failed to achieve expected results (McKinsey, 2018). While IT issues are prevalent across both private and public sector organisations, the challenges presented to public organisations are exacerbated by the litany of other challenges such as conservative views and risk aversion to IT and innovation, increased citizen’s expectations, lack of digital literacy and leadership in public organisations and even <sup>fi</sup>nancial austerity which result in a low return on IT investment in public organisations.

A major reason for this poor translation may be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the unique property of IT investment decisions in public organisations in that many of these decisions have competing value-based goals (Andersen & Henriksen, 2007). Value-based goals are goals related to public safety, education, privacy, public welfare and tax rates, re<sup>fl</sup>ective of the diverse groups of stakeholders whose needs the public organisations need to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll. Such goals are typically intangible and often in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one another (Caudle, Gorr, & Newcomer, 1991). As an example, a typical tradeo<sup>f</sup> of public organisation is the expectation of higher protection from the government versus lower regulatory burden and costs (Deloitte, 2017). Citizens expect that the government steps up its regulatory role (e.g., conduct audits or checks). At the same time, the public organisations do not want to increase their regulatory burden given that this increases costs and resources required to perform these regulatory functions (e.g., more paperwork and audit manpower). Thus, the pursuit of one goal leads to the expense of the other. As another example, improving national security through the use and capture of more personal data compromises individual privacy. Clearly, public organisations are often confronted with these types of tradeo<sup>f</sup>s: First, the goal of investing in IT to improve service provision comes at the expense of another area, e.g., public organisations’ IT expenditure goals on improving quality of public safety reduces the spending on other areas such as education. Second, the value-based goals in public organisations may directly con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one another.

Prior studies noted that con<sup>fl</sup>icting values that are unresolved lead to performance issues in public organisations (e.g., resulting in analysis paralysis among public o<sup>fi</sup>cials) (Thacher & Rein, 2004). Mitigation or con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies<sup>2</sup> to resolve such con<sup>fl</sup>icts thus become pertinent for ensuring good governance and performance in public organisations. Strategies to mitigate such value con<sup>fl</sup>icts have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior public management literature<sup>3</sup>. For instance, it has been demonstrated that public organisations employ <sup>fi</sup>rewalls as a strategy that allow di<sup>f</sup>erent organisations or departments within organisations to individually assume and realise con<sup>fl</sup>icting values, without leading to paralysis. While these strategies have been documented, we do not <sup>fi</sup>nd prior studies that attempt to discuss how IT facilitates mitigation strategies which help resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. Scholars generally agree that IT can be utilised to create value in organisations which has led to a rich body of literature on business value of IT (Ravichandran, Han, & Mithas, 2017; Saldanha, Mithas, & Krishnan, 2017; Trantopoulos, von Krogh, Wallin, & Woerter, 2017). However, fewer studies focus speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on IT value in public organisations with the exception of a few studies (Pang, Lee, & Delone, 2014; Teo, Tan, & Wei, 1997). The lack of theoretical bases focused on the public sector makes it harder for researchers to address key questions about IT value in public organisations.

The dominant argument in the business value of IT literature suggests that IT enables the <sup>fi</sup>rm to create business value through organisational capabilities. For instance, a recent study has demonstrated that IT enables organisational capabilities such as business <sup>fl</sup>exibility and operational competence (Benitez, Ray, & Henseler, 2018). Braojos, Benitez, and Llorens (2019) suggest that IT and capabilities work together and are complementary, allowing organisations to achieve desired organisational outcomes and performance (e.g., IT infrastructure capability and social media capability complement each other and result in online customer engagement).

However, studies in IT value also suggested that tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between organisational capabilities exist (Benitez et al., 2018; Xue, Ray, & Sambamurthy, 2012). Similar to the literature on IT value, these tradeo<sup>f</sup>s are examined to a larger extent for for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organisations and less so for public organisations. In some ways, such tradeo<sup>f</sup>s experienced by for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organisations resonate with the con<sup>fl</sup>icting valuebased goals presented to public organisations identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior literature. While these studies allude to the presence of competing or con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals in public organisations<sup>4</sup> (Fedorowicz, Gogan, & Culnan, 2010; Wilkin, Campbell, & Moore, 2013), research examining how IT can be harnessed by governmental institutions in mitigating these tradeo<sup>f</sup>s have largely been ignored. However, this perspective is useful in providing an alternative lens to deepen our understanding of how public organisations can mitigate the challenges and avoid poor outcomes, making the best use of taxpayers’ dollars. It has been suggested that understanding value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts in the public sector is key to deepening our understanding of IT value in public sector organisations (Campbell, Mcdonald, & Sethibe, 2009; Pang et al., 2014).

As such, this study aims to expand the extant literature by theorising how IT can help public organisations achieve two or more competing value-based goals facilitated by mitigating strategies, thereby creating public value and pushing the public-value frontier<sup>5</sup> forward. By public value, we refer to the value of an entity or organisation that serves the community and contributes to the public (Meynhardt & Bartholomes, 2011; Moore, 1995). Pang et al. (2014) theorised the di<sup>f</sup>erent ITenabled organisational capabilities which allow the public sector to achieve public value, but their work did not provide the mitigation strategies associated with the fundamental tradeo<sup>f</sup>s or con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals that public organisations typically face. This study extends Pang et al.’s work by providing the associated solutions for resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals faced by public organisations through a processual lens. This requires the understanding of the dynamics in IT value creation within public organisations. Therefore, we take a qualitative approach to uncover the underlying dynamics of IT value creation in public organisations. We attempt to address this through the research questions: (i) How does IT create public value in public organisations? (ii) How does IT resources help public organisations manage the tension between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals to achieve public value?

To answer the above research questions, we conducted a qualitative study by collecting data from the public organisations of a European country. This government has undergone a volatile period since 2009 due to the global <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis resulting in massive budget constraints in the years ensuing. This context presents a unique opportunity for us to examine our research questions focusing on IT value creation when dealing with extreme con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. The crisis led to limited resources and hence presents severe tradeo<sup>f</sup>s to the public sector organisations as they constantly need to tradeo<sup>f</sup> between the lack of resources (e.g., cost and labour) and other public value-oriented goals (e.g., e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and responsiveness to citizens). This study also allows us to examine the best practices adopted by the government when confronted with con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals and how it was able to attain signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements by the use and application of IT in public administration after the <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis as evidenced by the rankings produced by the United Nations<sup>6</sup>. Using data collected from 10 public organisations which underwent this signi<sup>fi</sup>cant crisis, we delineate the processes by which IT helps the government manage tensions between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals, through the development of organisational capabilities and mitigating strategies, resulting in public value. The case was analysed using the capabilities framework which proposed that the relationship between “IT resources and organizational performance in governments is mediated by organizational capabilities” – public service delivery, resource acquisition, innovation, co-production and public engagement capabilities (Pang et al., 2014). We make use of this capabilities approach to focus on the underlying dynamics to examine the mechanisms through which IT resources result in public value.

This study contributes to the existing literature in the following ways: First, we construct an integrated analytical framework that can be applied to provide insights on how IT resources will help public organisations better manage the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s arising from competing valuebased goals. Second, in applying an existing analytical framework to the case, we derive a series of developmental prescriptions for government to synchronise IT for managing these tensions in order to generate public value. In contrast to Pang et al. (2014), this study focuses on the mitigation of these tensions whereas Pang et al. (2014) uncover the IT-enabled capabilities in the public sector leading to public value.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst provide a review of the literature on public value, con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals, and mitigation strategies in public organisations, IT-enabled capabilities, and IT impact in public organisations. The second section provides the description of our proposed analytical framework to answer our research questions. Next, we discuss our research method. Finally, we describe our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclude with the implications of this study.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Unique characteristics of the public sector

As noted in prior literature (Eskildsen, Kristensen, & Juhl, 2004; Wright, 2001), there are several clear di<sup>f</sup>erences between public sector from the private sector. A typical characteristic associated with public organisations is its bureaucratic culture whereas private sector organisations are typically less bureaucratic and <sup>fl</sup>exible (Parker & Bradley, 2000). Partly due to this culture, the decision-making structure in public sector arguably tends to be more hierarchical and slower paced compared to the private sector. While there may be heterogeneity due to regional di<sup>f</sup>erences between public versus private organisations, we focus our attention on the di<sup>f</sup>erence in the goals and values of these two types of organisations. The main goal of private sector companies is usually tied to increasing shareholder value whereas the key goal of any public organisation is to increase public value. The latter tends to be more pluralistic compared to a more unidimensional focus of the private sector. In line with these goals, the values upheld by public organisations generally focused on legitimacy, lawfulness, accountability and justice, whereas private sector generally focused on pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, competitiveness, customer relationship and revenue generation.

## 2.2. Public value

Value is a complex and broad concept relevant to every organisation. According to Nobel laureate Herbert Simon, an organisation has to make decisions based on several types of value premises (Simon, 1950): (i) the objectives of the organisation – which depends on what value the activity is to be focused on, the groups of people served and the level and quality of service; (ii) the e<sup>fi</sup>cient use of resources; (iii) regulations that abide by the standards of fair play and are aligned with human rights and (iv) personal values of the individual, e.g., salary increments, promotions, etc. These value premises apply not only to private sector organisations but also to public sector organisations, which in the latter case is known as “public value”.

The notion of public value is largely pluralistic. Bozeman (2007) de<sup>fi</sup>nes public value as the “rights, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and prerogatives to which citizens should (and should not) be entitled; the obligations of citizens to society, the state, and one another; the principles on which governments and policies should be based”. A subjective and relational view of public value is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Meynhardt (2009) suggesting that public value is socially constructed based upon the values that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the relationship between individuals and society. In order to incorporate these multiple conceptualisations of public value, we utilise Bryson, Crosby, and Bloomberg (2014) de<sup>fi</sup>nition that suggests public value is based on a multitude of perspectives. Public value, a type of value speci<sup>fi</sup>c to public sector, broadly encompasses the notion of creating what the public values or social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Bryson et al., 2014).

In addition to the notion of public value, we also use the concept of public value frontier that refers to the aim of public sector to ful<sup>fi</sup>l multiple yet competing values simultaneously. For example, economic growth and social welfare, which on its own, do not work well together to produce a singular-focused value such as pro<sup>fi</sup>ts (Pang et al., 2014). Typically, for public sector to extend the public value frontier requires some value to be achieved at the expense of the other (de Graaf & van der Wal, 2010). Broussine (2003) suggests that public leaders often have to be able to initiate concerted action within their organisations and at the same time establish value for a set of stakeholders with multiple and competing interests. For instance, in the case of promoting tourism in a city, public leaders have to cater to the needs of a wide range of stakeholders – citizens, businesses, taxpayers, etc., who have di<sup>f</sup>erent interests. Citizens may be concerned with how the increased number of tourists will a<sup>f</sup>ect their lives. Businesses will be concerned with how best to attract a greater number of tourists to their establishment. These di<sup>f</sup>erences and wide range of stakeholders thus exacerbates the degree of con<sup>fl</sup>ict in creating public value. Overall, the aim of creating public value poses a di<sup>fi</sup>cult task to public organisations because its diverse stakeholders result in pluralistic goals which often con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one another (van der Wal & van Hout, 2009).

## 2.3. Con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals and con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies in the public sector

Public organisations’ goals often su<sup>f</sup>er from “value incompatibility” (de Graaf, Huberts, & Smulders, 2014, p. 4), which refers to situation where the values are competing, i.e., the pursuit of one value will compromise another. Previous research focusing on the public sector suggests evidence of value incompatibility and often <sup>fi</sup>nds situations whereby value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts occur and situations whereby seeking a set of public values compromises other values in the pursuit of these values (Spicer, 2009). Value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts occur when one value directly goes against another value; or that one goal cannot be achieved without the diminution of the other. To illustrate the <sup>fi</sup>rst type of con<sup>fl</sup>ict, government experience con<sup>fl</sup>icts between cost reduction and being responsive to citizens since the very act of being responsive requires investments in resources.

Another example of a value-based con<sup>fl</sup>ict is the situation whereby the government decides between sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing individual privacy for public safety and guarding against terrorist acts. In this situation, the government has to decide on the acceptable method of investigating terrorism within the limits of existing laws so as not to create undue fear of privacy intrusion for the general public. This characteristic is found predominantly in most public organisations’ goals, as compared to private sector organisations where market and pro<sup>fi</sup>t traditionally are the guiding forces (Caudle et al., 1991). On the other hand, public organisations often cannot have solely a consequentialist or utilitarian principle (de Graaf et al., 2014, p. 4) thereby leading to a need to resolve value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

Thacher and Rein (2004, p. 457) describe how having unresolved value con<sup>fl</sup>icts can lead to psychological stress and results in a state of paralysis and that the response of the public sector, i.e., the set of mitigation strategies, can be seen as balancing con<sup>fl</sup>icts or making a tradeo<sup>f</sup>. Scholars in the public administration literature have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several mitigation strategies used by public administrators to resolve value-based con-<sup>fl</sup>icting goals such as <sup>fi</sup>rewalls, bias, casuistry, cycling, hybridisation and incrementalism. While the literature has documented evidence of these mitigation strategies to manage value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in public governance (Steenhuisen & van Eeten, 2008; Tummers & Rocco, 2015), studies have not examined whether IT can facilitate these mitigating strategies. Further, literature does not inform us how IT resolves con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals predominant in public organisations creates public value.

## 2.4. IT-enabled capabilities and IT impact in public organisations

Under severe constraints, the goal of public organisations in moving towards the public-value frontier becomes more challenging and IT may be able to alleviate some of these di<sup>fi</sup>culties by creating capabilities for public organisations to achieve stakeholder value without having to tradeo<sup>f</sup> on either of the con-<sup>fl</sup>icting values. We argue that IT provides public organisations with capabilities, i.e., IT-enabled capabilities, necessary to resolve these con<sup>fl</sup>icting values so as to achieve public value.

This notion of IT-enabled capability traces its root to the extensive literature in organisational capability (Winter, 2000), which refers to the coordination of resources, processes, routines and activities within the organisation to generate productive activities of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. While IT-enabled capabilities have been well explored in the private sector, research on ITenabled capabilities in the public sector or public organisations have been relatively muted, with an exception to Pang et al. (2014). The bulk of the literature pertaining to IT in public organisations centres on e-government. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this set of literature, though valuable, have not been able to address research questions such as ours. The majority of studies in this area focuses on e-government and a review of these studies can be found in Andersen et al. (2010). As a result, calls to move from technology adoption studies to value-based studies have been emphasised (Belanger & Carter, 2012).

Although there are studies employing resourcebased theory to explore the e<sup>f</sup>ects of capability on organisational outcomes in the public sector (e.g., Pablo, Reay, Dewald, & Casebeer, 2007), current studies do not focus on this aspect of IT-enabled capabilities. This gap in the capability literature is also apparent within other <sup>fi</sup>elds in public administration. Studies on the organisational capabilities in public organisations (Andrews, Beynon, & Mcdermott, 2015)<sup>7</sup> is limited and the link between IT capabilities and their strategies in resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts in public organisations has not been studied in depth. To the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to examine how IT helps public organisations achieve capabilities to manage tradeo<sup>f</sup>s and achieve value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals through con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution or mitigation strategies. Thus, it is imperative to study how IT can be successfully assimilated in public organisations by resolving or managing the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s and achieve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals in order to move the public-value frontier forward which is the objective of this paper.

## 3. Proposed analytical framework

To construct a theoretical lens that help make sense of how IT can be used to resolve value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in public organisations, we synthesise the IT-enabled capabilities and IT impacts literature so as to examine IT value in public organisations using a capabilities lens. In terms of IT impacts, this study speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focuses on the mitigating strategies that organisations can deploy which will lead to public value. Pang et al. (2014) propose a theoretical based framework suggesting that IT resource enables public sector organisations to develop their IT capabilities which leads public organisations to move the public-value frontier forward. IT capability is a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to mobilise and deploy ITbased resources in combination or co-present with other resources and capabilities.

Using Pang’s framework, <sup>fi</sup>ve types of IT-enabled capabilities are proposed: (i) public service delivery (ii) resource acquisition (iii) innovation (iv) coproduction and (v) public engagement. See Table 2 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of each capability. Pang argues that IT resources can develop these di<sup>f</sup>erent sets of capabilities in public sector organisations. The impact of IT resources on public organisations’ capabilities can be explicated by the di<sup>f</sup>erent roles IT resources have been theorised to play in organisations. In particular, IT “automate, informate and transform” organisations (Anderson, Banker, & Ravindran, 2006; Chatterjee, Richardson, & Zmud, 2001; Dehning, Richardson, & Zmud, 2003; Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1985). While this theoretical framework is valuable in that it provides a processual view of IT value creation in public organisation, this framework does not inform us about how IT-enabled capabilities resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals to achieve public value. To counter this issue, we employ the rich characterisation of mitigation strategies for resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts proposed by de Graaf et al. (2014) as shown in Table 1. In line with recent work suggesting that capabilities are the basis on which organisations execute strategies (Helfat & Winter, 2011; Leinwand & Mainardi, 2011), we propose that IT-enabled capabilities will allow organisations to execute strategies that help resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. By identifying ITenabled organisational capabilities (Pang et al., 2014), we will be able to tease out the mechanisms through which IT resources resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icts in value-based goals in public organisations (de Graaf et al., 2014). We combine the two approaches to allow for a <sup>fi</sup>nergrained analysis of IT-enabled capabilities in public organisations. In this way, we are able to generate new and deeper insights into how IT-enabled capabilities create public value by better managing the tensions created by con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Political background of research site

Since the end of 2009, several European countries struggled through a multiyear <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis where many were unable to re<sup>fi</sup>nance their government debt or bailout their national banks. This situation was a result of a con<sup>fl</sup>uence of reasons: shrinking economies, high unemployment rate, bursting of domestic housing bubble with over-indebted bank mortgage defaults. This signi<sup>fi</sup>cant political impact resulted in the crafting of massive bailout programs for many countries in the Eurozone. This crisis had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant adverse economic and labour market e<sup>f</sup>ects, with unemployment rates reaching as high as 30%. During these turbulent years, the incumbent governments were faced with the balancing act of implementing austerity measures to contain spending, and to revive the bleeding economies to appease the unhappy populace. This pursuit of public-value creation by improving government services and keeping costs low further accentuates the importance of our study on how IT can mitigate value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s.

While our case site results in con<sup>fl</sup>icting valuebased goals that involve two con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals (e.g., cost and another con<sup>fl</sup>icting goal)<sup>8</sup>, this political situation confers a rare and unique opportunity for us to collect data which represents the most extreme case of such con<sup>fl</sup>icts. The degree of con<sup>fl</sup>ict is likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the resolution process far more than the type of con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

## 4.2. Data collection

e exploit this rare crisis experienced by one European country to collect data to answer our research questions. Qualitative data were collected from interviews conducted at major public organisations in this European country. This target country su<sup>f</sup>ered a major <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis since 2009 and faced severe constraints in the years ensuing. As mentioned earlier, public-value goals may be inherently con<sup>fl</sup>icting, and the recent <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis poses severe challenges and constraints to public organisations which further increased the degree of con<sup>fl</sup>ict. As such, the context of this country presents a unique period where valuebased tradeo<sup>f</sup>s were dominant and severe.

We collected data on 10 public organisations in the country from 2012 to 2015. We were able to gain access to 6 ministries out of all 13 ministries in the country. To provide a comprehensive analysis of the public organisations at di<sup>f</sup>erent levels, we also interviewed informants from 3 provincial governments and a regional health organisation. Details of each of the public organisations we examined are provided in Table 4. For each formal interview, we strived to interview at least one top executive in each organisation (e.g., the Director or Deputy Director) (see Table 5). The size of the ministries we had access to had su<sup>fi</sup>- cient heterogeneity ranging from 2921 to 46669 employees. Given that di<sup>f</sup>erent technological levels could a<sup>f</sup>ect public organisations capabilities, we use the technological budgets and IT investments for each ministry to proxy for this factor and found that they were su<sup>fi</sup>ciently di<sup>f</sup>erent.

Each interview lasted about an hour on average which were later transcribed. In the event where interviewees declined the recording for privacy reasons, extensive notes were taken manually. In total, we collected data based on 11 formal interviews and 6 informal interviews. Formal interviews are lengthy interviews whereas informal interviews are shorter follow up conversations which we used for clari<sup>fi</sup>cation or validation. External documents were collected and reviewed to understand the external stakeholders’ perceptions about the state of the overall public sector as well as focal organisations. Information from the various websites of focal organisations were useful in examining their online services and for obtaining statistics about a particular organisation.

## 4.3. Data analysis

We employ a case study approach (Yin, 2003) as it is deemed most appropriate for our research questions focusing on the processes underlying public-value creation. While the case study approach has limitations such as generalisability, many IS research has applied this method (Kaiser & Buxmann, 2012) especially for questions that are exploratory such as ours. Since our objective is not to establish or test causal relationships, we develop our theory by <sup>fi</sup>rst utilising existing frameworks on IT capabilities and con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution mechanism. This allows us to develop a “theory for explaining” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624) and then inductively analyse the process oriented narratives (Jaccard & Jacoby, 2010; van de Ven & Poole, 2005) to explain how IT creates value in public organisations by resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. Additionally, this approach provides evidence for supporting our proposed conceptual framework and new insights to emerge. To analyse the data, we <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted thematic analysis (Boyatzis, 1998) where we coded the data using con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies (Table 1) and IT capabilities (Table 2). We <sup>fi</sup>rst coded our data based on the <sup>fi</sup>ve proposed capabilities for each agency noting simultaneously the con<sup>fl</sup>icts experienced by the public sector organisations. During this step, we coded the entire dataset of interviews identifying all the relevant data extracts that correspond to the main themes: <sup>fi</sup>ve capabilities and con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies. Two of these mechanisms were found in prior literature and one was derived from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In each mechanism, we describe the nuances of value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s and how IT capabilities help the organisation in executing various mitigating strategies that resolve or manage the tension between value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s. ## 5.1. Strategies for managing tensions between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals

In times of crisis characterised by high uncertainty and constraints, IT resources were able to assist agencies in managing con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals through three di<sup>f</sup>erent con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies: bias, tunnelling and hybridisation. Through IT-enabled capabilities, con<sup>fl</sup>icting public values were resolved via bias, tunnelling or hybridisation, and this, in turn, helped the public organisation achieves positive outcomes. A single public value was attained through bias while public-value frontier, i.e., attaining multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icting values at the same time, was enabled through tunnelling and hybridisation. Bias removed the cost concerns of various public organisations through economies of scale and demand aggregation. Tunnelling allowed transparency and shared identity to develop, which increased collaboration, <sup>fl</sup>exibility and transparency.

As a result, even though the public organisations started with having con<sup>fl</sup>icting values and goals, they underwent all three con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies and were eventually able to move away from siloed data management systems towards a single data centre which facilitates the attainment of the goal of providing citizen-centric services and cost reduction through better resource acquisition capability.

## 5.2. How IT-enabled capabilities mitigate value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s to achieve public value

## 5.2.1. IT-enabled capabilities help resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting values through bias

Public organisations often have to grapple with competing demands. In disentangling the con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution mechanisms of IT, we often <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of IT resources leading to some form of capabilities that allows the public organisation to directly remove the constraint imposed by one of the two public values, very often in the form of cost savings. In other words, IT capabilities result in a mitigation strategy, bias, that allows the organisation to change focus from two goals to one goal and emphasise on a single public value (e.g., providing citizen-centric services). This type of mitigation strategy known as bias has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior studies (de Graaf et al., 2014). From our analysis, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that bias is mainly facilitated as a result of improved public service delivery and resource acquisition capabilities because public service delivery and resource acquisition capabilities of the public organisations were enhanced, and this, in turn, facilitated and circumvented the tradeo<sup>f</sup> such that one of the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s is completely removed. For instance, IT-enabled capabilities such as public service capability can help public organisations provide better services at lower costs. One of the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s is typically cost reduction and this was removed when these capabilities directly resulted in centralisation of information systems across various units and led to demand aggregation which resulted in lowered costs. Similarly, the resource acquisition capabilities as a result of reuse of various IT systems and routines also removed the concerns of cost. In other words, these capabilities facilitated the removal of one con<sup>fl</sup>icting goal through the use of the “bias” strategy, where one of the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s has been eliminated. In most of the public organisations in our dataset, bias was not only facilitated with the uni<sup>fi</sup>cation of the information systems across all the public organisations and the consolidation of data across agencies, but also with an increasing emphasis on the reuse of existing systems and the use of servicebased models.

5.2.2. IT-enabled capabilities facilitate tunnelling – resolving value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s through tunnelling The public sector is a large and complex system where several layers of organisational structures interplay with one another across ministerial, provincial and regional levels. Tensions resulting from the competition for resource allocation decisions and priorities of various projects are especially salient. In many ways, IT resources enabled the public organisations to develop better resource acquisition, co-production and public engagement capabilities. These capabilities then propelled the organisations to deploy a new strategy that resolves con<sup>fl</sup>ict – tunnelling, a strategy which has not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior work. Firewall (de Graaf et al., 2014), a common mitigating strategy in public administration, refers to the situation whereby policy actors establish institutional boundaries to ensure that di<sup>f</sup>erent values are managed by separate institutions. This strategy removes the value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts by assigning each institution to be responsible for a single value. The conventional approach where <sup>fi</sup>rewall is being utilised to compartmentalise values with institutional structures such that the responsibility of each functional unit is focused on one key value.

In our case, we <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that better IT-enabled capabilities to break down <sup>fi</sup>rewalls, a process which we conceptualise and label in this paper as tunnelling. Tunnelling refers to the mitigation technique where the di<sup>f</sup>erent business units in the same entity <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to establish a common goal or objective. This is a strategy which has not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior literature. As opposed to the <sup>fi</sup>rewall strategy which entails walling o<sup>f</sup> values in di<sup>f</sup>erent functional units and forming boundaries to circumscribe each value, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT resolves value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s by <sup>fi</sup>nding commonalities, creating a tunnel through existing <sup>fi</sup>rewalls compartmentalising con<sup>fl</sup>icting values. For instance, the regional health services created interoperability across all the IT systems at di<sup>f</sup>erent layers: clinics, hospitals and regional health services. IT resources help provide a point of entry to another agency with con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals (e.g., ensuring all time services availability and increasing costs to maintain and support infrastructure) using ITenabled capabilities. This strategy manages the tension of value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icts in public organisations by forcing the higher level of con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals present across di<sup>f</sup>erent units to decentralise into a set of <sup>fi</sup>nergrained goals. In some cases, this tunnel helps manage tension arising from con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals by forcing units to <sup>fi</sup>nd commonalities among each other.

To illustrate this, the agencies manage the tension arising from o<sup>f</sup>ering more citizen-centric services versus cost reduction with the aid of IT by a tunnel through the <sup>fi</sup>rewall and connect the functional units, through the use of a centralised database and the virtual invoice system. For instance, the virtual invoice system allowed the public organisations to process a total of 2 million invoices per year. As suggested by the Ministry of Education, Culture and Sports, the ministry bene<sup>fi</sup>tted from an improved transparency of budgets within the agency which led to better resource acquisition and co-production capabilities as a result of the implementation of a virtual invoice system:

We are working on the virtual invoice in order to have an analytical accountancy and so everyone know how much it costs the Ministry, the expenses associated to its work, and then, there is a second level, which is a cost structure by managing centers. We are moving those two variables. Then, in that cost allocation, there is obviously co<sup>fi</sup>nancing. (Ministry of Education, Culture and Sports)

“Tunnels” were created such that each agency is armed with a holistic view of all functional units which helps the ministries exchange data and better understand needs across the entire public sector. Key information such as demands, and budgets of all units are centralised and presented through the system to management. The tradeo<sup>f</sup> resulting from the competing goals of multiple projects proposed by di<sup>f</sup>erent units are ameliorated by demand aggregation and decentralisation of the goals. Instead of dealing directly with con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals across units, key metrics pertinent to every unit are presented via the system, enhancing the resource acquisition capability and creating co-production opportunities for joint projects funded by multiple units. Individual units focus on the amount of resources they can contribute instead of the amount of resources they need. In this way, budgets across units are pooled for common needs across units alleviating the tension between cost savings and initiating new services. Information from IT resources signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved the public organisation’s ability to prioritise services and allocate budgets to the most important need.

In this process, IT resources help create public organisations’ resource acquisition capability and coproduction capability by incorporating con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals from two or more stakeholders (e.g., citizens, departments, ministries and provinces). The actions among di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders with varying and competing interests tend to initiate a concerted action when data from multiple sources are pooled and presented to multiple stakeholders. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT resource facilitates the sharing of resources in times of budget constraints and prevents ine<sup>fi</sup>ciencies resulting from public organisations having to guesstimate the optimal allocation of resources. As a result, the public organisation was better able to prioritise on projects.

Further, employees started to identify themselves with the organisation-wide goals instead of unit-wide goals. The employees were able to associate themselves with the broader goals of the organisation and employees from di<sup>f</sup>erent business units were banding together and <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to work together to contribute to the organisation-wide goal. Interestingly, this led to higher employee satisfaction (e.g., Ministry of Finance, Education and Economy) and better knowledge sharing in other projects. Before the employees focused on and protected their own interests. However, as a result of the con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution process which led to greater clarity in terms of what is truly important to the organisation and promoted the identity to the values of the organisation. This is illustrated in the responses as follows:

IT is one of the criteria besides commitment, the highest added value to the organization that leads us to prioritize the citizen service. (Ministry of Foreign A<sup>f</sup>airs)

. . . obviously there are associated components of the systems . . . that people share and people will identify with the organization. (Ministry of Education, Culture and Sports)

There is technology transfer between regions: collaborative technological platform for each agency to share best practices, technological development and initiatives. (Ministry of Industry, Energy and Tourism)

Our <sup>fi</sup>nding provides an indication of IT reducing the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s caused by con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals by tunnelling or breaking <sup>fi</sup>rewalls, an important deviation from the established concept of <sup>fi</sup>rewall as a mitigating behaviour exhibited by public sector agencies.

## 5.2.3. IT-enabled capabilities resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting values through hybridisation

One of the dominant con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategies enabled by multiple strands of IT-enabled capabilities was hybridisation (de Graaf et al., 2014). Hybridisation refers to the strategy whereby multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icting values are achieved simultaneously. This type of resolution was made possible through the combination of capabilities including public service delivery, innovation, and public engagement. Most public organisations were able to harness the data for other purposes and more innovative projects through hybridisation. As an illustration, the con<sup>fl</sup>icting values between cost savings and innovation were attained via the consolidation of data management systems.

Data were standardised across public organisations by developing technical standards. This standardisation can help alleviate tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between public sector and industry. One of the key concerns from industry was the lack of standardisation across di<sup>f</sup>erent public organisations at various levels (e.g., local, provincial or regional government) which diminishes the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of economies of scale. This resulted in the ALPHA (name disguised) project which provides a set of reusable data and information for the industry to exploit economies of scale and simultaneously promotes transparency.

Another underlying capability that drives this strategy is the increased innovative capability of public organisation that stems from the rising expectations from external stakeholders. From our data, we <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of IT resources driving innovative capability when the public organisations were able to sense the changing and emerging needs of the public and provide more innovative solutions as a result. This innovative capability within public organisations results in the hybridisation of con<sup>fl</sup>icting values.

A good example that illustrates such capability is that of an online service called the “Digital Diplomacy” project, initiated by the Ministry of Foreign A<sup>f</sup>airs. This project aims to “contribute towards increasing its eficacy and transparency and facilitate connection and dialogue with citizens, while at the same time harness opportunities that arise from new technologies for promoting the ministry’s interests abroad”. Its goal is to modernise equipment and communication, and to adapt the provided services to the demands of a modern society. To achieve this goal, the Ministry has developed an extensive network of pro-<sup>fi</sup>les in social networks, including a webpage called “social network atlas” to facilitate interaction with the general public. This use of social networks is a perfect example of engagement capability to promote interaction with citizens.

A culture of reuse permeated across the public agencies which enabled and promoted technical standards across the entire government. Standardisation of data allowed cross-agency exchanges and promoted reuse and transparency. Public engagement capability also drives hybridisation strategy removing the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between cost savings and innovative services to cater to the public. As the public organisations engaged with the public through publishing data, di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders were also allowed to partake in the ecosystem. Third-party developers started to harness data published by these public sector agencies for creating innovative apps. For instance, the Ministry of Industry, Energy and Tourism was able to attract third-party developers with open data initiatives. Third party developed apps improve tourists’ satisfaction by complementing tourists’ experience and o<sup>f</sup>er interactive 3D virtual spaces simulating the tourist destinations. Users of these apps were able to examine every corner of these tourist destinations in very high resolution and access the destinations that are not open to the public. These apps were then adapted for other context such as museums. Users were able to use holographic scans and 3D techniques to generate high-resolution images of the most important masterpieces in the museum.

In the above cases, tradeo<sup>f</sup>s such as cost reduction hybridisation removed tradeo<sup>f</sup>s by resolving multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icts simultaneously, enabling both cost reduction and innovation, as well as cost reduction and better engagement. As one respondent suggests, IT was a catalyst which produced many changes and enabled the innovation capability across the public sector:

Speaking of information systems involves talking about evolution, it’s part of our daily challenge for new projects representing a growth, continued evolution and adaptation of our organization according to new requirements. (Provincial government)

In the short to medium term, we will acquire new powers of information systems . . . with which we will face new integrations which in turn allow us to o<sup>f</sup>er new features to the citizen. (Provincial government)

Overall these changes led to the ability of public organisations in achieving multiple con<sup>fl</sup>icting values simultaneously. For example, the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between costs and the development of innovative citizencentric features increased through innovation, public service delivery and public engagement capabilities. As suggested by respondents below:

The relationship of the government with the citizen has to move in this direction and this relationship will not strengthen without technology. (Ministry of Agriculture)

The current economic situation forces us to adapt and <sup>fi</sup>nd new procedures that allow us to do more for less and we are working along that line, analyzing new business models that are being implemented in ICT. (Provincial government)

## 6. Discussion

In this study, our aim is to provide an account of how IT resources can strengthen organisational capabilities in public organisations of a European country to resolve tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in order to achieve public value. To do this, we adopted a con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based lens. Additionally, we elucidate how IT enables the public organisation to achieve public value by resolving or managing the tensions between value-based con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals. We exploit a unique environment in the country that we study, one that su<sup>f</sup>ered from a <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis, so as to tease out <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained mechanisms of how IT-enabled capabilities might mitigate tradeo<sup>f</sup>s arising from con<sup>fl</sup>icting valuebased goals in public organisations. As the country’s economy was only starting to improve after a <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis, the political environment was still subject to substantial turbulence and <sup>fi</sup>nancial constraints when this study was conducted. Most projects in every public organisation were confronted with the con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals between citizen-centric value and cost savings. While cost is one of the many con<sup>fl</sup>icting values that confront public organisations, the degree of con<sup>fl</sup>ict presented in this country allows us to tease out the con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution mechanisms during which there is maximum level of con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

We drew on con<sup>fl</sup>icting values perspective to explain how IT resources help public organisations generate value through IT-enabled capabilities. Previous work (Pang et al., 2014) delineates several mechanisms of IT-enabled capabilities in public organisations and ignores how tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between valuebased con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals are resolved through ITenabled capabilities in public organisations. Our work proposed a framework and delves deeper into the detailed elements required for the mechanisms to occur using a con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goal perspective.

As a result, this study has resulted in new insights into how con<sup>fl</sup>icting public values are resolved through IT-enabled capabilities in public organisations. Our primary <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented in Tables 6 and 7 and Figure 2.

Figure 2 provides an overview of how IT creates public value by managing the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s resulting from con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. Through IT-enabled capabilities, these resolution strategies led to the creation of public value. From our analysis, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the three mitigat ing strategies to resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting values in public organisations. Overall, we found evidence of two established strategies – (i) bias and (ii) hybridisation (de Graaf et al., 2014) from the public administration literature whereby IT resources enable di<sup>f</sup>erent capabilities – public service delivery, resource acquisition, public sector innovation, co-production and public engagement capabilities, that facilitate the public organisations to resolve or better manage the tensions between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. We found evidence of these strategies across the range of public sector organisations in our sample which varied in terms of size. While every attempt was made to ensure that we had a wide range of public sector organisations, there may be other strategies which are present in other agencies not available in our dataset. However, we are analysing a cross-section of the dataset which will not allow us to capture other strategies which is timedependent (e.g., cycling). Thus, we do not rule out the possibility of other strategies. Additionally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a new and distinct con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution strategy – tunnelling, which has not been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior literature. Tunnelling refers to the strategy where di<sup>f</sup>erent public organisations across the public sector <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to establish a common goal or objective. This strategy breaks down existing boundaries across public organisations, creating a tunnel across organisations by establishing a common goal across all organisations such that they will cooperate with one another. This new strategy highlights the possibilities that IT-enabled capabilities can generate in public organisations especially in situations with tradeo<sup>f</sup>s that are abound in the public sector.

IT-enabled capabilities facilitated the resolution of tradeo<sup>f</sup>s because these capabilities allowed for the deployment of various strategies. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that public service delivery capability, resource acquisition, facilitates bias reducing the need of public organisations to focus on cost savings through the centralisation of systems and demand aggregation which led to economies of scale. Resource acquisition, co-production and public engagement capability also facilitated the strategy of tunnelling as public organisations were able to <sup>fi</sup>nd common goals and collaborated with one another to resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals. Finally, public service delivery capability, innovation and public engagement capability could also lead to hybridisation which again removes the need for con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals.

## 7. Conclusion

The public sector has evolved over the years from a traditional public administration characterised by a bureaucratic, top-down structure with lack of transparency, to an approach which is market driven, and <sup>fi</sup>nally to one which is decentralised, transparent and citizen centric. Despite this evolution, public organisations are still confronted with the situation of having to resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals salient in public administration. From our study, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that IT provides public organisations with the capability to respond directly to the perennial challenge of tradeo<sup>f</sup>s from con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. Using a capabilities framework, we delineate the mechanisms by which IT resolves or better manage tensions between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals to achieve ITenabled capabilities. In particular, IT resolves or reduces the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between value-based goals through mitigation strategies to achieve public value.

Our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to contribute to extant literature by examining how IT helps to mitigate these tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in public organisations. Prior studies have noted that con<sup>fl</sup>icting values are especially predominant in the public sector (Pang et al., 2014) and can be attained through various IT-enabled capabilities but neglect to identify the strategies by which IT can be used to help resolve these con<sup>fl</sup>icts. In contrast with prior work, our study documents how three strategies actively produce resolutions by mobilising IT. As a whole, our study demonstrates the relevancy of IT to resolve tradeo<sup>f</sup>s arising from con<sup>fl</sup>icting values in public organisations. Our results indicate the need for future research to systematically consider IT to resolve tradeo<sup>f</sup>s in order to attain public value. Future studies could leverage on these concepts to maximise the deployment of IT resources in public organisations.

Our paper contributes to multiple strands of the IS and public administration literature. First, our paper is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to embark on a study that juxtaposes IT value creation with con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution, i.e., how IT resolves or at least reduce the tensions between con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals to generate value. Previous scholars in IS business value have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the need for more theory building (Schryen, 2013) and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution lens may be useful in extending studies in IS business value. We contribute to a nascent literature on examining con<sup>fl</sup>icts in public service delivery and the implications of using IT for public organisations. The theoretical literature on value con<sup>fl</sup>icts in the public administration literature has long emphasised the importance of con<sup>fl</sup>icting public values a<sup>fl</sup>icting government and their policy implementations. However, most of these studies have focused on the mitigating strategies of public sector organisations in dealing with con<sup>fl</sup>icting values. None of these studies examine the e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT in these scenarios. Our study clari<sup>fi</sup>es the ways IT resolves value con-<sup>fl</sup>icts and, in the process, help public organisations attain the value of IT. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a repertoire of strategies by which public organisations can use IT to mitigate multiple and con<sup>fl</sup>icting values. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we demonstrate that IT can help public organisations achieve con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals by e<sup>f</sup>ectively reducing the tension that public organisations experience and simultaneously provide more and better services. Further, while prior studies address in part questions related to the creation of public value, few studies focus on extending the public-value frontier like our study. In particular, our study focus on understanding how this is achieved through the deployment of IT.

Second, it adds to a growing number of studies which evaluates IT in public organisations. While most studies focus on the adoption and use of e-government, in contrast, we observe the impact of IT on creating public value within public organisations. Third, we contribute to the IS literature which seeks to obtain a deeper understanding on the generative processes of IT value in the context of public organisations, which are reported to have starkly di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics from private sector. Ours is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst few studies which combine the IT capabilities framework with con<sup>fl</sup>icting public value lens to provide evidence on how IT helps public organisations cope with con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals and attain public value and performance. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, rather than just focusing on the mechanisms whereby IT lead to public organisations’ performance, we identify the ways by which IT helps resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icts in public value by examining these mechanisms of IT value creation in a highly constrained environment, i.e., a country whose public sector organisations experienced con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals, following a crisis.

We acknowledge the limitation of this study in that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are derived from one country and thus cannot be generalised to all settings. However, we are currently analysing data from multiple companies in the private sector using the same approach described in this study. We also do not claim to have an exhaustive list of strategies whereby IT can help manage the tensions arising from con<sup>fl</sup>icting valuebased goals. Rather, we provide the <sup>fi</sup>rst steps for future research to build upon the con<sup>fl</sup>icting valuebased lens that we have introduced in this paper. Future research can extend this work by studying other countries and strategies by which IT helps public organisations manage tradeo<sup>f</sup>s from con<sup>fl</sup>icting value-based goals. Nevertheless, our proposed framework should generate further interest and application in future studies.

This study provides meaningful implications for elected o<sup>fi</sup>cials and managers in the public sector. Budget shortfalls persist in many countries, and IT budgets have become an easy target for cuts. For instance, the National Association of State Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cers (NASCIO) in the USA revealed that during recent years one of the most critical issues facing its members is managing budget reduction; including de<sup>fi</sup>ning strategies for savings and reducing or avoiding costs (NASCIO, 2017, 2018). Our research results show how public organisations can resolve con-<sup>fl</sup>icting goals, such as budget reduction, via IT-enabled capabilities. From a practitioner point of view, the examples of the application of IT-enabled capabilities discussed in this article could be used as blueprints by IT managers of public organisations in order to de<sup>fi</sup>ne strategies to mitigate value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s within their organisation. Further, policy makers and IT managers in public organisations can utilise the di<sup>f</sup>erent strategies (bias, tunnelling and hybridisation) found through this study and leverage IT resources to develop capabilities that enables them to circumvent value-based tradeo<sup>f</sup>s more e<sup>f</sup>ectively. Public organisations can implement strategies as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this paper depending on the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s they are confronted with. They are also able to understand how these strategies can be deployed with the help of di<sup>f</sup>erent IT resources.
