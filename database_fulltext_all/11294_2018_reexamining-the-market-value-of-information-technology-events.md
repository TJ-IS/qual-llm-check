# Reexamining the Market Value of Information Technology Events

Anitesh Barua,<sup>a</sup> Deepa Mani<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712; <sup>b</sup> Indian School of Business, Hyderabad 500032, India Contact: aniteshb@gmail.com (AB); deepa\_mani@isb.edu, http://orcid.org/0000-0002-2458-6961 (DM)

Received: November 14, 2012 Revised: November 19, 2013; April 17, 2015; May 1, 2016 Accepted: May 28, 2016 Published Online in Articles in Advance: February 9, 2018

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0718

Abstract. The widespread use of announcement period returns to assess the financial impact of information technology (IT) events implicitly assumes that the market can completely price IT investments during the announcement period. However, some studies in strategy and information systems have suggested that long-term abnormal returns may be a more appropriate measure of the value of IT events. To reconcile these streams of research, we develop and test an exploratory framework involving the maturity and scope of an IT event to assess the suitability of short- versus long-term abnormal returns. We conceptualize event maturity in terms of difusion of the technology or phenomenon, and scope as the extent of complementary organizational changes that need to be implemented and managed. We posit that because of lack of widespread knowledge of best practices and cases of success and failure in a period of low technological maturity, the market may find it dificult to price such an event completely during the announcement period. Similarly, the challenge of acquiring and interpreting information on a firm’s capability to manage wide scope of change may be an impediment to pricing high-scope events. We test our framework using a sample of 642 large outsourcing contracts and 1,700 electronic commerce initiatives. We empirically demonstrate that announcement period returns are indeed a complete measure of event value for cases characterized by high maturity and low scope; however, long-term abnormal returns are realized for events involving low maturity and/or high scope, which questions the validity of announcement period returns. Our results are robust to alternate model specifications. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for theory and practice, and directions for future research.

## 1. Introduction

It has been of considerable academic interest to assess whether information technology (IT) initiatives have a significant impact on the market value of firms. A rich stream of research on firm-level IT impacts has deployed market measures of performance based on capital market theories (for summaries, see Im et al. 2001, Melville et al. 2004, Mani et al. 2013). Application areas include the financial value of innovative IT (Dos Santos et al. 1993), enterprise resource planning (ERP) (Ranganathan and Brown 2006), and e-commerce (Subramani and Walden 2001, Agrawal et al. 2006). The event study approach deployed in these studies assumes that during the announcement period, the market accurately prices all publicly available information and, in turn, beliefs regarding future cash flows from the IT event. However, emergent research in information systems (IS) (e.g., Mani et al. 2013) posits that capital markets may not price an IT event completely during the announcement period, as manifest in significant longterm abnormal returns following the event.<sup>1</sup> To reconcile these seemingly conflicting findings, we present an exploratory framework to assess the suitability of short versus long time horizons in valuing IT events.

Our framework incorporates two key constructs— the maturity and organizational scope of an IT event. Technology maturity is conceptualized in the IS literature in terms of difusion of the technology (Gurbaxani 1990) and indicates the extent to which the benefits of the technology and their organizational drivers are well understood and documented (Weiss and Dale 1998). However, following prior research in strategy (e.g., Adner 2002, Lambkin and Day 1989, Adner and Levinthal 2001), we conceptualize and operationalize maturity from both demand and supply perspectives. We argue that it may be dificult for capital markets to price immature IT events characterized by insuficient instances of prior adoption. The absence of case studies of success and failure and the scarcity of information on best practices and critical success factors in periods of low technological maturity may render it challenging for the market to fully price the IT event during the announcement period.

The uncertainty inherent in an IT event may reduce with the maturity or adoption of the technology over time. However, the realization of value from the IT event may also be contingent on a range of organizational complements such as changes to organizational structures, business processes, and decision rights (Barua et al. 1996; Brynjolfsson et al. 1996, 2002). The extent of complementary changes required to create value determines the scope of the IT event. For example, ERP implementations require significant changes in business processes, information flows, decision rights, incentives, and training outlays; success hinges on the managerial ability to implement and sustain these organizational changes (Jarrar et al. 2000). Thus, large-scale ERP investments may be considered high-scope events. We argue that in the short run, markets may find it dificult to price IT investments characterized by large scope, leading to significant longterm abnormal returns following the event. Indeed, the ability of firms to manage the complexity of highscope events is a source of competitive value, and hence may not be disclosed explicitly. Furthermore, research in IS and strategy (e.g., Clemons and Row 1991, Ray et al. 2004) finds that capabilities required to manage IT investments are intangible, heterogeneously distributed, and imperfectly mobile across firms. As a result, even if firms were to disclose information about organizational complements to their IT investments, markets may still find it costly and challenging to price such information. For these reasons, we posit that announcement period returns are a complete measure of the value of the IT event only when the underlying technology is mature, and when the event involves low scope. We suggest that announcement period returns should be used with caution for a large class of IT decisions that may be mispriced by the market in the short run, and that a longer time horizon may be required to capture the complete value of such decisions.

We illustrate the application of our maturity–scope framework using two diferent IT events: (i) the organizational decision to outsource IT and IT-enabled business functions and (ii) the decision to invest in online or e-commerce. Both outsourcing and e-commerce have a relatively long history; the early years were marked by a few pioneers such as Kodak outsourcing its IT to IBM in 1989, and Amazon.com and eBay.com ushering in new ways of doing business in 1994 and 1995, respectively. However, both outsourcing and ecommerce gained adoption rapidly, and are viewed today as fundamental to modern businesses. These IT events also involve significant variance in scope. Outsourcing events range from the transfer of ownership of basic infrastructure including networks and desktops for cost savings to highly sophisticated equity sharing arrangements involving strategic business processes. Similarly, e-commerce investments can vary between the development of stand-alone, transactional websites to holistic initiatives involving processes and systems that are seamlessly integrated with the rest of a firm’s operations.

Our outsourcing data comprise 642 large initiatives implemented between 1996 and 2010. We analyze adoption rates as well as growth in the number of vendors for both IT outsourcing and IT-enabled business process outsourcing (BPO) over time and find that both demand and supply sides attain critical mass, and hence maturity, in the year 2001. We also draw on prior research in outsourcing to use the type of contract as a proxy for the scope of the outsourcing initiative.<sup>2</sup> We hypothesize and find support for the presence of long-term abnormal returns for outsourcing contracts implemented in periods of low outsourcing maturity irrespective of contract type. Furthermore, regardless of maturity, variable price outsourcing contracts (involving high scope) are associated with long-term abnormal returns. Announcement period returns are a complete measure of outsourcing value only for fixed price contracts (associated with low scope) implemented in periods of high outsourcing maturity. Our e-commerce data involve 1,700 publicly announced investments between 2000 and 2008. As with IT outsourcing, these investments often involve significant uncertainties, originating from both maturity and scope, in both costs and benefits that are dificult for the market to predict ex ante. In this case, following extant literature (e.g., Agrawal et al. 2006), we analyze the content of the announcement to assess the task characteristics and derive the scope of the e-commerce event. We find that e-commerce announcements made in a period of low maturity or those with high scope are associated with long-term abnormal returns. Announcement period returns are appropriate only for low-scope events announced in periods of high maturity. Thus, the results for two diferent classes of IT events are consistent with predictions from our theo retical framework.

We check the robustness of our results to alternate specifications (e.g., the Ohlson 1995 model). Long-term abnormal returns may be an outcome of either information asymmetry between the firm and the market or compensation for a technology-specific risk factor;<sup>3</sup> however, such analyses are outside the scope of our exploratory study. A large body of prior research on the market value of IT has almost exclusively focused on announcement period returns to estimate the market value of IT investments, while a few studies in strategy and management and a nascent literature in IS have pointed out the pitfalls of ignoring long-term returns. Our study adds to the literature through a parsimonious framework involving maturity and scope that determines the relevant time frame for market models of IT value. The main contribution of our framework is that it provides insights into not just whether but also when the use of announcement period returns versus long-term returns is appropriate to assess the value of an IT event. While we analyze outsourcing and ecommerce decisions in this research, our framework can also be extended to other domains of management research.

The balance of this article is organized as follows. We introduce our framework in Section 2 and illustrate its applications to outsourcing and e-commerce initiatives in Section 3. We present the results of our empirical analyses in Section 4, discuss their implications for research and practice in Section 5, and provide concluding remarks in Section 6.

## 2. A Framework to Assess the Suitability of Short- and Long-Term Market Returns 2.1. Event Maturity

Maturity and scope are two distinct attributes of an IT event in our framework that determine the relevance of short-term versus long term market returns to the event. Technology maturity is conceptualized in the IS literature from the demand-side perspective in terms of levels of adoption or difusion of the technology (e.g., Gurbaxani 1990). This demand-side approach has also been used in the strategy literature to understand the evolution of technologies. For example, Adner (2002) is representative of studies that rationalize a demand-side S-curve (which is theorized to be driven by consumers’ valuation of performance improvements over the life cycle) to assess technological maturity. However, Lambkin and Day (1989) note that these difusion models do not consider supplyside influences on market size and rate of difusion. They argue that “the rate at which a new market develops is . . . influenced strongly by the number and types of suppliers that enter the market” (p. 5). They use the foundation of population ecology to develop a model of supply-side growth based on the logistic curve. Similar to the studies focusing on the demand for the technology, those emphasizing the supply-side perspective also rely on the logistic curve. There appears to be some consensus (e.g., Adner and Levinthal 2001) that both demand- and supply-side perspectives should be considered in tandem in modeling the maturity phenomenon, which is the approach we adopt in conceptualizing and operationalizing the maturity construct.

When a technology is relatively new or immature with low levels of adoption, there is considerable uncertainty that investors face in valuing its features, benefits, standards, and implementation procedures. Studies of stock market learning (e.g., Zajac and Westphal 2004) argue that the market’s assessment of a firm’s ability to create and sustain economic rents from a strategic event derives from its processing of publicly available information on prior instances of the event, including postevent changes in financial performance and resources that enable successful assimilation and exploitation of the event. Kishore et al. (2004) argue that knowledge about technologies and firm-specific applications, which is tacit in immature phases, disseminates over time through employee turnover, professional communities, and consulting firms, leading to the emergence of best practices and sharing of cases of success and failure. As a result, the greater the number of prior instantiations of the event, the lower the market’s marginal cost of pricing a related event. Prior instantiations help with markets’ “learning to learn” (Cohen and Levinthal 1990) and also reduce the level of information processing required to price an event. Thus, the market can assess the potential of a technology more easily with increasing maturity. These observations lead to Hypothesis 1:

Hypothesis 1. Financial markets are slow to price IT events with low technological maturity. Therefore, firms announcing IT events with low technological maturity will realize significant long-term abnormal returns.

## 2.2. Event Scope

While the above theoretical arguments suggest that for events involving high technological maturity, there may be suficient information on critical success factors available to capital markets, we posit that information related to the scope of the IT event is also important for accurate pricing. We define event scope as the extent of changes complementary to the technology, including redesign of business processes, decision rights, incentives, and strategies required to extract value from the underlying IT. Prior work in IS attests to the competitive value of technology investments (Clemons and Row 1991), which are complex and uncertain in terms of outcomes. For instance, Dewan et al. (2007) find that technology investments increase earnings volatility three times more than other capital expenditures. In the case of these complex investments with extensive scope, competitive value is contingent on the ability of the investing firm to efect organizational changes to processes, structures, and policies that complement the technology. These complementary changes are critical to the success of both internal IT investments as well as interorganizational IT investments such as the adoption of a new supply chain system. Indeed, these complements, if improperly managed, lead to major organizational turmoil, even “jeopardizing the core operations of the implementing organization” (Hong and Kim 2002, p. 25).

We note that this driver of competitive value yields technology investments their partial excludability and restricts their competitive value; that is, it is dificult for the firm making the technology investment to prevent others from making use of its knowledge on drivers of success of the investment. Such knowledge is intangible and is often embodied in people in the organization. The imperfect definition and enforcement of property rights related to technology investments generate spillovers or the feasibility to acquire information about the technology with paying the market price for that information (Grossman and Helpman 1991). Such partial excludability and spillovers limit incentives for disclosure of management of technology investments. Indeed, while disclosure may help the market price the event more accurately and even reduce the firm’s cost of capital, such benefits may bear the proprietary cost of competitive losses that may not be justified by an improvement in investor forecasts (Verrecchia 2001). Thus, while information on the scope and complexity of the investment may be available to the market, there may be a scarcity of public information on the management of high scope, making it challenging for markets to assess the likelihood of the success of the investment.

Furthermore, the capabilities complementary to technology investments are largely “intangible”—tacit, contingent on accumulated firm capabilities that are dificult to describe, not formulaic or replicable across firms, and involving ambiguous action–outcome linkages (Ray et al. 2004, Conner and Prahalad 1996). Research in finance (Daniel and Titman 2006, Hirshleifer et al. 2012) relates the distinction between tangible and intangible information to market mispricing. Tangible information refers to explicit measures of past performance such as sales or cash flows that can be observed directly from the firm’s financial statements (and is therefore easy to process). On the other hand, intangible information is the orthogonal component of information about growth opportunities and future performance, which is unrelated to past accounting performance (and is therefore relatively dificult to process; Daniel and Titman 2006). Hirshleifer et al. (2012) point to the high costs of processing intangible information that in turn impedes accurate pricing. Empirical research suggests this dificulty persists across various classes of investors, including investment analysts and domain experts. For these reasons, we argue that markets may not incorporate information on highscope IT events in market prices of the focal firm. Thus, only in the case of high maturity and low scope do we expect announcement period returns to be a complete and accurate measure of event value. These observations lead to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2A. During periods of high maturity, financial markets will be slow to incorporate information on events with high scope. Thus, firms announcing IT events with high scope will realize significant long-term abnormal returns.

Hypothesis 2B. During periods of high maturity, financial markets will be accurate in pricing low-scope events;

that is, firms announcing IT events with low scope in periods of high maturity will not realize significant long-term abnormal returns.

Based on the above discussions, we note that low maturity of the underlying technology and high scope of the IT event pose two distinct challenges. While low maturity makes it challenging for the market to know the intrinsic potential of an unproven technology, high scope implies that the market needs intangible information on the firm’s capability to deal with high scope and ensuing complexity. Together, as shown in Table 1, maturity and scope of the IT event determine the ex ante uncertainty in future cash flows and, in turn, the appropriateness of market models and short-term market returns.

## 3. Application of the Framework to IT Outsourcing and E-Commerce

In this section, we illustrate the application of our framework to the contexts of (i) outsourcing of technology and business functions and (ii) e-commerce events. Specifically, we analyze the appropriateness of shortversus long-term market reactions as a measure of market value of these events.

## 3.1. IT and IT-Enabled Services Outsourcing

We begin with an analysis of returns to outsourcing across periods of high and low maturity and events of low and high scope. Large outsourcing decisions are financially material events, information about which is available in the public domain. The outsourcing literature documents that large-scale outsourcing impacts key performance measures (e.g., Gottfredson et al. 2005, Bin et al. 2006) that influence investors’ and debtors’ decisions, and carries significant operational and strategic risk including loss of innovation (Earl 1996, Lonsdale 1999, Andreas and Trott 2006, Mani et al. 2010). Given the financially material nature of outsourcing contracts, the latter are publicly disclosed by the outsourcing firm in the form of media releases and/or U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) filings. For example, the SEC filing (shown in the online appendix) for the outsourcing contract between General Motors (client) and Electronic Data Systems (EDS) (vendor) specifies that EDS was entrusted with a gamut of services (details provided in the online appendix), including (i) infrastructure (mainframe, supercomputing, midrange and distributed computing hardware and software, voice, data and video communications services and networks, end-user hardware, and replacement), (ii) IT services (participating in planning infrastructure, development, implementation and maintenance, infrastructure integration, and monitoring), (iii) applications software for business planning, financial, human resource management, sales, service marketing, engineering, purchasing, product control and logistics, production/manufacturing, materials management, and legal, (iv) help desk support, (v) timesharing services, (vi) data management, and (vii) disaster recovery and business continuity. It is evident that the scope of this deal is large and complex, and the filing identifies the contract as variable price, as would be predicted by contract choice theory.

There is also considerable interest in the media about large-scale outsourcing initiatives. For example, a \$2.2 billion outsourcing deal signed between ABN Amro and IBM was widely covered in diverse media. The description of the deal included details on the large number of vendors involved as well as the high complexity of the initiative spanning a gamut of services from infrastructure to business process outsourcing. Furthermore, with respect to maturity of the technology, the coverage of the ABN Amro deal in Fortune (Clancy 2014) stated, “this sort of installation is the one preferred by many large companies because it provides more flexibility and security, while building on previous technology investments. Research firm Gartner figures that almost 50% of the biggest U.S. companies will run hybrid cloud services by 2017.” The above examples demonstrate the ability of the market to acquire information on and infer the maturity and scope of the outsourcing contract from public information sources at the time of its implementation.<sup>4</sup>

Our conceptualization of maturity begins with the observation that the adoption of outsourcing may be driven in part by supply-side externalities (whereby adopters can choose from more service providers over time) as well as competitive pressures, which make outsourcing a strategic necessity (Gottfredson et al. 2005). Theories of institutional isomorphism posit that as the eficiency benefits of an organizational practice become clearer, the practice gains legitimacy over time. While early adopters are motivated by eficiency gains, late adopters are driven by ensuing legitimacy gains. Ang and Cummings (1997) demonstrate that cost savings from outsourcing, paucity of slack resources, technological uncertainty, and risk of obsolescence play moderating roles and strengthen the relationship between institutional isomorphism and the firm’s decision to outsource. All of these factors help create a point of “critical mass” of outsourcers beyond the “innovators” and “early adopters.” This point, at which outsourcing becomes self-sustaining, is identified as the point of maturity of outsourcing. Our assumption is that beyond this point, there are enough instances of outsourcing adoption to inform the market regarding the potential value and other parameters of outsourcing events. We conceptualize maturity in terms of three parameters of the outsourcing phenomenon representing both the demand and supply of outsourcing services. Specifically, maturity of demand for outsourcing is captured by the number of clients as well as the number of unique contracts in our data. The number of unique vendors represents maturity of the supply of outsourcing services. For each of the three Rogers (1962) curves, we identify the first period of significant discontinuity as the point of maturity of outsourcing.

We conceptualize scope of the outsourcing initiative in terms of the contractual structure that is used to govern the outsourcing relationship. The two dominant types of outsourcing contracts are fixed and variable price contracts. Research in outsourcing (e.g., Bajari and Tadelis 2001, Mani et al. 2012) notes that firms selfselect one of these contracts to minimize the trade-of between ex ante provision of incentives and ex post renegotiation costs. As a result, complex tasks that are responsive to the firm’s business environment require lower levels of completeness in task specification, are associated with a higher probability that ex post adaptations are needed, and are more likely to be governed by variable price contracts: “cost-plus contracts are preferred to fixed-price contracts when a project is more complex” (Bajari and Tadelis 2001, p. 387). Thus, the contract is a proxy for the complexity and volatility of the outsourced task—fixed price contracts are indicative of a relatively stable and simple task, while variable price contracts indicate more volatile and complex outsourced tasks. Variable price contracts typically involve greater investments in coordination and cooperation. Note that, since the underlying task is not completely contractually described, its ownership and control cannot be transferred to the provider through an arms-length contract and is shared by participant firms. Shared ownership is manifest in interdependencies between the client and the vendor(s) such as shared resources, task sequencing and scheduling, performance monitoring, and other work design–related constraints (Gulati and Singh 1998). Investments in coordination help the client and vendor manage interdependencies and integrate efort by sharing process knowledge on an ongoing basis to sustain shared meanings and understanding of the outsourced task and its execution. Given that variable price contracts require significantly greater levels of complementary investments in mechanisms and applications for information and knowledge sharing, joint action, monitoring, continuity, and structural changes to realize maximum value from outsourcing, we use such pricing as an indicator of high scope.

3.1.1. Market Valuation of Outsourcing Events. To support Hypothesis 1 in the context of outsourcing, we note that in the phase of low outsourcing maturity, there is considerable uncertainty about the potential benefits of outsourcing, hidden costs, risks, and pitfalls, especially in the absence of best practices to deal with the externalization of IT and IT-enabled services. Past success with unproven technologies or management practices may not be indicative of the ability to handle immature technologies in an era of disruptive innovations. It is therefore costly for the market to acquire, process, and predict a firm’s chances of success with its outsourcing initiative. Changes in various performance measures (e.g., operational eficiency) following the implementation of the outsourcing contract may provide cues (either positive or negative) to the market regarding the impact of the initiative. Consequently, the market may learn to associate such signals with the benefits of outsourcing and reprice the event over time, resulting in long-term abnormal returns.

For outsourcing deals inked during a period of high maturity, the market has already witnessed many instances of outsourcing, and is therefore generally informed about potential costs, benefits, and risks of such events. However, if the scope of the outsourcing initiative is high, the market still needs to know whether the firm has the capability to manage such scope and exploit the event by transforming the organization.<sup>5</sup> Specifically, complex initiatives may involve significant efort in redesigning the outsourced task(s) for modularity, in an attempt to minimize interdependencies between the vendor and diferent stakeholders in the client organization. Furthermore, as noted earlier, a client firm may have to invest significantly in cooperation and coordination mechanisms such as joint action (Mani et al. 2012, Puranam and Gulati 2005), colocation of resources (Dyer and Nobeoka 2000, Helper et al. 2000), and relationship management to build trust and common ground with the vendor. Therefore, variable price contracts require cooperation and coordination mechanisms that are idiosyncratic to the focal client and involve significant changes in structures and processes. However, given the competitive value of investments made by the client in cooperation, coordination, process controls, or overall governance to deal with the complexity of the outsourcing deal, there is little information available in the public domain that helps the market gauge their nature. This lack of information may result in mispricing of the outsourcing event in the short run (Hypothesis 2A).

When outsourcing takes place in a period of high maturity with low scope, there is minimal uncertainty in the market regarding the true potential of the technology and the ability of the firm to manage the deal. Owing to high maturity, best practices in outsourcing management are known, and complementary organizational changes required to extract value from externalization may be replicable across firms due to limited scope. For example, compare the outsourcing of payroll processing in 2006 to a more complex deal involving contact centers. By this time, basic business process outsourcing such as payroll had been around for well over a decade and was considered a well-understood phenomenon. Furthermore, externalization of payroll processing involves changes limited to a relatively small set of accounting processes. By contrast, outsourcing a contact center involves linkages to many functions including sales, marketing, and customer service, as well as operations and research and development (R&D). Thus, although contact center outsourcing is almost as mature as payroll processing, the scope of the latter is significantly larger, resulting in higher levels of uncertainty for the market in assessing its value. Beasley et al. (2009) found that outsourcing deals with an operational focus announced after 1999 experienced significant short-term returns. Such deals are likely to have low scope and be governed by fixed price contracts. Furthermore, the significance of returns for events announced after 1999 suggests that increasing maturity helps the market discern the potential of outsourcing. These observations provide support for Hypothesis 2B.

## 3.2. The Case of E-Commerce Announcements

As a second application of our maturity–scope framework, we examine market returns to investments in e-commerce between 2000 and 2008. Fueled by the large-scale adoption of the World Wide Web, investments in e-commerce witnessed a dramatic growth in the late 1990s and have grown to be a pervasive force reshaping every aspect of a firm’s operations and strategy in virtually every industry. There has also been significant variance in the outcomes of e-commerce initiatives. While many new online firms (e.g., Amazon.com, eBay, Alibaba.com, and Priceline .com) and brick-and-mortar companies (e.g., Dell Inc., Cisco Systems, P&G, and Cemex) achieved significant success with e-commerce, numerous initiatives from both traditional and online firms experienced failures or disappointing outcomes. Furthermore, e-commerce undertakings vary widely from stand-alone transaction websites to the development of comprehensive functionality integrated with internal systems and processes, which can be a highly complex undertaking, especially for large organizations. As in the case of outsourcing, we determine the maturity of e-commerce using the Rogers (1962) difusion curve analysis.

We draw on prior research (Agrawal et al. 2006, Subramani and Walden 2001) to operationalize scope of the e-commerce initiative in terms of complexity of the event. Agrawal et al. (2006) code an investment as low complexity when the underlying task entails automating or linking existing processes and using out-ofthe-box functionality. High business/technology complexity involves the development or modification of online strategy, building “industry-first” applications, integrating multiple technology platforms, navigating rapid technology change, and developing new infrastructure for e-commerce. We follow Agrawal et al. (2006) to code the scope of 1,700 e-commerce initiatives (from content analysis of their public announcements) as high or low.

In a study of the impact of e-commerce initiatives on various measures of firm performance, Zhu (2004) found e-commerce capabilities and IT infrastructure to be strong complements. Zhu (2004) conceptualized e-commerce capabilities in terms of informational, transactional, customization, and back-end integrational functionality of the website. While much of this information may be disclosed as part of the announcement, we posit that there may be a high cost to process this information to price an e-commerce event. For example, the complementarity between e-commerce capability and the underlying IT infrastructure and processes may not be apparent to the market, especially when the phenomenon itself is immature. In the early stages, firms often considered e-commerce as a standalone activity and developed IT systems and processes that were not integrated with their brick-and-mortar counterparts. As an illustration, Charles Schwab, an IT pioneer in financial services, initially developed online-only processes when it invested in Internetbased trading. Later on, it realized the value that can be gained from integrated online–ofline operations, which eventually became common knowledge of being a critical success factor among brick-and-mortar firms. While such best practices become widely known with increasing maturity, large-scope e-commerce initiatives remain a challenge for most organizations. Such events involve integration with virtually all of a firm’s traditional systems and business processes, and may even involve new business strategies (e.g., Progressive Insurance’s business strategy of allowing customers to state how much they are willing to pay for car insurance). Therefore, as in the case of outsourcing, we argue that it is challenging for the market to incorporate information on, and models of, firm capability in valuing a high-scope e-commerce initiative. Overall, we posit that e-commerce initiatives announced in periods of low technological maturity, irrespective of scope, and initiatives with extensive scope, irrespective of their maturity, will be incompletely priced during the announcement period, resulting in long-term abnormal returns following the event.

## 4.2. Measures

Event Maturity. As noted earlier, we estimate maturity as the first period of significant discontinuity in the Rogers (1962) curves that characterizes the adoption and growth of unique outsourcing contracts and clients, and the number of unique vendors providing outsourcing services (Table A-2 in the online appendix). Outsourcing contracts signed before (after) the period of discontinuity are characterized as implemented in periods of low (high) outsourcing maturity. Taken together, the curves emphasize that the demand and supply of outsourcing services reach a critical mass in our sample in the year 2001. Indeed, practitioners argue that the Y2K phenomenon gave important impetus to the fledgling outsourcing industry (Thakker and Mathur 2010). They suggest that the Y2K phenomenon led to greater standardization of business processes, development of change management skills, and better partnership management, all of which supported organizational decisions to outsource more IT and IT-enabled business functions. Contracts implemented before 2001 and the allied clients and vendors of outsourcing services, categorized as innovators and early adopters or providers of outsourcing, accounted for about 11% to 14% of our sample. Our categorization is broadly consistent with studies on difusion of innovation (based on Rogers 1962), which find that innovators and early adopters account for nearly 15% of total adopters, while the early majority, late majority, and laggards constitute the rest of the sample. Thus, we code MATURITY as 1 if the outsourcing contract was implemented in 2001–2008 and 0 if the contract was implemented in 1995–2000.

As in the case of outsourcing, we identify the period of maturity of e-commerce investments as the first period of significant discontinuity in the Rogers (1962) curve that characterizes these investments. Our empirical analysis reveals 2004 to be the year of maturity of e-commerce events in our data (Table A-3 in the online appendix), which is also corroborated by accounts in the business press (e.g., The Economist 2004).

Event Scope. In the case of outsourcing, we use the type of outsourcing contract (fixed price or variable price) as an indicator of the scope of the contract. SCOPE is coded as 1 for variable price contracts and 0 for fixed price contracts. For e-commerce events, we assessed scope based on the operationalization in Agrawal et al. (2006, p. 865), where events involving “automating or linking existing processes, using outof-the-box functionality” were coded as low scope, and where those involving online strategy building, “industry first” applications, or integration of multiple technology platforms were coded as high scope events.

Announcement Period Returns. We use estimates of the market model (details shown in the online appendix) to predict daily returns for each firm i over three event windows<sup>6</sup>—a 3-day period [<sup>−</sup>1, <sup>+</sup>1], a 12- day period [<sup>−</sup>10, <sup>+</sup>1], and a 20-day period [<sup>−</sup>10, <sup>+</sup>9] surrounding the announcement of the outsourcing initiative. Prior research using the event study approach argues, “the chosen event window should be long enough to account for dissemination of information regarding the announcement over time” (Swaminathan and Moorman 2009, p. 57). A vast and rich body of work on event studies (Agrawal and Kamakura 1995, Geyskens et al. 2002) estimates cumulative average abnormal returns for <sup>+</sup>10 days to <sup>−</sup>10 days and tests the significance of various event windows using the t-statistic described by Brown and Warner (1985). Significance of the returns is based on the market model standardized residual method with Scholes–Williams betas. Our approach to selecting the announcement window follows this research.

Long-Term Abnormal Stock Returns. We use two methods (details in the online appendix) for estimating postevent risk-adjusted returns—a characteristic-based matching approach, also known as the event-time portfolio approach, and the Jensen’s alpha approach, also known as the calendar-time portfolio approach. Mitchell and Staford (2000, p. 296) describe the eventtime buy-and-hold abnormal return (BHAR) as “the average multi-year return from a strategy of investing in all firms that complete an event and selling at the end of a pre-specified holding period versus a comparable strategy using otherwise similar nonevent firms.” However, certain studies (Fama 1998, Mitchell and Staford 2000) argue that the event-time BHARs may spuriously reject market eficiency since they magnify underperformance through compounding single-period returns. Furthermore, the use of event-time returns does not adequately account for potential cross-sectional dependence in returns, resulting in biased estimates. Therefore, we also estimate calendar-time abnormal returns using the Fama and French (1992) three-factor model. In each calendar month over the sample period between 1996 and 2008, we construct a portfolio comprising all firms that have implemented an outsourcing contract in the previous 36 months. Given that the sample firms are not uniformly distributed over the sample period, the number of firms in a portfolio is not constant over time. In turn, the portfolios are rebalanced each month to calculate excess returns.

Confounding events are an important concern for long-term returns. Following the finance literature (e.g., Eberhart et al. 2004), we address this concern in three ways. First, we searched for public announcements of major events, including R&D increases, changes to top management, mergers and acquisitions, and strategic alliances after the focal event and eliminated those records where such events were detected. Second, our approach to sample selection involves large focal events and follows prior research in financial economics that examines the performance impact of major decisions. Finally, our recommendations for the length of the window follow those of Kothari and Warner (2008), who note that the bias introduced by confounding events is especially pronounced for time horizons greater than three years.

## 4.3. Analyses

We test whether announcement period returns are a complete measure of both types of IT events by estimating the significance of abnormal returns to the focal firms for the three-year period following each event. Significant long-term abnormal returns following an event are indicative of incomplete market pricing of the event (Kothari and Warner 1997, Fama 1998) during the announcement period. Given cross-sectional dependence problems of event-time abnormal returns and the relatively low power of calendar-time returns (Eberhart et al. 2004), we examine the robustness of our results to both measures. We subsequently estimate the significance of long-term abnormal returns for low- and high-scope initiatives within each period of technological maturity (low and high) to test our hypotheses.

Consistent with prior research in outsourcing (Mani et al. 2013, Gopal et al. 2003), we control for the endogeneity of contract choice using a two-stage regression model (Heckman 1979) that accounts for self-selection of the outsourcing and contracting decisions (Table A-4 in the online appendix). Our results are robust to clustering by firm as well as by vendor and year of implementation of the contract. A Hausman test finds that random efects estimators are consistent and eficient.

## 4.4. Results for Market Valuation of Outsourcing Events

To assess the relevance of contract type as a proxy for event scope, we estimate a probit model of contract choice with clustered standard errors (Table A-4 in the online appendix). Consistent with prior research on outsourcing contracts (e.g., Gopal et al. 2003, Mani et al. 2012), indicators of high scope such as increased number of task segments, business process outsourcing, greater contract value, and coordination requirements are associated with a higher likelihood of the choice of a variable price contract.

4.4.1. Long-Term Abnormal Returns. Panel A of Table 3 reports the three-year postevent BHARs for fixed and variable price outsourcing contracts implemented during 1995–2000, the period of low maturity in our sample. Firms that implemented fixed price contracts during 1995–2000 were characterized by three-year BHARs of 34.7% $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ relative to firms that did not outsource, while firms that implemented variable price contracts during this period had three-year BHARs of <sup>−</sup>24.3% $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The results for calendar-time abnormal returns are consistent with those for event-time returns. The implied three-year return on a portfolio of fixed price contracts implemented during 1995–2000 is 15.0% (p < 0.10), while the equivalent return on a portfolio of variable price contracts is <sup>−</sup>20.3% (p < 0.05).

The gains from fixed price contracts may be attributable to the simple nature of the outsourced tasks, whose ownership and control can be easily specified in the contract with the provider. Scale economies of the provider and specialized competences help reduce the costs of ownership of the outsourced tasks, thereby creating savings in task ownership for clients and increasing their shareholder value (Oh et al. 2006, Beasley et al. 2009, Mani et al. 2013). Complex tasks governed by variable price contracts require significant client-specific complementary investments to mitigate risks of cost overruns, opportunism, and coordination failures. In periods of low outsourcing maturity, there may be little awareness of the complexity and capabilities required of these contracts, resulting in eficiency losses. Consistent with Hypothesis 1, we find that, regardless of scope, financial markets are slow to price contracts implemented in periods of low maturity, resulting in significant long-term abnormal returns (positive for fixed price and negative for variable price contracts) following the implementation of these contracts.

To assess whether the market gains (losses) from fixed (variable) price contracts are from real economic gains (losses) that the market misprices, we also assess abnormal operating performance of the sample firms for the three-year period following the implementation of the outsourcing contract. We find that in periods of low outsourcing maturity, the median income eficiency of firms implementing fixed price contracts for the three-year period following the implementation of the contract represents an increase of 23% over the year preceding contract implementation. The equivalent estimate for variable price contracts is <sup>−</sup>53%. Furthermore, consistent with prior research (e.g., Healy et al. 1992, Eberhart et al. 2004), we also estimate abnormal cash flow for the sample firms relative to control firms for the three-year period following the implementation of the outsourcing contract. We find that for fixed price contracts implemented in 1995–2000, the median abnormal cash flow for the three-year period following the implementation of the contract represents an increase of 55% over the year preceding the implementation of the contract. The equivalent change for variable price contracts is <sup>−</sup>98%.

Panel B in Table 3 reports the three-year postevent BHARs for fixed and variable price outsourcing contracts implemented during 2001–2008. During this period of greater outsourcing maturity, we find that fixed price contracts, involving relatively simpler tasks and lower scope of complementary organizational investments, are characterized by insignificant longterm abnormal returns. Thus, over time, financial markets learn to price the value created by fixed price outsourcing engagements. These results support Hypothesis 2A—during periods of high outsourcing maturity, financial markets will be eficient in pricing fixed price contracts characterized by low scope, resulting in insignificant long-term abnormal returns to these contracts relative to firms that do not experience the event.

Variable price contracts, involving a relatively higher scope of changes, continue to be associated with negative long-term abnormal returns in the more mature period. Event-time and calendar-time abnormal returns for variable price contracts during this period are <sup>−</sup>10.1% $( p < \bar { 0 . 0 1 } )$ and <sup>−</sup>14.0% $( p < \bar { 0 } . 0 5 )$ , respectively.

4.4.3. Outsourcing Capabilities and Returns. The results of our first stage model of contract choice indicate that firms select variable price contracts to govern complex outsourced tasks that span multiple business segments, are characterized by high levels of uncertainty in their business environment, and whose execution involves high levels of interdependence and coordination between the client and the service provider. The results are consistent with prior research on contract choice (e.g., Gopal et al. 2003, Mani et al. 2012, Bajari and Tadelis 2001) and confirm that the contract choice is an indicator of the underlying complexity of the outsourced task.

Table 5 details results of the second stage of the model that regresses long-term abnormal returns on specific attributes of the outsourcing initiative, thereby providing insights into what it is that the market misprices. Specifically, we regress the abnormal returns on (i) measures of complexity of the outsourced task (total contract value (TCV), total number of business segments that the outsourced task spans (TOTAL\_SEG), and the type of outsourcing initiative (TYPE)) and (ii) proxies for the event-specific capabilities of the firm that are critical to making appropriate investments in organizational complements (experience of the outsourcing firm in managing similar outsourcing initiatives (EXP), prior association between the outsourcing firm and provider (PRIOR), and the duration of the contract that reflects expectations of continuity of the relationship (CONTINUITY)). Models I and III do not include whether the contract was implemented in a period of high outsourcing maturity as an independent variable, while Models II and IV do.

Our results confirm that long-term abnormal returns to fixed price contracts are an outcome of mispricing the complexity of the outsourcing initiative; i.e., markets may be unaware that these contracts are best suited for the governance of simple tasks with limited scope, variability, and coordination requirements. However, in variable price contracts, such returns are additionally an outcome of mispricing the capabilities required to manage such complexity through appropriate complements. Markets underestimate the role of prior experience of the firm in managing similar outsourcing initiatives, prior association with the vendor in creating value in variable price contracts, and the continuity of the relationship as indicated in the contract duration. Interestingly, there is little information cost incurred by the market in acquiring information on these event parameters, especially parameters such as contract duration. However, the fact that they are mispriced (as seen in their contribution to abnormal long-term returns) indicates the challenges in interpreting how they may be proxies for cooperation, less opportunism, and better coordination. The results are consistent with our theorization that in the case of low-scope contracts, markets misprice the potential of the underlying technology, while in variable price contracts, they misprice the event-specific capabilities that are integral to managing the wide scope of organizational changes that complement the IT event.

The results are shown in Table A-6 in the online appendix. Consistent with the case of outsourcing, there were no significant long-term abnormal returns for low-complexity e-commerce announcements made in a period of high maturity, suggesting the announcement period returns are a complete measure of shareholder value of the event. Thus, both outsourcing and e-commerce initiatives show results consistent with our framework, in turn providing evidence of its generalizability.

## 4.6. Robustness Checks

We assess the robustness of our results to alternate dependent variables and empirical specifications. First, we use the Ohlson (1995) valuation model to investigate the value relevance of information on outsourcing initiatives to market prices instead of abnormal returns. We find that in periods of high maturity, the market incorporates information on both types of IT events in share prices in the year of implementation of an event. By contrast, we find that the market fails to incorporate information on the event in periods of low maturity. The result (reported in Table A-7 in the online appendix) is consistent with our finding of significant announcement period returns in periods of high maturity.

Second, we control for the endogeneity of implementation in periods of low maturity that may result from systematic diferences in capabilities between early versus late adopters so that the observed diference in market returns between these two time periods is an outcome of mispricing of these capability diferences rather than mispricing of the maturity. Given the difficulty in finding appropriate exclusion criteria for the decision to adopt the IT initiative in an earlier period, we assess whether there are systematic diferences in specific capabilities and performance between early and late adopters of the IT events in our sample. We follow prior research in management (e.g., Kalaignanam et al. 2013) to conceptualize the capabilities of a firm as its ability relative to its peers to transform its input resources into outputs. Specifically, consistent with Dutta et al. (2005), we use stochastic frontier estimation to measure firms’ capabilities. If a capability can be viewed as a transformation process, a stochastic production function (described in the online appendix) measures the relative eficiency of a firm for that capability, with respect to its peers, in converting the respective inputs into outputs. We find that there is no significant diference between the technological and marketing capabilities of early versus late adopters (Table A-8 in the online appendix). The results are robust to absolute as well as industry-adjusted measures of capabilities. Together with the results for performance diferences between these two categories of firms, we conclude that firms that are early adopters of the IT events in our sample are not systematically different from late adopters in terms of their capabilities and performance.

## 5. Discussion of Results

The presence of long-term abnormal returns in periods of high maturity (for high scope) suggests that the ability of a firm to manage the scope of an event is only imperfectly transferrable across firms and technology regimes. Otherwise, prior instances of adoption and management of the underlying IT and ensuing success and failure of the investing firms would sufice for the market to accurately price the event during the announcement period. Similarly, if past success of a firm with IT could accurately predict its performance and success with future IT events, then there would be no long-term abnormal returns, for the market would use the focal firm’s IT history to accurately price the new event. However, our result suggests that the market is unable to do so. This finding is consistent with research in strategy and IS that technology environments of firms are characterized by high information clock speed (Mendelson and Pillai 1998) and discontinuous change (Christensen 1997) so that the absorptive capacity of the firm includes an event-specific element (Lim 2009, Cooper and Molla 2012). The result is also in line with observations that proven IT pioneers such as Walmart have faced significant challenges in adopting new technologies such as radio frequency identification (Weier 2007), while FedEx, the exemplar of IT-based competitive advantage, has had dificulties in transitioning from insourcing to outsourcing.

Our results have important implications for theory and practice. The funding of IT-driven business models has shifted significantly to the private sector over the past few decades. Therefore, the extent to which the stock market properly values these events is critical. However, our findings suggest that in the short run, capital markets may not correctly impound into market prices technology investments that are characterized by uncertainty in future cash flows resulting from either low levels of maturity or a significant scope of changes in the organization. Disclosing detailed information on a firm’s management of the technology investment, including supporting structures, processes, and capabilities, may help the market better price the event around the time of announcement. However, disclosure also raises a trade-of to the investing firm between reducing the competitive value of its informational advantage and raising capital at better terms that reflect its success with technology investments, thereby lowering the dilution suffered by existing shareholders owning the technology initiative and mitigating problems of adverse selection and moral hazard (Bhattacharya and Ritter 1983). An empirical analysis of potential information asymmetry between the firm and the market may provide a supporting mechanism behind incomplete pricing during the announcement period and represents an interesting avenue for future research in the field.

Furthermore, we find some evidence that markets may not pay due attention to information on some of the management variables such as prior experience that may mitigate the uncertainty surrounding technology investments. If markets do not reward firms that are more capable of managing uncertainty associated with IT investments, there could be a potential misallocation of resources in which firms that are more efective at managing uncertainty and realizing value from IT investments may be undercapitalized relative to firms that are less efective at realizing value. An analysis of diferent IT management capabilities and their roles in firm valuation is also an interesting direction for future research.

Finally, in the current study, although we provide some evidence that long-term abnormal returns to IT events are likely due to mispricing, future research should also examine whether long-term abnormal returns are compensation for a technology-specific risk factor.<sup>7</sup>

## 6. Conclusion

The assessment of the market value of large IT events has been an important area for IS research. It may be a convincing way to demonstrate the value of IT to C-level executives, who are often concerned about maximizing shareholder value. However, to be accepted as a robust and reliable methodology, the estimation of market value of IT must contend with challenges, stemming primarily from the high costs of acquiring and processing intangible and ambiguous information required by capital markets to price an IT event accurately. We develop a framework to help select the right market-based approach to assess the value of IT events. The framework involves the technological maturity and the scope of complements associated with an IT event, and suggests that with the exception of one class of events, the commonly deployed announcement period returns in IS research are not a suitable measure of event value. An implication of our study is that IT events marked by low maturity and/or high scope will not be accurately priced by the market during the announcement phase, and that long-term abnormal returns may capture the true market value of major IT decisions. Furthermore, in the case of highscope IT events, firm attributes that are indicative of event-specific capacity may be initially underpriced by the market.

In our study, we focus on the impact of outsourcing and e-commerce on future cash flows and the incomplete estimation of such value by the market. The market misprices the resultant cash flows, resulting in long-term abnormal returns to the event. However, large technological investments may impact the riskiness of the firm, leading investors to hold priors that these IT events are proxies for risk. As a result, investors may attach higher discount rates to firms that implement these events, leading to greater abnormal returns. Future research could use event-specific data to separate these two mechanisms underlying longterm abnormal returns and assess whether IT events impact cash flows, the discount rate, or both. Future research should also focus on mispricing mechanisms such as information asymmetry, and also incorporate more firm-specific attributes and capabilities in models of long-term abnormal returns to establish a comprehensive information set that markets initially misprice. Such an approach will help better uncover the contribution of a firm’s IT management capabilities to market value as a direct response to Carr’s (2003) “IT Doesn’t Matter” argument.
