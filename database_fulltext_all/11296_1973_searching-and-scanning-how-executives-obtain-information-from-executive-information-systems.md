## Abstract

Executive information systems may be used in different ways by managers in retrieving information. Two common modes of use are scanning, or general browsing of data; and focused search, or seeking answers to specific questions or well-defined problems. The purpose of this study was to investigate the antecedents of these two different modes of EIS use and also to examine their implications for perceived performance changes.

The results show that, when executives focus their use of EIS to answer specific questions or solve well-defined problems, they help to fine-tune operations and verify assumptions—in other words, they help to make the organization more efficient. However, an EIS may also lead an executive to challenge fundamental managerial assumptions and preconceptions when using it to scan through information without having specific questions in mind. In this mode, an EIS may be used to help formulate problems and foster creativity—thereby improving organizational effectiveness.

EISs were found to contribute to gains in efficiency much more frequently than to gains in effectiveness. Companies that want to achieve greater effectiveness should pay attention to the role of the EIS in the scanning behavior of their managers. Factors that influenced the extent to which managers would engage their EIS in scanning included the extent to which EIS scanning was undertaken by others in the organization and the characteristics of the EIS itself.

Keywords: Executive information systems, information search behavior, information scanning, focused information search, IS performance

## Introduction

The important position information holds in executive decision making has been documented extensively. One of the main roles executives have is monitoring information from a myriad of sources about their organizations and environments (Mintzberg 1973). The greater the uncertainty in the environment, the greater the need for information processing (Galbraith 1974; Thompson 1967). Guided by their social context, culture, organizational norms, history and power considerations, executives select information to tell them where, when, and toward what their attention should be directed and to help them formulate or define organizational problems (Mason and Mitroff 1981; Schwenk and Thomas 1983). Without new or updated information, they would be unable to determine if their views of the environment and their organization's position within it remain appropriate. Given proper problem formulation, information helps executives establish options and select courses of action (Mintzberg, et al. 1976; Simon 1965). Information can also help stimulate creativity (McCaskey 1982), generate scenarios, determine environmental trends, monitor performance, and control activities (Gorry and Scott Morton 1971). Furthermore, information is the fuel for planning and "strategizing" (Mintzberg 1981). Without information, executives simply cannot function.

Executive information systems (EIS) are flexible tools that provide broad and deep information support and analytic capability for a wide range of executive decisions (Houdeshel and Watson 1987; Rockart and DeLong 1988). They are designed to make the information contained in the lower-level systems in the organization available in a form that is easy to access, easy to use, and germane to decision making (Stevenson 1994). In addition, they provide executives with access to external information such as news, regulations, and competitive analyses (Young and Watson 1995). The data can come from a variety of sources including transaction processing systems, financial reporting systems, commercial information sources, text files, and manual data collection. Information contained in EIS varies by organization and runs the gamut from finance, marketing and sales, human resources, and manufacturing or operations, to external competitive benchmarks of various types. The reasons why and ways that executive information systems are used are as varied as the executives who use them. Depending on functionality and inclination, EIS may be used for performance monitoring, "what-if" analyses, trend spotting, problem identification and resolution, and generally keeping up-to-date. Some executives use their systems every day, or even several times a day, to stay abreast of current developments, typically viewing a consistent set of predefined reports. Some executives define new reports as the issues they face change and develop. Some use their EIS to scan broadly across a wide variety of information external to the organization's databases. Still others use them sporadically when they require a specific piece of information (Matthews and Shoebridge 1992).

The investments required to create, implement and maintain EIS are substantial and ongoing (Watson, et al. 1991). At the same time, as with many technologies, success is far from guaranteed and failures are common (Young and Watson 1995; Watson 1990), Consequently, a great deal of EIS research has focused on attributes of EIS and EIS support that lead to perceptions of user information satisfaction, acceptance and success (e.g., Bergeron and Raymond 1992; Bergeron et al. 1991; Young and Watson 1995).

This paper holds the view that the relationship between EIS and success is more complex than this. Because EIS are highly flexible tools, executives may behave in various ways when using their EIS to retrieve information. Consequently, this paper investigated how EIS are used by executives and whether or not these different types of use are related to different perceptions of changes in organizational performance. A field study of seven organizations using EIS at the senior management level was conducted to examine the relationships among individual differences, system characteristics, organizational context, information retrieval behaviors, and perceptions of organizational performance.

## Previous Research

## Information retrieval

There are many different ways to acquire information. For example, we can happen across information purely by chance when we are not even thinking about obtaining any, we can scan broadly to see what strikes our interest as when we read magazines and newspapers, we can conduct regular reviews of a predefined set of reports, and we can explicitly search for a specific fact. These modes of acquisition form a broad continuum ranging from general and unintentional to specific and goal-oriented. These modes are divided into four categories: undirected viewing, defined as general exposure when the viewer has no specific purpose in mind; conditioned viewing, or directed exposure not involving active search; informal search, which is an unstructured effort to obtain specific information; and formal search, which refers to a deliberate effort to find a piece of information (Aguilar 1967). More recently, the terms scanning, performance monitoring, and focused search have been used to describe a continuum of modes of information retrieval (Huber 1991). Noticing was also mentioned as a completely undirected form of information acquisition.

While the distinction between acquisition and retrieval is subtle, it is important. Acquire means to come into possession, while retrieve means to obtain (Concise Oxford Dictionary 1990). The former can be active or passive, while the latter is only active. Undirected viewing or noticing is a form of information acquisition, but not active information retrieval, while the remaining modes are active forms of acquisition and, hence, comprise information retrieval. In order to clearly differentiate the ends of the continuum, two characteristic modes of information retrieval behavior are highlighted. Scanning (Aguilar 1967) is the behavior people exhibit when they browse through information without a particular problem to solve or question to answer, while focused search (Huber 1991) occurs when people are looking for something specific. Both modes can be used for acquiring various types of information, such as facts about the external environment or internal operations. Either approach can be used for performance monitoring, depending on how it is undertaken. If the same reports are viewed regularly, the behavior is more like focused search, but if performance is monitored by randomly viewing results to see if anything catches the viewer's attention, it is scanning.

In general, it is not the report that is viewed, but rather the underlying behavior which leads to that report being viewed, that indicates whether focused search or scanning is taking place. In the EIS context, focused search is the behavior executives perform when they are using the EIS to verify performance results or look up a specific piece of information. If the underlying need driving the information search is, "what's the answer to this question," the person is performing focused search. Scanning is the behavior executives perform when they are browsing through data in order to understand trends or sharpen their general understanding of the business. It is what they do when they are "seeing what's new." If executives begin a query with the thought "I wonder if there is any information here I might find useful," they are scanning.

## Information retrieval and organizational performance $^{1}$

There is strong support for the proposition that scanning a broad range of information sources leads to improvements in performance (Huber 1991; Thomas, et al. 1993) and also strong support for the proposition that scanning is particularly important for senior management (El Sawy 1985). Chief executives in high performing companies scan more frequently and more broadly in response to strategic uncertainty than those in low-performing companies (Daft, et al. 1988). In organizations in decline, executives tend to rely on highly formal, focused information systems and conduct relatively less broadly targeted, informal scanning (Miller 1993). There is evidence of this at Battery March and Mrs. Fields Cookies. The information systems in those organizations influenced people to sacrifice environmental scanning activities in order to become more efficient, which may well have led to the substantial decline of both companies (Gill 1995).

Why is scanning linked to performance? Top management teams with a well-developed ability to access and analyze information have their attention caught by a wider range of variables when making decisions and taking action and, consequently, perform better (Thomas and McDaniel 1990). In addition, people who are exposed to more information have better-developed intuition and, as a result, respond more quickly and effectively to the information they encounter (Eisenhardt 1989b). Executives who use relatively more information tend to interpret issues as positive for the organization and feel they have more control over them. In fact, scanning and performance may be self-reinforcing, with broad scanning providing opportunities for improved performance and improved performance providing slack resources for broader scanning. (Daft, et al. 1988).

While most executives focus their scanning on external sources of information (El Sawy 1985), scanning can and should also involve searching within the organization to identify activities and situations that might influence future performance (Thomas, et al. 1993). The common practice of “management by walking around” typifies within-organization scanning behavior.

There is also evidence, however, that scanning is not the only key to superior performance. Organizations must not only explore new possibilities, but must also exploit old certainties. "Maintaining an appropriate balance between exploration and exploitation is a primary factor in . . . survival and prosperity" (March 1991, p. 71).

When people look for information to answer specific questions or solve well-defined problems, they help to control operations and verify assumptions through focused search, typically leading to improvements in efficiency—doing things better (Huber 1991). At the same time, focused search can be limiting. When executives are engaged in focused search, in most instances, they will see what they expect to see (Festinger 1957). In fact, misconceptions tend to persist and accumulate in spite of seemingly sufficient contrary evidence (Thomas and McDaniel 1990). Information may be able to challenge fundamental managerial assumptions and preconceptions, however, if people scan or browse through it without having specific questions in mind. In this mode, information may be used to help formulate problems, and foster the development of a greater number of possible solutions, providing the potential to lead to improvements in effectiveness—doing different (and hopefully better)

things. Nevertheless, if an executive is not receptive to new and unanticipated information, or if new information simply does not arise during the scanning process, creative insight and consequent improvements in effectiveness are unlikely. Hence, EIS should support both focused search and scanning to ensure improvements in efficiency and effectiveness respectively.

## EIS and information retrieval

Most EIS provide status access, querying and analytic capabilities for a broad range of information sources (Silver 1991, p. 225), thereby facilitating both focused search and scanning behaviors. Several researchers have categorized these EIS capabilities in similar ways. EIS have been described as having three major levels of functionality: the automated briefing book, exception reporting, and active exploration (Applegate 1989). While any of these three usage modes could be used to conduct either focused search or scanning, generally one or the other predominates. Automated briefing books and exception reporting lend themselves to focused search, while active exploration corresponds more closely to scanning. Similarly, two methods of executive computer use have been identified: status access and personalized analysis (Rockart and Treacy 1982). Status access is oriented more toward focused search and personalized access more toward scanning. In the same vein, two modes of information retrieval behavior were referred to in the description of the EIS at Lockheed-Georgia (Houdeshel and Watson 1987). At one extreme there were executives who browsed through displays and, at the other, executives who regularly viewed a particular sequence of screens. These descriptions correspond closely to the notions of scanning and focused search.

Finally, there is clear evidence in the assessment of Conoco's EIS that that system led to improvements in efficiency through focused search (Belcher and Watson 1993). Benefits of the system included improved productivity, improved decision making in terms of quicker identification of and response to market opportunities, and information distribution, services and software replacement cost savings. The Industry Statistical Data application in the Conoco EIS provided consistent market information that was routinely updated. Similarly, a commodity trading group used the system to track inventories and prices for each product. Both of these examples used language that emphasized the routine way in which the information provided was accessed.

To summarize so far, there is support in the EIS literature for two main modes of information retrieval, scanning and focused search, and there is also support for the argument that scanning is more likely than focused search to lead to gains in effectiveness. Scanning and focused search both seem to have the potential to lead to gains in efficiency. Finally, focused search seems to be more frequent than scanning.

## Factors affecting information retrieval behavior

Given the potential difference in the effects that scanning and focused search have on organizational performance, it is important to understand the factors that are likely to differentiate between them. The factors investigated here can be divided into three groups: individual differences, system characteristics, and organizational context.

## Individual Differences

Three individual differences were investigated: tolerance for ambiguity, locus of control, and degree of innovativeness.

Tolerance for ambiguity. A person who is tolerant of ambiguity is able to deal with it, but does not necessarily like it. Such a person "seeks it out in order to reduce it: he is tolerant of it only as long as he can do something about it, and his cognitive task is to get rid of it" (Rydell and Rosen 1966, p. 149). Tolerance for ambiguity has been found to be related to a desire to seek out ways to create uncertainty (McCaskey 1976). Hence, it would seem that executives who are relatively more tolerant of ambiguity would tend to be more prone to scanning behavior because they are not fearful of the ambiguity that often results.

Locus of control. Locus of control refers to the degree to which people believe they are masters of their own fate. People who have an internal locus of control believe they control what happens to them, and people who have an external locus of control believe they are controlled by outside influences, such as other people or chance (Levenson 1974). Internals are more likely to search for information before making decisions, and also tend to take jobs that are less structured, indicating a potentially higher propensity toward information scanning (Robbins 1993).

Degree of innovativeness. It has been argued that “everyone can be located on a continuum ranging from an ability to ‘do things better’ to an ability to ‘do things differently’” (Kirton 1976, p. 622). Adapters, who “do things better” are “characterized by precision, reliability, efficiency, methodicalness, prudence, discipline and conformity; are concerned with resolving problems rather than finding them; and seek solutions to problems in tried and understood ways” (Kirton 1976, p. 623). Innovators, on the other hand, are “seen as undisciplined, thinking tangentially, approaching tasks from unsuspected angles; could be said to discover problems and discover avenues of solution; and query problems’ concomitant assumptions” (Kirton 1976, p. 623). These definitions suggest that adapters are less likely to be information scanners and innovators are more likely to be scanners.

## System Characteristics

Previous research does not provide us with either theory or strong empirical evidence about system characteristics that might encourage scanning or focused search behavior. Organizational theory suggests, however, a simple typology of potentially important properties.

Organizations have been viewed as open systems in which the behaviors of the members are interrelated (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967). The argument is that, as organizations become larger, they have a tendency to “differentiate into parts, and the functioning of these separate parts has to be integrated if the entire system is to be viable” (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967, p. 7). These concepts of differentiation and integration can be used in combination with the notion of flexibility to create a typology of EIS characteristics.

Differentiation exists when an EIS includes a diversity of applications, has several levels of detail, and is frequently modified and updated. Systems that contain environmental and competitive information from many diverse sources are more likely to support scanning behavior than those that contain only standard operating and control reports which previously have been available in paper form (Rockart and Treacy 1982). It has been suggested that access to extensive detail is crucial to EIS success, because it enables executives to find problems and spot opportunities, rather than just solve problems and exploit opportunities (Mohan et al. 1990). The frequency with which new and unusual information is included in the system may also make it more likely to support scanning behavior (Daft et al. 1988).

Integration exists when an EIS combines information from diverse sources on single screens. Although it did not refer to scanning behavior specifically, one study argued convincingly for cross-functional systems that bring together information from diverse sources (Wetherbe 1991). The study indicated that these systems are most likely to improve organizational decision making. Cross-functional information would arguably be more interesting and more unusual than the more common “information silos.” Hence, it would be more likely to encourage scanning behavior. Similarly, “combining data from multiple sources enables executives to explore new relationships and improve their grasp of the business” (Rockart and DeLong 1988, p. 139).

Flexibility exists if the data are independent of the use to which they are put, and/or if the system provides analytic and modeling capability. Obviously, the availability and quality of analysis and modeling support will affect analysis and modeling behavior. One study showed that analysis capability enables executives to "explore," a concept very closely aligned with scanning (Mohan et al. 1990). Another argued that a system that does not predefine how the data are to be used is more likely to encourage scanning behavior (March 1987).

While the evidence is preliminary, it would seem that the greater the differentiation, integration and flexibility an EIS possesses, the greater the likelihood that it will encourage scanning behavior among the executives who use it. ## Organizational context

There is little question that context plays an important role in the way that technology is implemented and used (Kling 1980; Symons 1991). This study investigated three factors that contribute to organizational context: social influences, perceived environmental uncertainty, and job characteristics.

Social influences. Perceptions about technology are often socially constructed, at least in part (Fulk et al. 1987). Culture has been found to play a strong role in determining how an EIS is used in an organization (Green and Murphy 1994). Work group influence (defined as the opinions of superiors, subordinates and peers) was found to be significantly correlated with internalization of the EIS (defined as dependence on the system, ownership felt by the user, and routinization of usage) (Bergeron et al. 1991). These findings indicate that individual information retrieval behaviors may be related to the social influences experienced. Specifically, scanning may be more prevalent in organizations in which scanning is accepted and encouraged, and focused search may be more prevalent in organizations where it is the more accepted and expected behavior.

Perceived environmental uncertainty. Uncertainty about the environment is a fundamental problem with which executives must cope (Thompson 1967). One of the primary means for doing so is collecting more information (Galbraith 1974). The higher the perceived environmental uncertainty, the greater the felt need for information (Gordon and Narayanan 1983). In fact, information is often defined in terms of its ability to reduce uncertainty (Daft and Macintosh 1981). In the EIS context, this perspective would lead one to believe that scanning behavior is likely to reduce uncertainty more than focused search, so executives who feel uncertain about their environments are likely to perform this behavior more than executives who feel certain. There is, however, another perspective on uncertainty. When uncertainty is overwhelming, individuals often resist "wasting time" in scanning, preferring to focus on those aspects of a situation with which they are comfortable (McCaskey 1982; Vallacher and Wegner 1987; Weick 1979). Taking this perspective would lead to the opposing belief: with greater perceived environmental uncertainty, scanning behavior is reduced. As has been suggested, the reality may be a composite: scanning behavior may have an inverted U-shaped relationship with perceived environmental uncertainty (Lawrence and Dyer 1983). At very low and very high levels of uncertainty, there may be very little scanning, and in the middle region, scanning may increase substantially.

Job characteristics. The nature of executives' jobs may influence their information retrieval behaviors as well. Executives who are responsible for convergent and relatively more structured aspects of organization management (e.g., auditing, control, financial accounting), may be less prone to scanning behavior than executives whose responsibilities are more divergent and less structured (e.g., marketing, sales, strategy; March 1991). Similarly, the longer the time orientation executives have, the more likely they are to be able to engage in scanning behavior (El Sawy 1985; Mintzberg 1973).

## The Field Study

The overarching research question guiding this study was, how and why do executives' EIS-based information retrieval behaviors vary and what is the impact of those variations? A field study of EIS in seven organizations was conducted to explore whether or not individual differences, system characteristics, and organizational context are related to information retrieval behaviors and whether or not those behaviors are related to perceptions of performance improvement. The factors that differentiate between scanning and focused search behaviors were also investigated. Appendix A provides the detailed rationale for the research methods followed and describes how the data were analyzed.

Two of the seven organizations investigated were among the top 10 financial institutions in Canada. Four others were among the top three companies in their respective markets. The remaining site was a large government organization. Sales for the public companies ranged from \$103 million to \$12.9 billion. The government agency had an annual budget of \$1 billion. Six of the companies were headquartered in Canada and the seventh was based in the United States. One EIS had been in place for a year, four for approximately two years, one for five years, and one for more than eight years. Two of the systems used Pilot's Command Centre, three used Comshare's Commander, one used Powerplay from Cognos, and one had been built in-house. In four of the seven organizations, the most senior executive in the organization participated in the research and, on average, participants were one level away from the president (standard deviation = .83). The mean age of the users was 47 (s.d. = 7.1). The mean tenure in the company was 15.8 years (s.d. = 10.5) and the mean tenure in the job was 4.2 years (s.d. = 4.9). Half of the respondents had graduate degrees and only one had no university education. Half of the degrees were in business and the remainder were divided among arts, science, social science, and engineering. The respondents exhibited a wide range of system usage, varying from a few minutes a week to three and a half hours per day (mean = 1.4, s.d. = 1.7).

## Fine-tuning the research methods

The initial study site was treated in part as a pretest of the research method. Two lessons emerged from the investigation at this organization, resulting in minor changes to the methods and the evolution of the model.

Perceived environmental uncertainty. The interview questions relating to perceived environmental uncertainty proved unworkable. When executives were asked about their ability to predict how components of the environment were changing, their understanding of the interrelationships among elements of the environment, their ability to predict the impact that environmental change would have on their organizations, and their ability to predict the outcomes of their decisions (Milliken 1987), they became either confused or agitated. The reason for this appeared to be the lack of a benchmark against which to compare uncertainty. Given the myriad of confused and conflicting evidence obtained, together with the frustration respondents felt with the questions, perceived environmental uncertainty was dropped from the later interviews.

Predisposition toward scanning. The largest single contribution the investigation of the first organization made to the study was that it suggested the existence of a predisposition toward scanning. An initial analysis of the interview transcripts pointed to the fact that some executives are more prone to scanning behavior generally, regardless of the source of the information. ## Findings

Interview comments were used to score the information retrieval behaviors and evaluations of effectiveness and efficiency. They varied widely among the seven organizations. Table 2 shows the frequency of the key constructs by company. (To maintain confidentiality, the companies have been arbitrarily, but consistently, named A, B, C, D, E, F, and G.)

In total, 30 of 36 interviewees (83%) exhibited focused search behavior, nine (25%) exhibited scanning behavior, 25 (69%) attributed improvements in efficiency to the EIS, and six (17%) attributed improvements in effectiveness to the EIS.

Six executives did not use their systems for focused search. One said, "It is not my prime information vehicle, because it's not good enough and not fast enough. It makes the information harder to get at." Another said, "The system is not at the right level of detail. Paper is just as good."

Scanning was only observed in conjunction with focused search. Not a single individual exhibited scanning behavior exclusively. However, 21 people exhibited focused search, but not scanning. Exclusive focused searchers said things like, "I take a quick glance at how we are doing compared to the rest of the world using two or three views of the same reports. I go deeper when specific questions arise such as pricing and inventory." Executives who retrieved information through scanning as well as focused search said things like, "I flip around, depending on my curiosity," "I play around with sales by sorting them in various ways which usually raises questions," and "I dig around to get meaning and understand the quarter or the budget. I am completely random on other reports."

There was strong support for the relationship between focused search and perceptions of efficiency. All but five of the 30 interviewees who exhibited focused search behavior thought their EIS improved efficiency. For example, several thought their system improved the speed with which information was disseminated through the organization. Others thought that the information had become more accurate. The relationship between scanning and efficiency was also strong, with all nine of the scanners believing the EIS improved efficiency. While compelling, these results are not conclusive, as all of the scanners also used the EIS for focused search as discussed above. It could have been their focused search behavior, rather than their scanning behavior, that led them to their perceptions about efficiency.

No relationship was found between focused search and perceptions about improvements in effectiveness. However, there was a fairly strong relationship between scanning and effectiveness. Of the 30 people who engaged in focused search, 22 did not believe their systems led to improvements in effectiveness, while six of the nine scanners did. Their comments included statements such as, “improves our ability to make good decisions,” “provides better understanding,” “gives me new ideas about the business,” and “generates thinking and soul searching.”

## Individual differences

As discussed above, interviews in the first organization indicated that some executives are more prone to scanning information, irrespective of the source of that information. While a predisposition toward scanning behavior was not a predictor of EIS scanning behavior, it was found to be a necessary condition. $^{2}$ Of the executive users, 19 (53%) exhibited a predisposition toward scanning. Comments that differentiated them from people without such a predisposition included, "I look at information based on changing area of interest," "You need to immerse yourself in what's going on. Useful information comes from everywhere," and "sifting through, extrapolating and reworking information to give me what I want." Nonscanners said things such as "I look at trends in bottom line results more than anything else," and "Numerical measures of performance...are key." Of the 19 people with a predisposition toward scanning, eight scanned the information in their EIS and 11 did not. Only one of the 17 executives who did not seem to have a predisposition toward scanning scanned the information in his EIS. There appeared to be no relationship between a predisposition toward scanning and focused search.

The research model included three types of individual differences that might influence the ways that people retrieve information from their EIS. Given the strong relationship between a predisposition toward scanning and system scanning, a check was made as to whether the individual differences had any relationship to a predisposition toward scanning. The study found that degree of innovativeness and tolerance for ambiguity were strongly linked to a predisposition toward scanning generally, but that other facilitating conditions were required for scanning behavior to actually take place with the EIS. $^{3}$ Certain system characteristics may provide the necessary facilitating conditions, as discussed below.

## System characteristics

Three system characteristics were expected to affect the propensity to undertake scanning behavior when using the EIS: differentiation, integration, and flexibility. During the investigation, it became clear that these three characteristics were extremely relevant to the success of the EIS. For example, a senior vice president at one of the organizations, commenting on why he was relatively unhappy with his EIS, said:

[B]ut is it everything I really need? I find the answer there is "no," and that [the problem] to me is more an imbedded feature of the whole system in that we're dealing with pre-canned routines.... It gets to be an inhibitor. If something needs calculation you can't.... Another large part of it is lack of integration.

This executive felt the system lacked both flexibility and integration—the main reasons he did not rely on it to meet his information needs.

At another organization, a satisfied user indicated that he found the system to be "Fantastic! It provides you everything you can possibly want to know." For him, differentiation was a key factor leading to the success of the EIS.

The following paragraphs provide a brief description of each EIS, emphasizing the way each provided (or failed to provide) differentiation, integration and flexibility.

Company A and Company C implemented their EIS primarily to provide access to monthly financial information. For the most part, the information was only slightly more current than that available on paper reports. In both cases, executives complained that the data were too aggregated to be helpful and were not conducive to ad hoc analyses. In Company A in particular, three executives described their penchant for immersion in the numbers in order to uncover new relationships, but indicated that the EIS was too constraining to enable this kind of behavior. Neither company scored well on differentiation or integration. While

Company C's EIS had some analytic capability, the data the system contained were not detailed enough to make analysis insightful. Although several general scanners were interviewed in both organizations, the only person in either company who scanned using the EIS was the Chief Accountant in Company C. He did so to ensure that the data, for which he was primarily responsible, were correct.

In contrast, Company B had an EIS that they believed contributed \$4.8 million to the bottom line in its second year of operation. This system enabled executives to compare the margins they were obtaining for their products to the relevant competitive benchmark in real time. It also allowed them to cut the data in whatever way they wished. Company B had decided to develop a system that would collect as much information as they could think of at the transactional level to enable executives to look at sales information in any form that might be relevant and to make comparisons to the appropriate market price rather than to blended averages. While very deep, the system was not broad, since it focused exclusively on sales information. It had a high degree of integration and flexibility. Three of the six executives interviewed used the EIS to scan information and create scenarios.

Company D had by far the largest system of those investigated. It contained more than 1,500 financial reports and a myriad of operating metrics. Most of the information was updated monthly. The system also enabled executives to review entire newspapers and journals from around the world. While amount of differentiation in this system was very high, integration was not. There were only a few examples of information from different sources appearing together on one screen. Because they used the same shell, technically speaking, flexibility was exactly the same as for Company C. However, in this organization, executives made much more use of the capability because there was so much more information available. Three of the five executives who took part in the research in Company D used the EIS to scan information and broaden their understanding of their organization and its environment.

## Organizational contextual factors

## Social Influences

The research model proposed that social influences supporting an information retrieval behavior would be positively related to that behavior. Only Company B and Company D exhibited clear social support for the use of the EIS. The most senior manager at Company B had the following comment:

[T]he way that I drive it [the use of the EIS] is by questions. Because, often I'll call up and the person will say, "What, what happened, how did you know that?" And I will say, "Well, look at your screens." So this is part of the process of learning.

His objective was to ensure that his subordinates would make the same decisions he would. The other executives at Company B all commented on how their leader's directives vis-a-vis the EIS encouraged them to use the system more and made it more relevant for them. The senior manager used the system for both focused search and scanning, but only two of his subordinates used the system for scanning. The three individuals who did not scan the system had convergent jobs; each was responsible for the day-to-day operations of a regional company. Everyone in the organization used the system for focused search activities.

The president of Company D also took an active interest in the system and had become convinced that putting information in the EIS gave it more impact than delivering it any other way. Because of this impact, he often conceived of new reports that would focus his subordinates' attention on issues and measures he felt were critical. In fact, he felt that EIS enabled him to be an educator and used it to teach his subordinates to think about profit the way that he did. As with the senior executive in Company B, this president scanned the system extensively and two of his subordinates followed suit. All of the executives in the organization used the system for focused search.

In all of the other organizations combined, none of which had an established scanning expectation, only two EIS scanners were found.

Companies A and C provide evidence of the importance of negative social influences. Company A prided itself on its technical sophistication. Managers had access to whatever information they wanted, albeit often in raw and not easily accessible form. The idea for the EIS had come from the information center, and development continued to be driven by the information center. Everyone else considered the system to be "just another tool in the arsenal," and no one outside the information center touted its benefits. Company C had a long history of frustration with management information. Historically, systems had been developed by controllers and had been ignored by everyone else. The EIS was a second attempt, conceived to replace a failed effort by the systems organization. This time, development had been spearheaded by the controller's division. Once again, no one was touting benefits or encouraging use outside a group that had little credibility in terms of ability to deliver.

Hence, it seems that there is support for a relationship between support for scanning and scanning behavior itself. However, causality is unclear, as the organizations with support for EIS scanning also had the best systems in terms of integration and flexibility.

## Job Characteristics

Of the interviewees, 21 had jobs that were predominantly convergent in nature (e.g., controller, operating manager) and 15 had divergent jobs (e.g., president, strategic planner). Those with divergent jobs were more likely to have a predisposition toward scanning and those with convergent jobs were less likely to have that predisposition. $^{5}$ There was no relationship between convergence/divergence and EIS scanning or focused search behaviors. Hence, as was the case with innovativeness and tolerance for ambiguity, job type was closely related to a predisposition toward scanning generally, but did not guarantee scanning or focused search in the EIS context.

## Discussion

The field study enabled refinement of the research model and, consequently, development of a fuller specification of the circumstances surrounding information retrieval behaviors relating to EIS (Vaughan 1992). The revised model that emerged is shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Revised Research Model

Perceived Improvements in org. performance:

The study found that 75% of the executives in the sample did not use their EIS to scan information; rather, they used them to answer specific questions or to monitor performance. These executives believed their systems provided them with faster access to the same information they always looked at—in other words, the EIS helped to make them (and by extension, their organizations) more efficient. However, some executives scanned information using their EIS without having specific questions in mind. These executives found that their EIS helped them broaden their outlook, challenge their preconceptions, and provided them with more insight into their businesses, thereby helping them to improve organizational effectiveness in addition to improving efficiency. It seems that system characteristics, a predisposition toward information scanning, and strong organizational support all interact to determine how an EIS will be used and how successful it will be as a consequence.

Although the study showed that focused search is linked to improvements in efficiency, it shed no further light on this construct. None of the antecedents tested were related to focused search behavior. Consequently, focused search was dropped from the revised model.

A predisposition toward scanning behavior appears to be a necessary condition for scanning an EIS. However, it is not a guarantee. In the study, system characteristics and social influences also affected the way in which an EIS was used and the impact it was perceived to have on the organization. Without integration, flexibility and/or strong social influences for EIS scanning, even executives with a predisposition toward scanning were not likely to scan the information in their EIS.

A predisposition toward scanning behavior was linked to tolerance for ambiguity and innovativeness, but not locus of control. In addition, those with divergent jobs were more likely to have a predisposition toward scanning than those with convergent jobs. However, it is not possible, from this research, to untangle the causality in the relationship between job characteristics and a predisposition toward scanning. It may be that people who take divergent jobs tend to have a greater predisposition toward scanning, and it may be that people in divergent positions have no choice but to undertake this behavior.

A similar difficulty with causality relates to social influences. Strong support from senior management was related to strong social influences, as was high system quality along the dimensions of differentiation, integration and flexibility. It is not possible to tell from this research if the social influences, the qualities of the system, or a combination of both is responsible for converting a scanning predisposition into EIS scanning.

Some important messages for both executives and information systems researchers emerged from this investigation. Most significantly, this study found that EIS contribute to gains in efficiency much more frequently than to gains in effectiveness. While the EIS promotional literature frequently suggests such systems will enhance organizational effectiveness (enabling organizations to stay ahead of competitors, make better decisions, and so forth), the reality is that this is less likely to occur than more conventional efficiency improvements. For the most part, a lack of strategic intent in the use of EIS was found.

Companies that do want to achieve greater effectiveness should pay attention to the role of the EIS in the scanning behavior of their managers. This study found that EIS users who did not exhibit a predisposition toward scanning were unlikely to use the EIS for scanning either. Managers who did exhibit a predisposition tendency toward scanning might, or might not, use the EIS for scanning. Other factors that influenced the extent to which such managers would scan their EIS included the extent to which EIS scanning was encouraged or expected (social factors), as well as the characteristics of the EIS itself (system factors). These are all things under the control of the organization. An expectation for scanning behavior can be purposefully created and maintained; similarly, EIS characteristics are controllable. Therefore, if EIS scanning behavior is deemed desirable (and this study suggests it should be, since it is linked to both efficiency and effectiveness), the conditions that facilitate EIS scanning can be consciously established.

An implication of this study is that companies wishing to see their EIS employed for scanning should consider a proactive program for training their executives in this mode of EIS use. Only about half of the EIS users who had a predisposition toward scanning actually used their EIS in a scanning mode. Part of the reason for this may simply be that the nonscanners did not have sufficient understanding of their system's scanning capabilities. Furthermore, a program of training in EIS scanning would contribute to the establishment of an organization-wide scanning expectation, which would in turn increase the likelihood of EIS scanning by managers.

Another message for practitioners relates to the characteristics of the EIS. Integration and flexibility seem to be related to scanning behavior, while differentiation does not. Hence, managers and developers should realize that adding more information and applications to an EIS will not necessarily lead to scanning behavior and potential improvements in effectiveness. Rather, cross-functional information and the ability to combine information from diverse sources coupled with latitude in how the information can be used are more important.

In this study, efficiency and effectiveness were employed as the dependent variables. This was done in part because these constructs, so frequently mentioned in the general managerial literature, have strong intuitive appeal for practicing managers. As it turned out, they also proved useful in distinguishing the types of impacts associated with EIS in real organizations: EIS scanning was shown to be linked to both, whereas EIS focused search was only linked to efficiency.

This study adopted a novel approach to characterizing system qualities. Normally, studies of information systems' impacts employ more technology-specific indicators, for example, query response time, number of screens, or perceived user friendliness. The more general characterization in terms of differentiation, integration and flexibility proved to be easily measured, meaningful to managers, and effective at differentiating among the various EIS encountered in the study. As well, two of the three indicators proved to be strongly associated with EIS scanning and, through scanning, with organizational performance.

Finally, this research demonstrated the benefits of interweaving data collection and analysis and the importance of grounding IS research in reality as well as the literature. The findings at the first study site redirected the remainder of the project and resulted in a model that reflects reality better than did the original literature-based model.

The research provides a rich understanding of how these seven systems were used by 36 executives with a wide range of personalities, jobs, and information inclinations. The clear patterns that emerged, in spite of the wide variations found in the seven sites investigated, provide strong initial support for the model and a starting point for further research into the relationships it suggests (McCutcheon and Meredith 1993). The study, however, exhibits some limitations and the results must be treated with caution. First, seven organizations are not statistically generalizable, nor are five or six users in each organization necessarily representative of all the users in those organizations. Second, although statistics were used to test many of the relationships, the sample sizes were small and somewhat susceptible to the vagaries of the data. A broader, confirmatory study is needed to determine if the results found here are generalizable across a wider range of organizations and EIS (Lee 1989). Finally, the notions of efficiency and effectiveness were not theoretically distinguished from each other in this study. Rather, they were allowed to emerge from the interviews. Understanding and explicitly operationalizing their distinctions remains an important area for future research.

Many opportunities exist to extend the model. For example, the relationships between how information is retrieved and how it is used may provide more insight into the impact that EIS have on their organizations. It is plausible that more scanning occurred in this sample than would generally occur in organizations because participants were relatively senior managers whose work has a large “informational” component (Mintzberg 1973). Further research is required to investigate this possibility. Finally, because of the difficulty in data collection, perceived environmental uncertainty was not evaluated in this research. Further work is required to determine how best to capture the construct and understand its relationship to information retrieval behaviors.

The results of this research into EIS and their impact on efficiency and effectiveness should help executives and organizations understand and evaluate the potential these systems have for improving organizational performance. It may also encourage the education of executives in how to use EIS—not just how to access them, but also how to retrieve, think about, and use the information they contain. The results can also help organizations determine whether EIS technology is appropriate for meeting their objectives and if the potential benefits are worth the investment.

## Rationale for and Details of the Research Method

The approach selected to study EIS in organizations was the field study (Stone 1978). While conceptually similar to case studies as a method of analysis, it has some distinct differences. Field studies employ systematic means for obtaining and recording the variables of interest. Case studies, on the other hand, are often less systematic, although more thorough, relying on intensive first-hand observation of the phenomena of interest. In most instances, as in this one, field study data are obtained through questionnaires and/or interviews. As such, field studies tend to intrude less upon the system being studied than case studies, where the risk that data collection will modify the system under study is greatly increased (Webb and Weick 1979).

There are a number of reasons for using a field study rather than a survey or an experiment to investigate information retrieval behaviors (Benbasat et al. 1987). First, because system characteristics and job factors were proposed to play a fundamental role in eliciting information retrieval behaviors, studying EIS outside their natural settings would severely limit the depth to which they could be studied (McBride 1995). Second, although the concept of EIS has been in existence for more than 15 years (cf: Rockart and Treacy 1982), the technology supporting EIS continues to evolve (Frolick 1994). Certainly with the advent of high-performance personal computers, graphical user interfaces, mouse technology, and on-line analytical processing (Newing 1994), the look and feel of EIS have changed dramatically and the way that executives use them has changed in concert (Gillan and McPherson 1992). As such, it is appropriate to study these systems in situ. Third, while experimental research has been successful at assessing various components of the relationship between systems and behaviors (e.g., Chorba and New 1980; Elam and Mead 1990; Todd and Benbasat 1987), it is arguably more important to understand whether or not EIS engender different information retrieval behaviors in context than to determine whether or not these behaviors can be induced experimentally. Finally, given the early state of understanding about the relationships under study and the preliminary nature of the information retrieval constructs themselves, attempting to develop a survey instrument or experimental conditions to uncover or elicit focused search and scanning would have been premature.

A convenience sample of potential field study sites was identified by asking EIS vendors and consultants to provide names of organizations with systems that met the following criteria:

1. The EIS must have been installed for at least a year, and preferably longer, so that usage norms and stable opinions about its usefulness would have been established.

2. A minimum of five members of the executive team with access to the EIS had to be willing to participate in the research.

3. Access to developers and a "hands on" demonstration of the EIS had to be provided.

A total of 19 organizations were approached for participation in the field study. Of these, four did not have systems that met the criteria, six felt the involvement was too great, one could not convince enough senior managers to consent to be interviewed, and one declined without reason. The EIS in the remaining seven organizations were studied in detail.

In order to triangulate our perceptions and their significance (Eisenhardt 1989a), data were collected from four sources: semistructured interviews, paper and pencil surveys, user observation, and memos and reports. (The interview guides and survey instrument are available from the authors.) Both individual and organizational levels of analysis were pursued to understand the interrelationships among individual and organizational impacts. Corroborating evidence provided by several executives in each organization provided a basis upon which to compare organizations to each other and assisted in the determination of behavioral and technical differences across organizational settings. Discussions with several executives in the same organization defined the range of behaviors that were possible within a single context and ensured that the way the concepts being studied had meaning for them in their environments.

Semistructured interviews were used to collect information from executives who used the systems and from those responsible for developing them. A total of 48 interviews were conducted: 36 with users and 12 with developers. Interviews lasted from 45 to 90 minutes and were taped and transcribed for subsequent analysis. For system users, the interviews focused on how the system was used, why it was used that way, and the benefits that accrued from its use. For example, they were asked how much time they spent with their EIS daily, what time of day they used it, whether or not they used it at home, and how often they referred to it over the course of a day's work. If they used the system more than once a day, they were asked what triggered their use. They were also asked to describe how the EIS fit into their overall information retrieval pattern. Did they use it exclusively? As a support to other information sources? For specific kinds of information? Reference was not made specifically to focused search and scanning; rather, the respondents were probed to understand their categories of use.

Data were analyzed in four steps, following Reich and Benbasat (1990):

Assimilation. In the first step, the executive interview data were transcribed onto worksheets. For example, each comment made by an executive user was analyzed to determine if it related to his or her general approach to information, characteristics of EIS use, individual impact, or organizational impact. The interview transcripts were coded according to whether or not the themes under investigation were present, rather than how often they were raised (Berelson 1954). Appendix B contains a sample executive worksheets. In addition to coding the interviews, scores from the supplementary surveys were tabulated.

Interpretation. In the second step, these summaries were interpreted to determine the absence or presence of focused search, scanning, efficiency, and effectiveness. Similar analyses were carried out on the developer transcripts to determine the objectives that drove system development and the attributes and dimensions of the system. These findings were then corroborated with the memos and supporting documentation that was available.

Rating. In the third step, the systems' relative merits were rated. This was accomplished by listing the attributes of each EIS and then rating the EIS relative to each other, with the best EIS receiving the highest rating and the worst EIS, the lowest. Each of the categories was then assigned a value of 1 through 5. For example, extensive information depth received a score of 5 and limited information depth received a 1. Means of individual attribute scores were then calculated. These are reported in Table 3. Appendix C shows a sample EIS worksheet.

Comparison. Finally, comparisons were made among the systems and their users to determine what if anything affected information retrieval behavior. When appropriate, nonparametric statistics were used to support the qualitative research findings.

Two researchers independently undertook the data analysis and classified both individual behaviors and the nature of the systems themselves. There was agreement on all but one of the classifications. After discussion, that disagreement was resolved.

Performance was explored in detail with EIS users to determine whether there were differences among the relationships between the information retrieval behaviors and efficiency and effectiveness. Rather than asking specific questions about efficiency and effectiveness, which might have prompted biased responses, the questions were designed so that executives would describe the benefits that the EIS provided to their organizations. Questions included: What does the EIS do for the organization? Does the EIS make you more competitive? Has the EIS changed the nature of meetings, discussions, and work in the organization? Efficiency and effectiveness were investigated at the organizational level because this is the level that ultimately defined performance. At the individual level, a net gain for one person may be a loss for another. It was felt that executives were capable of making organizational level assessments because of their position and perspective (cf. Geringer and Hebert 1991). Additional questions were designed to probe into the magnitude and nature of specific performance improvements. A final question asked what difference it would make personally and to their organizations generally if the EIS were no longer available.

Assessments of differentiation, integration, and flexibility were obtained from an analysis of the interviews with developers. Specific questions in those interviews revolved around the characteristics that make up these dimensions as outlined in Table 1. For example, developers were asked to describe the type, amount, and currency of the information their systems contained. In addition, a part of each interview focused on the rationale for the system's development, the frequency with which the information within it was refreshed, and the plans and process for enhancement.

Organizational contextual factors were discussed in the interviews with the users with the exception of time orientation. It was operationalized in the supplementary questionnaire. First, executives were asked about social influences vis-a-vis the EIS. This section of the interview consisted of questions regarding perceptions about how useful other people in the organization found the EIS and how they thought it should be used.

Perceived environmental uncertainty was operationalized following Milliken (1987). The executives were interviewed regarding their ability to predict how components of the environment were changing, their understanding of the interrelationships among elements of the environment, their ability to predict the impact that environmental change would have on their organizations, and their ability to predict the outcomes of their decisions. In addition, questions were asked regarding the support the EIS provided to reduce these uncertainties.

Questions about job characteristics explored the degree of convergence/divergence in the executives' tasks. This discussion included whether or not the work was routine in nature, the potential for "right answers" and the typical time frame for decision making. In addition time orientation was measured in the supplementary questionnaire using the scale devised by Lawrence and Lorsch (1967). This is a five point scale aimed at understanding the percentage of time devoted to working on matters with time frames ranging from one week or less to longer than five years.

Data about individual differences were collected via a short survey instrument the executives completed at their leisure after the interview. For the most part, existing scales from the psychology literature were employed. This enabled the use of rigorously validated scales for these constructs rather than having to create and test the scales in this study. Tolerance for ambiguity was tested using the Rydell-Rosen Ambiguity Tolerance scale (Rydell and Rosen 1966). Locus of control was measured using the locus of control questionnaire developed by Levenson (1974). The Kirton Adaptive-Innovative index (Kirton 1976), with continuum endpoints labeled “adaptive” and “innovative,” was used to measure innovativeness. Demographic characteristics were operationalized via single questions.

Time was also spent observing each executive actually using the EIS. These observations were conducted for two reasons: to corroborate the data about information retrieval behaviors obtained in the interviews and to determine if alternate behaviors were being employed. While being observed using the EIS, executives were asked to "think aloud" in order to describe how they were selecting screens to look at, what they saw, and what they planned to do about it (Todd and Benbasat 1987). Once again, comments were recorded on tape for subsequent analysis. Finally, written documentation in the form of memos and reports was used, when it was available, to corroborate interview discussions about changes to the EIS, as well as performance improvements attributable to it.
