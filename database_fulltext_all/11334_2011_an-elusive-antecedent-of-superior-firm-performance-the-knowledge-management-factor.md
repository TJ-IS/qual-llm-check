# An elusive antecedent of superior <sup>fi</sup>rm performance: The knowledge management factor<sup>☆</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

Knowledge management (KM) concepts, principles, and technologies provide a foundation for understanding and building systems for acquiring, assimilating, selecting, generating, and emitting knowledge—a crucial resource of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In the knowledge management community, it is commonly contended that knowledge, and capabilities for processing it, comprise a major resource that can differentiate one <sup>fi</sup>rm from another in the sense of yielding better performance or a competitive edge. However, aside from anecdotes, there has been little to substantiate this contention. Can any empirical link be discovered between a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM success and that <sup>fi</sup>rm's <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance? To develop an answer to this question, we use an independent research company's reports of <sup>fi</sup>rms judged to be highly successful in their KM initiatives, plus related data reported by COMPUSTAT. As an initial investigation of the linkage between KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, as measured by <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratios, this study uses the Matched Sample Comparison Group methodology to evaluate research hypotheses. The analysis reveals a heretofore elusive antecedent of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance—evidence that superior KM performance is indeed a predictor of superior bottom-line performance. This study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature by demonstrating that KM, a basic foundation for IS, is an important factor to consider from the standpoint of achieving strong <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. As such, it suggests that KM furnishes an important context for understanding designs, applications, and possibilities for IS with respect to achieving such performance. In the context of devising and executing KM initiatives, both technological and human treatments of knowledge need to be cultivated and integrated in ways that lead to superior KM performance. This study also contributes to the management literature by going beyond anecdotes and case studies in buttressing the proposition that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM competencies are an important ingredient in that <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance. It solidi<sup>fi</sup>es the raison d'etre for investigating KM phenomena and methods (computer-based and human), both within and across modern organizations. It gives practicing managers evidence that bottom-line bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are indeed associated with superior KM strategy and execution.

## 1. Introduction

Interest in knowledge management (KM) has risen steadily since the 1990s to a point where many <sup>fi</sup>rms regard it as a key element of their operations and strategies. In a related vein, the same period has witnessed rapid growth in the number and diversity of scholars who conduct research in and/or who teach about KM—to a point where it is seen by some as an emergent discipline [24,37,76,78]. In general terms, knowledge management is “an entity's systematic and deliberate efforts to expand, cultivate, and apply available knowledge in ways that add value to the entity, in the sense of positive results in accomplishing its objectives or ful<sup>fi</sup>lling its purpose” [43, p. 593]. Organizations have turned to KM practices and technologies to consolidate, grow, and reconcile the knowledge assets that enable them to compete in today's turbulent business environment.

For a considerable time now, KM has been viewed as being critical for effective execution of an organization's operations [22,30,34,56,64,79]. Practitioners and researchers contend that KM, if done appropriately, can have important strategic consequences for <sup>fi</sup>rms—improving their competitive positions by increasing <sup>fi</sup>rm productivity [87], strengthening agility [29], maximizing intellectual assets [83], fostering customer loyalty [50], enhancing innovation [2,65], generating shareholder value [11], etc.

However, beyond anecdotal evidence and individual case studies, there is only limited quantitative empirical support linking a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM initiatives to its performance [66]. Because systematic empirical investigations of this link are scarce and mostly based on perceptions of embedded participants, there is some doubt about the generality of claims that superior KM performance is indeed an antecedent or predictor of superior <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. That is, this linkage—while thought by many to exist—has been elusive. Accordingly, there are calls for empirical expeditions to settle the issue.

Argote and Ingram [4] theorize that embedding knowledge in people, tools, and processes can result in competitive advantage, and then call for empirical studies to understand conditions under which such initiatives enhance competitiveness. Similarly, the knowledge chain theory, which theorizes that any of nine basic KM activities can be performed in ways that lead to advantages in productivity, agility, innovation, and/or reputation, draws attention to a need for more empirical research into connections between KM activities and competitiveness [44]. More recently, Feng et al. [32] contend that while KM research has concentrated on concepts, plus the application of various technologies for acquiring and storing knowledge, there is a need to study whether KM initiatives can impact <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. In line with this assertion, Tanriverdi [82] argues that a better understanding of possible connections between KM capability and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance depends on both theoretical and empirical research.

Here, we respond to this need by investigating business effects of successful KM initiatives. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the study develops a theoretical link between KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, and empirically examines this association in terms of both pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios. By KM performance, we mean the degree to which an organization's KM activities leverage its resources to meet the goals or ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the purposes of KM initiatives [47]. This view is in keeping with that advanced by Darroch [21]: effective KM allows a <sup>fi</sup>rm to glean greater value from all available resources (not just its knowledge resources [42]), serving as a coordinating mechanism for transforming the resources into capabilities. In gauging KM performance, we do not rely on perceptions of individuals within <sup>fi</sup>rms being examined, but rather on eight years of ratings by large international panels of KM experts independent of the <sup>fi</sup>rms being rated. Similarly, our measurements of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance do not involve perceptions of those embedded within the <sup>fi</sup>rms, but rather accounting-based performance <sup>fi</sup>gures for the <sup>fi</sup>rms. Using an organizational level of analysis, this study investigates the elusive linkage within a multi-industry context, suggesting that the results are likely to be applicable and generalizable across organizations as well as industries [88].

We begin with research background that, as a summary and elaboration of a position paper [47], provides a context for appreciating this study. Covering both theoretical foundations and prior empirical studies, this background leads to a statement of hypotheses. Then, we present the research methodology, followed by a section that describes the results of data analysis. Finally, we discuss implications, contributions, and limitations of this research.

## 2. Background

Knowledge management denotes any process that involves activities of generating new knowledge through derivation or discovery, acquiring valuable knowledge from outside sources, selecting needed knowledge from internal sources, altering the state of knowledge resources, and embedding knowledge into organizational outputs [43]. Mukherjee et al. [69] suggest that knowledge is a critical asset for competitive success, arguing that the way a <sup>fi</sup>rm creates new knowledge determines its effectiveness. The task of attempting to <sup>fi</sup>nd a de<sup>fi</sup>nitive relationship between the effects of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM activities and that <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance is not a trivial undertaking [61]. Even though knowledge is widely regarded as a strategic resource, KM's impacts on business performance are not yet well understood, as <sup>fi</sup>rms seem to be “unable to … estimate the value generated by KM initiatives in terms of impact on business performance” [14, p. 576]. Nevertheless, it is important for a <sup>fi</sup>rm's senior leaders to know if, and the extent to which,

KM performance is linked to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Here, we review progress in this regard.

## 2.1. Knowledge resources and firm performance

In this section, we summarize and expand on a theoretical base outlined by Holsapple and Wu [47]: “the resource-based theory (RBT) of the <sup>fi</sup>rm aims to explain why <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to gain and sustain competitive advantages [3,85]. The theory asserts that the main driver of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is a set of unique <sup>fi</sup>rm resources that are valuable, rare, dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imitate, and non-substitutable by other resources [8,19]. According to the theory, such resources are usually rent-yielding and likely to survive competitive imitation when protected by isolating mechanisms such as time-compression diseconomies, historical uniqueness, embeddedness, and causal ambiguity [8,28,75]. Moreover, an important assumption of the theory is that the resources needed to conceive, choose, and implement strategies are heterogeneously distributed across firms, which in turn are posited to account for differences in firm performance [36].”

Examples of resources considered by RBT include assets that are human, monetary, material, technological, brand recognition, and knowledge [9]. One line of research emanating from RBT not only identi<sup>fi</sup>es a <sup>fi</sup>rm's knowledge as a key source of competitive advantage, but also holds that differences in <sup>fi</sup>rms' performances are due to differences in their knowledge processors, knowledge processes, and organizational knowledge [20,32,61,81]. Knowledge processors are systems—both human and computer-based—that manipulate knowledge resources; knowledge processes are comprised various con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of knowledge manipulation performed by the processors; and organizational knowledge is what is being manipulated [43]. The basic idea is that it is possible to intentionally manage knowledge in a way that yields relatively superior performance. This takes the form of KM initiatives that are designed to squeeze greater value from knowledge resources through more effective knowledge processors (technological and human) and improved knowledge processes involving the manipulation activities of acquisition, selection, assimilation, generation, and emission of knowledge [43].

## 2.2. Previous knowledge management studies

Unlike <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, which is characterized in terms of standard accounting measures, there are no standard means for measuring ingredients that underlie KM performance. Practitioners struggle to ascertain measures that can work in their own respective organizations; to date, these efforts tend to be <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c [77]. Hanley and Malafsky [38] offer a fairly detailed and practical examination of issues such as what to measure, how to measure it, and messages conveyed by those measures. Now, one might ask whether knowledge accounting (KA) can be developed to adequately deal with knowledge valuation concerns. Examining economic and institutional pressures that condition knowledge accounting efforts, Stone and Warsono [80] identify and analyze six alternative KA approaches. They conclude that each extant KA approach is imperfect in the sense of not being simultaneously relevant, reliable, and objective.

Synthesizing the threads that run through prior research on the relationship between KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, some major themes emerge. First, it appears that KM performance is an important factor for managers to consider. Second, many of the studies (but not all) show a positive relationship between some aspect(s) of KM performance and some aspect(s) of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, suggesting the linkage is worthy of further investigation. Third, most of the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on perceptions of independent and dependent variables by persons embedded in the <sup>fi</sup>rms being studied. ## 3. Research hypotheses

A resource-based theory of KM holds that a <sup>fi</sup>rm can outperform competitors by exploiting its knowledge assets (including digital representations), knowledge processors (including technological ones such as decision support systems), and knowledge processes (including those involving technological implementations). Knowledge processors and processes are keys to competitiveness because they determine the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy with which a <sup>fi</sup>rm can apply and leverage its knowledge assets. A <sup>fi</sup>rm's portfolio of knowledge assets, knowledge processors, and knowledge processes are resources that differentiate it from its competitors. Although it is complex to attain/maintain/retain these resources and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to integrate them, <sup>fi</sup>rms that successfully do so tend to enhance their abilities for creating value—in the PAIR directions (e.g., productivity gains via enhanced coordination, agile commercialization of new products, more innovation, and cultivation of reputation). The value that KM contributes ultimately impacts the bottom line of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports [35]. Based on RBT, Holsapple and Wu [47, p.36] take the position that “<sup>fi</sup>rms that are relatively successful in leveraging KM resources also enjoy superior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance in terms of … pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios….cost ratios.” Here, we investigate two of the hypotheses proposed in that position paper:

Hypothesis 1. Superior KM performance is positively related to higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios.

Hypothesis 2. Superior KM performance is positively related to lower cost ratios.

As discussed below, instances of these hypotheses involve speci<sup>fi</sup>c pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios that are commonly used to assess a <sup>fi</sup>rm's <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance.

## 4. Methodology

To test research hypotheses 1 and 2, we need to measure KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Reviewing 108 articles appearing in a decade of KM literature, Chen and Chen [15] identify eight categories of approaches for measuring KM performance. Here, we adopt a collaborative approach to KM performance measurement that involves a large external panel of experts. As for <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures, we adopt commonly used ratios available via an external database. With the foregoing data, we employ the Matched Sample Comparison Group (MSCG) method to test variations of the two research hypotheses.

## 4.1. Matched sample comparison group method

The matched-sample-comparison-group method is an analytical comparison of the levels of some variable under investigation for two samples spanning a time period of interest [67]. The MSCG method has been applied in a variety of <sup>fi</sup>elds, including <sup>fi</sup>nance [53], marketing [55], accounting [6], management [33], and information systems [32]. An important bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the MSCG is that “the performance of the matched control sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms serves as a benchmark and helps remove the confounding effects of extraneous variables and market forces that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm performance” [9, p. 177].

In this study, the two samples are a treatment sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior KM performance and a carefully selected control sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms matched to the treatment sample by size and type. The variable of interest is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio—a pro<sup>fi</sup>t or cost measure used to compare <sup>fi</sup>rm performance of the two groups. We test the hypotheses for such common ratios as: return on assets, return on sales, and total operating expenses to sales. The time period is a <sup>fi</sup>ve-year window.

## 4.2. Most admired knowledge enterprises

As suggested by Holsapple and Wu [48], we identify <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior KM performance by adopting winners in the yearly study of Most Admired Knowledge Enterprises (MAKE<sup>sm</sup>) by Teleos and its KNOW Network [59]. Teleos is an independent KM research company. It operates the KNOW Network—a Web-based professional knowledgesharing network of KM practitioners from around the world. Since 1998, Teleos and the KNOW Network have annually conducted the MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study. Well-known among KM practitioners, this study uses a Delphibased method to determine those organizations (both for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t and non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t) that are “winners” in undertaking initiatives for generating, acquiring, selecting, assimilating, and emitting knowledge in pursuit of competitive advantage. The panel of KM experts is comprised of an international assortment of CEOs, CFOs, and CIOs for Fortune Global 500 <sup>fi</sup>rms, plus about 300 chief knowledge of<sup>fi</sup>cers and KM practitioners who belong in the KNOW Network.

In a <sup>fi</sup>rst round of consensus building, the panelists put together a worldwide list of candidate organizations. In a second round, each panelist nominates up to three of the candidates as being superior-KM-performance organizations within his/her own company's major business sector and up to three additional superior-KM-performance organizations from all business sectors. Firms nominated by at least 20% of the panelists are considered <sup>fi</sup>nalists. In a third round, each panelist rates each <sup>fi</sup>nalist with respect to eight KM performance criteria (KNOW Network 2009):

1. Success in establishing an enterprise knowledge culture. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance in creating and sustaining an enterprise knowledge-driven culture that facilitates the move ment and capture of knowledge.

2. Top management support for managing knowledge. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's senior management leadership in promoting and driving KM initiatives, and in training and developing knowledge workers.

3. Ability to develop and deliver knowledge-based goods/services. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance in developing knowledgebased innovative products, providing high quality knowledge-based services, and delivering knowledge-based cutting-edge solutions.

4. Success in maximizing the value of the enterprise's intellectual capital. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's efforts in continually investing in intangible assets and in making sure their value added contribution continues to increase.

5. Effectiveness in creating an environment of knowledge sharing. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's success in creating and enhancing a work environment that fosters best practice sharing and knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow among employees in a global setting.

6. Success in establishing a culture of continuous learning. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's performance of establishing and sustaining a culture that encourages continuous learning in an organization at all these three levels: individual, team, and <sup>fi</sup>rm.

7. Effectiveness in managing customer knowledge to increase loyalty and value. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's success in managing and exploiting customer knowledge to ensure customer loyalty and to increase lifetime customer value.

8. Ability to manage knowledge in ways that create shareholder value. This criterion evaluates a <sup>fi</sup>rm's capability to manage and exploit knowledge in ways that effectively generate and increase value for shareholders.

Ratings for each criterion are made on a 1 (poor) to 10 (excellent) scale. Scores are summed for each <sup>fi</sup>nalist, with criteria being evenly weighted. Thus, the highest possible score a panelist can give to a nominated organization is 80. The twenty <sup>fi</sup>nalists receiving the highest totals from the panelists are designated as MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners of the year.

## 4.3. Sample selection

Our sample consists of all organizations recognized as MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners in any of the eight years from 1998 to 2005. This yields 42 organizations, with many organizations having been winners in multiple years. Of the 42 organizations, 11 are excluded because their <sup>fi</sup>nancial data are not available via COMPUSTAT (e.g., they are non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t organizations, private organizations, or foreign <sup>fi</sup>rms whose stock is not traded on a U.S. stock exchange). This leaves 31 <sup>fi</sup>rms that have been collaboratively judged by a large expert panel as having exhibited superior KM performance.

Next, we assemble a matching group of 31 control <sup>fi</sup>rms. This is done in a 4-step manner commonly used by other researchers [7,9,32,73]. First, the primary four-digit Standard Industrial Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (SIC) code for each of the 31 superior KM performers is found. Second, for each treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm in the sample, a set of candidate control <sup>fi</sup>rms is formed, comprised of those having the same primary four-digit SIC code. Third, each candidate set is reduced to those <sup>fi</sup>rms that, according to the COMPUSTAT database, have eight-year average sales within 70% to 130% of the corresponding treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm. A target of +/−30% is recommended for matching a control sample to each treatment sample within an industry comparison group [7,9]. Fourth, if there is more than one <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfying the 30%-range target, we chose the one whose eight-year average sales are closest to the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm. If there is no <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfying the 30%-range target for a four-digit SIC code, we identify a corresponding control <sup>fi</sup>rm at the primary three- or two-digit SIC code (there are six such <sup>fi</sup>rms: 3 M, HP, Intel, Microsoft, Sony, Xerox). Finally, if there is no <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfying the 30%-range target at primary four, three, or two-digit SIC code, we then identify a corresponding control <sup>fi</sup>rm based on the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm's non-primary SIC code (there are <sup>fi</sup>ve such <sup>fi</sup>rms: Accenture, Cisco, Canon, Siemens, IBM).<sup>2</sup> Note that no matter what SIC code is used, the 30%-range target is consistently met by all the control <sup>fi</sup>rms in this study. Appendix A presents the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms (superior KM performers) and their control <sup>fi</sup>rms (not superior KM performers).

Similar approaches have been used to match control <sup>fi</sup>rms with treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms in other management studies [26,57]. Here, using the four steps, the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm and control <sup>fi</sup>rm in each pair are “matched” along two dimensions: (1) they belong to the same industry and (2) they are similar in size. The underlying assumption is that some variation in <sup>fi</sup>rm performance can be explained by cross-category and <sup>fi</sup>rm-size differences, but can be controlled by appropriate matching of industry categories and <sup>fi</sup>rm sizes [9]. This is consistent with prior research in accounting, which suggests that industry category and <sup>fi</sup>rm size are critical predictors of the selection of accounting methods for calculating costs such as depreciation and amortization [71].<sup>3</sup>

We conclude that it is reasonable to believe that the <sup>fi</sup>rms in each matched pair have reported results in terms of similar accounting methods, and thus their pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios are comparable [9]. The whole procedure of selecting control <sup>fi</sup>rms provides a solid foundation for this study's basic assumption that potential differences between the two groups of <sup>fi</sup>rms in pro<sup>fi</sup>t and cost ratios are attributed to the primary difference between the two groups: the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the treatment group have been recognized as superior KM performers, while <sup>fi</sup>rms in control group have not.

## 4.4. Comparability test and choice of statistical tests

To check whether the two groups are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different in terms of <sup>fi</sup>rm size, we use several general <sup>fi</sup>rm-size measures—sales, total assets, and number of employees—to compare the two groups. Moreover, as both MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners and the control <sup>fi</sup>rms are skewed toward larger <sup>fi</sup>rms, it is likely that the <sup>fi</sup>rms are highly diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed [9]. To ensure diversi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the two groups are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, we compare the 31 pairs of <sup>fi</sup>rms by using the entropy measure of related diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation [16].<sup>4</sup>

To decide whether to choose a parametric or non-parametric test for the comparisons, we examine the normality of <sup>fi</sup>rm size and entropy measurement data by using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As a powerful normality test, the Shapiro–Wilk method is especially suitable for small samples [31]. As Table 2 shows, results of the tests, which compare sample distributions with a normal distribution, indicate rejection of all the null hypotheses that there are no differences between the distributions of each measurement data set and a normal one. Because the four data sets are not normally distributed, the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test is more suitable for examining differences between the two groups in <sup>fi</sup>rm size and entropy.

Table 3 shows descriptive statistics and comparability test results for the two groups of <sup>fi</sup>rms based on eight-year averages (1998–2005). Compared with single-year observations, averages should be more reliable for the comparability test because they are more normal and less variable. The mean sales for the MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners and the control <sup>fi</sup>rms are \$42.78b and \$40.15b, respectively. That is, mean sales for control <sup>fi</sup>rms are about 94% of the average for treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms. The nonparametric tests show no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in sales, total assets, number of employees, or entropy. Therefore, we conclude that the two groups are well matched for both <sup>fi</sup>rm size and diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

## 4.5. Financial performance halo effect test

Bharadwaj [9] points out that a halo effect may confound data collected via perception-based identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This raises the question of whether there is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance halo effect in our study. That is, there is a possibility that perceptions of Delphi participants about a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM competences may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's past <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. If so, then an alternative explanation is that <sup>fi</sup>rms are MAKE<sup>sm</sup> winners because of outstanding past <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, rather than outstanding KM performance. To address this issue, we use the method of Brown and Perry [12], which is a good <sup>fi</sup>t in contexts where factors extraneous to constructs of interest might strongly affect what results are obtained from large-scale surveys. The Brown and Perry method involves <sup>fi</sup>ve measures of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance: return on assets (ROA), relative market to book value (RMBV), sales, growth, and risk.<sup>5</sup>

Our application of this method is sketched out and illustrated in Holsapple and Wu [48]. If Delphi panel participants perceive high KM performance for a <sup>fi</sup>rm that is, in fact, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's prior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, then there would be notable correlations between winning a MAKE<sup>sm</sup> award and levels of the <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance measures speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by Brown and Perry. We test this with a logistic regression model having a binary dependent variable (winner vs. control) and <sup>fi</sup>ve independent variables for the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance measures.<sup>6</sup> As for the time frame used, we adopt Bharadwaj's [9] convention for analyzing the possibility of a halo effect: we use the <sup>fi</sup>ve years before the <sup>fi</sup>rst inclusion of a MAKE<sup>sm</sup> <sup>fi</sup>rm. Suppose the initial designation of <sup>fi</sup>rm X as a MAKE<sup>sm</sup> winner occurs in 2000, then we use X's performance data for 1995 through 1999 in testing the logistic regression model.

Neither individually, nor collectively, do we <sup>fi</sup>nd statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant evidence that the performance measures affect selection of MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners. Following the Brown and Perry [12] method and the Bharadwaj [9] time-frame convention, we conclude that our treatment sample does not appear to suffer halo effects from prior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance results.<sup>7</sup>

## 4.6. Profit and cost ratios

To measure pro<sup>fi</sup>t performance for the two groups of <sup>fi</sup>rms, we employ <sup>fi</sup>ve common pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios: return on assets (ROA), return on sales (ROS), operating income to assets (OI/A), operating income to sales (OI/S), and operating income to employees (OI/E). Calculated as the ratio of net income to assets, the ROA measure indicates how pro<sup>fi</sup>tably a <sup>fi</sup>rm employs its assets. The ROS measure, which is the ratio of net income to sales, re<sup>fl</sup>ects how much pro<sup>fi</sup>t a <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to generate for each dollar of product sold. The OI/A and OI/S measures focus on operating returns only and exclude income earned by the <sup>fi</sup>rm from other sources such as interest income and investment income [9]. Finally, the OI/E measure indicates the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of every single employee in a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Here, we use three ratios to measure cost performance for the two groups of <sup>fi</sup>rms: total operating expenses to sales (OEXP/S), cost of goods sold to sales (COGS/S), and selling-general-administrative expenses to sales (SGA/S). De<sup>fi</sup>ned as the sum of COGS and SGA, OEXP shows the total expenditure necessary for operating a <sup>fi</sup>rm. As the most general and encompassing measure of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's total cost of operations, it has been widely used in studying <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of a <sup>fi</sup>rm [68]. Similarly, as two of the most widely noted accounting measures, COGS and SGA focus on production and overhead expenses of a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, the second main hypothesis can be organized into the following three sub-hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2.1. Superior KM performance will be positively related to lower OEXP/S ratio.

Hypothesis 2.2. Superior KM performance will be positively related to lower COGS/S ratio.

Hypothesis 2.3. Superior KM performance will be positively related to lower SGA/S ratio.

We contend that testing the foregoing eight hypotheses is of substantial importance for advancing understanding in the KM <sup>fi</sup>eld, and for empirically justifying (e.g., to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's management) the value of giving careful attention to KM strategies and execution.

As a side note, observe that the <sup>fi</sup>ve performance measures used in the halo test are mostly different than the eight metrics used in the foregoing hypotheses. There are several reasons for this. First, we use the classic approach of Brown and Perry [12] for investigating potential presence of a halo effect, including the <sup>fi</sup>ve variables that they specify. Subsequently, this approach has been adopted for numerous studies in such research <sup>fi</sup>elds as management, accounting, and information systems. Second, replacing the classic halo variables with the eight accounting ratios would be a major deviation from prior research. It is not the point of this paper to attempt to justify such a major change in the classic approach to halo testing. Third, and conversely, framing our hypotheses in terms of the <sup>fi</sup>ve independent variables used for the halo test is problematic. These variables refer to <sup>fi</sup>ve-year averages, as prescribed by the classic halo testing method; as such, none is comparable to what we are studying in the hypotheses. Moreover, with the exception of ROA (which we do examine on an annual basis), none of these is one of the standard accounting ratios widely used in the literature as dependent variables for gauging <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (i.e., sales growth and logarithm are not ratios; market/ book is a market measure rather than <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance measure; nor is risk a common measure of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance).

## 5. Results

Suppose a <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM initiatives are suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for it to be recognized as a winner in year t. It is unlikely that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's KM success is restricted to t only, even though it may have been unrecognized as a winner in any other years. This is because results from launching KM initiatives take time to be recognized, although their effects on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance may have already taken hold. There can also be a residual effect of KM performance on <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. Even though a <sup>fi</sup>rm may be replaced by other winners in a subsequent year, its KM initiatives can still bear fruit with respect to <sup>fi</sup>nancial measures (i.e., they do not suddenly stop at the end of t). Thus, we must decide on what neighborhood of t is to be used, thereby resolving the issue of in what speci<sup>fi</sup>c years a winner <sup>fi</sup>rm's <sup>fi</sup>nancial data are to be included in our analysis. Following the method of Olson and Pagano [70], we use a <sup>fi</sup>ve-year window starting with t-1. A <sup>fi</sup>ve-year window to study <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance has been adopted in various disciplines [49,60,74].

Again, a non-parametric test—the Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test—is used to evaluate the research hypotheses. Table 5 shows the Shapiro– Wilk normality test results for these ratios by using treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm data from 1998 to 2005. The statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results, suggest the data sets are not normally distributed and the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test is more appropriate for this study.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that every pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratio in each of the eight years is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher for superior KM performers than for control <sup>fi</sup>rms, thus supporting hypotheses 1.1–1.5. Fig. 1 depicts means of ROA ratios of treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms and control <sup>fi</sup>rms in all eight years.<sup>9</sup> Notice that both curves experience downturns between 2001 and 2002. This is likely due to general economic weakness affecting most <sup>fi</sup>rms regardless of their KM efforts. The ROA decline for treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms began earlier than that of control <sup>fi</sup>rms, continuing from 1999 to 2002. Anecdotally, from our contacts with KM practitioners at the time, it seems that 1999–2000 were tough years for directors of KM initiatives in terms of resources, spending, and positions. To the extent that this was indeed a trend, and to the extent that there is a positive linkage between KM superiority and ROA, the longer decline in ROA may (in part) be due to less emphasis on KM—even among <sup>fi</sup>rms regarded as being exemplars of relative KM superiority. We should expect declines in emphasis to have at least as great an effect on KM-intensive <sup>fi</sup>rms as on other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Be that as it may, the key message in Fig. 1 is that treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms consistently outperform matched <sup>fi</sup>rms in terms of ROA—and the difference is substantial.

Interestingly, this substantial absolute difference between the two curves is fairly stable over the eight-year timeframe of this study. The average annual differences in ROA for treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms versus control <sup>fi</sup>rms range from 0.038 to 0.081, with a mean of 0.062. However, the ratio of average ROA for treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms to that of matched <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fl</sup>uctuates greatly, ranging from 84% in 1998 to 942% in 2003. The treatment ROA average is more than double that of matched <sup>fi</sup>rms in every year but 1998, in spite of exogenous disturbances (e.g., economic weakness, bursting of the e-bubble, natural disasters, terrorism)—and perhaps because of a greater capacity to cope with them due to effective KM. Herein lies a compelling message for top management of any organization: Pay attention to KM, devote resources to KM, and cultivate superior KM initiatives at strategic, operational, and episodic levels.

As for cost ratios, total operating expense to sales (OEXP/S) is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower for superior KM performers than for control <sup>fi</sup>rms in all eight years, thus supporting hypothesis 2.1. This <sup>fi</sup>nding offers at least some evidence that the approaches these top performers take for implementing productionrelated KM initiatives coincide with (and may even contribute to) greater production ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (e.g., less waste as inputs are converted into outputs), more agile production (e.g., recognizing and acting on opportunities for obtaining lower-cost inputs), and/or more innovative production methods (e.g., breakthroughs that yield new and less expensive processes for converting inputs to outputs).

Inconsistent with the prediction in hypothesis 2.3, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the ratio of selling, general, and administrative expenses to sales (SGA/S) is higher for MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners in seven of the eight years, with statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences found in four of these seven years. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with a supposition that much of the spending on KM is captured in an expense category. Unlike COGS, where the costs of unsold products are not reported until they are sold, all SG&A costs must be expensed up front—in the period in which they are incurred. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts realized from such costs may accrue in the same period, or may not appear for quite some time after they are reported. While we do not have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient accounting data to assess the timing of these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, nor to sort out which SGA expenses are for KM and which are not, the results do suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior KM performance tend to incur higher overhead costs per unit of sales. This is in accord with results reported in Bharadwaj's [9] study.

Note that some spending on KM (e.g., purchasing a patent) may be treated as an investment rather than an expense, depending on accounting regulations and <sup>fi</sup>rm practices. Knowledge assets, to the extent they are captured on a balance sheet, affect the denominator of ROA which was examined in hypothesis 1.1. High levels of reported knowledge assets tend to reduce the ROA ratio. But, if these assets can be effectively leveraged via KM practices, then the return from them may greatly increase revenue—and, hence, the ROA. This could be a factor in the relatively high ROA levels experienced by treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 6. Discussion

Based on the resource-based theory of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, plus relevant theory and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from knowledge management research, we have argued that KM can be conducted in ways that drive superior <sup>fi</sup>rm performance as measured in <sup>fi</sup>nancial terms. To examine whether this contention is consistent with real-world phenomena, our study has investigated whether there is a strong linkage between superior KM performance and superior <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance. This is done in a way that avoids possible bias due to reliance on perceptions of embedded informants for measuring independent or dependent variables.

Results of this research furnish substantial, empirically-based evidence that superior KM performance predicts superior pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. The positive results for ROA and ROS suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms successful in leveraging knowledge resources generate more pro<sup>fi</sup>t for each dollar of assets they control and for each dollar of products they sell than <sup>fi</sup>rms of comparable size in the same industry. Moreover, positive results for OI/A, OI/S and OI/E indicate that such <sup>fi</sup>rms derive more operating income from each dollar of their assets and output, and that each employee in such <sup>fi</sup>rms generates more pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

There are three possibilities for interpreting the linkage we <sup>fi</sup>nd between KM performance and pro<sup>fi</sup>tability performance: 1) undertaking the strategies and behaviors that yield superior KM performance constitutes an antecedent for superior pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, 2) superior pro<sup>fi</sup>tability causes a <sup>fi</sup>rm to design and execute superior KM initiatives, or 3) superior pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and superior KM performance are joint consequences of some undetermined third factor. In keeping with resource-based theory, reported anecdotes, and individual case studies, we adopt the <sup>fi</sup>rst interpretation. The second interpretation would be inconsistent with all of these rationales, although our study does not rule it out as a possible explanation. While the third interpretation is a possibility, for the present we opt for the <sup>fi</sup>rst until some such factor can be determined. In short, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings for pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios suggest that superior KM performers have a knack for undertaking KM in ways that produce superior pro<sup>fi</sup>tability.

Seven of these 31 MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners have also been recognized as “IT leaders” by Information Week. These seven companies are 3 M, Chevron, GE, HP, IBM, Monsanto, and Sun Microsystems. The dual recognition may suggest a positive association between IT capability and KM performance. Given the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed link between KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, we argue there may exist a three-link causal relationship from IT capability to KM performance to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. This argument is actually in line with Tanriverdi's [82] <sup>fi</sup>nding that IT capability in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm performance through the mediation of KM competence. Future research is thus needed to look into this three-link causal relationship and to advance our understanding of the roles of IT and KM in <sup>fi</sup>rm performance.

The mixed results for cost ratios suggest that superior KM performers are likely to achieve lower total costs (i.e., OEXP) than <sup>fi</sup>rms of comparable sizes in the same industries, but do not necessarily have lower production costs (i.e., COGS) or overhead costs (i.e., SGA). The non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results for COGS/S in four of the eight years indicate that high success in KM initiatives does not always translate into superior production cost containment. It may well be that excellent KM performance can in some situations lead to new production methods that incur greater production cost in the interest of achieving higher agility (e.g., response ability) or higher reputation (e.g., quality) so as to maximize revenue. While not hypothesized, results for the SGA/S ratio are in line with White and Dieckman's [86] <sup>fi</sup>nding that SGA expenses are highly correlated to gross margin (i.e., sales less cost of goods sold). That is, superior KM performance tends not to be free, but appears to be achieved and sustained via higher overhead costs relative to other <sup>fi</sup>rms of similar sizes in the same industries.

By solidifying the heretofore elusive link between KM performance and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance, this study tells business managers that they should pay careful attention to knowledge management in the interest of bottom-line performance for their <sup>fi</sup>rms. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that they must do much more than merely have some form of KM practice if their <sup>fi</sup>rms are to enjoy <sup>fi</sup>nancial success relative to competitors of comparable size. They should develop a clear strategy for making their KM performance superior (e.g., along the lines of realizing the MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study's eight criteria as described in Section 4.2). Moreover, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings strongly imply that it is indeed possible for managers to implement a KM strategy such that the <sup>fi</sup>rm experiences higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t ratios than its competitors. Similarly, there is a strong managerial implication that managers can implement a KM strategy such that the <sup>fi</sup>rm experiences a lower operating expense ratio than its competitors. There is some evidence that the relative COGS/Sales ratio may also bene<sup>fi</sup>t. However, managers must understand that, as long as the spending that enables KM work is a major expense item, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will likely experience a higher SGA/Sales ratio than its competitors of comparable size.

In short, this study empirically justi<sup>fi</sup>es the prudence and value of giving careful attention to KM strategy and execution. However, it does not give managers details about what their KM strategy should be or how that strategy should be implemented. These form the next step of investigation, given that the rationale for doing so has been empirically established by the <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported here. The MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study's eight criteria do not offer many details, but they deserve consideration as a frame of reference for managers in developing and executing KM strategy. Other studies offer some help in this regard.

For example, Davenport et al. [23] suggest that top management support is critical to KM performance because the implementation of KM initiatives is resource-intensive and the necessary resources are more likely to be available when top management support exists. Kayworth and Leidner [58] propose that developing an organizational culture of continuous learning is important to KM performance because such a culture can facilitate four key KM activities: knowledge creation, knowledge storage, knowledge transfer, and knowledge application. The knowledge chain theory, discussed earlier, identi<sup>fi</sup>es nine categories of KM activity that can be focal points for KM performance that leads in the PAIR performance directions. As guidance for managers, Jones [54] has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed over sixty speci<sup>fi</sup>c kinds of activity that belong to these nine classes. These can be used to assist strategists in devising plans for KM initiatives that aim to yield superior KM performance [40].

The practice of measuring KM performance is an important component for building KM initiatives. Measurement of its knowledge, knowledge processors, and knowledge processes helps a <sup>fi</sup>rm's management understand its strengths and weaknesses for designing, implementing, and evolving its KM initiatives [38,77]. Managers may employ MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study criteria for self-assessment—auditing their strengths, weaknesses, and progress. They may also compare their KM practices and technologies with those employed by <sup>fi</sup>rms that have been recognized as MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study winners (particularly, those in their native industry). Not only does this give a way to benchmark its own KM endeavors, it may also provide a <sup>fi</sup>rm's managers insight into how to improve its KM performance.

## 7. Conclusion

By demonstrating that KM performance has observable direct effects on the accounting-based performance of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, this study contributes to the KM literature by going beyond case studies or surveys in providing empirical evidence of the importance of KM. It contributes to the accounting literature by studying the previously unexplored link between <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance and KM performance. It contributes to management practice by furnishing evidence that superior KM does indeed correlate with superior <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. It contributes to the nascent science of competitiveness [39], which postulates that knowledge and its management comprise a major aspect of the foundation for competitive <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Finally, it also contributes to the MSCG method by providing an extended approach for control <sup>fi</sup>rm selection. The traditional approach helps identify a corresponding control <sup>fi</sup>rm satisfying the 30%-range target at a primary four, three, or two digit SIC code, and thus ensures that the control <sup>fi</sup>rm matches the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm by size (level of sales) and type. However, through the traditional approach, researchers may not be able to identify a corresponding control <sup>fi</sup>rm, as was the case in this study. The extended approach, which uses one of the non-primary SIC codes of the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rm, provides researchers with another opportunity to <sup>fi</sup>nd an appropriate control <sup>fi</sup>rm. Thus, the extended approach should be a good complement to the traditional approach.

With few exceptions, our analysis supports the hypotheses. Those exceptions are illuminating and have plausible explanations. Nevertheless, there are limitations to the study reported here. First, our methodology presumes that <sup>fi</sup>rms in MAKE<sup>sm</sup> winner lists are representative of <sup>fi</sup>rms with superior KM performance. Although this is an independent source of data derived from myriad perspectives, MAKE<sup>sm</sup> study recognition is ultimately based on perceptions rather than objective measures of KM performance. Therefore, the MAKE<sup>sm</sup> assessment is still a proxy and open to questions with respect to its validity (i.e., it may be possible that MAKE<sup>sm</sup> results are subject to an unknown bias of some sort). Second, there is the issue of identifying a well matched control <sup>fi</sup>rm for each of the treatment <sup>fi</sup>rms. For some of the winner <sup>fi</sup>rms, an appropriate control <sup>fi</sup>rm of similar size cannot be found at the primary four-digit SIC level. Consequently, a primary three- or two-digit SIC code, or non-primary SIC code, is employed to identify a corresponding control <sup>fi</sup>rm for these winners. Third, we would prefer to have a larger sample size. The sample size here is comparable to that of other studies dealing with <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [e.g., 9,73]. However, sample size may moderate generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. A fourth limitation is that there are no data publicly available for the eight individual measures that are compounded into the knowledge management success measure for MAKE<sup>sm</sup> winners. Thus, we cannot do more detailed analysis with respect to speci<sup>fi</sup>c KM success characteristics. Even if such details were available, the sample size could limit the ability to gain further useful information.

This study does suggest avenues for future research that can build on what has been presented here. For instance, robustness of the results could be investigated. This could include using a larger sample as new data become available. It could include using additional criteria (beyond industry and <sup>fi</sup>rm size) for determining matched <sup>fi</sup>rms—such as the degree of outsourcing, cost structure, or supply chain characteristics. It could include using any of these as a matching criterion instead of industry and <sup>fi</sup>rm size. It could include analyses that involve windows other than the <sup>fi</sup>ve-year period adopted from prior literature in other <sup>fi</sup>elds. It could include an adjustment of the matched comparison method to use multiple matching <sup>fi</sup>rms, rather than focusing on a matched pair.

Another research direction is to explore the means whereby superior KM performance can be achieved. This depends on discovering key variables that link KM performance to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, that link performance antecedents with performance outcomes, and that take into account contingencies. The ontology of knowledge management [43] is an example of a starting point for identifying such variables. In a related vein, models for gauging KM performance need to be developed so that both researchers and practitioners can take into account the most important KM resources, plus a <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to extract value from them. Linkages between such models and models of <sup>fi</sup>rm performance can then be investigated to better understand details of strategies, operations, and tactics for achieving superior KM performance in the interest of improved <sup>fi</sup>rm performance—all within an encompassing competitive environment that may be dynamic or stable, global or local, heavily regulated or not, in an economic downturn or upturn.
