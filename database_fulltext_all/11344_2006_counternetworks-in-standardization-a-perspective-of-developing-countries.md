# Counter-networks in standardization: a perspective of developing countries

Ping Gao\*

\*Institute for Development Policy and Management, School of Environment and Development, The University of Manchester, Harold Hankins Building, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9QH, UK, email: ping.gao@manchester.ac.uk

Abstract. Present information systems studies focus on technology from developed countries. Little is known about the development and implementation of indigenous technology in the developing world. This paper investigates China’s experience of developing and deploying wireless local-area network (WLAN) standards. First, drawing upon actor-network theory, I interpret WLAN Authenti cation and Privacy Infrastructure (WAPI) standardization as a process of the actor-network formation. Specifically, different actors were enrolled into two counter-networks, namely the defensive network and the challenging network. These actors and the two counter-networks struggled to dominate the interest inscriptions in the strategy of WLAN standardization. China’s WAPI initiative failed because it could not establish a strong defensive network. Furthermore, I analyse the social, institutional and technological elements that determined the process of WAPI standardization. This case study demonstrates that counter-network is a useful concept to analyse the mechanism of actor-network formation. My analytical model in which the macro contexts are connected with the micro network dynamics can be drawn upon by other actor-network studies on technology design and implementation. Practical implications for developing countries to develop the indigenous technologies have been given.

Keywords: actor-network theory, China, counter-network, developing countries, standardization. WLAN

## INTRODUCTION

Technology standardization is the critical research frontier for information systems (Lyytinen & King, 2006). However, existing literature focuses primarily on technology from the developed world (Walsham, 2001), and little is known about the development and implementation of indigenous technology in other countries. This paper contributes to the literature by focusing on developing countries. Specifically, I analyse a case in which China has made an effort to develop wireless local-area network (WLAN) standards, and has tried to use them to replace the incumbent technologies controlled by foreign companies.

The principal WLAN standards in use are the 802.11 series of the Institute of Electrica and Electronic Engineers (IEEE), the so-called Wireless Fidelity (Wi-Fi) technology. Because a wireless network broadcasts messages using radio, it is particularly susceptible to eavesdropping. Hence, security is a challenge in the standardization of WLAN products. The security flaw has been a major concern for users, especially business users, when adopting the Wi-Fi technology. The IEEE has made efforts to improve it, but the progress has been slower than that which the market requires (Crockett, 2004; Yang et al., 2004; Sprint, 2005). Consequently, organized by the Ministry of Information Industry (MII), China developed its own security scheme for WLAN called WLAN Authentication and Privacy Infrastructure (WAPI). While the technological advantage of WAPI has not been seriously challenged, its implementation method has received strong objections. As a result, WAPI has failed to be implemented.

According to the 2004 General Accounting Office survey of US companies with a presence in China, standards and certification as represented by the case of WAPI are the most serious issues on an extensive list of World Trade Organization (WTO) commitment areas (Updegrove, 2004). The analysis of China’s experience of standardizing WLAN products is important from both a theoretical and practical perspective. In contrast to current standard studies that treat standard development and implementation separately (Gerst et al., 2005), this paper analyses China’s attempt to standardize indigenous WLAN technology in an integrated approach. I wil identify which factors have shaped the process of WAPI development and implementation, and how these factors have affected this process. Important lessons for the developing countries to improve their standardization system will be derived from this research.

This paper has theoretical as well as practical contributions. Actor-network theory has gained increased attention by academics, including standardization scholars (e.g. Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997). In line with Klein & Kleinman (2002) and Walsham (1997), my research results argue that researchers should combine actor-network concepts with other theories – in this paper the classification of elements influencing the standardization process by Lyytinen & King (2002) – and relate the micro dynamics of actor-network to the macro contexts. The analytical model of this paper, in which the social, institutional and technological contexts are connected with the standardization process, can be drawn upon by other actornetwork studies on technology design and implementation. Using this model, I have observed that China, influenced by the planned economic system and encouraged by the populism and nationalism mode, has adopted a closed process of standardization rejecting foreign cooperation, despite having no technological capability of implementing new WLAN standards. Consequently, China has proposed an immature WLAN technology and an unfeasible plan of implementing it. Foreign competitors have formed a strong challenging network that has forced the Chinese government to indefinitely postpone implementing WAPI standards. I argue that developing countries should set up a Research and Development (R&D) management system that values tech-neutralism. This case study demonstrates actor-network theory, especially its concept of counter-network, enables us to better understand the standardization process compared with the nationalism perspective currently being used (Braa et al., 2004; Suttmeier, 2005).

This paper has five sections. The second section introduces the theoretical ground and analytical framework, and the method for data collection and analysis. The third section presents the WAPI case. The fourth section analyses the social, institutional and technological constraints on the practice of WAPI standardization in China. The last section discusses the characteristics of the Chinese strategy of standardization, and derives theoretical and practical implications from the case analyses for standard development and implementation in developing countries.

## RESEARCH APPROACH

In this section, I justify my case selection, clarify the theoretical ground of standardization study, develop my analytical framework, and introduce the methods of data collection and analysis.

## Case selection

The purpose of this paper is to understand standardization in the globalization context from a developing country perspective. Following the suggestions of Glaser & Strauss (1967) that researchers should select a case based on their theoretical purpose, I focus on the Chinese project of WAPI standardization.

Driven by the globalization trend, more and more developing countries play important roles in information technology development. Unfortunately, the mainstream research conducted solely in the context of developed countries cannot readily be applied to the developing economies for such reasons as dissimilarity in economic, political and technological environ ment (Walsham, 2001). This paper provides guidance for the future practice of standardization in developing countries, as seen from the Chinese experience.

With the world’s biggest market, the largest consumer base, and the strongest growth trajectory, China presents an interesting case to study (Zakaria, 2005). Particularly, China distinguishes itself from other developing countries for its pace-setting role in technology standardization. In recent years, the Chinese government has supported its information industry to compete with the international giants in technology development. Here are some significant examples. China has made efforts to create a national system for high-definition video disc to compete with the Digital Video Disc as a foreign standard. Consequently, on 8 July 2004, the national standard called Enhanced Versatile Disc was officially published. China has worked hard to promote a domestic technology for interoperability among electronic devices in the home. As a result, in June 2005, China published the Intelligent Grouping and Resource Sharing standard, which was meant to compete with the similar overseas standards developed by the Digital Home Working Group. China has worked on its own Audio and Video Coding Standard to compete with the international Moving Pictures Expert Group Standard. In software, China has developed its Linux-based office applications to substitute the Microsoft system used in government. Furthermore, China is one of the main stakeholders in the third-generation (3G) mobile telecommunications technology. International Telecommunica tions Union (ITU) has ratified three 3G standards based on the Code Division Multiple Address (CDMA) method. The Chinese version Time Division Synchronous CDMA (TD-SCDMA) competes with the European version CDMA2000 and the US version Wideband CDMA (Henten et al., 2004; Linden, 2004; Suttmeier & Yao, 2004; Shen, 2005).

The Chinese experience in technology standardization has yet to be explored by information systems researchers. In this paper I focus on WLAN, another area in which China has made an effort to take a dominant role. There are several reasons for me to select the WAPI case. First, WLAN is a promising technology with fast diffusion in the market and hence deserves my research attention. More and more people use Wi-Fi for its convenience in establishing ad hoc internet connections and peer-to-peer networks. In 2004 in the USA, 12.9% of inhabitants had a broadband connection, and about 10% of these broadband users used Wi-Fi. In the UK, the broadband penetration totalled 11.9%. Of this figure, 6% consisted of Wi-Fi users (UNICTTF, 2005). Moreover, in the USA in 2005, more than 50% of companies had wireless access capability, and by 2007 it is expected to reach 96% (Sprint, 2005). In addition, the WLAN access to the internet tends to become popular in public areas. In the USA during the first quarter of 2005, there were 70 wireless internet access points per million of the population. In the UK, this figure was 140 (Analysys, 2005). Particularly, in 2005, McDonald’s offered wireless internet access in 6000 stores, and Starbucks installed hotspots in more than 4300 coffee branches (Beucke, 2005).

Second, WAPI standardization involves the debate between the Chinese and foreign stakeholders in the WLAN market on issues like the necessity of having several competitive standards, the method of standard development and implementation, and the role of the government in standardization. A deep analysis on the WAPI case enables us to understand the reality and future challenges that the developing countries will face in developing and adopting the indigenous technologies. Third, in contrast to the present standardization literature that is largely about successful experiences, this paper reports on a failed case. Such a case is particularly useful to developing countries when drawing lessons for their future standardization efforts

## Theoretical ground

By nature, standardization is an issue of technology study at the core of information systems research. People hold different viewpoints on the relationship between humans and technology, the so-called ‘agency’ problem (Rose et al., 2005). Accordingly, three prominent approaches to technology study can be distinguished: technological determinism, social determinism and socio-technological methods. The technological determinists argue that the development of technology is based on its economic value. The social determinists focus on the social shaping of a technology (Kling, 1980; Bijker, 1995). Actor-network theory belongs to the socio-technological school, which consists of and links together human beings and technology. It talks about the heterogeneous nature of actor-networks. Further, actor-network theory addresses the issue of technology development by looking at actors who possess enough power to change the direction of technology development (Callon, 1991; Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997).

Actor-network theory has gained increasing attention from researchers trying to understand the socio-technological nature of information systems (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). The early work of information systems scholars is represented by the collection of papers in Proceedings of IFIP Working Group 8.2 Conference in 1996, ‘Information Technology and Change in Organizationa Work’ (Orlikowski et al., 1996). A recent set of illustrative applications of actor-network theory in information systems studies are contained in a special issue of Information Technology & People (Hanseth et al., 2004). Technology standardization is one of the research issues where actor-network theory has played a role (Lyytinen & King, 2002; Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002).

A standard is a set of technical specifications that adhere to a particular technology, and standardization is the process of developing, ratifying and implementing the standard (Gers et al., 2005). Standardization involves interwoven networks of technological and social elements. The complexity of such networks cannot be accounted for simply by invoking eithe social or technological factors (Fuller, 2000; Lyytinen et al., 2007). Actor-network theory is a powerful tool to analyse the development and diffusion of standards as a socio-technological process (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). Here are some examples of such research efforts. Monteiro & Hanseth (1996) have successfully applied the inscription concept of actor-network theory to examine the construction of information infrastructure based on standards. In disclosing the historically negotiated process on the design of the first-generation mobile telecommunications standards, Lyytinen & Fomin (2002) notice diverse social groups of actors, i.e. government organizations, engineers, entrepreneurs, consumers, etc., have to be enrolled and their interests must be aligned. Faraj et al. (2004) clarify how actors have acted and reacted to each other and to the emergent technological definition of the internet browser. They observe how this spiral development pattern incorporates complex interplay between different actors around the beliefs on what a browser is. They attribute the complex interdependence of disparate elements of the Web browser as it is known today to the attempt of actors to skew the development process to their advantage. Allen (2004) explains the early development in the personal digital assistant technology by exposing the enrolment strategies of different actors.

Potentially, actor-network theory offers an approach to socio-technological change. It is a technique that can bypass the traditional divide between micro- and macro-level analyses (Latour, 1999). Nevertheless, the criticism remains that, in practice, people emphasize micro level analysis of actors’ strategies at the expense of larger social processes. In other words, the current actor-network studies focus on the micro dynamics of network building but pay little attention to the influence of institutional or social routines on it (Allen, 2004).

Because of its non-contextual nature in application, actor-network theory has mainly served as a mere methodology of describing an artefact as a result of network building and social interaction. As such, the strength of actor-network theory has been largely limited to providing some new terminologies to describe the social construction of technology (Winner, 1993). In fact, the context analysis at the macro level is critical for an actor-network study. An actornetwork analysis should be supported by a larger, macro-level analysis of the context (Walsham, 1997; Klein & Kleinman, 2002).

Therefore, actor-network research in general, and its application in technology standardization issues in particular, faces the challenges of improving methods so that its analytical power can be better harnessed (Walsham, 1997). The question then is how to conceptually bridge the divide between micro- and macro-level analyses, while still preserving the unique strengths of an actor-network style analysis (Allen, 2004). One solution is to incorporate other theories or concepts into actor-network methodology. Specifically, the relevant theories and concepts are to be used to define the contexts and thus bridge the gap between actors and the structura elements (Walsham, 1997; Klein & Kleinman, 2002).

In using actor-network theory, there also exist the concerns of promoting its development. Presently, most of the actor-network papers refer to traditional concepts of this theory. In particular, a number of researchers focus on the details of the micro process by using Callon’s (1991) description of the four translation moments relating to problematization, interessement, enrolment and mobilization. In fact, actor-network theory is not a stable and unified body of knowledge. Its developers and other researchers frequently revise or extend elements of this theory (Walsham, 1997). As a result, although actor-network theory has some basic elements, which have remained relatively stable over the past two decades of its development and use, some new concepts have appeared in use (Law, 1992). For example, the concept of counternetwork is promising. It may potentially draw the attention of researchers for its capability of explaining the mechanism of the actor-network process. This can be demonstrated by the significant work of Braa et al. (2004). They use this concept to describe the politics of health information systems.

In conclusion, actor-network theory is an appropriate tool for standardization study. However, researchers need to address the methodological challenge of neglecting contex analysis so as to take full advantage of this theory in disclosing the mechanism underpinning actor-network dynamics. They should also consider using the explanatory power of the innovative concepts of actor-network theory. These concerns will be taken into account in my research design.

## Analytical framework

The aim of this paper is to disclose the social interaction around the WAPI technology, providing insights into the standardization mechanism in developing countries. The actornetwork theory suits my initiative and hence I use it as an analytical tool. Specifically, one potential application of this theory is the investigation of the process whereby the respective interests of different human and non-human elements are aligned into a social and technological arrangement or artifact – in this paper the WAPI standards and their implementation method (Walsham & Sahay, 1999).

‘Successful networks of aligned interests are created through the enrolment of a sufficient body of allies and the translation of their interests so that they are willing to participate in particular ways of thinking and acting that maintain the network’ (Walsham & Sahay, 1999, p. 42). The actor-network theory examines the motivations and actions of human actors that align their interests around non-human actors. ‘Inscription’ and ‘translation’ are key concepts for understanding the interest alignment to form an actor-network. An inscription is a form of anticipated characteristics which actors, in one way or another, try to build into an artefact Furthermore, there are several aspects to inscription: What is inscribed? Who or which type of actor tries to inscribe it? How or in which form is the interest inscribed? What is its strength, or how difficult is it for an actor to realize the inscription (Callon, 1991)? Translation implies that an actor reinterprets or appropriates the interests of other human actors and the interests embedded in non-human actors according to one’s own interests, and then has these interests represented in the inscription. The actors’ interests are flexible and can be translated, thus enabling the interest alignment and the maintenance of an actor-network (Monteiro & Hanseth, 1996).

Politics exist in the process of interest alignment of competing agenda (Bowker & Star, 1999). Further, Castells (2000) gives us the notion of counter-network that describes how different actors set up contradictory networks, namely counter-networks (Braa et al., 2004). My first task is to disclose the process whereby actors struggle to dominate the network building, and finally form a common actor-network through interest translation by actors and the compromise of two counter-networks, which I hereby call defensive network and challenging network.

In conducting an actor-network analysis in the present research, it is necessary to place the investigated phenomena of interest alignment in the specific contexts (Boland & Schultze, 1996; Walsham, 1997; Klein & Kleinman, 2002). As Rose et al. (2005, p. 146) put it, the process of actor-network formation ‘cannot be understood in isolation from the situational conditions which both make it possible, and frame its subsequent interpretation.’ In context categorization I am informed by the work of Lyytinen & King (2002) that identifies three factors influencing a standardization process: innovation system, market place and regulatory regime. Here the innovation system represents the technological context, which is the competencies and resources of R&D that are capable of developing novel technologies. The regulatory regime is the institutional context, namely authorities, administration systems, laws and policies that relate to standardization activity. The market place is the social context in which the standard is developed and implemented. My second task is to explain the actor-network dynamics in the WAPI standardization process by positioning it in the technological, institutional and social contexts. ## Methodology and data analysis

My goal in studying China’s initiative of WAPI standardization was to determine why certain outcomes emerged as a function of their environment. This is clearly a complex, emergent phenomenon. Hence, I have adopted a contextual, process-oriented research methodology (Holmes & Poole, 1991; Langley, 1999). I will start with the process analysis. In the third section, ‘The dynamics of counter-networks: micro analysis’, using the terminology of actornetwork theory as the unifying lexicon of analysis, I will develop a process-oriented narrative of WAPI standardization in China. I will trace the process that new actors have been enrolled into the defensive or challenging network, and their interests have been aligned there. I will disclose how the two counter-networks have fought with each other to dominate the formation of an actor-network (Figure 1).

Through this narrative it is possible to reveal how events, activities and choices are ordered over time (Walsham, 1993). Specifically, according to critical events, the whole process is divided into three distinct phases. In the first phase, WAPI standards were published. The second phase was characterized by the fierce debate between the Chinese standard developers and international stakeholders in the WLAN market on the compulsory implementation of WAPI technology. In the third phase, China decided to indefinitely postpone the WAP implementation.

The key point of my methodology is to link the macro environment with the micro dynamics of counter-networks. After describing the three stages of WAPI standardization process in the next section, in the fourth section I identify the technological, institutional and social elements that influence this process (Lyytinen & King, 2002).

## Data collection

As mentioned above, this paper focuses on the WAPI standardization process, which is a complex and emergent phenomenon. My main research task is to find connections between the specific social, institutional and technological contexts and chosen strategies of developing and deploying WAPI standards (Figure 1). Hereby I need to capture the process of WAP standardization, analyse the dynamic contexts within which the standardization took place, and explore accounts from key stakeholders concerning the contents and motivations behind this process at different stages (Walsham, 1993). WAPI standardization involved a defensive network and a challenging network, formed by actors from China and the USA, respectively. With Chinese as the mother tongue and English as the working language, I was able to access the original data sources and collect the data in different ways.

For the past 2 years, China’s initiative of implementing WAPI has been the focus of domestic and foreign media, researchers, governments and the information industry. To make use of the WAPI events to increase the ‘click rate’ by customers on it, Sina, the most popular Chinese internet portal, opened a Bulletin Board System entitled the China Information Industry Standard Focal Channel (see the website http://tech.sina.com.cn/focus/wlan\_gb/). It started operation in July 2003, shortly after China announced compulsory WAPI implementation. A discussion forum was set up in which people from different backgrounds, including WLAN users, information technology professionals and scholars participated in the debate. Sina also uploaded news reports from other media about the WAPI standardization process. It posted the government decrees and statements concerning WAPI online. By 9 August 2005, 536 documents were collected by Sina. Moreover, Sina became the arena for the government to publish the national policy. For example, on 16 February 2004, a key official responsible for the WAPI project was invited to have an interactive dialogue with the public. On 22 April 2004, the official statement concerning the postponement of WAPI implementation was first published through Sina.

Sina provided a valuable source of preliminary data for me to grasp the process of WAPI standardization, and observe the major technological, institutional and social factors driving the WAPI standardization. Given that the authoritative media in China was very conservative, Sina enabled the author to consider different perspectives avoiding the objective bias in interpre tation (Klein & Myers, 1999). Yet, for the research purpose, Sina as a data source lacked credibility. Hence, I only used it to identify the original data sources for analysis. I used briefings from the authoritative institutes of China and the USA, and the archival documents of government decrees and statements. These files were usually available on the websites of the relevant authorities. Moreover, I paid attention to English data sources such as China Daily, Electronic Engineering Times and InformationWeek. Indexed in ABI INFORM. a recognized academic database, these outlets followed the WAPI events. In particular, the China Daily, published by the State Council of China, was an important reference to the background of policy making in China, while the Electronic Engineering Times and InformationWeek helped me understand the foreign perspectives.

Consequently, from late 2003 to mid-2005, I was able to set up a research database consisting of 211 printed documents. As per Figure 1, they are categorized into two types: the process data and the context data. Before using them, I performed cross-validation on the data from multiple sources (Klein & Myers, 1999). As a demonstration of cross-validation, I used multiple quotations whenever possible in the analysis and discussion sections of the paper.

To further improve the reliability of the above accounts, other data sources were used to triangulate the evidence (Klein & Myers, 1999). I even worked in China’s telecommunications administration for several years. This personal experience helped me establish close connections with key actors in the Chinese telecommunications market. Thus, I was able to interview key players concerning WAPI standardization (see Table 1 for an introduction to my informants with pseudonyms, and a summary of interviews). To capture different perspectives and to avoid biased interpretation, I selected people with different backgrounds as my informants. As experts on China’s telecommunications policy, standardization and R&D management or WLAN technology, these informants provided valuable information, helping me clarify the events of WAPI standardization and understand the factors behind them.

Interviews were conducted in two ways. First, during the collection of preliminary data, when I found that there were important events taking place, I phoned my informants to find answers to problems raised and confirm the credibility of the ‘news’ from the public sources. Each phone call lasted between 15 and 30 minutes. Afterwards, I made notes in the margins of the relevant documents. As a consequence, 37 files containing interview notes were compiled.

## THE DYNAMICS OF COUNTER-NETWORKS: MICRO ANALYSIS

Following my framework shown in Figure 1, in this section I delineate the micro process of WAPI standardization in China. Table 2 summarizes the timeline of key events.

## June 2001 to October 2003: WAPI development and publication

The incumbent Wi-Fi technology did not offer a good security mechanism for users and hence was destined to be eliminated from the market. Specifically, Wi-Fi used a poor security solution, requiring all users to share a single common key, which was kept in a softwareaccessible location. As such, it could easily be broken. On the one hand, more and more individuals were ready to use wireless access to the internet and the WLAN market was going to boom. On the other hand, the security flaw of Wi-Fi restricted the interests of enterprises, government branches, etc. in WLAN services (Crockett, 2004; Sprint, 2005). In these social and technological contexts, to protect more users from being locked into this inferior technology and to promote the growth of the collective user market, Ministry of Information Industry, China (MII) was interested in quickly deploying new WLAN standards with better security solutions. Consequently, in June 2001, MII published an R&D plan of drafting national and industrial standards in wireless broadband fields, including two projects about WLAN security. Initially, BWIPS had 10 members: the China Electronic Standard Institute and the National Radid Monitoring Centre under MII, the Research Centre for Commercial Key of China affiliated to the Standard Administration of China (SAC), five state universities, and two companies with close relations with MII. Shortly thereafter the most important Chinese information technology producers, such as Founder, Huawei and Lenovo, that were in the leading positions in the Asia-Pacific region, joined in to make a total of 26 BWIPS members. MII also appointed two liaison officials from its Division of Standardization, Department of Science and Tech nology to BWIPS (see BWIPS homepage at http://www.chinabwips.org/).

On 16 May 2002, SAC issued Decree Number 41, which included the two projects undertaken by BWIPS in the issuing plan of national standards during 2002–2003. The standards were given the titles of GB15629.11 and GB15629.1102, which involved, respectively, the media access control layer and physical layer of WLAN network (SAC, 2002).

By the end of 2002, the first versions of these two standards were finished. These standards adopted a new security solution called WAPI, which was composed of two parts: WLAN Authentication Infrastructure (WAI) and WLAN Privacy Infrastructure (WPI), which encoded the user identity and transmitted data, respectively. On 26 December 2002, the Office of the State Commercial Cryptography Administration (OSCCA) ratified the security algorithm of WA and WPI (OSCCA, 2002).

On 8 January 2003, MII organized an evaluation of these two standards with votes being cast by 23 people from 12 organizations affiliated to MIl and SAC. The expert comments were as follows: WAPI standards were based on advanced technologies; they were practical to combat the security flaws of current WLAN solutions; and they met the specific requirement of the domestic market. In conclusion, it was felt that the implementation of WAPI technology would promote WLAN market development in China (Pan & Fu, 2004).

Consequently, according to the application from MII, on 26 May 2003, SAC published these two technological solutions as national standards, effective from 12 May. The decree alleged that they were based on incumbent international standards ISO/IEC9902.11 and ISO IEC8802.11b, and their design considered the interconnection of WLAN products (SAC, 2003).

On 9 July 2003, a joint meeting was held to formally announce the publication of WAPI standards. Officials from eight authorities, including MII, SAC, Administration for Quality Supervision, Inspection and Quarantine (AQSIQ), Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST), State Reform and Development Commission (SRDC), etc. participated. In the press conference, they introduced the technology advantage of the WAPI standards and outlined the market benefit they might have for the Chinese information industry. They pledged that because WAPI implementation concerned the national interests of security, they would continuously support it (Bo, 2003; see also BWIPS website for the briefing). This meeting did not stress how to implement the WAPI technology, which turned out to be the top concern of foreign actors in the WLAN market.

In short, WAPI was a government project, and WAPI standardization was managed through a planned process. China claimed that WAPI provided better security solutions than Wi-Fi and would therefore be implemented.

## November 2003: announcing the schedule of WAPI implementation

Mannion & Clendenin (2003) described the situation as follows: ‘Though China’s Wi-Fi standard has been nearly 2 years in the making, the WAPI encryption algorithm seems to have caught the industry by surprise.’ IEEE wanted to maintain its control on WLAN standards, for which it was working on the 802.11i version, aiming to provide higher-level security and to substitute the incumbent standards (Yang et al., 2004). On 23 November 2003, Paul Nikolich, Chairman of the IEEE 802 Local and Metropolitan Area Network Standards Committee, wrote a letter to the SAC Chairman and MII Minister. He wrote: ‘We are concerned that mandatory use of the standard would prohibit the use of 802.11 standard products and thereby limit choice and increase costs of users.’ He argued that the Chinese security standard for wireless networks could fracture Wi-Fi, the de facto WLAN standard, which meant its implementation could undermine efforts to develop a global standard for WLAN and drive up the cost of network equipment for end users. Paul Nikolich acknowledged that 802.11 security should be improved, and offered to engage Chinese authorities on this subject. He promised that IEEE was open to the possibility of incorporating WAPI into 802.11i to avoid splitting the WLAN product markets into two (IEEE, 2003).

Unfortunately, the suggestion of IEEE did not receive attention from the Chinese side. On 26 November 2003, AQSIQ and SAC jointly issued Decree Number 110, which required that from 1 December 2003, it was prohibited to produce, import and sell WLAN products that did no comply with WAPI standards. On 1 December this was modified by Decree Number 113, which granted a new deadline of 1 June 2004 (AQSIQ and SAC, 2003a; 2003b).

These decrees signalled that China would refuse to adopt Wi-Fi that needed technological improvement for better information security. The corresponding problem with WAPI was that it was a proprietary protocol controlled by the Chinese government. According to the ‘Commercial Encryption Management Regulation' that was published as Decree Number 273 by the State Council in October 1999 (State Council, 1999), the WAPI algorithm was a national secret and hence could only be authorized to specific Chinese companies. Eleven such Chinese companies, including Huawei and Lenovo, were designated by the government to have the algorithm (see BWIPS webpage for a complete name list of these firms). Foreign equipment vendors that wanted to sell WLAN products in China were required to license WAPI through a manufacturing agreement with one of these Chinese companies (NRCCA, 2003). To make matters worse, there was only a 6-month grace period, until 1 June, to comply with the mandatory standards (AQSIQ and SAC, 2003b). This touched off strong reaction from foreign stakeholders.

To protect their vested interests, the industry giants dominating the WLAN market initiated the challenging network to resist WAPI implementation. They first voiced their objection through the US Information Technology Office (USITO). Located in Beijing, USITO was a trade group designed to protect the interests of the US information industry in China. USITO concluded that whatever national security argument China might give, the way of implementing WAPI would involve national protectionism. Specifically, USITO addressed the concerns that foreign products would be locked out of China’s WLAN market if they could not reach an agreement with a local partner. Because the Chinese firms, many of which were competing with foreign vendors, were not under any obligation to license WAPI to their foreign counter parts, this was possible or they might charge too high a fee for authentication (Mannion & Clendenin, 2003; Zhu, 2003).

The Chinese requirement to compulsorily implement WAPI was strongly against the interests of Broadcom, the largest WLAN chip producer in the world. On as early as 27 January 2004, its CEO, Alan Ross, told Reuters (Sorid, 2004): ‘We are not playing their game. We are not going to lose our technological edge through the risky disclosure of our intellectual property. To the extent that the only way to enter the Chinese market is using their encryption scheme, we are not committed to doing that today.’ Soon after, on 30 January 2004, Wi-Fi Alliance declared that it was sharing interests with Broadcom and decided to reject WAPI. Its chairman, Dennis Eaton, criticized the WAPI standards as being only favourable to Chinese firms, as the encryption algorithm was controlled by BWIPS composed of Chinese organizations. The WAPI implementation method forced foreign suppliers to produce their chips in China, which led to the concern that their confidential techniques might be leaked to their Chinese competitors. Dennis Eaton threatened that if compromise could not be reached, its members would stop shipping WLAN equipment to China after 1 June (Liu B, 2004).

In February, 2004, more powerful actors were enrolled into the challenging network. Key figures from the Information Technology Industry Council (ITI), the Chamber of Commerce, the Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA), the National Association of Manufacturers (NAM) and the US–China Business Council (USCBC) met in Washington. These key US government and industry bodies made an effort to squelch WAPI. ITI president, Rhett Dawson, declared that it was unacceptable for foreign manufacturers to be expected to co-produce WAPI products with Chinese competitors to meet a unique regulation (State Council, 1999). The NAM delegate described WAPI rules as the obvious trade barriers and they trampled the most basic provision of WTO, i.e. national treatment. The USCBC president, Robert Kapp, referred to the rules of implementing WAPI as improper regulatory protectionism. SIA president, George Scalise, put it even more dramatically when he stated that the WAPI requirements raised serious national treatment concerns. He also argued that adopting a unique standard would slow the development of the information industry in China as it meant that foreign manufacturers had higher costs to produce gears for the Chinese market. The meeting participants warned that China’s WAPI events went beyond the wireless industry and could undermine confidence in its WTO implementation efforts, and they appealed that China should work together with international standardization communities on a mutually acceptable solution (ITI, 2004; Mannion, 2004; SIA, 2004; USCBC, 2004).

This collective movement drew some congressmen to join in the challenging network. Representatives Philip Crane and Charles Rangel, Senator Max Baucus, Craig Thomas and Gordon Smith urged government officials to be actively involved in this matter, and requested that the Chinese ambassador to the USA should deal with this issue. Some congressmen even sent a letter to President Bush and asked him to take the WAPI case to WTO against China (USITO, 2004a). As a result, the challenging network was substantially intensified with the participation of three senior US government officials. On 3 March 2004, the Secretary of

Commerce, Donald Evens, Secretary of State, Colin Powell, and Trade Representative, Robert Zoelick signed a letter to two Vice Premier Ministers of China, Wu Yi and Zeng Peiyan, who, respectively, were in charge of international trade and high-tech policy. They pointed ou that China’s move of implementing WAPI created a precedent for using standards as barriers to international trade (Palmer, 2004; USTR, 2004). This letter turned out to bring significan diplomatic pressure on China to negotiate a compromise over the issue.

WAPI was a big concern for Intel, the ‘number one monopoly’ in the Chinese WLAN market. In spring 2002, Intel invested hundreds of millions of dollars to launch Centrino processors based on the Wi-Fi standard. Laptops with Centrino processors were becoming the most popular application for WLAN and the profit was rolling in. But Intel’s interest in China’s WLAN market was substantial, far beyond the profits arising from Centrino (Fang & Fang, 2004). Hence, Intel was cautious to say ‘no’ to WAPI. It was on 10 March 2004, when a strong challenging network had been established with the involvement of the US government, that the spokeswoman of Intel’s Beijing Branch announced ‘Intel is not able to reach an agreement with the Chinese companies authorized by the Chinese government nor is it possible to find out a practical way to guarantee the quality in the execution of cooperation.’ Intel criticized that China developed this technology internally and refused to openly share details with others. But Inte would not rule out eventually using WAPI, provided that China was open to making technica changes. Intel claimed that it made this decision after 3 months of studying the WAP implementation rules, and having active talks with the Chinese government and relevant institutions and firms on issues like techniques (Liu B, 2004). My informants from a Chinese information technology manufacturer that had been authorized with the WAPI algorithm confirmed that Intel decided to reject WAPI because the Chinese side had asked for a royalty that was too high for Intel to accept (Fordahal, 2004).

The Chinese government kept silent. BWIPS’s spokesman alleged that it was a matter of national sovereignty that a country can set up its own standard in its own market for the sake o national security. BWIPS claimed that even without foreign cooperation, Chinese firms were able to manufacture their own WAPI compatible products (Zhu, 2003). Some Chinese computer makers, being BWIPS members, had submitted applications to China Qualification Centre (CQC) to process certifications for their WAPI products (Liu B, 2004). Founder, the second largest computer maker in China, reported the first success in developing A760 WAPI chips and access points. On 2 April 2004, CQC issued the certificate to Founder’s NB700 laptop that had an embedded A760 chip (CQC, 2004; USITO, 2004b). However, two informants of mine from MI confirmed the ever-existing suspicion that Founder’s access points were too expensive for normal customers to use (Mannion & Clendenin, 2003; Anonymous, 2004).

In the whole process, the defensive network seemed to have only BWIPS as the active actors with limited support from the market. Some manufacturers in Taiwan, including major players in the international WLAN and semiconductor markets like BenQ, CyberTAN and D-Link, expressed their willingness to endorse the WAPI standards and supply compatible equipment. In the USA, wireless chip makers, Atheros, Cisco’s Linksys, and Netgear said they would build the specifications into products designed for the Chinese market. Most companies like Hewlett-Packard and IBM took the stance of ‘wait and see' (Clendenin & Mannion. 2004: Zhu. 2004),

These actors of the defensive network were relatively weak players in the WLAN market. Their interests resided in benefiting from the possible market re-shuffle due to implementing new standards. In contrast, IEEE, Wi-Fi Alliance and its members like Broadcom and Intel dominated the WLAN market. They formed a strong challenging network (Fang & Fang, 2004).

In sum, there were two counter actor-networks fighting to dominate the interest inscription on WAPI standardization. The challenging network enrolled the foreign industrial giants, associa tions, and the US government. In contrast, the defensive network continued to be weak.

## April 2004: postponing the implementation

The 15th plenary session of the US–China Joint Commission on Commerce and Trade (JCCT) took place on 21 April 2004 in the USA. Established in 1983, JCCT was a government-togovernment consultative mechanism that provided a forum to resolve trade concerns and promote bilateral commercial opportunities. This was a special session co-chaired by Secretary of Commerce, Donald Evans, and Trade Representative, Robert Zoellick, on the US side and by Vice Premier Minister, Wu Yi, on the Chinese side. WAPI was one of the seven issues of this meeting. The JCCT meeting achieved concrete results on key concerns. The USA promised to ease restrictions on the export of some high-tech products to China, and would support China’s aspiration to seek ‘market economy status’ in the WTO. With respect to its WAPI standards, China would suspend indefinitely the proposed implementation of WAPI as mandatory standards, and revise WAPI standards to take into account comments from foreign firms (JCCT, 2004; Li, 2004).

The US government and information technology industry cheered for the agreements (Liu J, 2004). BWIPS was shocked as it had not received any information to suggest that this would be the result. Shortly after on 22 April, through Sina, BWIPS published a statement that the JCCT decision did not mean that WAPI was cancelled, but instead would be implemented sometime later in a new way (BWIPS, 2004). On 29 April, AQSIQ and SAC published Decree Number 44 to enforce the decision of the central government to postpone implementing WAPI (AQSIQ and SAC, 2004).

In brief, the Chinese government finally led the defensive network to concede to the challenging network. This move was necessary for the two governments to maintain a network in issues where there were substantial interests on both sides.

## THE CONTEXTUAL CONSTRAINTS ON WAPI STANDARDIZATION:MACRO ANALYSIS

Based on the framework in Figure 1. in this section I analyse the macro constraints on WAPI standardization in China. In the section, ‘Why did China’s WAPI initiative fail?’, I argue that China’s WAPI initiative failed because it could not establish a strong defensive network. In the section, ‘What made China’s WAPI initiative fail?’, I further attribute this failure to the social, technological and institutional contexts of WAPI development and implementation.

## Why did China’s WAPI initiative fail?

The Chinese developers and potential foreign adopters of WAPI technology conflicted on two issues. The first one concerned the coexistence of competitive standards. The Wi-Fi Alliance claimed IEEE 802.11 had become the de facto industry standard, and WAPI was a deviation from the mainstream (IEEE, 2003; SIA, 2004). The Chinese side criticized Wi-Fi for its serious security flaws, which had hindered the WLAN market development, and thus called for new standards (Zhu, 2003). The question of a unique standard or competitive standards in the market is a traditional debate in standardization communities. Both have pros and cons. A unique standard supports the scale of economy, but it might lead to a monopoly and lock in the customers to inferior technology. To have multiple standards means that the users must be able to afford the cost of interconnection and interoperability (Farrell & Saloner, 1985). Hence, Wi-Fi Alliance could not simply require China to give up competitive standards. In fact, China could have stopped Wi-Fi with the excuse of its poor security mechanism (Liu B, 2004).

The second cause of conflict was the method of WAPI implementation. WAPI did not support efficient compatibility with Wi-Fi, but China took it as compulsory standards and restricted using Wi-Fi technology (AQSIQ and SAC, 2003b). The WAPI encryption code was only granted to Chinese firms; thus, foreign companies were forced to cooperate with them in order to get into China’s WLAN market (NRCCA, 2003). While BWIPS insisted that China had the right to adopt its own standards in its own market in a way required by ‘Commercial Encryption Management Regulation’ (State Council, 1999), Broadcom and its supporters from the USA saw it as discrimination and a contradiction to WTO law regarding national treatment (Liu B, 2004; Mannion, 2004; Sorid, 2004). For China, the argument is that the ‘WTO Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade’ stipulates that a country can take protective measures in the consideration of national security, which might give it the excuse to develop its own proprietary standards (Zhu, 2003; Suttmeier, 2005). Given that the US National Security Agency tampered with the data encryption standards and required some of its manufacturers in their products to create encryption backdoors in order to check the data (Microsoft’s Windows package was an example), why could not China control the WAPI code (Reavis, 1999; Wirbel, 2004)?

To the end, the dispute on WAPI was a conflict of interests. The counter-network compromise was a result of political negotiation and the trials of power. Intel successfully mobilized other manufacturers, industrial associations, congressmen and the US government, forming a strong challenging network where different actors firmly stood together to boycott deploying WAPI. But BWIPS failed to set up a strong defensive network. Only BWIPS itself showed a solid interest in implementing WAPI by the schedule stipulated by relevant decrees. Furthermore, it was in the national interest of China to back off the original plan of implementing WAP in exchange for the support of the USA in more important trade issues.

Intel adopted a pragmatic strategy in forming the challenging network (Fang & Fang, 2004). In the beginning, when Intel still considered the possibility of cooperation, it exercised its voice of objection through USITO. Intel formally announced the rejection when the state officials participated in the challenging network, which meant that the WAPI issue became the concern of a normal trade relationship between two countries. The national interest of the USA was involved in the WAPI issue; hence, the challenging network would not compromise easily (David & Steinmueller, 1996; Updegrove, 2004). In contrast, BWIPS gave ambiguous, contradictory information on the compatibility and advantage of WAPI technology. Its only strategy was to protect itself by relating WAPI standards with sovereignty and national secrets (Zhu, 2003).

## What made China’s WAPI initiative fail?

According to Lyytinen & King (2002), I can assume that the Chinese WAPI standards failed to be implemented because of institutional, social and technological restrictions (Figure 1). In this part of this paper I analyse these elements.

## Technology: beyond the control of China

The central government withdrew its support to WAPI first because of technological concerns. China was weak in chip design; hence, it was ideal for BWIPS to get the backup from foreign chip makers. This key technology located in the upstream of WLAN value chain was controlled by US companies such as Wi-Fi Alliance members, like Broadcom and Intel. To protect their monopoly on WLAN technology, these firms resisted cooperating with BWIPS. Although some manufacturers in Taiwan and the USA were willing to stand on the side of BWIPS, they did not have the core technology either (Fang & Fang, 2004; Zhu, 2004). Consequently, until the middle of April 2004 when China backed off from the WAPI project, there were no WAPI products coming out of companies outside China’s mainland.

WAPI itself was immature in design from a market perspective and hence could not translate the interest of foreign competitors as well as the central government of China. Foreign firms had been concerned that the production costs of WAPI might be too high. As an example, very soon after China announced the compulsory implementation of WAPI standards, Dennis Eaton cast doubts over the ability of the WAPI authentication scheme to scale sufficiently to support WLAN without using expensive access points (Mannion & Clendenin, 2003). These concerns turned out to be necessary. A few Chinese manufacturers already had some WAPI compatible products ratified by Chinese authorities, but their products involved manufacturing costs that were too high to have a market value (Anonymous, 2004). Consequently, the central government had no interest in WAPI technology as it could not win on the market. An official from MI informed me in more detail

Chip technology is a bottleneck for the Chinese information industry. We have been unable to manufacture WLAN chips that are not only technologically advanced but also economically feasible. Some companies might have qualified WAPI products in their laboratories, but now they are definitely unable to start mass production of WAPI chips for commercial use. The same situation applies to China’s R&D sector as a whole: we can fabricate some high-tech products – we were able to do so four decades ago when we already launched A-bomb and space shuttle, but we have trouble in industrializing the R&D achievements. A relevant example is high-speed computers. Ten years ago the national R&D authority announced that China successfully produced such computers called the Galaxy Computer, and awarded the project group with the first prize of national science and technology achievements. However, it is clear till now we still have to rely on the US to sell us high-speed computers.

## Institutional context: a planned system in R&D and standardization management

There were institutional reasons why BWIPS came up with an immature technology and an unfeasible plan for its implementation, around which an actor-network could not be formed. In China, R&D management basically adopts a planned system, and this has hindered advances in technology. Through a closed process, some layman officials decide projects and allocate funding. The evaluation of R&D project results is a make-up undertaken by insiders with interests in them; hence, basically all projects would end with positive evaluations (Rao et al., 2004). Mr. Yang, who was responsible for telecommunications R&D management and is now a consultant to the MII minister, argued that the standardization administration adopts a similar method. For the case of WAPI, BWIPS was established ‘silently’, and it carried out its work ‘silently’ (Fang & Fang, 2004). MII decided the WAPI project through a closed process rather than a consulting mechanism. In setting up BWIPS to undertake the R&D on WAPI. MII did not publicly invite bids for competition. Before the WAPI projects were finished, SAC had listed them in the plan of issuing national standards, which already granted them the positions o being national standards (SAC, 2002). The evaluation committee was appointed by MII, and it was composed of people from MII, SAC, etc., that were also the backing institutes of BWIPS (the name list of these firms is available at BWIPS webpage). Here, SAC adopted a closed process of administrative examination and approval on applications from different ministries as standard developers. No public selection was involved at all.

Restricted by the inefficient R&D management system, China did not enrol major stakeholders of current Wi-Fi technology into the actor-network of developing WAPL standards. Without the participation of Wi-Fi technology owners in BWIPS, compatibility became a big concern. The incumbent adopters of Wi-Fi technology could not participate in BWIPS to use their expertise to reflect on how to implement WAPI standards and how long a transition would be needed. As a result, the manufacturers using incumbent IEEE standards had only 6 months to have their products meet the requirements of WAPI (AQSIQ and SAC, 2003b). Yet, according to my interviewees from a domestic WLAN manufacturer and two Chinese network operators, normally such a transition would take 2 years. Moreover, it was against the interests of foreign firms to force them to cooperate with their Chinese competitors in order to enter into the WAP market of China. Finally, the interests of the customers had not been considered at all, as BWIPS had not conducted the necessary market research or customer surveys on what kinds of WLAN technologies were needed.

In 2001, China became a member of the WTO and committed to abide by the nationa treatment item (Suttmeier & Yao, 2004). In the case of WAPI, China granted the WAP encryption code to selected Chinese firms. Foreign companies took this as a breach of WTO law and threatened to sue China in WTO over the WAPI issue (USITO, 2004a). As a result, standards became an issue of trade where the central government had the final right to vote (David & Steinmueller, 1996). As usual, the US government could not see an infringement on WTO rules from its own perspective. In the WAPI case, China preferred to compromise so as to maintain an actor-network with the USA on issues of greater national interest (JCCT, 2004).

Joining the WTO has allowed China to move closer to market economy. But China has been stuck largely at the low level of the high-tech value chain. In order to take a leading role in setting the high-tech agenda, to reduce its dependence on Western innovation, and to avoid high royalty payments, the Chinese government used the proprietary standard approach to build innovation and indigenous technological capability (Cao, 2004; Suttmeier, 2005). The WAPI case was a part of this national strategy. The BWIPS spokesman disclosed that MII hoped that the implementation of WAPI might help China control the whole value chain of WLAN (Pan & Fu, 2004). In China, however, the distributed institutional arrangement in R&D and standardization administration did not support this strategy. Under this system, the industrial ministries and MOST were responsible for the selection and management of key R&D projects. SRDC took care of promoting the industrialization of important high-tech research achievements (Huang et al., 2004). In the case of WAPI, the Ministry of Security also needed to get involved to enforce the roles of authorizing the WAPI algorithm only to Chinese firms. SAC was in charge of coordinating the numerous regulators involved in standardization. But the coordination could not be efficient as China was so big and industries differed. For SAC in particular, with a position lower than ministerial level, it was difficult to manage other ministries (Suttmeier & Yao, 2004).

China lacked a coherent administration system to support its standardization strategy (Suttmeier & Yao, 2004). Consequently, in the case of WAPI, only BWIPS reacted to the international challenge and defended its products. However, according to my informant from MII, as an ad hoc R&D agent, BWIPS was not empowered to dialogue with foreigners. The seeming support from the government reached a peak on 9 July 2003, when eight authorities organized a meeting to announce the publications of WAPI standards. But no senior officials attended the meeting. By Chinese rules this meant that the governmental support to WAPI was weak (Bo, 2003). A professor of telecommunications policy compared the WAPI project with the TD-SCDMA case for me. China promoted the adoption of TD-SCDMA technology. Even the state leaders partook in the exhibitions of this Chinese version standard of 3G mobile networks. In contrast, the central government participated in the WAPI dispute at the last minute, and in the broader interests of promoting Sino–USA trade, it decided to indefinitely postpone the WAPI implementation (JCCT, 2004).

China especially lacked efficient cross-industry institutions and social networks to coordi nate and defend the interests of different Chinese stakeholders in the WLAN market, and to lobby the enrolment of senior politicians and central government that had more power in influencing the interest translation of foreigners (Callon, 1991; Chen & Ning, 2002). In contrast, US organizations such as USITO, USCBC, Wi-Fi Alliance, etc., has been found to be active.

## Social context

In recent years, as the Chinese economy has grown fast, nationalism and populism have begun to dominate the social mode (Zakaria, 2005). The indigenous development of proprietary technology has been taken as national pride as it secures China a measure of technological independence, and reduces burdensome royalty payments by domestic producers of high-tech goods (Linden, 2004). In the context that the state uses standards as a national strategy to control key technologies, the involved authorities have strong interests to ‘win honour for the country’, and make WAPI a point of national pride. The success in WAP standardization might allow China to hint to organizations such as IEEE that adopting the standards along the lines of what US sources see as optimal is not good enough, and that Chinese technology is superior to the dominant international standards. I have noticed that the official media have boasted that WAPI is the first set of national standards in which China controls key technologies (Zhu, 2003). As a result, although IEEE in its letters to the SAC chairman and MII minister showed the intention of cooperation (IEEE, 2003), China refused. China believed it did not have to cooperate with foreigners in setting up its national standards. China expressed that, even in the future effort to make WAPI international standards, it would prefer to work with international organizations like ITU and International Organization for Standardization (ISO), rather than IEEE which BWIPS considered to be a US standardization body (Liu B, 2004; Wirbel, 2004).

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

## The two-layer mechanism of standardization

This case study demonstrates that the standardization process is decided by a two-layer mechanism. The internal layer is composed of actors that fight for the interest inscription and translation. Further, the strength of the actors’ interests, their specific positions in the market, and their political power determine the interest alignment and hence the standardization process. In the WAPI case, BWIPS members were the actors with direct interest seeing WAP being compulsorily implemented because it meant intellectual property right income to them. Under a centralized system of decision, the state had the final word on WAPI implementation. The state had to evaluate the impact of WAPI implementation on international trade and economic development. It also needed to consider the interests of the consumers and operators. Hence, the decision of the state was based on the national interests as a whole and was not limited to the WAPI issue. The interests of incumbent foreign monopoly tended to be against WAPI that might challenge their established positions in the WLAN market, but they should be careful to say ‘no’ to avoid losing the Chinese market. They also knew that innovation was necessary because of the technological flaws in the security mechanisms o the existing standards. So the interests of the incumbents could be translated under proper contexts. Different actors stood in each of the two counter-networks, which fought for dominance on whether or not to implement WAPI.

The external layer defines the technological, institutional and social contexts, which determine actors’ interests, and the results of interest translation and counter-network compromise. China did not have the R&D capability of implementing new WLAN standards (Fang & Fang, 2004). However, influenced by the planned system of R&D administration. WAPI standardiza. tion was a closed process excluding major stakeholders in the WLAN market to join BWIPS. In fact, the populism and nationalism in vogue encouraged China to reject foreign cooperation in developing WAPI (Wirbel, 2004; Zakaria, 2005). Consequently, in terms of technology, WAPI was immature. It did not support good compatibility to incumbent standards and its implementation would involve relatively higher costs. Foreign competitors formed a challenging network to boycott the implementation of WAPI standards; however, China lacked a coherent institutional set-up to support BWIPS translating the interests of foreign manufacturers. Above all, the Chinese government would not enrol itself in the defensive network, and without such a powerful actor, the defensive network compromised to the challenging network and WAPI failed to be implemented.

## The Chinese standardization strategy: ‘new techno-nationalism’?

Suttmeier (2005) labelled the Chinese method of deploying standards as ‘new techno nationalism’. It meant that the state would work together with other sources, mainly the market size advantage, to promote technology development. But in the WAPI case, BWIPS played a key role in defending the plan to compulsorily implement WAPI. Moreover, it was intriguing that BWIPS refused to demonstrate the compatibility of WAPI with Wi-Fi, the costs to be risen from deploying WAPI, and the requirements for technological cooperation to implement WAPI. It was noticed that some reports gave a message contradictive to BWIPS claims but supported the concerns from the US side. Here I give three examples. First, in contrast to BWIPS who alleged that foreign companies did not need to disclose the technological secret in their cooperation with Chinese manufacturers to get WAPI algorithm, a chip maker in Taiwan claimed that it had obtained the code after offering details of its baseband architecture. Also the hidden cost was higher than even the Chinese imagined, and much support would be needed in the future (Clendenin & Mannion, 2004). Second, while BWIPS announced that Founder’s products were compatible to Wi-Fi, the insiders admitted it was hard to realize compatibility (Zhu, 2004). Third, China claimed it was by ‘Commercial Encryption Management Regulation’ (State Council, 1999) that it could not open the encryption algorithm to foreigners; however, it is hard to believe that the WAPI algorithm would be a national secret. I noticed that after the implementation of WAPI was postponed, China delivered it to ISO and wanted it to be ratified as an international standard (IEEE, 2005; Liu, 2005). This meant that China was willing to open the WAPI technology. This is because by ISO policy, intellectual property rights must be available to all implementers of the standard for free or with a reasonable royalty/license fee under reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and conditions (Caplan, 2003). According to an MII official in charge of developing national information technology standards. in fact the Commercial

Encryption Management Regulation’ did not specify what kinds of encryption should be state secrets. It was possible that China flexibly declassified the WAPI algorithm (Wang, 2004).

All in all, it is rational to suggest that, encouraged by the inefficient system in R&D and standardization administration, and under the nationalism and populism environment, the current WAPI initiative was a result of BWIPS plotting to ‘capture’ the interests of the state, customers and operators to implement an immature technology. The WAPI implementation was connected with the national strategy of developing indigenous high-tech, which let BWIPS get support from MII and SAC. BWIPS believed that, if WAPI was implemented, the operators would follow. Although the switch from incumbent Wi-Fi to WAPI would mean that sunk costs would be involved, operators owned by the state would have to follow the government decrees (Gao, 2005). Under the umbrella of meeting the requirement of national security, the customers had no choice, although this meant their current devices would be unable to use WLAN anymore; in fact, in the WAPI initiative, their interests had not been considered at all. BWIPS bet that, allured by the huge Chinese market, foreign vendors would compromise, sooner or later. The WAPI case supports the argument that individual companies might manipulate the technology standardization process to their own advantage (Bekkers et al., 2002).

## Implications

The research results of this paper have theoretical implications for academia. In the literature, scholars explain the standardization process from multiple perspectives using technologica diffusion, economic, social and political theories (Lyytinen et al., 2007). In this paper, I demonstrate that actor-network is a proper tool for conducting a standardization study (Hanseth & Monteiro, 1997; Lyytinen & Fomin, 2002). My analysis on the Chinese experience of WAPI standardizations supports the argument that an actor-network study should include a twolayered analysis so as to provide a better understanding of the mechanism of actor-network formation (Walsham, 1997; Klein & Kleinman, 2002; Gao, 2005).

I further argue that, in using actor-network theory, researchers should focus on the mechanism of interest translation and inscription of actors, and compromise of counter-networks. Furthermore, they should be able to not only describe the process of interest alignment and network formation, but also expose the macro factors that determine this micro process. This case study demonstrates that a context analysis is important as it enables researchers to capture the strength and flexibility of actors’ interests and their power to influence the interest inscription and the result of counter-network compromise, i.e. the willingness of interest translation and the capability to resist a translation. My analytical model (Figure 1) that draws upon Lyytinen & King (2002) to categorize the context for actor-network formation in WAP standardization process provides a useful reference for other actor-network studies. This confirms that researchers need to incorporate other studies with actor-network theory so as to look at the process of actor-network formation systemically and profoundly (Walsham, 1997; Klein & Kleinman, 2002).

The experience that China failed to deploy its indigenous standard reflects for us the elements underpinning technology adoption in the telecommunications market. It implies tha such a process is promoted by key actors under specific social, economic and institutional contexts (Lyytinen & King, 2002; Henten et al., 2004). Particularly, a telecommunications standard or system is selected mainly for economic and political reasons, rather than for technological merit. It is determined by the government, operators and foreign stakeholders through the trials of power and by making economic and political trade-off. As an example, in the second-generation mobile telecommunications market, China Unicom adopted both the Global System for Mobile Communications standard and CDMA system. This was exceptional in the world as it was contrary to the principle of economies of scale and the efficiency of interconnection. However, as a state-owned company, China Unicom had to follow the order from the central government, which decided to introduce Qualcomm’s technology in return for the support of the US government in its entrance into the WTO. Another example is the TD-SCDMA case. China hoped this standard proposed by MII would be adopted in its market. However, this technology was immature. As a result, the deployment of 3G technology was postponed (Linden, 2004; Suttmeier & Yao, 2004).

This case study has practical implications for standardization practitioners, especially those in developing countries. For authorities responsible for standardization management, my analysis suggests that the standardization processes accommodate a considerable heterogeneity of interests (Suttmeier, 2005). For developing countries, foreign pace-setters set the architecture that they have to follow (Morris & Ferguson, 1993). Furthermore, developing countries have a weak R&D capability. Cooperation between new technology developers in these countries and foreign owners of incumbent standards is extremely important to realize compatibility between new and incumbent standards, and to ensure the advantage of new technology. Specifically, China’s WAPI initiative failed because it broke this rule. WAPI was based on Wi-Fi but China excluded the foreign owners of this technology from the WAPI development process.

From the actor-network perspective, in R&D and standardization management, it should be a basic criterion that the selected standard and its implementation method are able to motivate the important incumbents and competent newcomers to participate in it, and for the public to benefit from the market (Lyytinen & Fomin, 2002; Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). To do so, countries should adopt the tech-neutralism principle. The tech-neutralism refuses the subjective interference of the governments, but requires them to adopt a scientific mechanism in R&D and standardization management (Suttmeier & Yao, 2004). By this principle, the governments should decide standardization projects according to their technology merits and market potentials. They will adopt a competitive mechanism to ensure that the most qualified organizations are selected to undertake the R&D tasks. The selections of the methods of standard devel opment and implementation will be based on proper understandings of the domestic R&D capability with less influence from nationalism, which is the dominant social mode in fastgrowing economies. In the case of WAPI, as a result of the closed method of standardization encouraged by the Chinese government, the above criterion and principle could not be met.

The Chinese experience of implementing indigenous technology tells developing countries that the debate around implementing a standard is a matter of interests, and the balance point of interests is decided by actors in the counter-networks through their trials of power in market, technology and trade. These countries should be aware of their weak bargaining positions, and know that techno-nationalism only works conditionally and market size advantage has limited power. In the case of WAPI, the market could be a leverage to lure foreign manufacturers to adopt WAPI, but it had a limited role as the WTO set the rules of the game. More implications can be gotten from the case of Linux. In China, as a result of the government mandate, the Linux-based operating system and office applications developed by Chinese software companies eroded Microsoft’s dominance in software procurement by government. This was possible as China did not commit to the WTO’s item of government procurement (Cao, 2004; Shen, 2005).

I argue that the developing countries need to improve their institutional contexts. First, the institutional set-up should support tech-neutralism. For China, an efficient law must be in place to formulate the standardization process, and a coherent standardization management system should be established. Second, these countries should encourage the formation of crossindustry institutions and social networks, and use them to coordinate the interests of differen actors in developing and implementing indigenous technologies. In developing countries, these institutions and networks are often poorly developed, and are sometimes neglected by policy makers and technology developers (Chen & Ning, 2002).

China could learn some lessons. It could focus on developing standards that were advanced in technology and low-cost in adoption. Ideally, the new technology would be compatible to incumbent standards, which is a good basis to enrol broad stakeholders, including the owners of incumbent standards into an actor-network (Farrell & Saloner, 1985; Lyytinen & Fomin, 2002). Although a strong sense of national pride has helped China as an emerging economy to take the initiative in developing indigenous technology, the Chinese government needs to be pragmatic, and must adopt an open, sharing principle in managing standardization activities (Wang, 2004). It should actively seek international cooperation and properly use the market as a leverage to achieve maximum benefits from the cooperation. An inspiring message is that SAC has pledged to revise some regulatory items, and has adopted the reasonable and non-discriminatory principle of implementing compulsory national standards (JCCT, 2004; Li, 2004).
