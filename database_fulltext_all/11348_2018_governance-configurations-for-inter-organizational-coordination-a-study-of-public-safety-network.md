# Governance configurations for interorganizational coordination: A study of public safety networks

Jane Fedorowicz<sup>1</sup>, Steve Sawyer<sup>2</sup>, Arthur Tomasino<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Bentley University, 175 Forest Street, Waltham, MA 02452, USA;

<sup>2</sup>The School of Information Studies, Syracuse University, 344 Hinds Hall, Syracuse, NY 13244, USA

Correspondence:

S Sawyer, The School of Information Studies, Syracuse University, 344 Hinds Hall, Syracuse, NY 13244, USA E-mail: ssawyer@syr.edu

## Abstract

We focus on networked arrangements of digital resources that are shared among otherwise independent units to advance conceptual and empirical insights about their governance. We are motivated by the simple observation that, increasingly, independent organizations are engaging in shared activities, often relying on purpose-built digital infrastructures to support this move to inter-dependence. To advance current conceptualizations of networked governance, we draw on data from 42 public safety networks and use fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis. We do so because fsQCA allows us to account for the realities of inter-dependence among the concepts and variables we consider and to illuminate the multiple viable governance patterns that are possible. The results show the importance of network-level governance competencies to manage stakeholders and information infrastructure to achieve high effectiveness of PSN. Analysis makes clear that there exist five configurations of PSN governance practices that enable high levels of network governance effectiveness. Common to all these configurations are the network-level competence in managing both stakeholders and the digital infrastructure, suggesting these are necessary (but not sufficient) network-level governance competencies. Building from the analysis, we advance the role of specific network-level governance competencies, and the current conceptualization of network governance more broadly.

## Introduction

n this paper, we examine the governance implications of inter-organizational systems (IOS) that support a collaborative organizational network. We focus our study on IOS that permit government agencies to work across previously impermeable organizational boundaries. Government agencies around the world experience extraordinary governance challenges as they move to take advantage of new digital technologies that allow them to communicate and share information with other organizations and government entities. Unlike corporate IOS, public-sector efforts to build out such arrangements are hampered by federated reporting and funding structures that limits an individual agency’s ability to collaborate freely, and particularly so when collaborative activity crosses jurisdictional boundaries. Given the complexities of public-sector collaboration, we ground our study in the work of Provan and Kenis (2008), who observe that contemporary organizations may choose to align themselves in networks, placing limits on their independence as actors, to gain more benefits from the other member organizations in the network. We examine how IOS, in particular those enacted in government settings, focus their governance efforts to achieve effective network performance on behalf of their stakeholders.

One of the earliest examples of collaboration-inspired systems to connect independent government agencies led to the development of CapWIN, a public safety network (PSN)

supporting police, transportation, fire and other agencies in two US states and the District of Columbia (Fedorowicz et al., 2007). In 1998, a downhearted man intended to jump off of a bridge that spanned the Potomac River. One side of the bridge is in the state of Virginia and the other end is in Maryland. The bridge itself was maintained by the District. As the man strode back and forth across the bridge, he crossed from one location’s domain to another. The first responders in each location used different technology and radio frequencies and had no ability to take leadership and share information or plans. As a result of their inability to coordinate, the incident closed the heavily traveled bridge and created gridlock throughout the region for an entire day. Even with this precipitating incident, it took 3 years to obtain initial congressional funding and the system finally went operational in 2005.

Along the way, CapWIN became a formal operational entity with a board of directors, 47 initial national, state, and local user agencies participating, and funding from multiple governmental appropriations and grants. As of this writing, it serves 137 agencies at the national, regional, state, and local levels, including police, fire, transportation, parks, emergency services and other functions. CapWIN does not ‘‘belong’’ to one single agency or geographic location. It is funded by user agencies and external political sources and must conform to a huge number of regulations emitting from the many governmental bodies in which its user agencies reside. CapWIN is a complex coordination structure that faces unique operational and technical challenges while aiming to meet competing requirements from its funder, user, and citizen stakeholders. CapWIN’s challenges are typical for PSNs. Governance of PSN operational and technical arrangements is inherently more complex due to the competing goals among its network of stakeholders. In this study, we examine the relationship between PSNs’ governance arrangements and the effectiveness of their performance. We focus on the network level of analysis relative to PSN governance, as proposed by Provan and Kenis. More specifically, we seek to augment the Provan and Kenis conceptualization with evidence of how elements of network governance (i.e., governance competencies) contribute to the effectiveness of the network in serving its members.

In pursuing this goal and contribution, we use case-based comparative analysis and the fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) approach developed by Ragin (2000, 2006, 2008, 2014; with Ragin and Rihoux, 2009). Three aspects of the research pursued here compel us to use this method. First, the number of PSN we study is large enough that simple case-by-case comparisons are not possible, even as the total number of cases is relatively small for parametric statistical analyses. Second, and more importantly, many of the factors that shape these PSNs are inter-dependent, making most parametric statistics-based approaches less useful. Finally, and as detailed below, fsQCA accommodates the concept of ‘‘equifinality’’ and so provides the means to assess multiple, potentially overlapping patterns (known as configu rations in fsQCA). While it enjoys a 20-year history in public administration scholarship, the fsQCA approach has only recently been introduced to the information systems (IS) community (e.g., Chong et al., 2013; Frisk et al., 2014; Schryen, 2013; Heidari and Loucupoulos, 2014; Rivard and Lapointe, 2012; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013; Zimmerman et al., 2013; Wagemann et al., 2016). Some readers may value this work for the methodological approach.

Conceptually, we extend Provan and Kenis’ (2008) notion of network effectiveness to the public sector, by applying it within the PSN domain. To do this, we identify and contribute four governance competencies that comprise PSN governance. Results show that stakeholder and technical governance are key contributors to PSN effectiveness, while organizational and financial governance are not. Our analysis produces five configurations of effective PSN cases, relating governance competencies to PSN type and legal authority.

The rest of the paper is structured into five sections. We first discuss the empirical basis of the study to illuminate the phenomena of PSN. In the next section, we review relevant literature on the governance of PSN, drawing from three relevant intellectual communities: information systems, infrastructure studies, and public administration. In the third section, we outline the research approach, including a brief overview of the methods, data collection efforts, and analysis. To supplement this section, we include two appendices that contain guidance about the calibration efforts (fundamental to conducting fsQCA) and the truth table that is at the core of the analysis. Following the section on methods, we complete the paper with two sections: findings and discussion, with implications.

## Public safety networks literature

The empirical context for this analysis is public safety networks (PSN) in the USA (see also Williams and Fedorowicz, 2012; Markus and Bui, 2012). Public safety networks are collaborative efforts wherein multiple government agencies develop and use a shared digital infrastructure (e.g., Klievink et al., 2015). Networks of public and private organizations are commonplace in Europe – where such arrangements have been extensively studied (e.g., Bekkers et al., 2009; Ferro and Sorrentino, 2010; Sanderson et al., 2015; Goldkuhl, 2016). In the USA, these shared infrastructure arrangements are of interest in the public administration literature (e.g., Provan and Kenis, 2008; Provan and Lemaire, 2012; Goldkuhl, 2016; Joha and Janssen, 2011; Williams et al., 2009; Fedorowicz et al., 2007).

## Organizational complexity of PSN

As the CAPWIN example helps make clear, a PSN involves multiple independent police, fire, courts, corrections, and other public safety agencies, along with private companies such as technology vendors, software services providers, technology consultancies, and independent contractors. The impetus for establishing a PSN is simple: They provide a means to increase data and information sharing among police, courts, and other public safety agencies. These otherwise independent organizations participate in a shared enterprise that is distinct from the participating units, yet are bound together through a combination of shared technological infrastructures and shared governance. A PSN provides participating agencies a set of pooled or shared informational resources (such as access to crisis incident notes, driver’s license databases, wants and warrants, court case and correctional proceedings, or hazardous material data) and means of sharing information (on arrests, incidents or investigations) – what Hanseth et al. (1996) have called an information infrastructure (II).

This federated approach to building a PSN (with an embedded II) means participating organizations and agencies choose to join because they share common public safety goals, even as each also pursues their institution-specific goals. To illustrate, a state’s police force, the county sheriff’s office, the local municipality’s police officers, and the National Park Police each share many common policing goals even as they have distinct objectives in their particular role (e.g., state police have responsibility across county lines, the sheriff’s office stands in support of the courts, while local police officers seek to maintain the social order and enforce laws and policy in the local jurisdiction (e.g., towns), and the Park Police has jurisdiction over Federal park lands).

The federated structure of the US government also means that there is no clear hierarchy of decision-making: There is no central decision-maker. The decision (or mandate) to create an II-like networked arrangement among otherwise independent agencies reflects the operational (but not always the political or institutional) realities of public safety. As such, PSN and other participating organizations cede some of their autonomy to the network concerning the services and sharing of resources defined by the goals of the network. For example, the State-level department of motor vehicles (DMV) may choose to share information on drivers’ licenses, license plates, and car registration with the other members of the PSN, even as they do not allow any of these members to update, add, or delete any DMV records. Likewise, various police departments retain control over arrest and warrant records – allowing different levels of access to these data to other members of the PSN. So, state police officers may get to see more information than might the child protective services or the specific court or parole officer from another agency participating in the PSN.

The operational complexity of a federated network of agencies increases appreciably when multiple levels of government participate (e.g., in the USA, policing occurs at the local and state level, courts operate at municipal, state, and regional levels, and public safety services such as emergency management and fire can be supported at any of these levels and are often found at multiple levels). This complexity reflects the real, long-standing, slow-changing, and deeply political realities of jurisdiction, geographic boundaries, funding, and tax structures that support these arrangements. These political structures reflect the elected, judicial, and executive separations of power evident within the local, county, and regional, state and federal levels of government – each defined by legal and geographic bound aries. Often these differences in jurisdiction and the separation of powers they reflect lead to tensions regarding roles, control, resource access, and plans for sharing. Taken together, the need for shared governance of a PSN – one that can support a network of relations among independent agencies – stands in contrast to the fierce desire for independence and strong hierarchical structures of contemporary U. S. government.

## PSN information infrastructure complexity

The institutional complexity of a PSN is further magnified by its technological basis. Like most II, a PSN is a mix of different types of information and communication technologies (ICTs). Some of the systems, like the DMV records, are quite old: legacies of mainframe-era computerization efforts. Others are very modern: relying on mobile devices, distributed computing, and flexible data structures. Each participating agency in a PSN has its own set of systems. One challenge for a PSN is how to link participating agencies systems through some sort of network of sharing (a gateway or shared hub). Many PSN also provide additional, networklevel resources that do not belong to any one participating agency (e.g., inter-agency communications and bulletins, secure access, and file-sharing or storage).

In all cases we have observed, the digital resources of the PSN combine purpose-built systems (e.g., to manage court documents, handle police reporting, and support incident response) to support sharing, along with access protocols and rules to draw from multiple systems and databases (e.g., court records, arrest records, and driver’s license records) – that remain under the careful control of their host agency.

Most PSN layer sophisticated multi-channel communications and high-volume data-sharing systems to support mobile access even though the core IS on which they rely were put into place in the 1970s. For example, in the USA each state has its own court system, and the Federal courts are organized by region (or ‘‘circuits’’). Beginning in the 1970s, IS supporting court administrative processes began appearing (mostly based on IBM mainframe technologies and IBM-initiated and supported bespoke applications). By the 1980s, more vendors, more systems, and more personal computer-based court automation systems were appearing: Some replaced or expanded the first generation of automation and some were the first efforts at automation – as the 50 states’ court systems moved toward automation at quite different rates of uptake (see Wiggins 2006). Federal courts moved in consort and continue to modernize their court automation in fits and starts as a function of political will in Washington, tax revenue, and the ability to stay linked to the various state-level court systems in each circuit (see Pasquale and Cashwell, 2015). The result is that in every locale, there are at least two court systems – a state and a federal – that may be of very different ages, functionality, integration and data structures. These systems are not controlled by the PSN (relative to functionality, service, funding and uses), but are key players. This hodgepodge of court-related technologies and databases became the impetus for the creation of the criminal justice information systems that constitute many of the PSN we studied.

Likewise, each of the nearly 20,000 police departments across the 50 states and other part of the USA (of which more than 90% have fewer than 25 officers) has a collection of systems for managing communications.<sup>1</sup> Adding to this is the extensive uses of mobile phones by police (e.g., Manning, 1996). Plus, each police agency has its own dispatch, report management, and crime-tracking systems. Some of these systems are quite sophisticated and are the envy of policing agencies everywhere. Most of these systems are rudimentary, often simple off-the-shelf applications, modestly maintained by third-party vendors. Linking the many policing-related first responders with the data and common ICT, they need to perform effectively led to the formation of the many policecentric PSN that round out the case studies supporting this research.

For example, Figure 1 depicts the technical architecture of one of the PSN included in the analysis, selected as representative of ICT complexity. The upper third of Figure 1 outlines the mobile access elements and services provided to officers in the field, bringing together municipal, county-level, and statelevel data resources. The lower left portion of the technical architecture outlines how the PSN connect to various other agencies that are local to the county (or region) or from Federal and State agencies that typically provide data to county-level government agencies. The lower right quadrant, nearly half of the diagram, illustrates the particulars of the PSN-level resources that would not be available to any one agency without the PSN. This quadrant includes both mainframe and high bandwidth mobile platforms that showcase the diversity of the underlying technologies.

This mix of off-the-shelf and bespoke IS, different ICTs, and platforms, and a mix of computing architectures is so common for PSN (and US government more broadly) as to be itself remarkable. That is, the II of a PSN is also in many ways an operating museum that reflects a collection of different computing systems and architectures that include 30+ year-old-custom-written software systems linked to the latest mobile technologies. And, once built or implemented, these systems are hard to replace given the sensibilities of contemporary USA approaches to government spending. We also emphasize that PSN must comply with a maze of local, state and national policies, rules, regulations and case law regarding information sharing, policing and public uses of data. For example, a restriction that Federal dollars are not allowed to support State-funded systems would lead to different government-funded computers being set up sideby-side.

The digital acts of sharing, and of delivering functionality and digitally enabled services, require governance over participating organizations’ uses, support and operations of this information infrastructure, and specific rules about data access and use (per Markus and Bui, 2012). Moreover, while PSN are designed for routine use, increasingly they are deputized as key players for critical incidents and natural disasters which makes them more visible as a political activity, too (Gil-Garcia et al., 2009). Given all this, and as a provocative tangent that deserves more attention, we assert PSN exist in one of the most difficult institutional incubators for collaboration and shared governance that one might imagine.

Despite their complex environment, PSN are growing in both their number and the roles they play in supporting policing, public safety and homeland security are expanding (IJIS Institute, 2012; Gillespie, 2005; Fedorowicz et al., 2007). Williams and Fedorowicz (2012) make the case that PSN entities reflect a bottom-up and middle-out set of technological and organizational arrangements and collaborations. As such, it seems likely that the findings on how governance competencies affect PSN effectiveness are worthy of consid eration as viable insights into other settings where networked governance arrangements are becoming more common.

## PSN governance literature

Governance encompasses the arrangements institutional players engage in order to allocate decision-making, manage shared resources, and enact mechanisms for coordination and resolution of differences (North, 1990; Ostrom, 2005; Provan and Milward, 1995; Milward and Provan, 2000; Provan and Kenis, 2008; Klievink et al., 2015). Governance can extend from primarily a strategic activity (Markus and Bui, 2012) to an administrative set of processes (e.g., Fountain, 2013, 2015). For this paper, governance encompasses strategic, tactical and operational endeavors.

A PSN’s governance efforts and structures must accommodate participants as independent actors who choose to join together and restrict their own independence in pursuit of specific value taken from shared activities. Value to participants arises primarily from the sharing of or access to resources not otherwise available independently (Ostrom, 2005; Cooper and Stephenson, 2012; Grant and Tan, 2013; Markus and Bui, 2012; Yang and Wu, 2015). The nature of the collaboration, the participation of multiple diverse agencies, the regulatory frames in which these operate, and the importance of the information infrastructures that embody a PSN means their governance is a resolutely shared activity and cannot rely on the command-and-control model that naı¨ve understandings of governmental agencies often invoke.

Public safety networks also embody an interesting balance of the sharing and consensus-oriented nature of collaboration, set against the unit-level command-and-control structures of policing, the carefully regulated, rule-bound procedures – and jurisdictions – of court processes, and the resource-constrained and politically charged nature of US public-sector operations (e.g., Jacobson, 2016; Pardo et al., 2008; Dawes et al., 2004; Ahn and Bretschneider, 2011). Our theorizing on PSN governance in particular is further shaped by the literature from three distinct intellectual communities: criminal justice, policing, and public safety; information systems; and public administration, as summarized below.

## Criminal justice, policing, and public safety

Much of the scholarship regarding policing, public safety and criminal justice are directed toward policy and not typically toward operational questions. This noted, there is a small but steady stream of academic research on ICT in criminal justice, policing and public safety (e.g., Chan, 2001; Dunworth, 2005; Manning, 2003). Very little of this literature focuses on PSN (exceptions include Gillespie, 2005; Williams and Fedorowicz, 2012; Fedorowicz et al., 2014; Jacobson, 2016). This literature emphasizes the need to plan for, effectively manage, and provide ICT-based services and access to data (and increasingly mobile access).

There is also a stream of operational-level research provided by several non-governmental organizations (e.g., IJIS Institute, 2012; Noblis, 2006; International Association of Chiefs of Police, 2000; Pardo et al., 2008). Many of these are useful comparative studies of existing network-level sharing arrangements (e.g., Gil-Garcia and Martinez-Moyano, 2007; Fedorowicz et al., 2007; Gillespie, 2005). Findings from this work point to the importance of building a common vision and shared purpose across the network’s members, mitigating the pernicious effects of resource scarcity (and particularly the vagaries of public-sector funding), and developing both a sound technological architecture and information systems development approach.

Given the renewed interest to see more cross-boundary collaboration (i.e., across both organizational and jurisdictional boundaries) in the public sector, scholars have begun to turn their attention to providing guidance on how to develop and manage such digitally enabled collaborations (e.g., Fountain, 2013, 2015; Fedorowicz et al., 2014) and encourage collaborative arrangements to managing shared resources (see also Dawes et al., 2004; Bryson et al., 2014; Yang and Wu, 2015). This literature emphasizes the importance of creating mechanisms to involve the many stakeholders of a cross-boundary collaboration in decisionmaking. Taken together, the relevant literature from the areas of criminal justice, policing and public safety emphasize the need for PSN to provide for governance of the technical infrastructure, to create common visions and goals, and to insure all stakeholders are involved in the governance.

## Information systems

The focus of the governance literature in IS for much of the last 40 years has been predicated on a single firm’s decisionmaking and control authority (e.g., Schlosser et al., 2015). Such a situation is distinct from and simpler than what is found in PSN [as Markus and Bui (2012) help to make clear]. This noted, there are two relevant strands of this literature that bear on the work developed here. First is the framing of PSNs as IIs (e.g., Hanseth et al., 1996) or e-infrastructures (e.g., Pollock and Williams, 2010). The second strand is the expansive literature on IOS, with much of the recent work focused on how such arrangements are sustained (e.g., Steinfield et al., 2011; Hartono et al., 2010; Rai and Tang, 2010). And, in this second strand there is, of late, literature on shared governance (e.g., Markus and Bui, 2012; Grant and Tan, 2013).

The II work emphasizes the openness of the network technologies’ design. An II (or e-infrastructure) conceptualizes digital resources as a confederated collection of options and possibilities rather than something that is pre-conceived, fully designed and implemented per set plans (Yoo et al., 2010; Constantinides and Barrett, 2014). Along with this technological openness is the conceptualization that there are many independent parties participating in the II’s development. Therefore, an II is not conceived as a singular system or reflective of a singular chain of decision-making. These principles of an II – openness and multiple independent participants – provides a clear contrast to the more controlled and structured concept of a conventional information system. This conceptualization aligns well with the empirical realities of an PSN. The II literature emphasizes the importance of providing governance mechanisms to involve stakeholders (the installed base in II terms) and to guide or manage the development and uses of the ICT elements of the infrastructure (e.g., Pollock and Williams, 2010; Hanseth and Nielsen, 2013; Monteiro et al., 2013).

There is also an extensive literature in IS encompassing examples of the power and value of IOS such as EDI, E-bay, Orbitz, airBNB, and the realtor.com site (in the USA) (e.g., Bekkers, 2009; Jacobson, 2016). The IOS literature emphasizes the control or leadership of a lead organization, one whose power structure provides the structure of IOS governance. And, while it is to the advantage of both the lead organization and the other participants to create collaborative institutional arrangements, this may not involve shared or networked governance. In the lead-organization approach common to IOS, issues of financial cost, protection of data resources, privacy, security, intellectual property, design, and deployment of ICT-based resources, etc., rest primarily with the lead. Participants must come to grips with being enrolled or subscribed to the services provided by others (Provan and Kenis, 2008). This control-oriented structure is not possible for PSN, given these are structured around multiple members who lack of a clear hierarchy or decision structure (see also Markus and Bui (2012, pp. 191–193).

Over the past decade, given the growth in both the types and visibility of IOS and other collaborative digital networks (e.g., open-source software and Wikipedia), we have seen an increase in the number of papers focused on large-scale IOS and II governance (e.g., Grant and Tan, 2013; Markus and Bui, 2012; King, 2013; Prasad et al., 2012, 2014; see also Tiwana and Kim, 2016). This thread of IOS research echoes the principles of II regarding openness and participation. Moreover, findings reported in this body work emphasizes the need for some form of formal governance structure, careful attention to the management of resources (both data and financial), and the need to develop an organizational body to embody and support the performance of these governance activities and to steward both the collaboration of the participating institutions and ensure that the digital infrastructure is sustained.

## Public administration

Scholars of public administration are actively engaged in conceptualizing networked governance, typically as contrast to the more traditional and hierarchical/bureaucratic models of governance that have dominated this literature (e.g., Bryson et al., 2014; Ahn and Bretschneider, 2011; Fountain, 2013, 2015). In a sweeping review of the extant body of work on network governance, Kapucu et al. (2014) differentiated among efforts to characterize networks (often relying on social network analysis) and the more pertinent work (for this paper) on models and forms of governing or sustaining multi-party networks. Relevant to our work, they distinguish between collaborative networks (informal and often community-centric) and governance networks (that provide more formal, if not professional, leadership to a collaboration among independent agents or entities). In doing this they point to several streams of work building on institutional theory (Powell, 1990), control structures (Marcum et al., 2012) and collaborative governance (e.g., Ansell and Gash, 2008; Lynn et al., 2000).

Within the literature on collaborative governance, the most comprehensive and well-developed conceptualization of both what it means to work with networked organizations and the needs of network governance are the work of Provan and colleagues (e.g., Provan and Kenis, 2008; Provan et al., 2007, Provan and Lemaire, 2012; Provan and Milward, 1995, 2001). Provan and Kenis’ (2008) conceptualize three broad forms of network governance arrangements: leadorganization (most familiar to IOS scholars), participant-led (more like peer-to-peer arrangements) and network administrative organizations (NAO) (comprised of independent actors who choose to form a structured collaboration and commit to shared or joint activities). Relative to the work we advance here, none of the PSN are (or can be) based on the lead organization form or peer-to-peer, leaving only NAO arrangements to consider.

Network governance approaches like Provan and Kenis’s NAO requires participants develop and sustain trust, reach a sufficient level of goal consensus among the members, and identify a set of network-level competencies (or abilities of the collective group per Provan and Lemaire, 2012).<sup>2</sup> The PSN in our study are a sterling example of the NAO model. That is, to be an NAO, a PSN must have an independent governing body, a reasonable number of participating organizations (the 61 in our study average nearly 200 member organizations per PSN), pursue both unique and common public safety goals, and share a need to improve data sharing and communication. Given this, we draw on the work of Provan and colleagues to test the advantage of this governance model for achieving the goals of the many communities it aims to support.

Provan and Kenis’ (2008, pp. 237–238) conceptualization of the network governance also requires balancing three sets of governance tensions: (1) The efficiency of the arrangements relative to the level of inclusiveness of decisionmaking; (2) The need for internal and external legitimacy; and (3) The need for flexibility in the face of changing situations relative to the importance of stability for participants. These trade-offs are pursued in response to local decisions and pressures, likely leading to different viable approaches, and suggests there is no one best way to organize a network governance model.

Provan and colleagues theorize on the impact and effects of network governance on network effectiveness (Provan and Milward, 1995, 2001; Provan and Kenis, 2008). In doing this they highlight the difficulty in assessing the effectiveness of a network due to: the complexity of satisfying myriad stakeholders, jointly constructing a common product with multiple independent partners, and the differences among constituencies concerning the measurement and definition of a common outcome (Provan and Milward, 2001). Indeed, they argue there are three groups with a stake in network effectiveness (the community, the network itself, and the network’s organizational participants), which we find correspond in the case of PSN to the public citizenry, the PSN’s own organization (that which is governed via the NAO), and the government agencies and organizations whose first responders and staff use the PSN.

According to Provan and Milward (1995), for network governance to succeed, the effort must be able to effectively (1) administer funds to support the network effort (see also Lynn et al., (2000); (2) attract and retain organizational members (see also Eisenhardt, 1989); (3) deliver a range of organizational services members desire (see also Kapucu et al., 2014); and (4) create mechanisms to maintain strong relationships with and among members (see also Provan, 1982). Provan and Kenis (2008) argue that these four elements of NAO, and networked governance more broadly, are ‘‘network-level competencies.’’

Here we focus on the relationships among these four network-level competencies relative to the network governance effectiveness by studying PSN, a particular form of NAO. Building from the initial guidance provided by Provan and Kenis (2008) and Provan and Lemaire (2012), we conceptualize these four network competencies as a mechanism to achieve PSN effectiveness. In doing so, and building on the work of Markus and Bui (2012), we specifically conceptualize the range of services being provided as the set of digital resources embodied in implementing and operating the ICT infrastructure that provides access to services and data, along with the network-level processes that allow members to use these technological resources.

## Research question

Given this overview of network governance and PSNs, the research question pursued here is:

RQ: What, if any, configurations of network level competencies distinguish effective PSN?

## The research model

Building from Provan’s (with Provan and Kenis, 2008; and with Provan and Lemaire, 2012) conceptualization of NAO governance, we pursue a research model focuses on assessing the roles of specific network-level competencies. As outlined above, we focus on network-level competencies for PSN governance because the PSN are governed through them. A competence is an ability, a means of doing, in support of the shared goals and interests of the actors who have come together to create a common resource – embodied here in both the ICT infrastructure and the set of practices that facilitate and are allowed in its use. Building from the literature summarized above, we pursue four specific network-level competencies:

Financial governance (Fin) This network-level competence encompasses budgeting, monitoring, evaluating, and controlling financial aspects of the PSN, including pricing services (e.g., Markus and Bui, 2012; Pardo et al., 2008).

Stakeholder governance (Stake) This network-level competence encompasses the sets of decisions and practices that will encourage, engage, and satisfy the PSN stakeholders (e.g., Provan and Lemaire, 2012; Markus and Bui, 2012; Hanseth et al., 1996; Pollock and Williams, 2010).

Organizational governance (Org’l) This network-level competence encompasses the ability of the PSN organization to make staffing decisions and set out services in support of the participants (Markus and Bui, 2012; Grant and Tan, 2013; Pardo et al., 2008).

Technical governance (Tech) This network-level competence encompasses ICT mechanisms and functions such as design of new functionality, prioritizing effort, managing the IT architecture and compliance with standards, dealing with data access and control, and putting in place servicelevel agreements for participants (Markus and Bui, 2012; Pollock and Williams, 2010; King, 2013; Steinfield et al., 2011).

These four network-level competencies help make clear the issues with shared governance, managing the digital and informational information, attending to resource scarcity, and engaging all the stakeholders. Building on these four elements, the model explored here emphasizes these four network-level governance competencies while also accounting for variation in a PSN’s underlying type and basic organizing arrangement.

Our knowledge of issues specific to PSN leads us to feature three other constructs in the research model that help to explicate differences and similarities among the PSN under study. First, we include two distinguishing aspects of PSN that may help to differentiate among the political forces that affect PSN formation: type and legal arrangement. In prior research (Sawyer et al., 2013), we have shown PSN with common goals and shared information infrastructure fall into two broad and distinct types (as police-oriented or as court-oriented). We include categorization of these types in the analysis to assess if the particular orientation of the PSN plays a special role in its governance.

Second, we seek to capture specific forms of initial organizing to determine whether and how legal arrangements matter to achieving a PSN’s goals. Networks may be initiated by the participants, a bottom-up approach, or result from a government mandate to form, a top-down approach. Both are evident in our PSN data set (Williams and Fedorowicz, 2012). Provan and Kenis (2008) see this as a factor contributing to network complexity. Taking the importance of the PNS’s legal foundation a step further, Markus and Bui (2012) propose that entities with similar governance arrangements are able to exhibit differing legal statuses; also, ‘‘…legal arrangements may differ in their consequences for the success of inter-organizational relationships’’ (Bethel and Liebeskind, 1998, p. 166). Legal arrangements have already been found to affect the value and scope economy of business organizations (Bethel and Liebeskind, 1998). Therefore, we include this important aspect of network organizations among those we examine in our model.

Finally, we include a measure of effectiveness in order to distinguish more effectively performing PSN from those that are not. Reflecting on Provan and Kenis’ (2008) notion of effectiveness as needing to accommodate the perspective of the community, the network and the participants, we develop a PSN effectiveness variable that encompasses the network’s sharing of resources (i.e., the primary purpose of the PSN), PSN functionality (what it does), and a self-reported measure of improvements in participant productivity. Taken together, there are six factors being assessed relative to their contribution to PSN effectiveness: the PSN type (court- or policeoriented), four network competencies (organizational, technical, financial and stakeholder), and the legal authority (mandate or elective agreement). Provan and Kenis (2008) propose that there are likely to be multiple ‘‘network governance configurations’’ (p. 233) that may affect the effectiveness of the network, meaning there may be multiple paths to effectiveness among a set of options. For this reason, we pursue the analysis using fsQCA (as noted above, detailed below and in Appendix A in Supplementary material).

## Research approach

We designed the research as a comparative case analysis, combining detailed case studies of a smaller number of PSN with a larger data collection effort to assemble a structured case study of a larger number of PSN. The data set allows us to employ Ragin’s fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (see Marx et al., 2014; Schneider and Wagemann, 2010; Ragin, 2000, 2005, 2006, 2007, 2008, 2009, 2014; Ragin et al., 2006; Fiss, 2011; Kent, 2008; Ragin and Rihoux, 2009). The fsQCA approach permits structured analytic support for two reasons: (1) It is designed to help with larger-scale case-based research and (2) it supports equifinality. This means we can leverage some of the situated and nuanced insights of a full case study while pursuing a structured comparison among cases.

## Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis

Originating as a means for doing comparative analyses in political science, fsQCA (and the simpler, non-fuzzy or crisp set, QCA) provides scholars the ability to conduct structured comparisons across multiple cases. As noted, this approach has drawn attention from many areas of social science, including – of late – information systems (Frisk et al., 2014; Schryen, 2013; Heidari and Loucupoulos, 2014; Rivard and Lapointe, 2012; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013; Liu et al., 2015; Zimmerman et al., 2013). Given its relatively recent appearance in the information systems literature, we begin by providing a general overview of fsQCA.

This analytical technique is best applied when the researcher anticipates that complex causality is present and the population of cases is too small for standard statistical techniques. Typically, the researcher has previously gained knowledge of the population and uses this knowledge to define, specify and measure the key concepts applicable to the system under study (e.g., Fiss, 2011; Kent, 2008).

Qualitative comparative analysis is premised on a casebased perspective, assessing similarities and differences across multiple cases (Ragin, 2014). The approach is based on set theory wherein analysis is focused on the degree to which any one variable is a subset or superset of another. In QCA, sets are dichotomous (or ‘‘crisp’’) and any one case is either ‘‘in’’ or ‘‘out’’ of the set based on the presence or absence of the set’s membership criteria in the particular case’s profile.

For example, in crisp set analysis, the set of employed workers can be represented by a variable with two values: 1 (employed or ‘‘in’’ the set) and 0 (unemployed or ‘‘out’’ of the set). Fuzzy-set thinking allows for membership in the set to range between 0 (full non-membership) and 1 (full membership). Using this example, a ‘‘part-time’’ employee would have a membership in the fuzzy-set of ‘‘employed’’ with a value somewhere between 0 and 1. Fuzzy membership means the worker might be assigned a value that is neither fully employed (1) nor fully unemployed (0), with 0.5 being maximally ambiguous (not sure if the person is employed or not).

## Data collection

The data set for this study were assembled by using a casestudy protocol conducted via telephone interviews contracted to a university survey research center. The protocol went through rigorous pre- and pilot testing (per Dillman, 1978) with the help of the professional research staff of the center. Once the survey protocol was stable, the telephone interviewing staff was trained to conduct the final survey. The telephone-based structured case-study interview protocol took 50 minutes, on average, to complete. All data were collected by phone, with the respondent following a webbased overview of the project and protocol while speaking with the survey center staff.

The case-study protocol included questions on respondent demographics, PSN initiation, purpose, functions, membership, usage, development, financing, technology components, outsourcing, standards, data, governance, goals, problems and performance. Based on extensive pre-interview secondary data collection leading to a data set on 275 PSN, we were able to involve key informants from 80 PSN operating at state, local, and regional (e.g., encompassing a city inside a county, or across two or more counties or states) levels. Key informants held senior leadership positions with the PSN. Of the 80 PSN who participated, information from 61 indicated their PSN was operational (they have a history of use). These 61 PSN constitute our initial data set for the analysis: 33 of these are court-oriented; 28, police-oriented.

## Analysis

For the analysis on governance, we first relied on a screening question – where we asked if the PSN had a governance structure in place. This is a prerequisite for assessing the value of its four governance competencies (as derived in the following section). The initial condition of a positive response to the question ‘‘Is there a formal governance body in the PSN?’’ was thus required for the PSN to be included in each analysis. Of the 61, 42 indicated that they have a governance structure, and a full set of governance-related data was collected for each of these 42 PSN.

Using the 42 operational PSN cases, we pursued fsQCA in three steps, as is typical (see Drass and Ragin, 2008). First, we created and calibrated measures for the elements of our analysis as combinations of questions from the structured case interview (again, see Appendix A in Supplementary material). For the second step, we used Ragin’s fsQCA guidance and the QCA 3.0 software<sup>3</sup> to create configurations using the seven elements representing causes for the outcomes of interest (effectiveness) (the truth table, central to the fsQCA analysis, is included in Appendix B in Supplementary material). In the third step we assessed the solution consistency and coverage (provided as part of the fsQCA computation) to better understand the explanatory power of the configurations that came from the analysis.

The first step required us to create and calibrate the four specific governance competencies (organizational, technical, financial, and stakeholder) and PSN effectiveness (summarized below and detailed in Appendix A in Supplementary material). The other elements of the analysis (legal arrangement and PSN type) are crisp set data so that only coding is needed. Our coding and calibration adjusts for a skewed tendency toward positive responses by respondents which can be attributed to two characteristics of the data set and most importantly, to the nature of these model elements. First, the need to rely on a single, internal, knowledgeable informant tends to reflect the positive perceptions of one inclined toward PSN success. Second, PSN in the 42 cases comprising this study include only those that are currently functioning, which requires that they are actually being used by at least some of the members, and thus must be providing at least minimal useful functionality. Finally, we are looking to produce configurations leading to high levels of governance competency or effectiveness, which means the data is to be measured against a scale of whether or not high levels are achieved, and not as a scale from low to high achievement. With these caveats, we combined our experience with PSN functioning to construct meaningful and discriminating coding and calibration mechanisms. The full set of coding schemes and calibration comprising each of the constructs in the analyses can be found in Appendix A. Examples of coding and calibration are given in the following subsections.

## PSN effectiveness element

We assess the relationship between PSN governance competencies and PSN effectiveness by computing an outcome measure of PSN effectiveness as a measure of technology uses, productivity and data sharing among PSN participants. We use three questions from the survey to create the variable (PSN effectiveness)<sup>4</sup>:

Q63: Does the PSN’s technology provide the expected functionality?

(1) Strongly agree, (2) agree, (3) Neither agree nor disagree, (4) disagree, (5) strongly disagree, (6) NA (1) Improved a lot, (2) improved some, (3) no change, (4) worsened, (5) don’t know

Q65: Based on your knowledge, has data sharing among the PSN’s participating organizations improved as a result of being in the PSN?

(1) Improved a lot, (2) improved some, (3) no change, (4) worsened, (5) don’t know

We chose to average responses from each informant to create a composite governance performance score. Affirmative responses such as ‘‘improve a lot’’ or ‘‘strongly agree’’ are assigned fully in (1) and negative responses such as ‘‘worsened’’ and ‘‘disagree’’ are assigned fully out. Responses ‘‘improved some’’ and ‘‘agree’’ are coded as more ‘‘in’’ the set of high effectiveness than out (.67) since the variable is being used to assess membership attributed to being a highly effective PSN.

A ‘‘no change’’ answer to Q64 or Q65 requires judgment, which we provided based on our knowledge of the data set and PSN background. For these questions, we coded such responses as more out than in (0.33) (equating the ‘‘no change’’ response to either a lack of improvement or as not as likely to be a highly effective PSN). Similarly, a ‘‘don’t know’’ response is coded as fully out, reasoning an informant would have knowledge of improvement if any, even a small improvement, occurred. We use the same reasoning for the coding of ‘‘neither’’ in Q63 to 0.33. But, we coded ‘‘not applicable’’ as maximally ambiguous (0.5) since it is a nonassessment (and as such we cannot judge set membership).

Coding of missing data also requires research judgment as this may be due to any number of reasons. Cases with multiple missing data responses were considered invalid cases and excluded from our analysis. Cases with only a single missing data were interpreted as 0.33 to capture a lack of an answer, similar to ‘‘no change’’ or ‘‘neither’’. For all responses of Q63, Q64, and Q65 (183 total responses) there was only one missing datum, which we thus coded as 0.33.

## Configuration computation and assessment

The second step, following coding and calibration, is to calculate the configurations. In the following section, we present the configurations of PSN achieving high effectiveness resulting from fsQCA analysis. We use Ragin’s QCA 2.0 software to calculate the causal combinations of our governance competency factors, legal authority characterization, and PSN types exhibiting a high degree of effectiveness. This requires several intermediate steps to develop the Boolean truth tables and establish parameters for doing the analysis. The truth table resulting from this work is included in Appendix B in Supplementary material.

The third step is assessing the set of configurations relative to solution consistency and coverage. Solution consistency represents the degree to which the case data aligns with the measures involved. A consistency approaching unity is desired (case data fully match up with measures), although a score above 0.75 is considered acceptable for establishing the sought-after relationship (Ragin, 2008). For our data, a high consistency measure indicates the PSN included in these configurations have strong membership in the set exhibiting high performance governance. Coverage can only be assessed once a high degree of consistency is present. Beyond the overall solution assessment, the raw and unique coverage of each configuration represent the numbers of cases with an overlap or intersection in each configuration. Solution coverage represents the degree to which membership in the configurations accounts for cases exhibiting the outcome. It is the case that the two sometimes work at odds with one another. Coverage demonstrates empirical importance rather than theoretical evidence (Ragin, 2008).

As noted above, we report the intermediate solution from fsQCA because it provides understandable, and conceptually relevant, results. We also note the intermediate solution is identical to the complex solution for our analysis. Our data set covers 15 truth table rows/configurations (see Appendix B in Supplementary material) of a possible 128 (i.e., 7 conditions, 2<sup>7</sup> = 128). The 113 ‘‘remainder’’ configurations (Ragin, 2008, p. 155) if utilized as counterfactuals, results in an over-simplified parsimonious solution (parsimonious conditions are shown double-boxed in Table 5).<sup>5</sup>

The third configuration is a single PSN that is policeoriented, is the only configuration that requires high levels of financial governance to achieve high levels of network effectiveness, and is the only police-oriented PSN that relies on an elective agreement. We label this PSN/configuration as a ‘‘special policing arrangement.

Inspection shows configuration #4 differs from configuration #1 on three of the four governance competencies. The PSN in configuration #4 share with those in configuration #1 a mix of police and courts-oriented arrangement, reliance on a mandate for legal authority and high levels of stakeholder governance competence. This noted, six of the nine PSN in this configuration are also in the first configuration (basic arrangement). In the fourth configuration, technology governance is not part of the path to high levels of network effectiveness (suggesting that the three PSN unique to this configuration have low levels of this competency, compared to the six PSN who overlap with the first configuration and must have high levels of technology governance competence). Configuration #4 also is characterized by the absence of organizational and financial governance competencies, suggesting that the three other PSN in this configuration have low levels of these competencies. We label this configuration as ‘‘minimalist’’ PSN.

Configuration #5 is also a single, and courts-oriented, PSN. This is the only PSN that relies on neither form of legal authority (making this a curious case). This PSN/configuration has either high (in) or low (out) levels of all four competencies, and accounts for very little of the coverage. Scholars trained in statistical analysis will be drawn to considering this case an outlier. However, set-theoretic analysis suggests this is not ‘‘away’’ from all others but rather there are no other PSN that share this configuration, and yet it belongs in the set of highly effective PSN. More concisely, configuration #5 is a viable path to high PSN effectiveness. For this reason, we label the PSN/configuration #5 as ‘uniquely informal.’

Looking beyond the specific configurations, we can also assess the relative roles or prevalence of all the elements relative to effectiveness. From this closer inspection, we advance six additional insights from this analysis:

PSN type (court- or police-oriented) does not matter much relative to effectiveness Configurations #2 and #5 (basic arrangement and uniquely informal) account for nine of the 19 court-oriented PSN involved in this analysis. Yet, only one configuration (#3, involving one PSN, the special police arrangement) has police-oriented type as part of the path to high effectiveness. Certainly, policeoriented PSN are more varied in form and functionality than are court-oriented PSN. This suggests that the relationship between PSN type and PSN governance may be more complex than our current focus on network-level governance competencies is capturing.

It is possible for PSN to perform effectively under either mandate or elective agreement Evidence shows both arrangements allow for high effectiveness. This makes sense, suggesting (some) governance competencies are more critical to effectiveness than the source and form of the PSN’s legal basis. This said, there is one PSN that is in the set of highly effective PSN that does not rely on either form of legal authority – as noted, its governance structure was reported as informal (and unique).

Curious results regarding organizational governance competence This network-level competence focuses attention on governing the PSN staff – that group and the formal organization charged with supporting and enabling collaboration across the digital platform and services while managing the shared resources to the collective benefit of all member organizations. This matters for nine of the PSN included in the analysis (members of configurations #2 and #5). But, organizational governance is not included as an element of high effectiveness for 10 PSN (in configurations #3 and #4). And, for 11 PSN, organizational governance seems to not matter. That is, PSN effectiveness is indifferent to the presence or lack of effective governance of the organization that exists to connect the members.

Technical governance competence matters for most The network-level competence of governing the PSN’s shared technological infrastructure matters for 26 of the 30 cases included in this analysis or is not an influential element (in three cases), and is a negative factor in only one PSN.

Stakeholder governance competence matters for all Every configuration leading to high effectiveness included the stakeholder governance competence.

Financial governance competence does not matter relative to PSN effectiveness This element was present in only one configuration (containing one case) and its negation is present in two configurations containing ten PSN.

## Discussion

The analysis shows there are multiple configurations of network-level governance competencies that support high levels of public safety network effectiveness. In the rest of this discussion, we explore each of the configurations, then highlight several of the patterns across the configurations. We follow these sections with a short discussion of the concept of equifinality. We conclude by reflecting on the implications for professional practice and for advancing research on network governance.

## Configurations

The five configurations help to make clear that different combinations of network-level governance competencies are possible, with each allowing PSN to achieve high levels of network effectiveness. These configurations showcase a wide variety of paths to high performance. Two of the configurations contain a single PSN, another two have some overlap, and the fifth is distinct from all others. Still, it is possible to achieve high levels of effectiveness for both court and policeoriented PSN, and to do so following similar and distinctly different paths. Likewise, it is possible to achieve high levels of effectiveness based on either mandates or elective agreements (and in the case of the configuration #5, ‘‘uniquely informal’’ via an informal set of agreements).

The configurations composed from the set of network-level governance competencies and legal authority variables suggests that the particulars of the institutional and operational environments in which each PSN exists shape how these organizations adapt to succeed (see also Gil-Garcia and Djoko (2016)). More directly relevant, Feldman (2016) highlights that organizations assembled to support inter-organizational information sharing will have to pursue what he calls ‘‘polycentric’’ governance – or the ability to adapt to the needs and interests of the members, and to share governance competencies in ways that further the needs of that set of collaborators. Our data support such locally tuned theorizing on the structure and function of network governance.

## Patterns across the configurations

The findings illuminate five patterns across the configurations: (1) It is possible for PSN to perform effectively under either mandate or elective agreement; (2) Curious results regarding organizational governance competence; (3) Technical governance competence matters; (4) Stakeholder governance competence matters; and (5) Financial governance competence does not matter relative to PSN effectiveness.

Relative to legal authority, there is very little that we can say beyond the clear implication that some legal basis is needed. Per Provan and Kenis (2008), such a legal basis is at the core of network governance. Both top-down and bottomup initiation are found to be successful options for origination. However, we observe that the four configurations where these options are included show no pattern other than the single common competency of stakeholder governance. This said, configuration #5 reflects a highly effective PSN that is based on a set of informal agreements. It is worth repeating that this does not make such a case an outlier in the statistical sense that most readers will impose. Rather, this configuration makes clear that there is the possibility that an informal collective action can lead to high levels of effectiveness. But, seeing there is only one PSN in this configuration, it represents a unique confluence of informal action (that may be hard to repeat elsewhere, or into the future should the actors change).

The curious results regarding the effects of the organizational governance competence deserve more extensive research. The conceptual premise of this study is that governance matters, making this result perplexing. Perhaps this result is a by-product of poor measurement or is the artifact of a calibration issue (see Appendix A in Supplementary material), but we clearly presume otherwise. More likely, it may be that the attention to the PSN is seconded to the roles it plays in supporting its members (which is why stakeholder governance competence and technology governance competence show more often in the configurations). Joukhadar and Rabhi’s (2016) focus on the governance of service oriented architectures (SOA) suggests that if the operational aspects of the sharing arrangements (in their case SOA, in our case, the PSN’s information and technology sharing arrangements) are serving the members, then the collaboration is well-governed. If this were the case, then these competencies would all be overlapping sets in the fsQCA analysis. Since this is not the case, more attention to the roles of organizational governance in ICH are needed.

Relative to technology governance competence, configurational analysis reaffirms what has been previously found in many studies – effective governance of ICT is a contributor to high effectiveness (e.g., Gil-Garcia et al.,

2007; Gil-Garcia and Djoko, 2016; Fedorowicz et al., 2007; Markus and Bui, 2012; Pardo et al., 2008; Prasad et al., 2012). So, while neither surprising nor new, it is useful to see this finding here as both a face-validity check for fsQCA and as a reminder that PSN exist in great part to support the digital infrastructure of sharing and collaboration for their member organizations.

Relative to stakeholder governance competence, configurational analysis makes it clear that this always important. Every configuration included stakeholder governance competence. As such, it appears that a high level of stakeholder governance competence is a necessary (but not sufficient) network-level condition for PSN to achieve effectiveness. That is, the set of PSN who exhibit high levels of stakeholder governance are all within the set of the PSN who exhibit high levels of effectiveness. This makes sense as competence in working with the many stakeholder organizations is at the core of network governance (e.g., Provan and Kenis, 2008), and – more broadly – the effectiveness of ICT (e.g., Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993).

Relative to financial governance competence, the fsQCA analysis suggests that this does not matter relative to PSN effectiveness. This element was present in only one configuration (containing one case) and its’ negation is present in two configurations containing 10 other PSN. This suggests PSN effectiveness is not achieved through network-level competence in financial governance. This finding does not mean that financial management is unimportant. What is suggests is that financial management is not a differentiating characteristic distinguishing high and low-effectiveness PSN. It may be telling us that spending undue effort on financial governance distracts from monitoring and controlling other, more critical aspects of the ICH. This counterintuitive result suggests that more research is needed.

More broadly, this set of findings suggests that while these aspects of network-level competencies and their contribution to PSN (and more broadly, NAO) governance must be accomplished, and likely done well given the many rules, policies and regulations surrounding these competencies, excellence in organizational or financial governance does not create a path to high effectiveness for PSN. Certainly, this finding deserves more attention as it challenges some deeply held assumptions about public-sector excellence and in particular about the role of governance relative to allocation of resources, generally (e.g., Tiwana and Kim, 2016; Kapucu et al., 2014). At issue, again, is not the unimportance of financial or organizational aspects of governance, but that doing these well, or not, are not drivers of a PSN’s effectiveness. One reason for this seemingly contradictory finding is the nature of network administrative organizations. In the absence of a single decision-making structure, and the budgetary and financial control this implies, it may be that the structure of the governance, not the management of resources, is the key competence (e.g., Pardo et al., 2012).

## Equifinality

The multiple paths or configurations achieving high levels of effectiveness suggests the systems concept of equifinality is an empirically supportable way of conceptualizing the sets of arrangements helping to achieve high levels of effectiveness (Von Bertalanffy, 1968). In the context of the PSN studied here, and IOS more broadly, the finding of multiple viable configurations suggests efforts to pursue a best-practices approach are problematic. Contingency scholars have long argued against de-contextualized best-practices guidance, and our findings will strengthen this call. More usefully, the findings make clear that patterns of arrangements – i.e., the configurations – can be discerned and that there is no clear one best way or easily copy-able ‘‘best practice.’’ Said simply: the data show there are multiple ways to achieve bestpractice.

The conceptual value of multiple paths recognizes there are specific local issues and arrangements shaping the ways in which actions unfold. Articulating and illustrating such local logics is at the core of pursuing case analysis (Yin, 2014). What comparative case analysis helps makes clear is there are some patterns or clusters (e.g., managing stakeholders, managing the technological infrastructure) common to many PSN/cases (these are the configurations) and these commonalities may appear in different combinations (the multiple configurations). From this insight, it is possible, if not critical, to return to the data to identify and explore in more detail the additional structured case data about cases in common configurations. Doing so provides insight into other similar characteristics of these cases, deepening the resulting analysis. This said, the presence of equifinality is clear: There is no one best way to effectively govern a PSN – there are multiple, definable, viable arrangements.

## Patterns across the six cases of low effectiveness

The configuration analysis provides additional insight on six cases that are not included in the set of high effectiveness PSN. These low-effectiveness PSN seem to share some commonalities: All six cases area police-oriented PSN. From the Truth Table (see Appendix B in Supplementary material), all six of these low-effectiveness PSN have high levels of the four network-level competencies included in the model, and all six were operating under an elective agreement. This suggests there are other factors that help to shape which PSN are out (and perhaps in) of the highly effective set.

## Implications for professional practice

Findings from this analysis leads to three implications relative to the design of PSN and their governance. First, the ability to involve, engage and satisfy stakeholders transcends all configurations and underscores the importance – if not necessity – of creating multiple mechanisms to connect with all participants. We see this as encompassing the political leadership as an integral part of public-sector institutional life; involving the professional administrators at the various involved participating agencies, developing close working relationships and decision-making arrangements with the range of technologists (including vendors) who must collaborate to support the mixed and split-up ICT infrastructure, engaging the ‘‘rank-and-file’’ officers and other end-users, and perhaps involving citizens – as a PSN is of and about communities (as difficult as that can be at times) (see also Pardo et al., 2008).

Second, findings make clear the importance of technological governance. If this is to be a competence, then we advocate locating technological expertise as high up the authority chain as possible. But, we further advocate expertise is internal, not a vendor-provided, resource. We advocate this because a competence is a property of an institution (as Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) articulated more than 20 years ago). And, nearly 25 years on, governance scholars actively embrace ICT as a competence or capability in their models (e.g., Tiwana and Kim, 2016; Constantinides and Barrett, 2014).

The third implication of this research for professional practice regards the role and importance of both organizational and financial governance. The empirical findings show these competencies are rarely stressed in the group of highperforming PSN. This suggests the particular rationales for such a counterintuitive set of findings deserves more attention. It also suggests that these functions are secondary efforts. A PSN must follow the laws and meet obligations – they cannot be bad at managing their organizational resources and they cannot be spendthrift of resources. But, it appears that excelling at these things – which may have value in other ways – does not lead a PSN to achieving high levels of effectiveness.

## Implications for network governance

Two aspects of the work presented here advance the current state of theorizing and discourse around Provan’s (with Provan and Kenis, 2008; Provan et al., 2007) concept of network administrative organizations (NAOs) – what Vaughan (1992) and Fisher and Aguinis (2017) call theory elaboration. We pursued our analysis using Provan and Kenis conceptualization of network governance because it provided a robust and comprehensive platform for theorizing on the ways in which PSN are governed. The current conceptualization of NAO, and networked governance more broadly, theorizes that networklevel competencies are important to the effectiveness of shared governance. One goal of our work was to provide additional conceptual clarity regarding networked governance – we wanted to elaborate this theoretical position.

Our analysis of PSN advances the current efforts to conceptualize NAO and network-level competencies in two ways. First, we add conceptual depth to a set of competencies used here, providing both a working definition and some initial measures of organizational, technological, financial and stakeholders competencies. Second, analysis of the PSN data shows us that there are multiple configurations of competencies that allow for highly effective forms of network governance, and some network-level governance competencies matter more than others.

Second, and building directly from our analysis of PSN, analysis suggests two specific network-level governance competencies – stakeholder governance and technological governance – are critical, and perhaps essential, elements for achieving high levels of performance in networked arrangements. In contrast, we observe network-level competence with organizational and financial governance may have no direct bearing on high performance (but likely have some sort of minimum need or expectation). Certainly, this is a challenging finding to consider and deserves additional empirical testing (see also Lynn et al., 2000).

For scholars in information systems interested in advancing network governance, work is needed to more carefully explore the roles of trust, number of network participants, and degree of goal consensus, and to consider other network governance competencies (see also Belanger and Carter, 2008;

Markus and Bui, 2012). We were able to focus our study on a specific set of cases (the PSN) exhibiting NAO governance structures. And, we focused analytic and conceptual attention on four network-level governance competencies that the literature made clear to us needed to be included, leaving for others to advance additional competencies.

Finally, we encourage scholars to consider the fsQCA approach as an alternative to help with case-to-case comparative inquiry. We opted to pursue a substantial cross-case analysis and designed this study to provide a structured minicase data collection. Others can extend our approach or pursue a smaller number of more detailed cases for greater analytic depth. The fsQCA approach complements case studies and possibly larger-scale empirical analyses – a bridge perhaps. Applying the approach requires substantial theoretical support for both selection of the elements to consider and to calibrate these elements for the logical analysis. As such, this is clearly a judgment call in research, for a method we – like others – are pleased to see become part of the canon of methods for information systems scholarship.

## About the Authors

Jane Fedorowicz is the Chester B. Slade Professor of Accounting and Information Systems at Bentley University. Her e-government research on interorganizational information sharing ranges from public safety networks to social media use by police and local communities. She is a Fellow and Past President of the Association for Information Systems.

Steve Sawyer is on faculty at Syracuse University’s iSchool. His research focuses on the relationships among changing forms of work, organizing and uses of information and communication technologies. Prior to returning to Syracuse, Steve helped start the College of Information Sciences and Technology at The Pennsylvania State University. He earned his doctorate at Boston University.

Dr. Arthur Tomasino is a researcher in IS and IT-enabled inter-organizational collaborations. He is currently working with faculty members at Bentley, and Syracuse Universities. He uses fuzzy-set qualitative analysis (fsQCA) for data analysis and is currently extending fsQCA’s use to flavorchemistry with Food Science & Technology researchers at Oregon State University.
