# Social Capital and Contract Duration in Buyer-Supplier Networks for Information Technology Outsourcing

Kiron Ravindran

IE Business School, University of Madrid, 28006 Madrid, Spain, kiron.ravindran@ie.edu

Anjana Susarla

Michigan State University, East Lansing, Michigan 48824, asusarla@broad.msu.edu

Deepa Mani

Indian School of Business, Gachibowli, Hyderabad 500032, India, deepa\_mani@isb.edu

Vijay Gurbaxani University of California, Irvine, Irvine, California 92697, vgurbaxa@uci.edu

his paper presents new evidence on the role of embeddedness in predicting contract duration in the context of information technology outsourcing. Contract duration is a strategic decision that aligns interests of clients and vendors, providing the benefits of business continuity to clients and incentives to undertake relationship specific investments for vendors. Considering the salience of this phenomenon, there has been limited empirical scrutiny of how contract duration is awarded. We posit that clients and vendors obtain two benefits from being embedded in an interorganizational network. First, the learning and experience accumulated from being embedded in a client-vendor network could mitigate the challenges in managing longer term contracts. Second, the network serves as a reputation system that can stratify vendors according to their trustworthiness and reliability, which is important in longer term arrangements. In particular, we attempt to make a substantive contribution to the literature by theorizing about embeddedness at four distinct levels: structural embeddedness at the node level, relational embeddedness at the dyad level, contractual embeddedness at the level of a neighborhood of contracts, and finally, positional embeddedness at the level of the entire network. We analyze a data set of 22,039 outsourcing contracts implemented between 1989 and 2008. We find that contract duration is indeed associated with structural and positional embeddedness of participant firms, with the relational embeddedness of the buyer-seller dyad, and with the duration of other contracts to which it is connected through common firms. Given the nature of our data, identification using traditional ordinary least squares based approaches is difficult given the unobserved errors clustered along two nonnested dimensions and the autocorrelation in a firm’s decision (here the contract) with those of contracts in its reference group. We use a multiway cluster robust estimation and a network auto-regressive estimation to address these issues. Implications for literature and practice are discussed.

Keywords: social capital; reputation; IT outsourcing; contract design

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) outsourcing or the externalization of IT-enabled business functions through multiyear contractual arrangements constitutes the largest component of technology spending in firms worldwide. The global market for IT and business process services has reached nearly \$1 trillion in 2015 (Gartner 2014). Sourcing IT capabilities from external service providers is fundamental to the modern firm, yet IT outsourcing arrangements do not always deliver as expected (Goolsby and Whitlow 2004,

DiamondCluster 2006),<sup>1</sup> with clients expressing high rates of dissatisfaction with outsourced projects. While there is a considerable body of literature on the nature of transaction costs and antecedents of governance arrangements in outsourcing, limited prior work exists on contract duration, a strategic and economic characteristic of interorganizational relationships.

Prior literature on contract duration has examined the costs and benefits of long-term contracts. For vendors, longer term contracts offer a tangible benefit in terms of a continued revenue stream and provide the necessary incentives to undertake asset specific investments (e.g., Crocker and Masten 1988). For both parties, longer term contracts reduce costs of bargaining and negotiating the terms of trade. Evidence from IT outsourcing supports this view. The trade press emphasizes the stability and control that result from long-term contracts (Tabbitt 2013). Practitioners similarly highlight the need for long-term contracts with low risk of business disruption, considering the interdependence between complex organizations and business transformation needs. The need for intense communication (Goo et al. 2009) and learning (Argyres and Mayer 2007) creates high switching costs for the client when contracts are cancelled midway. A long-term contract with a vendor minimizes the costs of transitioning and service disruption (Goo et al. 2007). The difficulty with structuring long-term contracts for IT outsourcing, however, is the threat of vendor lock-in and inflexibility (e.g., Susarla 2012). Considering the salience of the phenomenon, there is comparatively limited guidance in the literature on how duration is awarded in contracts for IT outsourcing.

Understanding the structuring of contract duration also poses a puzzle to traditional views of outsourcing, which has focused on theories of transaction cost economics (TCE) and relational governance. TCE is relevant to understanding why a contract for, say, application development may be shorter than that of an infrastructure agreement (Gurbaxani 2007, Deloitte 2009). However, TCE does not explain the substantial variation in contract duration for the same service across different clients and vendors. For instance, one contract for datacenter outsourcing is awarded for an initial term of 10 years<sup>2</sup> whereas another is awarded for five.<sup>3</sup> Theories of relational contracts would predict that firms repeatedly contract with a few trusted partners. However, IT outsourcers frequently cultivate a portfolio of multiple vendors. Even within a client-vendor dyad there is considerable diversity in the range and volume of services outsourced (Forrester 2011). The variation in contract duration across different engagements, even for similar services, raises questions about what determines contract duration.

We use a network perspective, so far (to our knowledge) unexamined in the context of IT outsourcing, to address how contract duration is structured in IT outsourcing. A firm’s embeddedness in a network of interfirm relationships presents both informational and control advantages in economic exchange (Uzzi 1996, 1999; DiMaggio and Louch 1998). We construct an interorganizational network of firms that outsource IT services over a 20-year period between 1989 and 2008. We examine how information transmission in the buyer-supplier network mitigates two types of contracting challenges that neither transaction cost economizing nor relational governance would address.

The first challenge is that transaction cost economizing need not eliminate all ex post hazards inherent in contracts (e.g., Rooks et al. 2006). For instance, even though parties may desire long-term contracting arrangements, they may lack an understanding of the set of contingencies to be included in contracts. Mayer and Argyres (2004) posit that firms learn about potential contingencies and hazards incrementally from experience. Because it is difficult to envision outcomes and set up detailed benchmarks at the outset (e.g., Susarla et al. 2010), the accumulated experience through embedded ties facilitates learning about market conditions and terms of exchange (Raub and Weesie 1990, Robinson and Stuart 2006). Hence the learning and experience from being embedded in a client-vendor network could confer the ability to manage the minutiae and details of outsourcing.

The second challenge is the difficulty of assuring that contractual obligations will actually be met (Klein and Leffler 1981). Relational governance arrangements relying on a small set of vendors limit a client firm’s ability to benefit from knowledge about contracting practices with other exchange partners. Conversely, dealing with a small set of clients limits reputational spillovers for vendors. Therefore, predictions from theories of relational contracts would suggest that the receipt and transmission of information is limited to firms that belong to the set of strongly tied exchange partners. Podolny (2001) posits that the network, in addition to acting like pipes that transfer information, also acts as a prism in transmitting information to actors beyond the dyad. In a buyer-supplier network, firms are likely to communicate problematic experiences with a business partner to other suppliers (Rooks et al. 2011), which could then be transmitted to other clients and vendors beyond the dyad. When transactions are embedded in dense business networks, information transmitted to parties beyond a focal client-vendor dyad makes it possible for potential exchange partners (of the focal client and vendor) to understand what transpired within an outsourcing agreement. The network then works as a reputation system that can stratify vendors according to their trustworthiness and reliability, which is important in the procurement of customized and idiosyncratic IT services, where it can be difficult to compare service providers.

We consider the following four different forms of embeddedness: structural at the level of the node (individual client or vendor), relational at the level of the dyad (the client-vendor pair), contractual at the level of the neighborhood of contracts, and positional embeddedness of the node with reference to the entire network. Structural embeddedness refers to the extent of relationships around an actor that impact their tendency to cooperate (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999) through the threat of loss of reputation in the overall network. Relational embeddedness pertains to the depth of ties or quality of relations and is an outcome of a cumulative history of relationships between the focal client and vendor. Third, we account for the impact of contractual embeddedness on contract duration. Because firms are embedded in an interorganizational network, the structuring of contract duration is likely to be influenced by duration of contracts that are proximate in the interorganizational network. Last, we consider positional embeddedness as a measure of being connected to other firms that are themselves central.

The analysis of interfirm networks using traditional means such as ordinary least squares (OLS) poses two significant empirical challenges. First, each observation represents a dyad, and is therefore, doubly indexed (Dekker et al. 2007) rendering observations nonindependent. Interobservation correlation arises due to unobserved errors being clustered along two nonnested dimensions, that of vendors and clients. We conduct a multiway cluster robust estimation (Cameron et al. 2010) that explicitly allows observations to be clustered along multiple dimensions. Second, firms in the network are influenced by (and attempt to influence) industry norms and decisions of other firms (e.g., Krackhardt 1992, Gulati and Gargiulo 1999). Thus, it is possible that the dependent variable observed per contract, in this case, duration, is not only the outcome of a calculated decision but also a reflection of the mean dependent variable of the contract’s reference group, represented by the neighborhood of contracts with which it is associated. To study this we use a network auto-regressive estimation (Butts 2008) that models the correlation between the dependent variable of the focal firm and that of the firms’ neighborhood.

Using data on 22,039 outsourcing contracts implemented between 1989 and 2008, we examine the impact of embeddedness on contract duration. To our knowledge, our study represents a first attempt to create and study a network of outsourced IT services and to relate network parameters to contract design. We find that, after ruling out alternate causal explanations attributable to transaction, firm, industry, region, and temporal characteristics, the embeddedness of participant firms in a broader network of outsourcing firms is indeed a consistent predictor of longer term contracts for vendors. In the case of clients, the results are nuanced. Clients are likely to use their structural embeddedness to get shorter contracts from vendors, yet those with higher positional embeddedness are likely to sign up for longer contracts. The dyad of a client and vendor is likely to consider the strength rather than the mere existence of a prior relationship in determining future contract duration. We also see, as predicted, that contract duration is positively correlated with the duration of contracts in the neighborhood defined by common vendors or clients. In summary, our results suggest that firms may mitigate the hazard of ex post transaction costs in long-term contracts by relying on the information available from embedded firms in the buyer-supplier network. A substantial amount of management literature has considered alliance networks (e.g., Baum et al. 2000), board of director networks (e.g., Carpenter and Westphal 2001) or other types of interfirm networks (e.g., Grandori and Soda 1995). However, there is limited empirical evidence on how interfirm networks impact contractual parameters. We hope to contribute to the broader evidence on interfirm networks beyond IT outsourcing as well.

Our results also offer normative implications for practitioners. Rooks et al. (2006) in their study of supply chain networks posit that purchasing arrangements are often structured by guidance from experts. A similar dynamic operates in IT outsourcing. The proposals used to evaluate sourcing choices usually focus on the overall track record of the vendor, but provide little information about the set of clients (including very experienced clients) with whom the vendor may have interacted. Whereas a few industry analysts have ranked vendors on their ability to provide specific services,<sup>4</sup> such reports focus only on a limited set of the leading firms in each category; a large majority of firms remains unranked. There could also be considerable heterogeneity across firms that have instituted such certification programs. The embeddedness of clients and vendors could provide another method by which to evaluate the potential reliability and ability of trading partners. Vendors can evaluate whether a strategy of breadth (i.e., greater structural embeddedness with a variety of client firms) is more advantageous compared to depth (relational embeddedness through deep ties with a handful of client firms). Similarly, client firms can see the influence of contractual norms in the history of their trading partners affecting their decision to economize on the costs of contracting.

## 2. Theory and Hypotheses

## Transaction Costs and Contract Duration in IT Outsourcing

IT outsourcing is characterized by substantial uncertainty in service specifications and the task environment, making it difficult and costly to enumerate an exhaustive set of requirements up front. Incompleteness in service requirements, in turn, renders it challenging to specify detailed benchmarks for service delivery (e.g., Susarla et al. 2010) or ascribe responsibility for contract failures to any one party (Banerjee and Duflo 2000). Furthermore, IT services are intertwined with business processes in a firm, and are heterogeneous and idiosyncratic in their delivery. For these reasons, IT outsourcing demands considerable transaction-specific investments that reflect the unique context of an individual firm (Susarla et al. 2010). These investments may not be scalable even between the same vendor and client because IT outsourcing may not involve the repeated exchange of identical services.

The combination of uncertainty and asset specificity in IT outsourcing enhances the likelihood of ex post opportunism. In particular, incompleteness of requirements ex ante makes it difficult to verify ex post whether the vendor is indeed expending the stated effort in providing high quality service and in containing costs. Any cost overruns in service delivery, therefore, are difficult to monitor or measure (Banerjee and Duflo 2000), and result in an asymmetry in understanding the true costs of delivering outsourced technology services. In such cases, there could be considerable ex post transaction costs in the form of costly bargaining and privately favorable redistribution of surplus (Susarla et al. 2009). When vendors anticipate bargaining over ex post surplus, they are reluctant to invest in noncontractible, client-specific investments (Klein et al. 1978). With complexity and uncertainty in exchange conditions, vendor firms, who commit to exchange terms over a long horizon, are unlikely to adapt to disturbances, thereby, leading to maladaptation and dissipation of gains from outsourcing (Susarla 2012). The danger then is that the services delivered through the outsourcing initiative could be out of step with changing business objectives of the client organization (Susarla 2012). For instance, the client may be locked into high prices that do not reflect the lower prices of inputs such as hardware and data storage or may be locked into performance benchmarks that do not reflect the changing value of underlying technologies. Furthermore, vendors could use their lock-in position to engage in haggling and appropriation of surplus (Crocker and Masten 1988, Susarla and Barua 2011) further exacerbating the likelihood of maladaptation. The perils of lock-in and underinvestment are enhanced when it is difficult to include too many explicit provisions to deter holdup. Such provisions can make the contract too rigid to address future contingencies, further raising the risk of maladaptation, haggling, and bonding costs (Williamson 1985).

A considerable amount of literature highlights the benefits of long-term contracting arrangements in interfirm trade relationships (Crocker and Masten 1988, Joskow 1987). Long-term contracts align incentives for both vendors and clients and reduce bargaining and renegotiation costs (Guriev and Kvasov 2005) through a stipulation of exchange terms for a longer period of time. Vendors are assured of a continued revenue stream and incentives for required asset specific investments (Susarla et al. 2010), whereas the clients could benefit from vendors’ relationship specific investments and lower risks of disruption of service (Goo et al. 2007). For clients the risk is that any termination of a contract before the expected length of outsourcing poses substantial risks such as disruption of service. Vendors anticipating contract termination could try to maximize resource utilization and redeploy critical personnel to a different client engagement, which lowers the business value of outsourcing to the client. Contract duration therefore is a strategic decision for clients to obtain vital resources for outsourced IT tasks and as such is an important contract feature that merits a deeper understanding. The tradeoff in designing contract duration is that of reducing the likelihood of maladaptation while still providing incentives for vendors to undertake noncontractible specific investments and reducing ex post inefficiencies. We next describe how embeddedness in the interorganizational network resolves this trade-off to design the appropriate duration of the contract.

## Embeddedness, Information Transmission, and Contracts

An extensive literature examines how embeddedness of economic relationships impacts the terms of trade (Uzzi 1996, 1999; DiMaggio and Louch 1998). In this study, we examine two ways by which information transmission through embedded ties helps to mitigate opportunism concerns and the likelihood of maladaptation in long-term outsourcing contracts. First, the network serves as a conduit of information about a particular actor. Mayer and Argyres (2004) posit that firms learn about potential contingencies and hazards incrementally as they accumulate experience, which helps improve communication, coordination, and governance. Because it is difficult to envision outcomes and to set up detailed benchmarks at the outset (e.g., Susarla et al. 2010), the accumulated experience through embedded ties facilitates learning about market conditions and terms of exchange (Raub and Weesie 1990, Robinson and Stuart 2006). Embedded ties provide information on various aspects of the exchange, including the reliability and trustworthiness of potential exchange partners, performance benchmarks, and normative incentives and penalties. The fine-grained information transfer through embedded ties helps share information acquired through learning by doing (Uzzi 1996). Such information helps participant firms reduce uncertainty in long-term exchanges by better anticipating and responding to contingencies.

Second, information transmitted through embedded ties establishes the reliability of firms. It has been posited that the network internalizes the information on the competencies and reliability of prospective partners (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999). Firms are aware that exchange partners would use information on behavior observed through embedded economic relationships to ascribe potential future behavior (Raub and Weesie 1990). Firms that have earned a good reputation through a cumulative history of good behavior are incentivized to maintain such behavior in continuing market relations and in abstaining from economic malfeasance. Thus, embedded ties act as a guarantor of good behavior in the future. Next, we develop specific hypotheses on the impacts of the four dimensions of embeddedness, i.e., structural, relational, positional, and contractual, on contract duration.

Structural Embeddedness. Structural embeddedness refers to the structure of relations of the actors (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998, Grewal et al. 2006). Structural embeddedness provides access to information about learning from doing and experience from other market arrangements that a firm has entered into (Uzzi 1996, Gulati 1998). Second, structural embeddedness could increase the perception that an agent is reliable and less likely to engage in opportunistic behavior.

Structural Embeddedness of Clients0 Clients with greater structural embeddedness have access to finegrained information transfer that facilitates learning to contract. A firm with an expansive network position has access to a diverse pool of market information (Burt 1987) that provides access to information about potential trading opportunities and exchange partners. Uzzi (1999) characterizes private information as information voluntarily transferred in an exchange. For example, private information can include details of contingencies identified in prior contracts, processes to enhance communication between parties, and benchmarking terms. By virtue of their structural embeddedness, clients can acquire information about managing the contract, setting up performance milestones, and benchmark against available information about similar contractual engagements carried out by their exchange partners. Prior research has characterized this informational role as search embeddedness that occurs when buyers use their “social relationships to identify and assess the reliability of potential transaction partners to whom they have no direct or close indirect social ties” (DiMaggio and Louch 1998, p. 620). Because client firms obtain information about the reliability and ability of potential exchange partners through the network, a client with more information about reliability and the ability of potential exchange partners is less likely to fear ex post shirking and maladaptation. Therefore we expect that with structural embeddedness, client firms are more willing to enter into longer term contracts. Thus:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> A contract’s duration is positively associated with the structural embeddedness of the client firm.

Structural Embeddedness of Vendors. Vendors obtain two benefits from structural embeddedness. First, a vendor who is likely to engage in malfeasance and opportunism is more likely to be easily detected when she tries to enter into multiple outsourcing initiatives. Thus, it follows that vendors with greater structural embeddedness have earned a better reputation for upholding the terms of the contract (e.g., Granovetter 1985). Vendors could benefit from a perception of lower malfeasance through the structure of embedded ties. Jones et al. (1997) posit that structural embeddedness safeguards exchanges by decreasing the amount of monitoring required and enhancing the commitment of parties. Second, a vendor that has transacted with a breadth of client firms has acquired a rich set of experiences in learning about how to contract. Such learning through embedded ties enables her to better delineate responsibilities and plan for contingencies (e.g., Mayer and Argyres 2004). This information can mitigate hold up concerns and provide clients with sufficient reassurance to engage the vendor in a longer term arrangement. Thus, we expect that:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> A contract’s duration is positively associated with the structural embeddedness of the vendor firm.

Relational Embeddedness. Relational embeddedness refers to the quality of the dyadic exchange. As posited by Granovetter (1985), market agents trust information from one’s past dealings and place a premium on such information. A history of interaction provides a vendor with a deeper appreciation of a client’s business and information needs. Gulati (1998) posits that relational embeddedness provides finegrained information about exchange conditions. A history of contracting relations implies that parties have expended time and energy in transacting with each other and that each have greater opportunities to learn from the cumulative experiences. At the level of the dyad, relational embeddedness implies a pattern of entering into and maintaining a history of successful outsourcing engagements. Relational embeddedness promotes information transfer, conflict resolution, and flexibility in ex post governance (Goo et al. 2009, Gopal and Koka 2012, Rai et al. 2012). Rai et al. (2009) suggest that in the fine-grained information transfer, trust and joint problem solving arrangements fostered through relational embeddedness enhance the vendor’s ability to comprehend the process of formalizing requirements and communicating with client firms; these are essential in long-term contracting arrangements. Thus, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.</sup> A contract’s duration is positively affected by relational embeddedness in the client and vendor dyad.

Contractual Embeddedness. It has been posited that fine-grained information transferred through embedded ties (Uzzi 1996, p. 679) “equip the new economic exchange with resources from previous embedded ties.” Firms that undertake an outsourcing arrangement after having entered into one in the past are likely to economize on contracting costs by designing future contracts based on contracting experience from prior contracts (Mayer and Argyres 2004). The interaction between clients’ legal departments and vendor personnel also creates a repository of contract design capabilities from prior contracting engagements (Argyres and Mayer 2007). Information and learning from prior contracts influence the current exchange arrangement; such experience spills over to one’s trading partners, resulting in an organizing vision (e.g., Swanson and Ramiller 1997) in the neighborhood of the contract. Such transmission of contractual expertise results in a diffusion of practices within the local network neighborhood (Krackhardt 1992) whereby the duration of a contract is influenced by the duration of contracts in the neighborhood.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3.</sup> A contract’s duration is positively affected by the duration of contracts in the interorganizational network neighborhood.

Positional Embeddedness. Positional embeddedness captures the impact of the positions that organizations occupy in the overall structure of the network (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999). A market agent’s position in the overall network provides assurance of performance to the set of agents with whom he works and acts as a guarantor of performance to others in the market. Thus the network implicitly assigns and maintains the status of market participants (Phillips and Zuckerman 2001) by differentiating them by the history of prior performance.<sup>5</sup> We examine the impact of positional embeddedness of clients and vendors.

Positional Embeddedness of Clients0 With stronger positional embeddedness clients are better able to demand advantageous contract terms due to their position of power, access to private information, and control of the information flow in the network. For a vendor, dealing with a client who has experience in dealing with more central vendors offers the advantage of having an exchange partner who is well versed with managing the uncertainty and contingency planning required in a long-term IT outsourcing arrangement. Thus, clients could benefit from enhanced access to information through their network position as well as the perceived advantages of experience acquired through embedded ties. Clients with greater positional embeddedness are then less likely to be concerned about the likelihood of malfeasance and ex post opportunism. Thus, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4A</sup> <sup>(H4A).</sup> A contract’s duration is positively affected by the positional embeddedness of the client firm.

Positional Embeddedness of Vendors0 Vendor investments in maintaining embedded outsourcing relationships ensure that each party engages in cooperative behavior and refrains from opportunism. Such positional embeddedness is valuable not only as a signal of past performance but also provides a strong indication of future behavior of market agents. Klein and Leffler (1981) posit that an agent that breaches contractual obligations faces a loss of reputation. Vendors in advantageous network positions risk losing the accrued benefits of embeddedness in breaching contracting obligations. Thus vendors with embedded network positions are more likely to be awarded longterm contracts, because they are perceived to be less likely to engage in post-contract opportunism.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4B</sup> <sup>(H4B).</sup> A contract’s duration is positively affected by the positional embeddedness of the vendor firm.

## 3. Data and Measures

Our data set is a collection of public announcements of outsourcing arrangements in which a client awards a vendor a contract to deliver one or more services in a multiyear relationship. A typical public announcement takes the following form: “Firm A awarded Firm B a contract worth \$X for providing service S for a duration $T . \mathbf { \mu } ^ { \prime \prime }$ The data set contains 22,039 unique contracts. Of these, 15,379 are nongovernment contracts for IT services outsourcing, between 7,039 unique client firms and 1,908 unique vendor firms from 1989–2008. The data set comes from the International Data Corporation (IDC), a leading market intelligence and advisory services firm that tracks such announcements to provide analysis and reports to their customers. This data set was then matched with financial data from Compustat, a commercial database of financial information on publicly-listed companies to extract revenue of U.S.-based public firms to serve as a control for the firm size. Summary statistics are provided in Table 2 and Figure 1 shows the change in contract duration and the number of contracts over the years. Client firms in our sample have between 1 and 170 contracts whereas vendor firms have between 1 and 260 contracts. Among the set of clients, the median number of contracts is 56 whereas for the set of vendors the median number of contracts signed during the same period is 150.

## Network Construction and Measures of Embeddedness

We construct two types of networks based on who contracts with whom. The first is a series of networks indexed by the year in which the contract was signed. The network for each year contains nodes representing customers and vendors that have signed an outsourcing contract either that year or in the past and edges representing the contracts that tie vendors and clients to each other. A network of this type is referred to as a bipartite graph and it contains ties between nodes of one set, vendors, and nodes of the other set, clients. Note that in such bipartite graphs no tie exists between two nodes of the same set, i.e., no tie exists between two vendors or two clients. Each tie is bidirectional with unit tie-strength, implying that the network does not distinguish between the initiator and the recipient of the contract and observing that the existence of a tie does not reveal the strength of the relationship. This network is represented as a matrix $\mathbf { P } _ { y }$ with the dimensions M × N where M is the number of unique clients and N is the number of unique vendors. The cells in $\mathbf { P } _ { y }$ contain values 1 if there exists an outsourcing arrangement between customer m and vendor n in the year y or before.

## Control Variables

Because contract duration decisions are likely influenced by various other factors at the level of the contract, firm, and industry, we introduce an elaborate set of control variables.

Service Type. Contracts have been shown to be longer when the service being contracted out requires relationship specific investments (Joskow 1987, Masten and Saussier 2000). Different IT service types have different needs for specific investments (Mani et al. 2010, Susarla et al. 2010). We control for IT Outsourcing, Business Process Outsourcing, Application Development and Maintenance, System Integration, Support Services, Consulting Services, and Others. Alternate specification of service has been tested for robustness and reported in §5.5.1 in the online appendix (available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.2015.0572).

Contract Size. Contract duration can likely be affected by scope. Therefore we introduce a log annual contract value into the regression models.

Firm Size. Larger firms could dictate contract terms to smaller firms, thus influencing duration. We use Compustat to obtain measures of firm size for public firms in the United States. We have client size figures for 1,876 contracts, vendor size figures for 4,666 contracts, and both client and vendor data for 809 contracts among the 8,690 U.S.-based contracts.

Industry Sector. To control for industry-specific contracting norms we include the one-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code of the client’s industry segment.

Outsourcing Trends. The year 2000 marked a significant upturn in the number of outsourcing contracts as a result of a number of Y2K projects. Over time, contract durations have shrunk and the median has stabilized at three years after 1999. To account for this we add an indicator for contracts before the year 2000.

Regional Differences. International variation in contract enforceability could potentially affect contract design. To account for this influence we include a dummy variable that takes a value of 1 if the contract was signed in the western hemisphere and 0 otherwise. However this estimation is likely to be biased due to a systematic violation of the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumptions required for the OLS estimation. There are two sources for this bias. The first arises from unobserved attributes of shared nodes. In a social network by definition edges are dependent. Each observation in our data is a contract that ties a client and a vendor. Two distinct contracts are associated with each other through a series of shared clients or vendors or both. As a result, the unobserved parameters of any one contract are likely to be systematically correlated with the unobserved parameters of other contracts with which it shares clients or vendor firms. The second source of dependence comes from correlated actions of the common nodes. The focal contract parameters are likely to be affected by the parameters of the contracts to which they are tied. As a result, contract design elements are both influenced by and in turn influence connected contracts. There is a reflection problem in that it could be difficult to empirically disentangle the effect of the reference set of contracts from the unobserved parameters or correlated actions (e.g., Manski 1993).

We adopt two novel approaches to address these two distinct sources of econometric challenges. The Multiway Cluster Robust Estimation (MWCRE) formulated by Cameron et al. (2010), places no a priori assumptions on the error structure that would be needed for typical solutions for heteroskedasticity correction. Instead the method explicitly assumes dependence across observations due to nonnested clusters of observations. Second, we explicitly account for the possibility that the dependent variable is directly correlated with the dependent variable of other observations due to unobserved dependence in a Network Autoregression (AR) estimation model.

## Multiway Cluster Robust Estimation

Observations in our data violate the i.i.d. assumption as each observation is likely to be correlated with other observations with which they share clients and vendors. This creates a problematic error structure in that observations could be clustered around clients and vendors. Furthermore, these clusters are nonnested, i.e., the clustering by the client dimension does not completely envelope clusters by vendor firms or vice versa. Nested clusters are routinely handled by aggregating and analyzing at the level of the highest cluster. However, such a treatment does not address the problems associated with nonnested clusters. Also, this issue cannot be addressed merely by using a standard robust error correction. Failing to control clustered errors leads to underestimating the standard errors and falsely rejecting the null hypotheses (Cameron et al. 2010). Fixed effects and random effects specifications using OLS are also inappropriate to address interdependence across dyadic observations (Gulati and Gargiulo 1999). Cameron et al. (2010) offer a robust technique to address nonnested clustered errors without imposing additional distributional constraints on the error structure. A brief explanation of the method is presented below.<sup>7</sup> Consider the model without clustered observation

$$
y _ {i} = x _ {i} \beta + u _ {i} \quad \text { for   contract } i \in \{1, 2, \dots , N \}.\tag{9}
$$

The i.i.d. assumption implies that $\mathrm { C o v } \{ u _ { i } , u _ { j } \} = 0$ In the case of nonnested clustered data, dyadic observation i is a tie between a client g and a vendor h. The model is therefore

$$
y _ {i g h} = x _ {i g h} \beta + u _ {i g h}.\tag{10}
$$

Now it can be seen that the Cov $\{ u _ { i g h } , u _ { j g h } \} \ne 0$ is due to the fact that error terms $u _ { i g h }$ and $u _ { j g h }$ contain residual information pertaining to common clients and vendors that belong to nonnested clusters of clients and vendors defined by $g \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , G \}$ and $h \in$ $\{ 1 , 2 , \dots , H \} _ { }$ , respectively. Furthermore, this covariance matrix is neither diagonal (as it would have been in nonclustered clusters) nor block-diagonal (as in the one-dimensional clustered case) thus limiting the applicability of sandwich estimators. The central feature of the MWCRE technique is to model the variance of the estimate as $\hat { V } [ \hat { \beta } ] = \hat { ( } X ^ { \prime } X ) ^ { - 1 } ( \hat { B } ) ( X ^ { \prime } X ) ^ { - 1 }$ , where $\hat { B } = X ^ { \prime } ( \hat { u } \hat { u } ^ { \prime } \cdot S ^ { G H } ) .$ X. The term $S ^ { G H }$ is an indicator matrix in which the $i j$ cell takes a value of 1 if contract belongs to the ith and jth cluster. The variance is then disaggregated in three separate components to account for belonging to the cluster of clients, vendors, and both as in Equation (11). Not accounting for the complete error structure would have deflated the standard errors and rejected the null falsely

$$
S ^ {G H} = S ^ {G} + S ^ {H} - S ^ {G \cap H}.\tag{11}
$$

## Network Autoregression

Connected observations are likely to exhibit direct dependence among the observations. In our case, the contract duration can be directly correlated to the duration of a previous contract as a result of clients or vendors economizing on contract design. This direct influence of a neighborhood of observations on a focal observation can be estimated using a Network AR estimation technique (Doreian 1989, 1990; Butts 2008). The Network AR is a standard regression model with additional components to include the auto-regressive effects, which allows us to estimate the outcome variable as both a function of individual level regressors and the resonance from its network neighbors (outcome) decisions. Equation (12) shows the estimated model

$$
Y _ {i} = \theta W _ {i, j} Y _ {j} + X _ {i} \beta + u _ {i}.\tag{12}
$$

The duration of the focal contract, $Y _ { i }$ is regressed on the independent variables X and on the autoregressive component, $\theta W _ { i , j } Y _ { j } .$ . The X matrix contains the variables of interest and the control variables used to rule out alternate explanations. The model is estimated using the software package Statnet (Handcock et al. 2003) on R. The Network AR estimation is an iterative maximum likelihood estimation technique that places a huge computational load. Our attempt to run a single model with over 17,000 relevant observations from all time periods, including all relevant control variables, proved beyond the capability of the software and the computers despite running it off a multiprocessor cluster server at Amazon Web Services. We therefore adopted a very similar, yet noniterative, maximum likelihood estimation approach discussed by Doreian et al. (1984). Because the results for both techniques are practically indistinguishable unless the correlation is unrealistically high, we report the noniterative maximum likelihood estimates of the model including control variables.

Thus we find support for Hypothesis 1B (structural embeddedness is important for vendors) but not for clients (lack of support for Hypothesis 1A). The average contract value per month equals \$1,069,633 (USD). For vendors the positive and significant coefficient of 0.01 (column 3) translates to an additional contract value of approximately \$11,000 (USD) in the total contract value on average for each additional contract signed in the past, holding all else constant. For the client, on average, each additional contract signed in the past translates to a decrease in contract duration of about five months, holding all else constant.

The mere existence of a prior relationship between the client and vendor is not significantly associated with a longer contract duration. However, the greater the cumulative duration of past contracts between the firms in a dyad, the more likely that this dyad would sign a longer contract in the future as predicted by Hypothesis 2. On average, the significant value of 0.01 (column 3) translates to an average increase of \$11,000 for each additional year of prior contract duration, and an increase of two months in the duration of the contract, holding all else constant. We see a nuanced support for Hypothesis 2 in that the strength of the prior relation predicts longer contracts, not merely the existence of a prior relationship.

In Table 4, column 5 we report the results from the noniterative Network AR model which shows that, in addition to the explanatory power of clients’ and vendors’ structural, positional, and relational embeddedness, and controlling for various alternate causal factors, there is still a small yet highly significant impact on the neighborhood of a focal contract. From the positive sign on the correlation coefficient we infer that the duration of the focal contract is positively influenced by the duration of its neighboring contracts. On average the significant value of 0.001 (column 4) translates to an increase in contract duration of about 0.4 months, all else constant. We therefore find strong support for Hypothesis 3 that the focal contract parameters are influenced by the contract parameters of its immediate neighborhood.

Positional embeddedness is captured in the network measure of eigenvector centrality. In column 3 we see that eigenvector centrality measures of both client and vendor are positive and strongly significant in its association with longer contracts. This is a measure that accounts for the centrality of the nodes to which the focal node is connected: We see strong support for both Hypotheses 4A and B suggesting that in addition to the focal firm being associated with many trading partners, the centrality of these other trading partners also influences the winning of longer contracts. Interpreting the coefficients in absolute terms is nonintuitive but the coefficients of vendor versus clients can be compared. We see that the coefficient of clients’ eigenvector centrality is almost three times as much as that of vendors (column 3).

## Robustness Checks

To rule out alternate causal explanations we test the robustness of our results using various alternate model specifications as reported below.

Alternate Coding of Service Types. Large outsourcing contracts typically contain bundles of services. In the reported model we coded a service type variable corresponding to the major service in the contract. However, to ensure robustness we test an alternate specification with 51 dummy variables for the various subservice included in the contract. While this does increase the r-square of the model marginally, there is no significant difference in the sign and significance of the variables of interest. Results of the extended specification are provided in Online Appendix Table A-1.

Bias Due to Firm Size. In the distribution of firms’ social capital we see that a few firms have very high levels of social capital. The effect of central nodes gaining centrality at a higher rate due to a preferential attachment for central nodes has been seen in various networks of social interaction (Newman 2001). To ensure robustness against these few firms skewing our results, we conduct two tests. First, we re-estimate the model excluding the top 25-percentile of vendors and clients in terms of the number of contracts signed. Second, we estimate a model with a log-transformed value for degree centrality. In both cases, there is little substantive change except that when the most central clients are removed, the role of client degree centrality becomes more significant suggesting the greater importance of centrality among the less central firms. Results of this estimation are presented in Table A-2 in the online appendix.

Simultaneity of Contract Parameters. It is likely that contract terms including duration and pricing are simultaneously determined during the contract design. To address this simultaneity, we re-estimate our model using a recursive simultaneous biprobit estimation (Greene 2003). Contract duration is first converted to a binary variable taking the value 1 when the contract duration exceeds 36 months. We include the variables for structural, positional, and relational embeddedness, as well as control variables for contract value, contracting region, and signing year before 2000. $$

$$

where High Duration = 1 if contract duration > 36 months and 0 otherwise

$$

This model explicitly accounts for the possibility of unobserved simultaneity in the determination of pricing and duration. Fixed price is simultaneously estimated using the variables of interest. In addition control variables of industry sectors, service type, and contract value are included. The exclusion restriction required for identification is ensured by the variables in the set $X _ { 3 } .$ . Results from this estimation are presented in Table A-3 in the online appendix. We find that even accounting for the fixed price to be simultaneously affected by the variables of interest, support for the hypotheses does not change substantially. However we see an interesting result that while degree centrality appears to drive the choice of fixed price contracts, accounting for this effect we see that positional embeddedness (eigenvector centrality) drives longer duration.

Premature Contract Terminations. Our data does not identify all of the contracts that were prematurely terminated. Whereas a new contract announcement boosts stock prices, contract terminations tend to be have a negative impact and are typically underreported. As a result, contract terminations are not as easily observable as contract signings. In our data set we have 118 contracts that were reported cancelled ahead of schedule. Therefore it is possible that some of our observations have an actual duration much less than the initial duration agreed on while signing the contract. However, if we could estimate the effect of centrality on only successful contracts we would have seen higher estimates. By not eliminating terminated contracts our estimates are likely to be biased lower, making our estimates more conservative.

To validate the above assumption, we perform a split sample OLS estimation of the contracts whose status is recorded as “Canceled” and compare it to those whose status is recorded as “Current or Unknown.” We then perform a t-test to compare the coefficients across the two equations. The only variables that are statistically different in the two subsets are AnyPrior contracts and eigenvector centralities (marked by <sup>†</sup> in Table A-4 in the online appendix), both of which can be attributed to a very small sample set. In the subset of cancelled contracts, all three variables are smaller in dimension than in the set of current or unknown contracts. This reinforces the robustness of our results that by pooling the entire set of contracts any unobserved contract termination is likely to place a downward, rather than upward, bias in our estimates thereby making our estimates more conservative.

Firm Size and Bargaining Power. Prior literature has shown that contract design elements can be associated with differences in bargaining power. The reported results use revenues as a measure of size. However we also considered the number of employees as an alternate measure of firm size. The estimated coefficient on the number of employees was not significant and hence has not been reported.

Clustering of Observations by Year. To rule out any year effects, we exploit the capability of the multiway cluster robust estimation to estimate a model with errors clustered around years in addition to vendors and clients. Because the levels of statistical significance of the variables of interest do not change we report only the model with the client and vendor, keeping only a control variable for pre-year 2000 contracts.

## 6. Discussion

The outsourcing of large and complex IT services through long-term contracts poses an empirical puzzle to theories of the firm. Early literature suggested vertical integration as the recommended solution to contractual challenges. However vertical disintegration in the form of outsourcing is increasingly the norm in many industries. Not only do firms outsource more, but they also tend to enter into shorter term arrangements with more firms than in the past. This simultaneous move to more contracts of shorter duration challenges predictions from the theories of the firm. Predictions from TCE would indicate that clients might consider a continuing arrangement with a vendor firm to limit renegotiation costs and incentivize relationship specific investments. Theories of relational governance would predict longer term relations with fewer firms rather than expanding the portfolio of trading partners. With costly renegotiation and cost of business disruption involved in terminating contracts, the choice of contract duration is of strategic importance in aligning incentives for clients and vendors and in realizing sustainable value from outsourcing. Our focus on the embeddedness in the interfirm network is an attempt to contribute to both theory and practice. Figure 4 presents the marginal effects of embeddedness measures on contract duration.

We have four main results. First, we find that the rich historical stock of contracts helps vendors win longer contracts in the future. For the vendors the historical stock of contracts likely translates into better experience in managing the minutiae of the outsourcing arrangement, suggesting an informational role of structural embeddedness in communicating the ability and reliability of a vendor to successfully win contracts. Contract duration is highly correlated with the total contract value; therefore, from a vendor’s point of view, there is clear evidence that more contracts beget longer contracts leading to more revenue. However clients who have a history of contracting with multiple vendors opt for shorter contracts. It is possible that for a client the type of learning that occurs through structural embeddedness could be general contract management, rather than partner specific learning important for a long-term arrangement. Future work can disentangle different types of learning that occur through positional and structural embeddedness. Second, we find that prior relationship matters and can lead to longer contracts in the future. However, it is the strength of the relationship and not just the presence of a prior relationship that predicts long-term contracts. Clients and vendors pledged into the long-term commitment can now be more assured of a longer period during which they may recoup their relationship specific investments. Third, we find that contract duration is influenced by the duration of neighboring contracts. By neighborhood, we imply other contracts signed by the client or the vendor. Considering that firms incur a cost in designing contracts, correlation between a focal contract’s duration with the duration of other contracts signed by the client and vendor in the past seems to suggest that firms economize on this activity of contract design. Fourth, we find that positional embeddedness of clients and vendors strongly impacts contract duration. Positional embeddedness captures the centrality of a node connected to central nodes, whose centrality in turn comes from being tied to other central nodes. This measure is sometimes referred to as a measure of prestige. Our fourth result suggests that the prestige of trading partners is another predictor of favorable terms in contract design. Furthermore, being tied to prestigious clients is almost three times as powerful in predicting contract duration as being tied to prestigious vendors. Positional embeddedness also conveys centrality or prestige to nodes with which no direct ties exist due to the fact that the centrality of each node is not only a function of its connected ties but also of remote ties separated by a few other nodes.

## Implications for Literature

A considerable amount of prior literature highlights the difficulty of establishing appropriate performance benchmarks and contractual contingencies in complex IT outsourcing arrangements (e.g., Bapna et al.

2010, Mani et al. 2010, Susarla 2012). Prior literature has demonstrated that reputation mechanisms are not very effective in the IT services sector (Banerjee and Duflo 2000). Furthermore, a firm’s reputation for good behavior is not observable outside the trading relationship (Brown et al. 2004). Given this difficulty in observability and verifiability in a number of market transactions, buyer-seller networks provide an important mechanism for transmitting information about exchange performance. DiMaggio and Louch (p. 634, 1998) posit “transacting with social contacts is effective because it embeds commercial exchanges in multiplex relations that extend over space and time, in effect holding the network hostage to appropriate role performance in the economic exchange.” We examine whether embeddedness of outsourcing relationships acts as a nonprice mechanism that offers assurance of contractual performance.

In line with Podolny (2001), we find that the network serves as both a prism and a pipe in transmitting information to potential exchange partners as well as conveying information to third parties outside the dyad in the outsourcing industry. Interestingly for clients, structural embeddedness does not have a significant impact on contract duration, suggesting that the quality of the dyadic exchange (relational embeddedness), the impact of localized market norms (contractual embeddedness), and their status and prestige in the overall network (positional embeddedness) provide greater learning and a greater ability to enforce contractual obligations promised by the vendor.

For clients, the contrasting roles of structural and positional embeddedness suggest that accumulating embedded exchange positions through the quality of the tie or through prestige of trading partners is more important than access to multiple vendor firms. For vendors, both structural and positional embeddedness serve as indicators of their ability and reliability to uphold exchange terms, offering assurance of contractual performance, and mitigating concerns of opportunism. Embeddedness of vendors could serve as a self-enforcing safeguard for implementing outcomes that may not be completely enforceable by formal contracts. The buyer supplier network thus acts as a market force in penalizing deviations and enforcing contractual obligations that are difficult to draft and enforce through formal contractual mechanisms alone.

The significance of contractual embeddedness shows the role of history and path dependence in the structuring of outsourcing arrangements. Our results suggest that individual clients and vendors do not undertake contract decisions in isolation but are influenced by the localized norms in the overall market. The importance of relational and positional embeddedness for clients has implications for the depth and breadth of sourcing relationships. For instance, a client could compare whether it is more advantageous to have deeper relationships with one or two vendors or to explore expansive relationships with high status vendors that provide access to a diverse pool of available information.

For vendors, the importance of structural, relational, and positional embeddedness could have different implications for the market niche and for the competitive position they wish to occupy. For instance, the pattern of vendor exchange relationships could support the interpretation that the vendor is focused on one industry segment (having a depth of ties to clients in an industry segment from relational embeddedness), rather than one who serves a broader range of services across different client markets (greater structural embeddedness with clients in different industries). A history of interaction with several experienced clients provides a vendor with a depth of understanding of a client’s business and information needs and the development of norms such as joint identification and trust (e.g., Levina and Vaast 2008). The market may then be likely to reward a vendor with deep knowledge of the clients’ business environment. By contrast, positional embeddedness implies that a vendor can conform to the standards of a client who has a greater breadth of experience with prestigious vendors. Such a vendor also has greater opportunities to learn from the cumulative outsourcing experience of their clients. Therefore, the rewards accrued from positional embeddedness as a result of deliberate actions on the part of vendors in entering into and maintaining a history of successful outsourcing engagements with multiple experienced clients could be different from the rewards accrued from relational embeddedness from a depth of engagements with a single client. The market may not necessarily reward an agent for entering into indiscriminate contracting engagements that do not conform to the market’s perceptions. Vendors may then seek to actively differentiate themselves by entering into a pattern of exchange relationships in an attempt to be assigned the right labels or niches by other market participants, which impacts the aggregate structure of competition in the IT services market (e.g., Podolny et al. 1996).

Our results could also have some implications for buyer supplier networks outside of IT outsourcing. Whereas IT outsourcing is characterized by uncertainties and difficulty in specifying performance expectations ex ante, outsourcing of professional services could exhibit similar challenges and risks. Examining contracts from a network perspective offers three key insights to the behavior of firms as economic actors. First, the network serves an informational role that influences perception about the ability of market participants to potential trading partners, transmitting difficult-to-obtain information about exchange opportunities as well as the actual services traded. Thus the interorganizational network functions as a conduit of reputation. Second, the network plays a role in ensuring that contractual obligations are self-enforcing, providing a mechanism for community enforcement, where information flowing through a community prevents the potential for opportunism. Third, at the aggregate level, diffusion of localized norms impact contracting practices.

With the growth of outsourcing of other complex activities in a multitude of industries, vertical integration is not always a solution in rapidly changing innovative environments. At the same time, a reliance on formal contractual arrangements enumerating all contingencies are just as likely to be limiting given the environmental volatility in other innovative industries. An embeddedness-based perspective contributes to the literature by highlighting noncontractual mechanisms that inhibit malfeasance and motivate collaborative arrangements.

## Implications for Practitioners

Academic evidence on commonly used reputation metrics to rate vendors such as capability maturity models and ISO certification is also mixed (Banerjee and Duflo 2000, Gopal and Gao 2009). Evaluating trading partners through a network perspective may provide an alternate metric for vendor evaluation. For vendors, an implication from this study is the role of network position in determining growth strategies, revenue generation efforts, and winning larger contracts. Because the prestige of a vendor’s trading partners is rewarded over and above the quantity of trading partners, it is likely that vendors may choose to be more selective in their client specific investments. Vendors may also consider a capability-building portfolio consistent with their network position. This is likely to be reflected in vendors specialized in an industry or a service. For client firms, our study offers normative implications in determining a long-term sourcing strategy. Clients seeking to evaluate trading partners may apply our study in quantifying the impact of prior outsourcing transactions because there is limited academic or practitioner work that offers a viable measure of reputation that can help outsourcing firms, vendors, investors, and analysts. Practitioner rankings of vendors usually only examine very established vendors. Furthermore, such systems do not address the reputation of the vendors’ past clients.

Over time, changes in IT outsourcing has led to industry-wide changes. Large multibillion dollar total outsourcing contracts have given way to shorter selective outsourcing deals. However, looking at individual firms we see that on average a particular client or vendor is likely to sign a slightly longer contract than the one it signed previously. This gradual increase in contract duration likely reflects a process of clients learning to contract. It is also likely that vendors mature through responding to similar RFPs over time. Thus participant firms’ learning (through embedded ties) can shift experienced firms to sign longer contracts thus reducing their renegotiation costs even while the industry on average may move towards shorter contracts. Future work can examine whether the impact of embeddedness on contract terms is conditioned by the overall industry environment.

For industry watchers, one of the implications of this work is that network structure could provide a means for competitive differentiation or segmentation. Service provider firms may seek to actively differentiate themselves by entering into a pattern of exchange relationships in an attempt to be assigned the right labels or niches by other market participants. A network of trading partners also provides access to social and technical resources of exchange partners. As a result, we might observe the emergence of clusters of interorganizational networks around different segments. This selective allocation of attention and resources may lead to participant firms’ improving their track record of contracting success.

## Limitations

Because signing a contract does not necessarily imply successful completion of a contract, measuring the stock of contracts signed does not necessarily imply a healthy reputation. Just as information on which contracts are awarded to whom propagates to other agents, it is equally likely that news of failure would resonate through the market. Any information transmitted is likely to include both positive and negative indicators of reputation. However, it is such dissemination of reputational information through the network that restricts opportunistic behavior. By not counting negative reputational indicators, our results are biased more conservatively.

Another limitation is that we do not observe all of the channels of social interaction between the firms. For instance, a vendor specializing in the aviation industry and a client from the same industry might share a connection through a trade organization. Our confidence in the results comes from the fact that the 22,000 observations for the 20 years since the start of the IT outsourcing industry represents almost the entire population of publicly announced outsourcing arrangements in history. Therefore it is unlikely that there would exist a tie between two firms in the trade organization without at least an indirect tie in the outsourcing network.

## 7. Conclusions and Future Research

Firms can distinguish themselves from their competitors through difficult-to-imitate actions such as a history of successful market engagements. This is especially relevant in the context of IT where there is significant uncertainty as well as the measurement difficulty that challenges contract design and execution. We take a first step in examining whether embeddedness of outsourcing relationships enhances long-term interfirm collaborations that involve considerable ex post transaction uncertainty. We find strong evidence to support our hypotheses and research framework. The positional embeddedness of participant firms and relational embeddedness across dyads have a significant positive impact on contract duration. In addition, we find that contract duration is positively influenced by the duration of contracts signed in the past by the clients and vendors involved in that particular contract.

Our study offers a number of future research directions. Given the challenges in obtaining a reputation score, academics and practitioners can use some of the methods developed in this paper to identify market segmentation strategies and to develop scorecards that permit industry-wide comparisons. Given the rich literature on the nature of embeddedness and social capital, future work can build richer frameworks to explain contract design in the contemporary practice of outsourcing and offshoring of manufacturing and business services. The nature of the data and the research question lend itself to analyzing systematic tie formation, growth dynamics of the industry, and the formation of niches of specialization. An extension of this work could be the examination of institutional effects in outsourcing. It would be interesting to study the impact of institutional mechanisms on embedded ties whereby the impact of embeddedness may be less pronounced with time or embedded ties may not develop in some sectors of the market.
