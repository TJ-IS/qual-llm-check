## A B S T R A C T

Previous models of turnover by IT professionals consider job satisfaction as a key indicator. One common model considers whether an organization matches the internal anchors of IT employees to provisions in the work place. This pattern is often broken by other considerations that disturb the relationship between job satisfaction and intent to seek employment elsewhere. Such disturbances present a problem in planning and are not globally considered in research models. A qualitative study of ten cases yields new insight into the disturbances that break the pattern leading to a more general model of turnover.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) personnel continue to exhibit high rates of turnover [1]. IT departments have difficulties retaining individuals with current skills [16]. IT projects suffer due to losing key technology personnel. Loss of key IT personnel is particularly problematic for organizations that strive to utilize technology to achieve competitive parity or advantage through advanced systems [46]. For these and other reasons, keeping IT personnel is crucial, yet practices deployed for personnel in other disciplines may not be appropriate [14]. IT jobs exhibit characteristics of spanning functional boundaries, demanding constant learning to remain current with technological advance, and possess pressures of time and budget constrained projects that can differ from more traditional jobs within an organization [18]. IT personnel also differ from other employees in their backgrounds, expectations and behaviors. These all combine to make further understanding of turnover among IT personnel crucial.

Past studies consider the characteristics of IT jobs and the turnover responses by IT personnel [25]. They consider job traits and conditions such as autonomy, emotional environments, rewards, and demands that lead to feelings of burnout, less job satisfaction, and turnover intentions. The basis of any advice is to modify the working environment to lower burnout, increase satisfaction, and reduce intention to leave the job. These models, however, fail to consider more individual needs of the IT professional. To compensate, other researchers consider turnover intent to be an outcome of an organization’s failure to satisfy the more personal needs of the individual in the provision of the job characteristics or workplace environment [4,32]. This perspective relies on the concept of internal career anchors [41]. Internal career anchors represent the wants and desires of individuals that must be fulfilled by a job.

Anchors for IT personnel include autonomy, creativity, managerial competence, technical competence, organizational stability, geographic security, identity, service, variety, lifestyle, challenge, entrepreneurship, and learning motivation (see Appendix for definitions) [3–6,20]. Individuals look to organizations to satisfy these anchors with ‘‘external opportunities’’ in the workplace, where external is relative to the individual and opportunity relative to job characteristics potentially satisfying the anchors. An organization must provide these external opportunities as incentives consistent with underlying career values, because according to discrepancy theories, a failure to satisfy the more important anchors results in lessened job satisfaction and an intention to seek other employment [22]. This pattern is also maintained when employees stay with jobs that satisfy their anchors because there is no perceived discrepancy to the external opportunities present in the position held. Though career anchors tend to provide a good explanatory model, disturbances to the pattern are not uncommon [32]. Satisfied IT professionals still leave their organizations even when there is no significant gap between their wants and external opportunities while some unsatisfied IT professionals remain with their current organization [4]. A common explanation for staying with a job while unsatisfied is the perception that job opportunities outside the current organization do not exist [26]. This condition is a disturbance to the pattern of turnover models shown in quadrant III of Fig. 1, but well documented and understood. However, the external job market is only one possible disturbance to the typical pattern of turnover intention. Consideration of leaving a job that satisfies the needs of an individual is not made by researchers, leaving a major portion of the picture unidentified as represented in quadrant I of Fig. 1. As with the disturbance of poor external job opportunity explaining the decision to stay with a job when discrepancies exist between provisions and anchors, we consider the possibility of disturbances also causing IT personnel to switch jobs when their anchors are being satisfied by the organization and extend the model of current thought to consider a broader perspective of possible disturbances.

The focus of this study is, therefore, to examine how IT personnel turnover decisions are disturbed by factors external to the relationship between the employee and the organization. Just what considerations lead an IT professional to switch jobs when they are satisfied with the job; i.e. when their internal career desires are satisfied by their organizations? Based upon a multiplecase study, results suggest that discrepancies, deficiency gaps of specific anchors to the perceived external opportunities provided by the employing organization, successfully explain an IT professional’s turnover intentions. Exceptions to the discrepancy model are found in external conditions, including personal identification with the IT profession, family obligations, and substantial gain not attainable within their organization. This study provides further insight to our understanding of career anchor discrepancies as well as disturbances to the patterns set forth by career anchor models.

## 2. Background

A career is viewed as a sequence of work-related jobs that an individual experiences in his or her lifetime [15]. Career anchors are an individual’s self-perception of his or her talents and abilities, needs and motives, and attitude and values established at certain levels [7,41]. Discrepancy theory applies to career anchors in that individual satisfaction is determined as the gap between the individual’s wants and that provided by the organization; the magnitude defined by specific levels of each anchor and the perceived provision level of the employing organization [33]. The closer the match between organizational provision and individual want, the higher the job satisfaction, leading to a lessened turnover intention [21]. To retain good IT employees, organizations should attempt to provide incentives, environments, and jobs that are consistent with underlying anchors [3,12].

Nevertheless, empirical evidence indicates that IT personnel turnover regardless their level of satisfaction, while others stay when dissatisfied [3,8]. One explanation is the availability of job alternatives. Although job dissatisfaction will induce turnover intention, employees will decrease their turnover intention because they perceive an unfavorable job market [19,28]. Perceived job alternatives are modeled by researchers to moderate the effect between general job satisfaction and turnover intention [47]. Fig. 2 shows the relations as currently represented in the literature. Satisfaction is determined by discrepancies between wants represented by career anchors and perceptions of how much of each anchor is provided by their job. Satisfaction is a direct input to turnover intention.

Other external factors may explain breaks in the pattern. Outside employment opportunities remain a possible factor if the firm competing for the services of an IT professional can dislodge the current anchors with a career shift, offer a substantial increase to an anchor or reward beyond current levels of satisfaction, provide an outside career opportunity that may not be an open path in the current organization, or provide a chance to advance new knowledge relevant to the future of the IT discipline. For example, effective IT career management should develop professional skills throughout lifelong careers to promote continued success of IT professionals [38]. This indicates that even though internal career anchors may be satisfied, IT personnel still may leave their current jobs if they perceive themselves becoming irrelevant to the IS profession.

## 3. Methodology

In the absence of theory for explaining turnover behaviors when career anchors are satisfied, this study considers factors that might create a disturbance in the turnover pattern expected due to an IT employee’s satisfaction or dissatisfaction. Just what factors will impact the relationship between satisfaction and intent to leave the job? To address this research issue, we adopted a multiple-case study approach to reconsider discrepancy theory’s nomological net on IT professionals’ turnover intentions [9,10,48]. More specially, Eisenhardt’s [9] suggested eight steps of theory (model) building drove the study. The steps include (see Table 1): get started, select the cases, craft the instruments and protocols, enter the field, analyze data, shape propositions, enfold the literature, and reach closure.

## 3.1. Data collection

Individual professionals served as the target cases. Cases were selected according to the principle of theoretical sampling. Unlike sampling done in quantitative investigations, theoretical sampling cannot be fully planned before embarking on a case study [9]. The specific sampling decisions evolve during the research process itself. During initial data collection, when the main categories are emerging, ‘deep’ coverage of the data is necessary. Subsequently, theoretical sampling requires only collecting data on categories, for the development of properties and propositions. The criterion for judging when to stop theoretical sampling is the category’s ‘‘theoretical saturation’’ [13].

To have sufficient experience to make judgments about turnover decisions of IT professionals, our selection criterion for cases include having held at least two IT jobs and having over 15 years of work experience. The sampling frame must offer enough similar aspects to exhibit comparable properties associated with the research question [10]. After contacting a number of potential interview candidates, we selected 10 IT personnel as our cases – more would be chosen if the data from the first 10 do not provide enough data to achieve theoretical saturation. Data collection took place at the convenience of each individual chosen. Table 2 summarizes the demographic data of the subjects.

The primary data sources were semi-structured interviews [35]. The interview teams consisted of two of the authors. Interview protocols were developed and refined several times. The interviews were taped, with agreement from participants. The semi-structured interviews lasted on average 1 h. The taped interviews were transcribed verbatim into text files. The following represent a sample of the questions that guided the interview process:

\- Did you ever work for another company?

\- Do you want to leave the current job? Why or Why not?

\- What do you think are your most important career anchors throughout your career life? Why or why not?

\- What’s the reason each time you changed your job? Was it related to the organization’s ability to satisfy your career needs? Were alternatives more attractive, and why?

Based on key constructs (identified in the Appendix), we developed an initial list of coding categories. This list was refined after the first two interviews and refined again after each interview to reflect both the information gained and the additional information of published research based on the ideas gathered in the interview. Once the research team agreed on the list of categories, each member separately coded the same interview file. We compared results and discussed differences until agreement was reached on the categories, meanings, and future coding procedures. One researcher then coded the ten interview files using the revised coding scheme, which provided not only structure but also flexibility for coding new or unexpected findings [9,43]. After the files were coded, further discussions were held until the coders achieved complete agreement. Descriptions of each case follow. ## 3.2. Case descriptions and coding categories

Case #1 had four jobs experiences. Now, for him, the most important aspect of the job is stability until retirement. At his age, he claims to not want any pressure from high paying jobs or from any kind of competition. The second consideration is that the workplace provides him with an enjoyable environment, because if he stays he will lose social connections with the broader IT professional group. The third belief was that the job requirements match his professional skill. These career anchors differ for him compared to even 10 years ago. Twenty years ago, when he was an entrant, the most important thing was to keep learning IT skills. After decades of work, learning professional skills is still very important along with salary and position, but there is more concern with minimized stress.

Case #2 has had three jobs experiences. When he was an entrant, the most important things for him were learning and advancement. After a decade of work, the most important aspect has become being a manager and leader. So the career anchor he emphasized changed from technical to managerial competence. However, he did not stop learning the technical aspects at any point in his career. Now, for him, he wishes that he could move to where he can change the software development environment in the broader IT professional community.

Case #3 has had three jobs experiences. When he was an entrant the most important thing for him was technical skill and challenge. Job security was and still is important for him. After he was once promoted to a manager, managerial competence became another important career anchor, 12 years after he had begun to work. Now, the most important things include organizational stability and geographical security, followed by management competency, service and autonomy.

Case #4 has had five jobs experiences. As an entrant, he believed work experience, advancement and challenge to be most important. After 13 years of work, he wanted to be responsible for just one subdivision and was willing to accept the challenge of a new environment to which to apply his IT skills. For this reason, he changed jobs across drastically different industries. Now, for him, the most important aspects include time to address family and health issues in a stable environment. In view of this, he left the IS profession and went back to his original industry and a new functional domain. The current position is not completely satisfactory, but the opportunity for mentoring by his superior makes him stay.

Case #5 has had two jobs experiences. When he was an entrant, simply getting a job was the most important thing for him. After that he enjoyed resolving each problem in the professional domain and staying abreast of new developments in his more specific domain (GIS). Currently, he has an excellent reputation in the GIS professional community. He did everything he could as part of the job for a simple reason – for fun, and could apply his skills to his job without his superior’s interference. His career anchors remain unchanged.

Case #6 has had five jobs experiences. When she was an entrant, she believed that learning, affirmation of her abilities, achievement at work, and reputation in the IT professional community to be important. After 13 years of work, she felt bored with the same job. Now, she has many of the same thoughts as 13 years ago, but has lost the courage to face a challenging new environment, possibly influenced by age and family. Now, she cares about security and lifestyle much more than the challenge of the job. Job opportunities are scare in her current town, so she does not choose to leave her current job.

## 4. Data analysis and relationships

The analysis and results are organized across the major relationships characterized by the data. First, we turn attention to the relationships in the existing model of Fig. 2 in order to show consistency of the data with established results. From that point we add to the existing model as continued analysis provides direction for the unidentified quadrant of turnover in spite of satisfaction.

## 4.1. Anchor confirmation and job satisfaction

There is past empirical support for the effects of discrepancy between internal anchors and external opportunities on satisfaction, but the specific wants of each individual are paramount [4,23]. The aspects of the job for which IT professionals experienced the greatest discrepancies were the ones with which they were most dissatisfied and those with the smallest discrepancies were the aspects with which personnel were most satisfied [17,24]. The internal and external anchors together account for more of the variation in job satisfaction than either set does alone [23]. Essentially, individuals and organizations are dependent on each other as an indispensable consequence of their interaction [27]. Career planning activities present an approach that management uses to close the discrepancy by coaching employees through self-assessment and goal setting to increase employees’ satisfaction [37]. The cases of this study support this theoretical assertion. The following example statements illustrate case situations, from which the relevant anchor surfaces (in parentheses):

Case #1: I am happy because my job matches my skills well (managerial or technical anchor).

Case #2: A previous position had a government environment with associated bureaucracy and class-consciousness. I had no authority to do what I thought best even though I was a manager, so I was not pleased with the position (autonomy). Case #3: My job requires me to regularly visit the customer sites; it is not convenient for meeting family obligations (geographic security). However, I am happy with my current job because it provides great job security for me, which is also important to my family (security).

Case #5: I work for a good company and I will never leave it because it satisfies my need for challenges (challenge).

Case #6: I must take commuter buses early every morning; this is not so convenient and creates some dissatisfaction (lifestyle). Case #7: I am not too happy with my job because I feel the tasks do not satisfy my need for a challenge (challenge).

Case #9: In fact, I do not like doing the same job. For this reason, the boss assigns me different IT tasks (variety).

In short, the cases support the notion that a smaller discrepancy between an IT professional’s internal career anchors and perceived organizational provisions lead to greater satisfaction. Fig. 3 reflects on the discrepancy model of Fig. 2 and incorporates the replicated and proposed relationships for this and successive concepts. The relationship to this point is $\mathbf { R _ { 1 } }$ in the figure, where a comparison is made between what the employee seeks and what the employee perceives their organization provides leading to an increase in satisfaction when the desires are met and lessening of satisfaction if the desires are not met. This represents the situation where the comparative aspect of discrepancy theory is maintained.

## 4.2. Job satisfaction and turnover intention

Researchers identify job satisfaction as a key antecedent of turnover intention [47]. Job satisfaction is generally defined as an affective response by an employee concerning individual’s particular job and results from the employee’s comparison of actual outcomes with those that are expected, needed, wanted, or perceived to be fair or just. Thus, job satisfaction negatively affects intention to leave an organization; and the intention to leave directly impacts turnover behavior [45]. For this reason, positive attainment of job satisfaction is important to retain quality IS professionals.

Case #4: The company could not satisfy my desire to be challenged, so I quit that job.

Case #7: I left that job because it did not present a challenge anymore, which I need to stay happy.

Case #8: I left that company because I was not happy with managerial promotions or the ability to pursue entrepreneurial activities.

## 4.3. Disturbance of perceived job alternatives

Studies have argued that employees intending to leave their organizations must consider opportunities for other jobs. Turnover intention is a joint interaction of the individual’s dissatisfaction and perceived existence of job alternatives [45]. Gerhart [11], in fact, points out that perceived job opportunities moderate the relationship between job satisfaction and turnover intention. Those job seekers with few alternatives will decrease their intention to leave. Empirical evidence in the IS literature also supports that job dissatisfaction may influence IS personnel’s turnover intention [25]. However, these individuals should decrease their turnover intention when they perceive no job alternative. The results of this study also endorse this assertion, for example:

 Case #2: I want my company to be a leader in software development. However, I do not know if the company could achieve this goal. But to find another job I would have to transfer to another industry.

 Case #6: The current job is so boring to me. However, the job market for assembly language programmers is very limited. If I try to leave here, I will have no chance to find another job.

 Case #9: I kept changing jobs due to the same reason; I always have alternatives to find a new job.

 Case #10: When I did not feel I had a future with the previous company, I moved because searching for a new job was easy.

Based on these observations, we conclude that perceived job alternatives do influence the relationship between job satisfaction and turnover intention. This relationship, shown as $\mathbf { R _ { 3 } }$ in Fig. 3, disturbs the usual pattern of developing turnover intentions. As part of this disturbance, a few cases mentioned that they stayed with the organization in spite of a mismatch due to established relationships. This link would be an addition to $\mathbf { R _ { 3 } }$ in the model, a different way to explain disturbances in the direction explained by a lack of job opportunities. Support for this situation came from a couple of notable instances:

Case #4: Although I have intent to leave, I stayed with the position because the vice-leader of the hospital serves as a mentor. I need to remain because of this cultivating relationship.

Case #6: Although it would be hard to find another job, my superior treats me very well and does his best to provide the best environment.

## 4.4. Disturbance of group identity

Of the different jobs identified for the cases and summarized in Table 3, 11 of the 46 jobs indicate a situation not yet covered by the model – where the employee was satisfied, but still changed jobs or intends to in the near future. This indicates that the unidentified disturbance in Fig. 1 is substantial enough to warrant attention by researchers. Various reasons surfaced in the cases, but two themes seemed to be the more repetitive, whether their job blocked maintenance of recognized membership within the IT professional community or strained relationships with managers and peers. IT professional membership was exemplified in many of the cases as concerns:

Case #1: The company merged with another, resulting in a change to the organizational style. It did not affect my stay in the company; in fact, the organization had been willing to provide me even a higher position. However, I would lose connection with IT professional groups. Therefore, I decided to leave the company and take my current position.

Case #2: After 13 years of working for my first organization, I was looking for a job with a chance to make decisions and improve my professional expertise. When the opportunity arose, I changed jobs and now I make decisions by myself and enhance my professional skills/abilities. The achievement of this goal is important to me. I expect my company to be a role model in the software development area.

Case #4: My prior job was very stable and routine. My technical growth was inhibited, so I moved to my current organization where I have a chance to do something totally new by building an MIS department from the beginning. It is very important to me to stay in the IT field.

Case #5: I enjoy solving each problem in the technical domain. Keeping state-of-the-art GIS knowledge and addressing technical challenges are important to my reputation.

Case #8: Each time I turned over was because my job was too easy for me and I could not learn anything new. This is important in customer relations, you must always provide a total solution for their IS/IT problems.

Case #10: I disagreed with a policy to give up the company’s IT R&D direction, so I left.

Identification with a particular professional discipline can be explained by social identity theory (SIT) [2,44]. SIT argues that people tend to classify themselves into various social groups and an individual will strive to achieve a satisfactory concept or image of him/herself within the group [44]. IT professionals, based upon social identity theory, would strive to find, create, define and maintain their places in the IT field. IT personnel use the same jargon to interact, compete for resources with outsiders, and manage the flow of information across the semi-permeable boundaries with other functions and professions [40]. Therefore, the social identity of the IT domain is solid. Maintaining membership in the IT professional community requires having the right position, staying current in the field, and working with others who can recognize their skills [3,4]. Renewal of technological skills is an important aspect in the maintenance of identity and satisfaction [39]. Based upon SIT, IT professionals would have to leave their current organizations, in spite of satisfied internal anchors, if staying with their current organization will result in degrading their identity with the professional IT community [40], though this may be to different degrees depending on the extent the employee identifies with their organization [42].

Cases of conflicts in the relationships with others in the organization are also evident to a degree. These are reflected in proposition $\mathbf { P _ { 1 } }$ in Fig. 3, that group identity, either with the profession or with coworkers, are a disturbance to the usual pattern. Instances of relationship conflict that interfere with the gain of identity within an organization include:

Case #1: At my first job, all of my colleagues moved to a new company where I eventually joined them.

Case #5: I liked my first job, but the boss deceived us regarding the state of the organization. After 2 years of that I had to move on because I could not trust him.

Case #9: The CEO took all credit for every idea. Since I challenged his authority I had to leave even though I liked the job otherwise.

Further, there are other potential disturbance factors that may be present and highlighted in Table 4 that may disturb the pattern in either direction. These are added to Fig. 3 as $( \mathbf { P } _ { 2 } )$ and require further investigation to determine more exact identification.

## 5. Discussion

Reports in the literature suggest that satisfying an IT professional’s internal anchors is a feasible approach to retaining valuable IT personnel. However, some disturbances to this pattern exist where satisfied IT professionals left their organizations and some dis-satisfied IT professionals still remained with their current organizations. Fig. 3 depicts an extended theoretical framework of IT personnel turnover. It suggests that job satisfaction, based upon discrepancy theory, is determined by the discrepancy between internal anchors and external opportunity $\bf ( R _ { 1 } )$ Further, job satisfaction impacts turnover intention $( \mathbf { R } _ { 2 } ) .$ Perceived job alternatives moderate the relationship between job satisfaction and their turnover intentions $\left( \mathbf { R _ { 3 } } \right)$ – when an unsatisfied individual does not perceive job alternatives, the individual will stay with their current organization. We also found that positive working relationships with coworkers can be a disturbance in the $\mathbf { R _ { 3 } }$ relation. Interestingly, the results also indicate that IT professional group identity plays a moderating role on the relationship between satisfied individuals and their turnover behaviors $\left( \mathbf { P _ { 1 } } \right) \mathrm { - }$ satisfied individuals still leave their current organizations when they perceive their current job does not project the social identity desired. These conditions are not exhaustive as others surfaced during the interviews as shown in Table 4 and indicated as ${ \bf p } _ { 2 }$ in Fig. 3.

For IT researchers, this study extends our understanding of the disturbances in the pattern of turnover intention dictated by discrepancy theory. Not only can a lack of perceived opportunity keep a dis-satisfied worker from intending to leave a job, satisfied individuals might leave in order to more fully join the IT professional community or avoid poor working relationships. Though not as frequent, conflict with management is present in some decisions of the cases in this study. Further, satisfaction implies a threshold in many cases, and another disturbance might be an opportunity that provides a large jump in a particular anchor. For example, an organization might present opportunities for satisfying a worker’s managerial anchor, but a new job may open even further opportunity and lure the worker to a new organization.

In light of this, there are four implications for the research community. First, this study organizes the IT literature into a matrix of IT professionals’ turnover intention that identifies an unexplained segment of the turnover phenomena. Second, the cases confirm the relationships among ‘‘internal career anchors,’ ‘‘external opportunities,’’ ‘‘discrepancy,’’ ‘‘job satisfaction,’ ‘‘perceived job alternatives’’ and ‘‘turnover intention.’’ The model can serve as a basis for further studies. Third, this study reorganized those relationships into ‘‘maintenance’’ and ‘‘disturbance’’ of the typical patterns associated with turnover intention, allowing a focus on either aspect to enhance our understanding of IT professional turnover behaviors. Fourth, the moderator role of ‘‘IT professional group identity’’ between ‘‘job satisfaction’’ and ‘‘turnover intention’’ (P ) provides new insight to explain why IT professionals have the ‘‘pattern disturbance’’ of the unidentified quadrant in Fig. 1. In addition, the working relationships within an organization are a form of identity within local groups, while other factors $\left( \mathbf { P } _ { 2 } \right)$ also may also add disturbances.

This study also has several implications for IT managers. First, IT managers must not only understand their IT personnel’s current anchors, but also create environments to help IT professionals achieve their career goals and satisfy the need to be part of a larger IT community. Possible approaches to satisfying this aspect include supporting the use of newer technologies and methods, encouraging participation in professional societies, job rotation, and providing training of IT workers. Second, the data add to evidence that an IT professional’s internal anchors may not be static through their professional careers. Therefore, IT management should keep track of individuals’ careers needs at successive points in time. Organizations should consider formal use of evaluation techniques that consider the individual concerns of each valuable employee. Finally, IT management should understand that perceived job opportunities play a significant role between job satisfaction and turnover intention of IT personnel. Organizations should not abuse this as an opportunity to retain individuals as job shortages can be a very transient condition.

In companies that organize around projects, IT personnel rely on continuous movement from project to project to develop their careers, the processes linking IT personnel to projects and projects to careers should be critically examined [18]. In a project environment, the objective of IT personnel career development is to improve the competence of project personnel by offering the possibility of gaining knowledge and experience [30]. This fits in well with the consideration of social identity theory such that the IT knowledge held by IT personnel belongs to a certain social group (IT domain professionals). The IT professional community holds more emotional significance with greater value attached to that membership rather than to the organization because they hold similar values and belong to same psychological group [42].

The generalization of results may be limited due to considering organizational employees in traditional roles and in a single country. By considering cases only from the Taiwanese society, national culture could be an influential factor due to collectivism in the Eastern society versus individualism in the Western society. Organizations may also create disturbances by considering substitutions for internal technology personnel, such as consultants and outsourcing. Such factors may lead to other disturbances in the discerned patterns. Studies should seek to identify and define further disturbances. To track IT professionals’ through a career, all cases are senior employees. How disturbances, especially those related to social integration theories, influence younger employees is another possible confound requiring further investigation as well as other potential socio-demographic influences. Lastly, though this study has added understanding to the patterns of disturbance, empirical studies to determine the direction and magnitude of the impact of disturbance factors on the relation between satisfaction and turnover intention is suggested.

## 6. Conclusion

As organizational utilization of IT continues to grow, the ability of an organization to retain valuable IT personnel is a critical factor in the attainment of strategic goals. It is suggested, based on discrepancy theory that satisfying an individual’s anchors is a feasible approach to retaining valuable IT personnel. However, the anomalies to such understanding still result in unwanted turnover. Based upon the interview results of IT personnel we identified the additional disturbance of membership in the IT professional community and the quality of working relationships as an important consideration in forming turnover intent. In building an environment for the IT workforce, it is important to consider not only established anchors, but also those factors that tend to suppress or enhance the normal pattern of turnover decisions.
