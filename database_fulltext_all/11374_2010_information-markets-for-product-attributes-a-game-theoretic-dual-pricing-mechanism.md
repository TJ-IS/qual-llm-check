# Information markets for product attributes: A game theoretic, dual pricing mechanism<sup>☆</sup>

Panos M. Markopoulos <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Ravi Aron <sup>b</sup>, Lyle Ungar <sup>c</sup>

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 19 February 2010 Accepted 21 February 2010 Available online 25 February 2010

Keywords: Electronic markets Product information Shopbots Product search engines

## a b s t r a c t

We model the availability of information about product attributes in Internet-based markets, focusing on a phenomenon that we term as information de<sup>fi</sup>cit (availability of less than perfect information about product attributes) and its impact on the equilibrium strategies of sellers and buyers in electronic markets. We create a model of a market for a differentiated product and investigate how buyer uncertainty regarding the attributes of the product drives market outcomes. We show that a market for product information can partly correct the inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies that arise from imperfect information and predict that product information will begin to be traded once appropriate micro-transaction payment schemes become available. We formulate a mechanism by which sellers can charge an information rent for <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained information about product attributes which we term as a dual pricing mechanism wherein sellers extract an information rent in addition to the price of the product from buyers. We analyze the equilibrium that results and comment on the nature of welfare gains. A key <sup>fi</sup>nding of the paper is that allowing sellers to charge dual rents leads to more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient markets.

## 1. Introduction

Several articles appearing in the trade and business press have noted that while electronic markets could potentially feature <sup>fi</sup>ne grained information about all attributes of a product or service, they rarely do. For instance, very few Realtors offer three dimensional views of the interior of homes or the school district's performance statistics on their web sites. Similarly online travel agents do not offer information about airline movie schedules on long haul <sup>fl</sup>ights to even <sup>fi</sup>rst class travellers even as online retailers of high end stereo systems do not offer detailed information about the distortion in sound and loss of <sup>fi</sup>delity induced by different types of connection cables and wires. Yet, to many buyers of these services and products such information is indeed valuable and furthermore, such information is available with the producer. Thus, there is a widely observed phenomenon of less than perfect product information being furnished in these markets which we term as the ‘Information De<sup>fi</sup>cit’ of the market.

The Information De<sup>fi</sup>cit phenomenon can also be clearly observed in the functionality of existing popular shopbots and product comparison web sites. For example, shop.yahoo.com currently features more than 10,000 digital camera bundles (including lens combinations). This is a complicated product space where each product has hundreds of different product attributes that maybe of interest to different consumers. However, currently, the product space can only be search along eight product dimensions.<sup>1</sup> A consumer that wishes to <sup>fi</sup>nd the digital camera with the widest lens angle or the lowest focusing range faces a daunting task. This shows that yahoo's database of product speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, derived by manufacturers' descriptions about their products, is of such low quality that can only be reliably searched for a very limited number of product dimensions: the product space has to be searched sequentially and a lot of useful information is completely missing even from the manufacturers own websites. A typical missing attribute for example is the LCD monitor luminosity value, interesting to people who shoot in bright day light, when the monitor can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to see. The situation has improved very little in the last 6–8 years during which period the number of searchable attributes in yahoo grew from six to eight. For home appliances such as refrigerators and microwave ovens the situation is similar. For example the product space of more than 10,000 refrigerators cannot be searched by size, making it impossible for consumers to narrow down their search by specifying the refrigerator dimensions that would <sup>fi</sup>t into their kitchens. It is thus predictable that there exists a market in painstakingly putting together all this information for interested consumers with specialized websites such as dpreview.com that currently offers a data set of about 700 digital cameras that is searchable along 27 parameters.

Clearly a novel approach in the design of shopbots and product comparison web sites is needed. A business model that rewards the quality of information that vendors make available to consumers, would not only help consumers collect better information about products before they decide what to purchase, but it would also allow consumers to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently search large and complicated product spaces, according to their own personal needs and preferences.

We demonstrated in [13] that sellers choose not to disseminate any more detailed product information than what is necessary to separate them from their competitors, for any positive cost, howsoever small associated with the dissemination of such information, in the absence of the ability to collect an information rent for providing such information. This paper formulates a mechanism by which sellers can charge an information rent for <sup>fi</sup>ne grained information about product attributes, as a Dual Pricing mechanism in which sellers charge a product price and a information rent from the buyers.

We also demonstrate that more detailed information is valuable to consumers that are in the “switching threshold” products, i.e. to customers that have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a best option (product/service) but are likely to change their mind about their candidate product in the light of more detailed information. An attendant issue that arises is: can product information be bought and sold by market participants? We show that the value of information is, under reasonable assumptions, orders of magnitude less than the products' prices and thus cannot be traded unless there is a widespread micro-transaction scheme available to market participants. We show that allowing sellers to charge an information rent results in welfare gains and under certain conditions such a move is actually a Pareto Improvement. In markets characterized by the Dual Pricing mechanism, the extent of information de<sup>fi</sup>cit will be greatly reduced as sellers have incentives to sell detailed product information to interested buyers.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides the related literature and Section 3 provides the motivation for our work. We introduce our model in Section 4. In Section 5 we demonstrate a result that is key for the subsequent analysis: if the existing information makes product offerings suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently distinct, and if sellers have no mechanism by which to charge buyers for information, sellers will not disseminate any more horizontal information. In Section 6 we allow one seller only to charge for detailed product information, and show that this results to welfare gains for both buyers and sellers. In Section 7 we allow both sellers to compete in selling both their product and their detailed information and show that again, welfare gains result for both sides of the market. We conclude in Section 8.

## 2. Related literature

In 1929, Hotelling initiated the theory of spatial product differentiation [9], with his “linear city” model. Samueleson introduced the “unit circle” model [20] which became well known with Salop's treatment in [19]. The unit circle model managed to avoid mathematical complications in the end points of the line segment, while preserving the explanatory and predictive power of the original model. A good map to this extensive body of literature can be found in [7]. For a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed treatment of both models see [23].

Following Stigler's introduction of a theory of buyer search in product markets [21], an extensive body of literature has been developed in economics, that studies the effects of imperfect information and buyer search, to price dispersion, product differentiation and market structure, e.g. [3,17], for a review see [22]. Optimal sequential consumer search in uncertain environments has also been studied extensively, e.g. [11,24], for a review see [18].

The effects of the reduction of consumers' search costs, with the advent of Internet, have been studied as well, for example see [1]. Bakos, successfully connected the prior work with the realities of the emerging Internet markets. We take Bakos's efforts one step further, by considering parallel, shopbot-aided search, as opposed to “fast sequential” search, and acknowledging that consumers may still be left with a degree of uncertainty, even after having studied the shopbot's results.

## 3. Towards more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient markets

Even very early on in the history of shopbot and product comparison services, it became obvious that it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to balance economically viable business models with bias-free product presentation and recommendations. Early examples were the cases of Bargainfinder, PersonalLogic, Ringo and Shopbot [16]. Andersen Consulting's Bargainfinder ceased operations brie<sup>fl</sup>y after it was launched in 1995, after many vendors blocked its access to their web sites. PersonalLogic required vendors' active cooperation, as they were required to provide the interface that explicitly disclosed their product features, so that they could be easily matched against user preference pro<sup>fi</sup>les in PersonalLogic's database. PersonalLogic was acquired by AOL in 1998 and the venture was abandoned shorty after. Ringo focused on entertainment products (movies and music) and used collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering technology [4] to make product recommendations. Ringo was incorporated into FireFly, which was acquired by Microsoft and ceased operations shortly after. Shopbot [5] was highly autonomous as it did not require active seller-side cooperation. The agent was able to parse semi-structured HTML documents and extract product features. It was acquired by Excite and replaced with a biased vendor-driven version. By 2002, as Menczer, Street and Vishwakarma claim, all surviving comparison shopping services were seller biased [16]. Examples of shopbots today include MySimon, DealTime, PriceScan, RoboShopper and many others. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess the degree of their independence from the large vendors that they cover.

Until today, sellers of products and services have been able to effectively oppose bias-free product comparison services. It appears that it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to avoid seller bias, when the intermediary's revenues are derived from the seller side. On the other hand, for the intermediary's revenues to be derived from the buyer side, the buyers would truly have to be presented with a very wide, and unbiased, range of product choices, a situation that would immediately result in seller opposition. Therefore, in the absence of comprehensive and exhaustive product information available to consumers online, suboptimal product decisions purchases appear to be the norm. The result is market inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Bakos, analytically shows [1] that sellers will not only fail to invest in better search technology for the buyers, but they will actually resist any scheme that reduces the buyers search costs, unless they (the sellers) can capture the bulk of the buyers' ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains.

Thus, a deadlock appears in the problem of designing a market mechanism that provides bias-free product information to buyers: if that mechanism is not controlled by sellers, they will effectively oppose it and if it is, they will use it to promote their own interests at the expense of information transparency. It is, therefore, imperative for scientists working on shopbot technology, to seriously consider the economic implications of the systems they propose and develop.

Our goal in this paper is to propose a design for a novel business model for the operation of shopbots and price comparison web sites, that can increase the utility of both sides of the market and remain bias-free and economically viable, by providing the right incentives to all market participants.

## 4. A model of an Internet market

We employ Salop's classical model of spatial differentiation [19], with uniformly distributed buyer preferences, altered to account for uncertainty about the sellers' exact positions in product space. Our market is a duopoly with n buyers for a good that is spatially differentiated along a unit circle (see Fig. 1). We assume n to be a very large number, close to in<sup>fi</sup>nity. Each buyer has a most-preferred product location $l ^ { * }$ and incurs $\vec { \textbf { d } } ^ { \mathfrak { w } } \mathrm { f i t } ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ cost $\bar { U ( l , l ^ { * } ) }$ when he purchases a good at location l. We model the <sup>fi</sup>t cost to be a linear function $t | l - l ^ { * } |$ of the magnitude of the distance between l and $l ^ { * } .$ . Buyers demand one unit of the good, subject to a reservation utility v which we assume is high enough so that all buyers are served.<sup>2</sup> All players are risk neutral and sellers supply a single good with constant marginal cost (normalized for convenience to be zero). Thus, the utility that Buyer j, with ideal product location $l _ { j } ^ { \ast } ,$ receives from buying a product from Seller i, located at $l _ { i }$ and priced at $p _ { i }$ is:

$$
B _ {j} = v - p _ {i} - t | l _ {i} - l _ {j} ^ {*} |
$$

We model this as a game where sellers move <sup>fi</sup>rst by simultaneously choosing a location for their product offering and choose their prices having observed each other's locations. We introduce the idea of a parallel search which is a mechanism that is analogous to a search using an electronic search engine except that it returns the approximate location of a good (and therefore, the seller's position). The central idea here is that in the absence of detailed and exhaustive product information, the buyer can only know an interval within which the product is located and not the exact location of the product, which happens to be a composite of all product attributes, not just the ones that are available to the search device. Thus, the buyer can only learn the sellers' approximate location within an interval of size 2·a such that the seller's true location is equiprobable anywhere within the interval. We term this interval as the seller's “uncertainty interval”. The buyer is assumed to use a free shopbot for the parallel search, incurring no search cost.

We now introduce the concept of a “D-search”, which is a “Detailed” or “Distance” search within the interval returned by the parallel search. After a parallel search has produced information about a seller, a buyer can choose to perform a “D-search” that will reveal the seller's true location. An important distinction between the D-search and the Parallel search is that while the parallel search is not targeted at any particular seller, and will return both sellers' approximate locations, a D-search targets a particular seller's offering and returns its exact location. We assume that each Seller j demands an information rent $I _ { j }$ to allow a D-search. The fee can range from zero (implying a free D-search), to any number ex-ante, unbounded. Notice that the buyer is not obliged to perform a D-search in order to choose to purchase from a particular seller. The buyer might decide that she wants to buy the product and <sup>fi</sup>nd out the “details” about it afterwards.

Conceptually, the difference between the parallel search and a D-search represents two different components in the product's location information. One component, accessible through the parallel search, represents information that the buyer can extract about a product from visiting the seller's or an intermediary's web page. However, this usually cannot produce all information relative to the product's true location. Finer details about the product are not always accessible and are known only to the seller. The seller controls this component of the buyer's search by deciding whether or not to reveal them. We have assumed however that a product's price and the seller's identity are among the product attributes that the buyer can extract which is keeping in line with most currently functional electronic markets.

## 4.1. Model critique

Our model assumes that consumers interact with a single shopbot that is their only source of product and price information. Obviously this is not the only way consumers collect information about different products in online markets, as today there exist a variety of sources for product information. For example, many online retailers have created online communities in order to provide prospective buyers with free product reviews by buyers who have already tried the products, and many infomediaries, like Epinions.com, provide similar reputation mechanisms [2,15,25]. There are also sources that provide expert opinions on product parameters (e.g., CNet).

![](/api/attachments/FUVBAFRP/fulltext/images/b6ca3b49f562702036bec55b6736b88512bfc3db8aa84477b0050a3d7a6ecbdf.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Fit costs for a buyer at distance x from expected location of a seller.

There are differences between user opinions such as found in online communities and editorial content such as found in CNet. In the former case the information tends to be “experiential” — i.e. buyers report their experience with an attribute of the product rather than the actual attribute. Therefore, the users' opinions often diverge considerably even when they rate the same product attribute, and the reviews are rarely comprehensive. As a consequence, it is often unnecessarily time consuming to collect reliable information, as it is required for an informed purchase decision.

We now come to online editorial content to be found on such sites as CNet, that have become very popular in the last few years and that in general provide better information than online communities. There are two aspects to these sites that are worth noting. First, their mere existence seems to validate our main point, that the product information available directly from the product manufacturer is to a large extend incomplete and that many potential buyers require more information before making purchase decisions. And while these editorial content sites often independently create and provide more information than is available in the manufacturer's own website they are still only able provide as much information as it is economically viable to produce. The effect that such editorial content would have in our model, would be to reduce on average the size of the uncertainty interval a. While we will not explicitly model this effect, the reader is able to observe in the comparative statics that we provide in the <sup>fi</sup>gures that follow the effects of this uncertainty reduction. All propositions and conclusions reached in our paper would still be valid, except that in the limit (reached only in theory), the provision of truly exhaustive product information in a manner that can be ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently consumed by the prospective buyers would leave no residual uncertainty and, thus, no room for a product information market. A second and intriguing aspect of these sites is that they collect revenue from a variety of sources and their value proposition for buyers is the <sup>fi</sup>ner grained information that they provide. Revenue accrues to these sites for providing such information except that it is collected from the manufacturers of the products via multi-part tariffs. This happens because the ownership of the information channel has been decoupled from the owners of means of production — a classic case of infomediation, while here, we have formulated a model in which producers also own information delivery mechanisms. Do such channels exist today? Indeed they do — in business to business markets. In private B2B markets (such as those run by GE, IBM etc.) the owners of the B2B market charge participants for information about products, suppliers etc. This is possible because in these cases the information rents are substantial and therefore do not depend on micro-payments.

Turning our attention to the way we have chosen to model consumer uncertainty, as an interval within which the consumer perceives the product to be located with a uniform probability distribution (we are able to extend our analysis to arbitrary distributions), we <sup>fi</sup>nd our choice to simultaneously limit and strengthen our ability to represent real markets. The limitation is due to the fact that the size of the uncertainty interval is not always disproportional to the amount of information that vendors have furnished about their products.<sup>3</sup> Indeed, by providing more information, vendors do not necessarily reduce consumers' uncertainty. For example, consumers may <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to resolve the uncertainty they have about a product from a vendor that offers page after page of product specs. Rather, the size of the uncertainty interval corresponds to the degree by which a buyer's uncertainty is reduced. It is arguably dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (and costly) for a vendor to reduce consumers' uncertainty in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient way. The authors' personal preference is for product descriptions that are carefully “layered”, providing important product information in summary form, but allowing prospective buyers to dig deeper and deeper into product details. Thus, our model is not restricted by the way consumer uncertainty is reduced, but it is rather general in its ability to capture the essence of reducing consumers' uncertainty.

## 5. Case-I: Dual pricing is infeasible

## 5.1. Analysis

In this section we assume that sellers cannot charge buyers for the information and are forced to set their D-search fee to zero. This assumption represents a world where there is no mechanism for the sellers to charge buyers for a D-search. They can only refuse to provide the information, or provide it for free. We relax this assumption in the sections that follow and contrast the results.

We assume that the decision to allow D-searches carries a “very small” cost for the seller. For simplicity we will assume that this cost is smaller than any other positive quantity that appears in our analysis, yet strictly greater than zero. The justi<sup>fi</sup>cation is that while it is relatively cheap for a manufacturer to provide high quality information about a product, the effort is not completely trivial. In fact our results would become stronger had we assumed this cost to be substantial, as it is in product categories such as PC-Games where the development of a PC-game demo version is an expensive and complicated project.

Proposition 1. For non-overlapping uncertainty intervals, in a fully served market, if the sellers cannot charge for their detailed product information, then for any positive cost associated with disseminating detailed information, they will choose not to disseminate it, even under competition for overlapping market segments.

We have proved this proposition in [13,12], where we studied the full range of N≥2 sellers' incentives towards product information dissemination. Here we only present a rough outline of the proof. Intuitively, from Fig. 3, if Seller 2 does not allow D-searches he will sell to buyers in -AKB, and if he allows D-searches, he will sell to buyers in -A′KB′, with -A′KB′ = -AKB. So the seller will be indifferent between allowing and disallowing D-searches. Then, for any cost associated with allowing D-searches, he will chose not to allow them.

In [13], we have shown that sellers will provide just enough information so that their uncertainty intervals do not overlap, but no more information than that, for any positive cost associated with information dissemination. We have further shown that in the presence of “strong consumer preferences” (high values of the <sup>fi</sup>t cost parameter) sellers will provide slightly more information, while never quite reaching the perfect information regime, where their uncertainty intervals would be reduced to zero size.

The result that is relevant to this paper, is the price equilibrium of this model, which has been shown to have both sellers charging $p ^ { * } { = } t / 2$ for their products. A simple proof is as follows: In equilibrium each seller will serve a territory of size $1 / 2 ,$ , which corresponds to half the market. Let $Q _ { i } ( p _ { i } , p _ { j } )$ denote the size of Seller i's territory when Seller i and j charge $p _ { i }$ and $p _ { j }$ for their products respectively. We know that $\begin{array} { r } { Q _ { i } \Big ( p _ { i } ^ { * } , p _ { j } ^ { * } \Big ) = \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ and that $\begin{array} { r } { d Q \left( p _ { i } ^ { * } , p _ { j } ^ { * } \right) = \frac { \delta } { t } { \Longleftrightarrow } d Q \left( p _ { i } ^ { * } , p _ { j } ^ { * } \right) = \frac { - d p _ { i } } { t } \Longleftrightarrow \frac { d } { d p _ { i } } Q \left( p _ { i } ^ { * } , p _ { j } ^ { * } \right) = - \frac { 1 } { t } . } \end{array}$ So we have $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 } { 2 } - \frac { p ^ { * } } { t } = 0 \Longleftrightarrow p ^ { * } = \frac { t } { 2 } . } \end{array}$ However, not all product location con<sup>fi</sup>gurations yield price equilibria. If it so happens that the vendors' location are “too close” in the product space, pure strategy price equilibria do not exist. In [13] we discuss in detail these well known results of the unit circle model. We show that in case of duopoly, the vendors need to be at least 1/4 units apart. For this reason, the analysis in this paper is only relevant to vendors that have located their products at least a quadrant's distance apart.

If the sellers cannot charge buyers for D-searches and can only disallow them or provide them for free, then for any positive cost associated with allowing D-searches the equilibrium is as follows: in stage one the sellers chose their locations uniformly, since they play simultaneously and cannot coordinate, and set their prices to $\begin{array} { r } { p = \frac { t } { 2 } } \end{array}$ disallowing the D-searches in all subsequent stages. This result is validated by most Internet consumer markets today, as product comparison web sites usually provide only basic product information, leaving out richer attribute level descriptions of many product characteristics.

This results to a market inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as can be demonstrated by the increase in expected buyer <sup>fi</sup>t costs per buyer, also termed the market's “deadweight loss”. The inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency depends on the exact location of the sellers inside their uncertainty intervals. For example if the sellers do indeed occupy their expected locations at the center of their uncertainty intervals then uncertainty has no effect on buyers' expected deadweight losses. It is important to understand how the size of the uncertainty interval a, which is our measure of uncertainty, affects market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. For simplicity of disposition we will assume that the buyers know the exact location of Seller 1 while they can only observe an interval of size 2a within which the product of Seller 2 is located. In equilibrium both sellers capture equal market shares and we can use Fig. 3 to calculate the expected <sup>fi</sup>t costs per buyer. ## 5.2. Discussion of results

Arguably there are costs associated with keeping track of detailed product information and being able to disseminate it. In our airline example an investment is needed for the airlines to provide travel agents with information on service parameters such as in-<sup>fl</sup>ight entertainment, leg room in different classes etc. Likewise, computer retailers <sup>fi</sup>nd that it is costly to furnish detailed information about all product features, and archive it in such a way as to make parallel search feasible. For instance, keeping track of every bundled software piece and every component manufacturer's feature is costly and therefore, the consumer often <sup>fi</sup>nds out about these features after the purchase on the point of consumption. However, from the point of view of the buyer, it is clear that what is important is their satisfaction with the product after they have bought it and experienced it by using it [10].

This Section argues that the “Information De<sup>fi</sup>cit” phenomenon, has an economic justi<sup>fi</sup>cation. It exists due to asymmetries of the buyers' and sellers' incentives towards more and better information. Sellers do not always care to provide such information, since it is as likely to please a customer as it is to fall short of the customer's expectations. However, it is always the case that at least some buyers will want information of higher quality that would help them identify their best option. For those buyers, the detailed product information has a de<sup>fi</sup>nite economic value.

## 6.3. Discussion of results

In Section 6.1 we see that the ratio of the equilibrium information price to product price, for Seller $2 \operatorname { i s } { \frac { 2 a } { 9 } } .$ This explain why, today, sellers do not sell detailed product information to buyers. To see why, assume that the amount of a seller's information that is not included in the product description is two percent of the product's attribute space.<sup>5</sup> As per our model $2 a = 2 \% ,$ or $a = 1 \% .$ Comparing the product price given by ${ \frac { t } { 2 } } ,$ to the information price of $\mathit { \Pi } _ { \overline { { 9 } } } ^ { a t } ,$ we <sup>fi</sup>nd that product information should be $\frac { 1 } { 4 5 0 }$ of the product price. Even for a product that costs \$300, information price would be less than 67 cents. The 67 cents cannot be absorbed in the product price, as we have seen that these quantities maximize different components of the seller's revenue. In the absence of a widespread micro-transaction payment scheme, sellers today are not capable of charging these small information fees to buyers that require more detailed product information. On the other hand, in cases where information has considerable higher value, for example for higher uncertainty a, higher product prices (which in our model translates to higher $t ) ,$ or for a buyer that requires multiple units of the product, there is already a market for consumer reports, as explained by our model. Indeed for industrial products such as steel and copper that are used in creating direct engineered goods, the product attributes (such as thermal conductivity, shearing stress etc.) are of so much signi<sup>fi</sup>cance that there are fully developed markets for the selling of such information.

Whereas no current shopbot uses D-searches as described in this paper, there are cases where buyers purchase information that would allow them to improve their product <sup>fi</sup>t. For example, after purchasing a photo printer many users <sup>fi</sup>nd that the printed colors do not exactly match what they see in their computer monitors because different combinations of printers, papers and inks produce slightly different color results. The users can correct these small discrepancies by the use of ICC color pro<sup>fi</sup>les that are computer <sup>fi</sup>les that translate between different color representations. These color pro<sup>fi</sup>les can often be purchased online by third parties. One such online vendor of printer pro<sup>fi</sup>les that we investigated (www.ddisoftware.com) charges approximately 1/20 to 1/60 of the printer's price. This price should theoretically be comparable with the D-search cost that we have estimated to be 2 orders of magnitude below product price. While the ICC pro<sup>fi</sup>les seem to be more expensive than this, we must consider the total cost of printing which includes printer supplies that can add up to many times the price of the printer. Under this consideration it seems that, indeed, the cost of information that would improve product <sup>fi</sup>t is about 2 orders of magnitude less than the total cost of the product.<sup>6</sup>

It is interesting that by selling detailed product information a seller is able to price discriminate: some buyers in the market pay $\begin{array} { r } { p = \frac { t } { 2 } } \end{array}$ for the product, while those that require detailed product information pay <sup>t</sup> + <sup>at</sup>. It is worth noting that the seller is able to price discriminate even if he cannot observe buyers' locations because buyers self-select their type when they chose to perform a D-search or not. Because in our model all buyers value their ideal product the same way (their value is given by v), buyers that have found a very close product match have little value for additional information and are thus unlikely to perform a D-Search. As it is well known, when there is heterogeneity in buyer valuation of some service or product, those who value the product most are candidates for discriminatory pricing. In our model the buyers that value the information most are those buyers whose expected value of the product is the lowest. Thus in consonance with the literature in price discrimination, we <sup>fi</sup>nd those that value the service most (in this case information) are the ones that pay for it most. It so happens that these buyers are also the ones whose expected value of the product is the least. This inverse relationship between “expected value of the product” and “expected value of information”, can be easily explained by the well known assertion in decision analysis that “information has maximal value under maximal uncertainty”.

Buyers also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the existence of a product information market. There is an increase in market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as measured by the decrease in aggregate “deadweight loss”, which is the sum of all buyers' <sup>fi</sup>t costs. Clearly, this represents the welfare gains from charging an information rent.

A premise of this paper is that the amount of information that is available to the buyers via the free parallel search is given exogenously. Making this parameter an endogenous model attribute has been the basic focus of our efforts in [13]. In fact, if vendors are allowed to charge for detailed product information, they may choose to furnish less of their information for free, thereby effectively increasing a and their optimal “D-search” prices. In this case the welfare gains may be less than this paper shows. This presents an interesting complication that deserves a closer look in future extensions. Furthermore, the vendors may have the incentive to increase the overall information complexity of their product, making speci<sup>fi</sup>c product design decisions towards that goal. While our model is unable to capture this complication, since the product space is assumed to be one-dimensional, we believe that this possibility should probably be discounted, as we do not believe that the information fees that the vendors can generate in a product information market are enough to in<sup>fl</sup>uence product design decisions, even if they are enough to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their information dissemination decisions.

Finally, the dependence of the value of information on the uncertainty parameter a means that the ability of the vendors to charge for information would reduce, if we were to increase the number of vendors in the market. This is because, as we showed in [13], increased vendor competition would reduce the amount of uncertainty to which the vendors are a-priori indifferent (in the absence of a product information market).

## 7. Case-III: Dual pricing and seller competition

We will know study the case where both sellers are endowed with a mechanism by which to sell their detailed product information, or, in other words, to charge buyers for D-searches. The sellers compete in selling their detailed product information, as well as competing in selling their actual products.

We will extend our game to account for both sellers' ability to employ dual pricing. The space of strategies is exogenously determined and there is an in<sup>fi</sup>nite number of stages. In stage one, the sellers simultaneously choose their price and location, and observe the opponent's choices. In each of the subsequent in<sup>fi</sup>nite number of stages, the sellers <sup>fi</sup>rst set their D-search fees and one buyer, randomly chosen from the population, enters the market to purchase one unit of the good. The sellers are thus allowed to change their D-search fees between successive buyers. The assumption we employ is that the sellers can easily recon<sup>fi</sup>gure their information services, namely D-searches, while it is relatively dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to recon<sup>fi</sup>gure their actual product. Thus, by the time they can actually recon<sup>fi</sup>gure their product, a very large number of potential buyers, for simplicity assumed in<sup>fi</sup>nite, has entered the market.

We show that in the resulting equilibrium the sellers charge $p ^ { * } { = } t / 2$ for their products, and require a constant D-search fee in each of the subsequent, in<sup>fi</sup>nite, stages. We also show that the sellers implicitly collude when they set their D-search fees, jointly maximizing their information revenues. The collusion result is driven by the ability of one seller to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly damage the opponent's information revenues by slightly undercutting the opponent's D-search fee. More precisely, even a slight undercutting of the opponent's D-search fee, leads to discontinuously reduced opponent's pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

An outline of the analysis we perform in this section is as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst study the mechanism by which buyers decide to perform D-searches on sellers. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, as before, buyers that are in the “switching threshold” are interested in the sellers' detailed information. Some of them will perform D-searches on one seller, while some will perform D-searches on both sellers. Let S be the number of D-searches from buyers that search for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time and s be the number of D-searches from buyers who search for the second time. We show that S is strictly higher than s, or in other words a strictly positive number of buyers will not want to perform a D-search for a second time, after having performed the <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the seller with the lower D-search fee will get all of S <sup>fi</sup>rst D-searches, that is, all D-searches that occur from buyers who search for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time. If both sellers charge the same amount for their detailed information, all buyers will be indifferent between the two sellers' detailed information. This result is somewhat surprising, as one might have expected that buyers might have targeted their <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search towards the seller whose expected location is closer to their ideal product location. As a result of this, we observe an in<sup>fi</sup>nitely repeated prisoner's dilemma scenario where sellers are able to threaten their opponent that unless they cooperate they will be punished by being undercut in their D-search fee, which will result in the loss of all <sup>fi</sup>rst D-searches. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a colluding competitor receives on expectation (S + s) / 2 D-searches while an opponent who has defected can be punished by being forced to receive s D-searches, with $s { < } ( S { + } s ) / 2$

However, we know that this will occur only if the second D-search further increases the buyer's utility, after the <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search has been performed. Thus, the option for a second D-search increases the buyer's utility for a <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search. The marginal buyer, at the end of the s interval, would have zero utility for a second D-search on a Seller, but strictly positive utility if a <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search is being considered. Therefore, the interval S where buyers perform at least one D-search will extend further than s, where buyers perform exactly two D-searches.

When sellers compete in selling their detailed product information at the same time as they compete in selling their product, we proved that the two revenues will be maximized separately. However, there is no pure strategy equilibrium if the sellers were to set their D-search fees in a one stage game. This is because a slight undercut of the opponents D-search fee captures all <sup>fi</sup>rst searches in the S interval which leads to a discontinuous increase in seller revenues. This contrasts, for example, with the case of product price competition, where an ε undercut of the opponent's product price leads to an increase in market share that is a function of ε. However, D-search fees are not driven down to zero, as the sellers can at least receive second D-searches, which are maximized according to the analysis in Section 6.

According to the “fold theorem” [8], any outcome where the sellers' pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are at least as high as the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts when they maximize their revenue from second D-searches, is supported by a set of equilibrium strategies. However, it is also widely accepted that the most likely outcome in this case is that sellers will achieve implicit collusion, as they can credibly threaten their opponent with a D-search fee undercut. ## 8. Conclusion and extensions

We study the conditions under which sellers make horizontal information available to buyers and <sup>fi</sup>nd that when sellers are unable to charge for product information, in the presence of even very small costs associated with the dissemination of information they will choose to make only enough information available to the market, to separate them from their competitors. Our model, thus, sheds light to some of the underlying causes for the information de<sup>fi</sup>cit that is seen in Internet-based markets.

Furthermore, we show that a market for product information can partly correct this inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and predict that product information will begin to be traded once an appropriate micro-transaction payment scheme becomes available. It follows from our analysis that allowing sellers to charge an information rent leads to more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient markets.

As an extension of this research we intend to investigate differences in information dissemination incentives between product manufacturers and product retailers. We will also extend our model to account for N sellers and other types of buyer heterogeneity where some buyers may demand <sup>fi</sup>ne grained information while others do not, and where buyer valuation of quality is characterized by heterogeneity [14]. Furthermore, we intend to incorporate the distinction between horizontal and vertical product information, providing as a consequence what we believe to be, a more complete treatment of product information dissemination in Internet markets.

We will bring together these analyses to comment on the implications of Dual Pricing markets and investigate the conditions under which these lead to welfare gains.

## Appendix A. Alternative modelling assumptions

Here we provide a brief discussion on the dependence of our results upon three important modelling premises. In the interest of brevity we consider the effect of the modelling premises on the validity of Proposition 1. This is because if Proposition 1 is valid, the ability of vendors to charge for detailed product information follows.

## The linear fit cost function

Any increasing, concave <sup>fi</sup>t cost function would yield results that are equivalent to Proposition 1: that vendors will not disseminate detailed product information if their uncertainty intervals do not overlap. Strictly concave functions would in fact allow for some overlapping of the uncertainty intervals that increases as the concavity of the alternative functions increase, in which case even less information is disseminated without the use of a market mechanism. We believe that, in fact, concave functions would be reasonable modelling alternatives as they have the property that <sup>fi</sup>t costs increase very fast as we start to move away from a buyer's ideal product. Of course, for longer distances, strictly concave functions would end up growing slower than the linear <sup>fi</sup>t cost function (or convex functions for that matter), but we consider this property desirable because we believe it to accurately depict “strong” consumer preferences, were products lose a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of their value for even small deviations from the buyer's ideal product.<sup>7</sup> Convex functions, for example quadratic <sup>fi</sup>t costs, do not have this property and their slow rate of increase near the buyer's ideal product location make them unrealistic for the purposes of our model.<sup>8</sup> In summary, the linear <sup>fi</sup>t cost function was chosen as a representative of a wider class of functions that yield similar results. Furthermore, the linear <sup>fi</sup>t cost function is convenient for mathematical analysis, and its <sup>fi</sup>t cost parameter intuitively corresponds to different levels for the strength of consumer preferences.

## Uniform consumer preferences

Uniform consumer preferences are the norm in the unit circle literature, as non-uniform preferences do not in general yield product location equilibria, where vendors have no incentives to re-locate their products. However, there are reasons to believe that Proposition 1 would still hold under the non-uniform probability distributions for consumer preferences that do indeed yield product location equilibria.<sup>9</sup> This is because, for a vendor to have found a product location that cannot be further improved, it has to be the case that by a small relocation to the left of his current position, as many customers are lost on one side of the circle as they are gained on the other. If this is not the case then the vendor can improve pro<sup>fi</sup>tability by relocating and the market cannot be in equilibrium. However, this is exactly the model behavior observed for uniform distribution of customer preferences and this is what drives Proposition 1. In summary, we do not believe that Proposition 1 depends on customer preferences being uniformly distributed, and there are strong reasons to believe that any non-uniform customer preferences distribution that yields product location equilibria would also yield Proposition 1.

However, if we were to introduce non-uniform preferences and relax our assumption that the vendors have reached a location equilibrium then it may happen that vendors <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves with one end of their market share having a more dense buyer distribution than the other end. It can be shown that under general conditions, in such circumstances vendors freely offer their product information (for low enough information dissemination costs) and no product information market can exist. This happens because now, the product information market cannot be successfully decoupled from the product market and the sellers strategically use their information to either increase product revenues, or to decrease the product revenue loss they would incur by withholding detailed information.

We know from Proposition 2, that after the buyers perform their <sup>fi</sup>rst D-search on Seller 1 and change their current best seller according to newly discovered detailed information, Seller 2 cannot do anything to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the product market shares. In other words, we know that the product market shares from buyers that perform a second D-search will be the same in size (although not necessarily in location) with the product market shares that would occur if buyers only performed one Dsearch on Seller 1. This is because, after the buyers perform D-searches on Seller 1, there is only one seller, Seller 2, that offers D-searches, hence Proposition 2 applies.

Consequently, the vertical cut in Fig. 13, enclosed by the “s” box does not change the sizes of the product market shares, even if it “shuf<sup>fl</sup>es” the gray and black areas, as shown in the bottom of Fig. 13. For simplicity, we depict this unknown “shuf<sup>fl</sup>ing” of the market shares with regions of equal black and grey color. Thus, the total sizes of the two sellers' product markets will be the same, after all D-searches have been performed, with the market sizes before any D-search was performed. In other words, any combination of product price and D-search fee for Seller 1 will result in the same market share with the base case of market shares before buyers perform any D-searches, for example, for Seller 1 in Fig. 12, buyers in -MM′. We know that this size equals <sup>1</sup> − <sup>p1−p2</sup>, and is thus maximized only by the competitors' product prices. This concludes the proof.
