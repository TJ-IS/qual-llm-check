# Resilience of super users’ mental models of enterprise-wide systems

Corinne M. Karuppan<sup>1</sup> and Muthu Karuppan<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Research on information system training has focused primarily on methods, while neglecting the effects of interruption intervals between training and system implementation. This empirical study examines the resilience of accurate mental models of an enterprise-wide system in a large health care facility. Accurate mental models were shown to withstand the passage of time and resulted in superior field performance following a crash conversion. Consistent with prior experimental research, certain types of learners were more likely to acquire these sounder mental models. In large organisations where the simultaneous training of users on a complex system is virtually impossible, scheduling is an important facet of the training paradigm. The main practical implications of this study involve the development of: (1) training programmes emphasising performance on far-transfer tasks, (2) training schedules designed to minimise knowledge erosion, and (3) criteria for selecting highly capable super users. ## Introduction

Over the past two decades, much emphasis has been placed on the design of effective information systems (IS) training programmes. Following Cheney et al.’s (1986) call for additional research into the ‘most effective forms’ of user training, a prolific research stream has focused on matching training methods and individual characteristics. Its main outcome has been the emergence of training programmes offering a variety of learning aids to accommodate a plurality of learning needs. Another training domain that could easily be moulded to suit individual characteristics is the timing of instructor-led training sessions. Since complex knowledge is rapidly forgotten (e.g., McCreery & Krajewski, 1999), training sessions for a new, enterprise-wide computer system should be scheduled as close to the launch date as possible. For organisations implementing enterprise-wide systems with hundreds or even thousands of users to train, the ‘earliest possible date’ may range from a couple of weeks to several months before system rollout. The study reported here examines the training issues associated with the introduction of an integrated health system including laboratory, radiology, and pharmacy modules, and an ordering and browsing system for nursing. Consequently, a large number of diverse users from different departments were training candidates. Because the sheer number of employees did not permit the thorough training of all users, a limited few received extensive training and assumed mentoring and support functions to other, less trained workers in their unit. These individuals were named ‘super users.’ Their role is essential because their understanding of the new equipment/machine/software will, in turn, affect the quality of the support they provide to the people who depend on them. Preserving the super users’ knowledge integrity after training should therefore be a priority. The primary purpose of this study is to develop and test a framework of the resilience of their mental models over time.

One approach to preserving knowledge integrity involves practice. Interaction with a system after training contributes to expanding and enhancing the knowledge base (Mack et al., 1983; Carroll & Rosson, 1987; Santhanam & Sein, 1994; Anderson, 1997; Anderson et al., 1999). However, this option may not be feasible. In the present study, the multi-tier, client–server system followed the distribution function model described by Woolfe (1995). According to this model, all the data are on the server, the presentation software is on the client, and the application layer is split between the client and the server. This type of implementation makes it difficult to produce portable training or self-running, server-independent demos. The organisation used a multi-node infrastructure in which the development and testing environment was separated from the training environment. Owing to cost considerations, the training environment was supported by a lower-end server with limited client connection. Therefore, the ‘training version’ was only available in the training classrooms. Upon completion of the training programme, users lost access to the system as a new batch of trainees gained access to it. Under these circumstances, an alternative approach for maintaining knowledge integrity may consist in developing effective learning strategies and schedules.

Multiple learning theories have underscored the importance of individual characteristics to the learning process. In the theory of operant conditioning, Skinner (1969) acknowledged the influence of a learner’s past history on behaviour. Cronbach & Snow (1977) also agreed that individual differences such as prior knowledge, skills, and previous experience influenced the learning process. Social learning theory (Bandura, 1977, 1986, 1997) echoed these sentiments in proposing that the conditions necessary to model others’ behaviours differed among individuals and therefore influenced their learning. These theories justify the inclusion of individual differences when studying the learning process, a core element of mental model formation. A review of the empirical research in IS led Zmud (1979) to conclude that three categories of individual difference variables influenced MIS users’ attitudes and IS success: demographic/ situational variables, personality variables, and cognitive/ learning style. This study only investigated the role of learning styles. Not only is this cognitive variable well grounded in learning theory (Bostrom et al., 1990), but it has also been popular in IS experimental research. Therefore, using learning styles as an independent variable facilitated comparisons across research designs.

Since combined experimental and field results provide a stronger basis for normative guidelines (Gordon et al.,

1984), we attempt to validate prior experimental research linking learning styles/modes to the formation of an accurate mental model in a field setting. We also expand the scope of this academic research by including another training parameter, that is, the timing of training in large organisations. With the costs of successful training approaching up to 15% of enterprise systems implementation budgets (Slater, 1998), our study’s contribution to practice could be especially significant in providing simple and cost-effective methods to schedule training and refresher sessions while minimising knowledge loss before system implementation.

## Research framework: Mental Model Resilience

The importance of individual difference variables on IS user performance is strongly rooted in social psychology and learning theories that postulate that individuals bring their intellectual, experiential, and psychological baggage in new learning situations (Skinner, 1969; Bandura, 1977; Cronbach & Snow, 1977; Bandura, 1986, 1997). Several experiments have shown that learning modes (Sein & Bostrom, 1989; Bostrom et al., 1990) influence the accuracy of a mental model of the system thereby leading to the user’s better performance. A learning style refers to the way individuals acquire and use information (Sadler-Smith, 1996). Some individuals prefer to learn from hands-on experience whereas others favour theory-based knowledge acquisition. In the same vein, some individuals excel at brainstorming and generating new ideas whereas others are more apt at synthesising diverse sources of information and organising it into a concise, logical form. There is a general consensus that learning styles affect the use of an information system (Lucas, 1974; Karuppan, 2001) and its understanding (e.g., Frese et al., 1988; Zuboff, 1988; Sein & Bostrom, 1989) through the development of mental models (Bostrom et al., 1990; Ford & Chen, 2001).

Prior research establishing links among learning styles, accurate mental models, and performance shares these common design characteristics: controlled learning environments in laboratory settings with relatively homogenous subjects in terms of age and education (students), simple computer systems (e.g. e-mail system), and simultaneous training of all users (Frese et al., 1988; Sein & Bostrom, 1989; Bostrom et al., 1990; Davis & Davis, 1990). Powerful tools to build theories and draw causal inferences (Farber, 1952; Gordon et al., 1984), these controlled experiments do not capture the inherent difficulty of training a large number of users on a complex, enterprise-wide system. Moreover, they rely on student subjects whose personal characteristics do not match those of a target population with diverse educational backgrounds and levels (Miller et al., 1983). A threat to the validity of controlled experiments with student subjects (Brunswik, 1955; Campbell & Stanley, 1963; Gordon et al., 1984; Gordon et al., 1986) may also lie in the differences in their perceptions of the experimental tasks (Latham & Dossett, 1978). For example, the loss of earnings/jobs resulting from the inability to use a computer system is not a ‘punishing’ stimulus for students. Finally, the above-mentioned experiments involved a limited number of subjects whose simultaneous training and evaluation were performed within a short time interval. The use of short-term retention tasks and immediate post-testing enabled the researchers to control for a host of variables such as opportunity to practice, amount of self-study, etc., which would have interfered with their ability to establish causal linkages. In essence, their objectives differed from those of the present study.

In large real-world settings such as the one described here, individuals are trained several weeks or even months prior to system implementation. As a result, they have ample time to forget newly acquired system knowledge. In fact, the complexity of the system knowledge further exacerbates this phenomenon (McCreery & Krajewski, 1999; Nembhard & Osothsilp, 2002). For complex systems, skill retention over time is a better indicator of training effectiveness than is immediate performance (Sauer et al., 2000). The proper method of assessing retention would be to administer the same test at different time intervals. In the present study, the cost, time, and scheduling requirements of the administration of a second test prohibited its implementation. Superuser performance on the actual system was used as a surrogate measure of retention.

The first objective of this paper is thus to assess the resilience of super users’ mental models to the passage of time. Since the accuracy of a mental model can only be evaluated after training, the second objective is to determine how learning influences the construction of an appropriate mental framework of the system and propose training schedules that will minimise system knowledge decay (Figure 1). In a nutshell, the Mental Model Resilience (MMR) model posits that learning modes can affect the development of an accurate mental model, which, in turn, can counteract the effects of time on memory loss to predict better field performance. Important variables in the learning equation also include other individual traits and job characteristics (e.g., occupation, hours worked, casual employment, and earnings) (Long et al., 1999). However, the focus of this study is limited to learning modes. The MMR model combines several learning and information processing theories to elucidate a training issue commonly found in practice. It ultimately leads to several suggestions to enhance and solidify mental models and foster retention.

The study was conducted in a large hospital in the Midwest of the United States. One of the authors was then a project architect in the system implementation team. Large health care organisations face specific training challenges when implementing new enterprisewide IS. The textbook suggestion of a parallel implementation strategy as a preferred method of conversion is inadequate because large organisations in general, and hospitals in particular, face unique constraints. For example, when a patient is logged into the clinically integrated system (CIS), a significant amount of data is gathered on that patient. Moreover, CISs are interfaced to a variety of medical devices and, in most cases, these devices can be connected to only one system at time. Running parallel systems would necessitate medical personnel manually re-entering the data into the second system. Medical personnel’s time and cost constraints make a ‘crash’ (as opposed to a parallel) conversion from an old computer system to a new one inevitable. This condition intensifies the need for effective training and user support. As is often the case in large system implementations, super users assumed the dual role of users and problem solvers/trainers (Potts, 1997). Their performance on the field was measured by the number of problem referrals to a higher tier of support. A review of the literature on learning-and-forgetting phenomena, mental models, and learning styles is presented in the next section. It is followed by a description of the methodology, a discussion of the results and their implications, and concluding remarks.

## Learning, retention, and performance

## Learning and forgetting

According to Gaines (1991), ‘A time lapse of more than a week or two between training and implementation can cause a significant lapse in employee performance.’ To our knowledge, there is no empirical work examining the relationship between the scheduling of user training and performance, but the related literature on learning and forgetting provides useful insights. The amount of prior learning as measured by experience and the length of the interruption interval between tasks are the critical contributors of forgetting (Bailey, 1989; Badiru, 1995; Jaber & Bonney, 1996; Anderson et al., 1999; Jaber & Kher, 2004). Indeed, the period of skill nonuse has consistently been negatively related to performance in the literature. In a meta-analysis of the literature focusing on the factors that influence skill decay, Arthur et al. (1998) found that longer non-practice intervals contributed to increased skill loss.

Forgetting is strongly associated with the currency of experiential learning (Smunt, 1987; Nembhard, 2000). Simply stated, lack of practice triggers forgetting. The nature of the tasks performed further influences this phenomenon (Farr, 1987). Procedural tasks that call for a series of discrete, motor responses such as operating a computer, are more easily forgotten than continuous control tasks involving repetitious movements like tracking (Naylor & Briggs, 1961; Prophet, 1976). Continuous tasks are more amenable to repeated practice and therefore overlearning (Naylor & Briggs, 1961; Adams, 1967). Moreover, they tend to be more integrated or coherent (Naylor & Briggs, 1961; Adams, 1967). These combined properties may explain why procedural tasks engender a more pronounced decline in retention (Prophet, 1976). Logically, cognitive tasks such as those involved in learning a new computer system should be more easily retained than physical tasks because they lend themselves to mental practice (e.g., Ryan & Simons, 1981). However, the reverse is true in the absence of practice (Arthur et al., 1998). Finally, individuals tend to forget complex knowledge more rapidly (Shields et al., 1979; Hall et al., 1983; McCreery & Krajewski, 1999; Nembhard & Osothsilp, 2002; McCreery et al., 2004). According to Bloom’s revised taxonomy of educational objectives (Anderson et al., 2001), the cognitive process dimensions follow a hierarchy from simple to complex: (1) remembering, (2) understanding, (3) applying, (4) analysing, (5) evaluating, and (6) creating. In the present study, the super users had to apply knowledge learnt in training to novel situations. When applying this knowledge, they had to navigate through the layers of functions and commands of a highly sophisticated, integrated system in order to complete a particular transaction. The pathway for the transaction further depended upon the status of the object (e.g., inpatient vs outpatient) of that transaction. In this dynamic environment, the user had to analyse and evaluate a multitude of available functions and select an appropriate pathway. Essentially, each new situation necessitated the creation of a new pathway. In short, identifying proper pathways involved higher-order thinking. This intensive processing should lend itself to more learning and therefore promote long-term retention (Farr, 1987), but the limited amount of training in the present study precluded this occurrence. Rapid learning is indeed associated with rapid forgetting (Jaber & Kher, 2004).

The learning and forgetting phenomena described above apply to situations where IS training involves (1) the transfer of complex knowledge, and (2) limited resources to educate large numbers of users throughout an organisation, resulting in a staggered schedule of relatively short training sessions. The following hypothesis was formulated accordingly.

## Hypothesis 1 The length of the interruption interval between the end of training and system rollout has a negative effect on performance.

Nevertheless, time gaps between training and actual implementation may not have a uniform effect across all trainees. This argument is grounded in memory research showing the resilience of an accurate mental model to the passage of time (Berry, 1988).

## Mental models and interruption intervals

Human beings develop mental representations of the world, of themselves, of their capabilities, and of the tasks they need to perform ( Johnson-Laird, 1983; Norman, 1983). Their interactions with the environment, with others, and with the artefacts of technology shape such mental conceptualisations or models. Through interaction with a target system, that is, the system being learnt or used, individuals formulate mental models of that system. In the early stages, learners will establish a link between the structure of the IS and its function. Such initial working models include many implicit assumptions (correct and/or incorrect) and are often incomplete. As learning progresses via subsequent interactions with the system, assumptions can be articulated or made explicit. This knowledge acquisition will lead to mental model modification and enhancement of its robustness (de Kleer & Brown, 1983; Norman, 1983). In novel situations, relevant mental models will be activated to fill in knowledge gaps (Winter et al., 2003).

Ausubel (1968) suggested the use of appropriate external aids or conceptual models of the target system (Mayer, 1983) to lay the foundation for knowledge construction. Training affects knowledge representation, which, in turn, affects performance. This is why mental models can be considered intermediate factors in the relationship between training and performance (Sauer et al., 2000). IS research supports the impact of an accurate mental model on user performance in terms of efficiency (Staggers & Norcio, 1993) and proficiency (Borgman, 1986; Sein & Bostrom, 1989; Santhanam & Sein, 1994). Users’ conceptual representations of a system empower them to predict system states following their actions and, consequently, guide their interactions with the system (Moran, 1981; Young, 1983; Carroll et al., 1987).

There is no adequate, independent measurement of mental models (Frese et al., 1988). The only observable manifestation of an accurate mental model is performance (e.g., Santhanam & Sein, 1994). One approach involves assessing users’ proficiency in terms of accuracy and speed when completing a series of hands-on tasks on the target system. Consistent with prior research, this study operationalised proficiency with multiple indicators including speed, scores on near-transfer tasks, and scores on far-transfer tasks. The relationships among these performance indicators are complex (Satzinger & Olfman, 1998). For example, a user may accomplish tasks at a fast pace, but at the expense of accuracy. Moreover, accuracy when performing a simple task cannot be equated with accuracy when performing complex tasks. If the user has developed an adequate mental model, better performance should occur qualitatively, that is, in creative, far-transfer tasks (Mayer, 1981; Sein & Bostrom, 1989). When asked to perform far-transfer tasks, users have to complete a relatively complex sequence of commands that cannot be found directly in a training manual (examples of such tasks are provided in the Method section). Rather, users will have to adapt information received during training or through interaction with the system to a changing situation and/or environment. On the contrary, near-transfer tasks are very similar to those tasks performed during regular training sessions (Weimin, 2001). They require using a single command or a simple sequence of two commands. Prior research on mental models has established that superior performance on far-transfer tasks is a better indicator of an accurate mental model than is performance on near-transfer tasks (Mayer, 1981; Bayman & Mayer, 1984; Borgman, 1986; Sein & Bostrom, 1989). In summary, individuals with more accurate mental models, as evidenced by higher performance on far-transfer tasks in particular, have a better comprehension of the system and are likely to exhibit sustainable, superior performance as they integrate new knowledge in a logical fashion. These arguments laid the foundation for the following hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 2 Accurate mental models of a target system result in better performance on the field.

A higher degree of comprehension largely accounts for the ability to recall information (Thorndyke, 1977), and strong correlations between recall and scores on knowledge tests support this claim (Berry & Clifford, 1987). Using the ‘suitcase metaphor,’ Towse et al. (2005) explain that the amount that can be stored (remembered) depends on packing efficiency and organisation of its contents. In other words, more IS knowledge can be stored in memory if it is logically organised. During interruption intervals, individuals focus on other mental operations, and the degradation of memory representation ensues (e.g., Peterson & Peterson, 1959). However, sound mental models have insulating properties that protect against time-induced memory seepage (Towse et al., 2005). The implication is that individuals with accurate mental models of a system are less susceptible to a decline of their knowledge base over time.

Taken together, these arguments indicate that accurate mental models of a system not only predict superior performance, but they also act as buffers against the passage of time. Long time spans between training and system implementation have adverse effects on performance, but the intensity of these effects might be modulated by the quality of one’s mental model, thereby suggesting interactive mechanisms. This reasoning led to the development of the next hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3 A mental model and length of interruption interval interact to predict performance, with the passage of time having a lesser effect on field performance when a mental model is more accurate.

## Learning styles/modes

Learning styles evoke a person’s preferred way of learning (Anis et al., 2004). Among the numerous frameworks of learning processes, Kolb’s (1984) model of experiential learning is one of the most widely used (Sadler-Smith, 1996). In Kolb’s model, cognitive growth is modeled along two orthogonal axes representing concrete-abstract and active-reflective continua, generating four learning abilities or learning modes: (1) concrete experience (Lance et al., 1998), that is, learning from feeling and from specific experiences; (2) reflective observation (RO), that is, learning by watching and listening, looking for the meaning of things, and viewing issues from different angles; (3) abstract conceptualisation (AC), that is, learning by logically analysing ideas and planning systematically; and (4) active experimentation (AE), that is, learning by doing and taking risks (Figure 2). An individual’s personal goals and objectives drive his/her choice of learning mode. In the Learning Styles Inventory (LSI) (Kolb, 1999), the scores in the four learning modes are combined to generate one of four learning styles that best describes an individual. These learning styles are (1) accommodator, (2) diverger, (3) converger, and (4) assimilator.

According to Kolb (1976, 1984, 1999), accommodators combine concrete experience and AE. They prefer to learn from ‘hands-on’ experience. They also tend to act on ‘gut’ feelings rather than on logical analysis and to rely on other people’s judgments in problem solving. In that sense, they are both adaptable and practical. They are good leaders capable of getting things done. Divergers combine concrete experience and RO. They approach concrete situations from different angles. They are imaginative, recognise problems, like to brainstorm, and are open-minded. They are good at generating a wide range of ideas. Convergers combine AC and AE. They have the capability of finding practical uses for ideas and theories. They are good problem solvers and prefer to deal with technical rather than interpersonal issues. They excel in defining problems and reason deductively in order to make decisions. Assimilators combine AC and RO. They are good at processing diverse sources of information and organising them into a concise, logical form. They are especially interested in abstract ideas and concepts and excel at creating models and developing theories.

There is a strong argument in the literature for designing teaching materials congruent with learning styles and/or modes (e.g., Mumford, 1994). Lectures may be a preferred method of learning for assimilators (Gardner & Korth, 1998), but they do not seem helpful to active experimenters (Rezler & Rezmovic, 1981). Both assimilators (Gardner & Korth, 1998) and convergers (Loo, 2004) show a preference for group work. Sein & Bostrom (1989) indicated that abstract conceptualisers’ learning was improved through the use of training materials involving abstract models (i.e., schematic diagrams) of an IS, whereas concrete learners benefited from analogical models (i.e., concrete entities related to an individual’s prior experience). Similarly, Bostrom et al. (1990) demonstrated that abstract models enhanced both abstract and active learners’ performance. Despite the above findings, Loo (2004) stated that the empirical evidence for a linkage between learning styles and learning preferences was rather tenuous. He argued that the statistically significant effects reported in the literature often revealed weak relationships between learning styles and learning preferences and wondered whether some of these effects were due to Type I error. Consequently, he advocated the use of a variety of learning methods rather than seeking customisation. This approach was followed in the present study.

Learning styles have also been associated with performance. In her study of factory workers, Zuboff (1988) found that operators who possessed intellective skills developed a better understanding of the computerised control system than did other operators. She identified abstract thinking as one of the central elements of those skills. Harrison & Rainer (1992) also reported that individuals exhibiting greater creativity demonstrated higher computer skills, perhaps because they were more open to new experiences. In an experimental study testing the effects of training procedures and learning styles on performance, Frese et al. (1988) concluded that ‘novices who [y] are active and exploring when learning a new computer system achieve better results’ because they develop a more coherent mental model of the system, irrespective of the training method. In an experiment involving novice users of an electronic mail filing system, Sein & Bostrom (1989) found that abstract learners (using Kolb’s operationalisation) performed better than concrete learners for near-transfer tasks but, more important, observed a strong significant effect for far-transfer tasks. As previously mentioned, better performance in far-transfer tasks is indicative of a more accurate mental model of a system. Bostrom et al. (1990) further explored the impacts of learning mode on mental model accuracy and concluded that, in general, abstract learners performed better than concrete learners and that, in most cases, active experimenters somewhat outperformed reflective learners. The explanation was that abstract learners were better equipped to decode and understand the underlying rules and structures of a computer system. Concrete learners, on the other hand, relied on prior experiences, which may be irrelevant when learning a new computer system. Since learning how to use a computer system can best be accomplished through actual use and experimentation (Carroll & Mack, 1984; Brown & Newman, 1985), active experimenters also had an advantage over reflective observers.

To facilitate comparisons with the IS-related research described in the above paragraph, learning modes rather than learning styles were the cognitive variable of choice in the present study. The literature indicated that abstract learners and active experimenters tended to develop more accurate mental models of an IS. The following hypothesis was thus formulated:

Hypothesis 4 Learning modes have an impact on the construction of an accurate mental model, with abstract learners and active experimenters outperforming concrete learners and reflective observers, respectively.

The relationships described above provide the foundation for a corollary hypothesis. Through the development of better mental models of the system, active experimenters and abstract conceptualisers are well positioned to display superior field performance despite the passage of time. In other words, an accurate mental model of the system is likely to act as a mediator of the relationship between learning modes and field performance. With its ability to better withstand the passage of time, the quality of the mental models developed by active experimenters and abstract conceptualisers would make them ideal candidates for early training. On the other hand, trainees favouring concrete experiences could not benefit from domain familiarity (Wells et al., 2005) when learning a brand new system, and reflective observers would find the opportunities for observation and inquiry quite restrictive in short training sessions. Under these circumstances, these individuals would be at a disadvantage to develop an accurate mental model, thereby increasing their proclivity to forgetting. Scheduling the training of these super users as close as possible to system implementation would therefore be desirable. The essence of these arguments is captured in the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 5 An accurate mental model mediates the relationship between learning modes and performance on the field.

## Method

## Sample

The organisation implemented a GUI-based CIS including laboratory, radiology, and pharmacy modules, and an ordering and browsing system for nursing. This client– server system replaced a mainframe-based, legacy system. Three educators were responsible to train approximately 1,800 users from 94 units. Approximately 300 users (super users) received more extensive training and served as ‘local’ help desks or experts (Armstrong, 1992; Bondarouk, 2006) to other workers (general users) in their unit during the first 2 months following system implementation. As indicated previously, the super users make up the sample of this study. Super users received about 8 hours of training, compared to a little over 2 hours for the general users. Since patient care responsibilities did not permit the simultaneous training of all users in a unit, the training schedule was staggered. Upon system implementation, these super users would provide technical support for users in their unit. They would also show the general users how to solve problems and thus further train them on the system (Potts, 1997). If unable to solve a given problem, super users would call the central help desk that would either assist them or transfer them to the proper support channel, for example the IS project team.

Super users were selected for each unit in each shift. Because the educational staff did not know of any special criteria to select the super users, selection was based on judgment rather than a scientific process. For example, the educators’ first choice was often either nurse managers or charge nurses (nurses who will be ‘in charge’ of the unit if nurse managers are absent) believed to be the most computer literate and identified as willing to champion the conversion project in their units. Other selection criteria such as empathy and ability to explain what they learnt might have proven useful in the selection process, but it is unclear whether the educators knew the users well enough to adopt these attributes. The number of super users in each unit depended upon the size of the unit. The goal was to have one super user responsible for about five general users in order to provide adequate support when it was needed.

Of the 243 super users whose responses were usable, 25 were males and 218 were females. This distribution was similar to that of the hospital nurses in general (approximately 10% males and 90% females), indicating that there was no apparent gender bias in the selection process. The participants’ ages ranged from 20 to 65 years, with an average of 40.94 years (s.d.: 9.15 years). Sixteen percent of the participants had completed high school, 42% of them had some college, 28% had an undergraduate degree, and 14% had attended graduate school (mean: 14.93 years, s.d.: 2.016 years).

## Measurement

Prior experience with Windows-based systems The users had no prior experience with the new CIS. However, it was expected that prior knowledge of the Windows operating system would facilitate their learning process and speed up the development of their mental model of the new, graphical-based system. Therefore, this variable was treated as a covariate in the analyses. Respondents were asked to rate their proficiency with the Windows graphical interface on a 5-point scale (1: not proficient at all; 5: very proficient).

Learning modes Several modes of training were used: Intranet presentations, instructor-led lectures, hands-on sessions, handouts, and open lab practice sessions. The handouts and lectures featured descriptions of the new system’s capabilities and both analogical (references to old system) and abstract (schemas of the new system) content. The presentations abounded with screen captures of the system. The training aids had not been specifically tailored to each individual, but their diversity was intended to meet a range of learning preferences. At the end of the last training session, the super users were asked to fill out the third version of Kolb’s (1999) LSI. First published in 1976, Kolb’s LSI measures a person’s learning mode and style. To overcome criticism regarding internal consistency of the items in the first version and test–retest reliability in the second version (e.g., Ruble & Stout, 1993), a third version of the instrument was developed (Kolb, 1999). An additional precaution taken in the present study was the measurement of learning mode rather than learning style as cognitive variable. The internal consistency reliability coefficients for the two learning mode dimensions tapped by the combination scales of AC–CE and AE–RO far exceed those of the four learning styles (Sewall, 1986). Kolb’s (1999) LSI includes 12 groups of four statements that respondents have to rank (1: least; 4: best) in order to describe how they learn best. Based on their combined AC–CE scores, super users were classified as ‘abstract learners’ (1) or ‘concrete learners’ (0). The same procedure was performed on the AE–RO scores to label the respondents as ‘active experimenters’ (1) or ‘reflective observers’ (0). The instrument had been pre-tested with members of the educational staff as well as general users. Some general users faced problems answering Kolb’s LSI. Additional instructions were provided on the instrument, and the educational staff was instructed to assist respondents if they had questions. Despite these measures, only 243 LSIs collected from the super users were usable. This corresponded to an 81.3% usable response rate. There were no significant differences in terms of demographic charac teristics between those who answered Kolb’s LSI correctly and those who did not. The sample was almost perfectly divided between active experimenters (n ¼ 121) and reflective observers (n ¼ 122), and between abstract learners (n ¼ 121) and concrete learners (n ¼ 122).

Accuracy of mental model At their last training session, the trainees took an on-line exam in the training classroom. The exam, administered and proctored by the educational staff, contained 35 questions. This test was used for two purposes: (1) credentialing medical personnel to use the new system (hospital’s objective), and (2) assessing the accuracy of the novice user’s mental model of the system (researchers’ objective). This test asked the participants to perform a variety of tasks. Some tasks involved basic procedures: ‘Where do you click if you want to use PCT and want to back out without signing?,’ whereas others required the participant to have a logical representation of the system and answer questions not covered specifically during training: ‘How would you order an Early AM or Routine AM on May 1 for the patient below?’ The former were classified as neartransfer tasks and the latter as far-transfer tasks. There were a total of 13 far-transfer tasks and 22 near-transfer tasks in the exam. The online grading system reported correctness or error for each question as well as the time it took an individual to take the test. This time is similar to the time necessary to complete tasks, an outcome measure often used in software training research to measure the accuracy of a mental model with longer times indicating indecisiveness (Bostrom et al., 1990; Satzinger & Olfman, 1998). Following industry norms, all users had to obtain a passing score in order to be certified to use the system and obtain a user account. Since this was a crash conversion, they had to obtain certification before the go-live date.

Time elapsed since training The time interval (in days) between the exam date and the system rollout date measured time since training. The first exam was administered on April 5 and the last one on May 3. Due to unforeseen technical problems, the system was implemented on June 2 rather than the originally planned mid-May launch date, and all super users were expected to be present. This means that a period of 2 months had elapsed between the first round of training and actual system implementation.

Super-user performance The typical measures of IS success such as extent of application use, amount of system use, and adoption were not relevant in this situation. This was an enterprise system whose implementation was imposed onto the users. Users had no other alternative but to use the system to provide patient care. Since super users were responsible for providing support to general users experiencing problems, they essentially operated as a first-tier ‘help desk.’ When unable to provide adequate support, they had to refer user problems to a higher tier of support, that is, the central help desk. This referral system and the use of call statistics are common in the information technology support literature (Armstrong, 1992; Boronico & Zirkler, 1996; Marcella & Middleton, 1996; Huang, 2002). In fact, performance has been operationalised as ‘number of referrals to a higher tier’ even in computer-based manufacturing (Pal & Saleh, 1993). Consequently, the number of calls placed by a super user to the central help desk over a period of 2 months after system implementation was used as a measure of super-user performance on the field. Based on his experience with implementations at other hospitals, the project manager from the software company’s consulting team decided that it would take about 2 months for users to become fully proficient and less reliant on the support infrastructure. Therefore, the super users’ commitment was limited to a period of 2 months.

All calls to the central help desk were logged, and information regarding the caller’s identity, the date and time when the call was placed, and the type of problem experienced were recorded. Calls pertaining to hardware and operating system problems as opposed to application problems were not of interest in this study and were not included in the analysis. General users had been instructed to seek help from their unit’s super user(s) when experiencing a problem since only the super users had received more extensive training. There were several concerns about this objective measure. First, a super user in a given unit might be unavailable for consultation because he/she was involved in other activities. In that case, a general user would have to call the help desk. The call logs revealed that less than 10% of the total number of calls had been initiated by general users; these calls were not included in the analyses since they did not measure the super-user’s performance per se. Second, some super users might have been unable to properly define the problem to the help desk consultant. As a result, application problems could have been logged as other system problems (operating system, network, etc.) and referred to the project team. For example, a user may have reported that the patient care system ‘was down,’ when in fact the user had too many active windows at a given time. To minimise the chances of this problem occurring, the researchers asked one of the project team members to help diagnose some of the problems logged, and call patterns were tracked on the logs. In reference to the above example, if the system had actually been down, a series of similar complaints would have been made at that particular time. It was therefore rather easy to discriminate between system and application problems. Finally, it is possible that general users might have sought the help of the closest co-worker before contacting the super user even though this process had been discouraged. There was no way to control for this occurrence since the system was used by approximately fifteen hundred general users throughout the hospital. However, if it did happen, it was not likely to affect the assessment of super users’ performance for several reasons. First, general users’ problems resolved by other general users could only be very simple since general users had been trained for only two hours on the system. Therefore, if faced with these simple problems, the super users, with their substantially higher level of training, would not have referred them to a higher tier of support, that is, the central help desk. Second, the process of seeking other general users’ help would be homogeneous across units, thereby affecting all super users equally. The only threat to this assumption would be the presence of more ‘computer-savvy’ general users in some units. The data do not support this phenomenon. Although the study only involved super users, we had access to the general users’ test scores with no identification. These scores followed the normal distribution, indicating that very high scores in the general users’ population were, by definition, rare (Wright & McMahan, 1992; Wright et al., 1994) and leading us to believe that the ‘burden’ placed on super users was uniform. Despite its imperfections, this outcome measure was the best indicator of field performance that could be obtained. After discussing with hospital staff the possibility of collecting data on general users’ satisfaction with their respective superusers’ performance, it became obvious that the process of seeking the cooperation of approximately fifteen hundred individuals under hectic conditions might be overwhelming and cost-prohibitive. The call data were readily available via the call centre tracking system. This convenient and unobtrusive means of data collection appealed to the organisation. The use of super user calls as a performance measure was thus totally driven by organisational practices and concerns. Moreover, the researchers’ lack of control over instrument administration at that point raised the possibility that respondents would not provide candid answers, fearing breaches of confidentiality if hospital staff was in charge of further data collection.

## Results

The purpose of this study was to determine: (1) whether accurate mental models were resilient to the passage of time, and if so, (2) whether learning modes shape the development of an accurate mental model of a complex, integrated system. Descriptive statistics and zero-order correlations are presented in Table 1. The average time elapsed between end of training and actual system implementation was 44.3 days. The average number of calls made by a super user to the central help desk during the 2-month period was rather low, ranging from 0 to 21 calls. Three variables were not normally distributed: scores on far-transfer tasks, scores on near-transfer tasks, and time since training. Scores on near and far-transfer tasks were skewed to the left. This was expected since super users had received more substantial training and therefore tended to obtain higher scores. The data transformation protocols outlined in Hair et al. (1998) and Cohen et al. (2003) were followed. Inspections of q–q plots and histograms revealed that the natural logarithm transformation was the most appropriate for these two variables. These transformed variables were subsequently used in the analyses. However, the distribution of time since training could not be improved. Records showed that training did not take place for 1 week, resulting in a gap in the distribution. Because a departure from normality is more problematic with small samples (Cohen et al., 2003), we had confidence in the robustness of the technique, and the original variable was therefore retained.

The pattern of correlations shows overall support for Hypotheses 1, 2, and 4. The time elapsed since training had a significant, positive correlation with the number of calls made to the help desk. Higher scores on near and far-transfer tasks and test-taking speed were associated with fewer calls after system implementation. Active experimenters scored higher on near-transfer and far-transfer tasks, but there was apparently no relationship between the CE-AC mode and the test performance measures.

The first three hypotheses were tested using the approach suggested by Baron & Kenny (1986), Aiken & West (1991), and Cohen et al. (2003). The number of calls made to the central help desk was regressed on the covariate, the independent variables (i.e., learning mode and time since training), the potential mediators (measures of mental model accuracy), and the hypothesised interactions in a hierarchical fashion (Table 2). When testing for interactions, it is desirable that the predictors and moderator be uncorrelated (Baron & Kenny, 1986). Since time since training and the three measures of mental model accuracy were significantly correlated, all four measures were centered (Cohen et al., 2003). This procedure eliminates non-essential multicollinearity. The model was significant, explaining 62% of the variance. In evaluating the estimated equation, we checked the normality of the error term of the variate. Normal probability plots of the residuals revealed that the assumption of normality had been met.

In support of Hypothesis 1, the time elapsed between the end of the training programme and system rollout had a significant impact on the number of calls made to the central help desk (Table 2, Model 2). As predicted, individuals trained earlier in the programme were more dependent on the central help desk’s assistance. The fourth hypothesis postulated that active experimenters and abstract conceptualisers would develop better mental models of a system, as indicated by higher test scores and test taking speed. After controlling for prior experience, the RO–AE mode significantly influenced performance on far-transfer tasks (7.2% of the variance explained). In both cases, the relationship was positive, indicating that active experimenters obtained higher scores than did reflective observers. Contrary to expectations, abstract conceptualisers (CE–AC mode) did not outperform concrete learners on any of the mental model measures. Neither learning mode was a significant predictor of speed. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was only partially supported.

To test whether an accurate mental model mediated the relationship between learning modes and actual performance on the field (Hypothesis 5), the following three conditions had to be satisfied (Baron & Kenny, 1986):

1. The mediators (measures of mental model accuracy) are regressed on the independent variables (learning modes). The independent variables must have significant effects on the mediators. As indicated previously, the RO–AE mode had a modest impact on neartransfer task proficiency, but a strong one on fartransfer task proficiency (Hypothesis 4).

2. The dependent variable (calls) is regressed on the independent variable. The independent variable must have a significant effect on the dependent variable. ## Discussion and implications

The results supported the MMR research model to a large extent. Consistent with the learning–forgetting literature, the timing of training emerges as an important predictor of performance. Not surprisingly, performance worsens as the time elapsed between training and system rollout increases. In accordance with learning and forgetting theory (Anderson et al., 1999; Nembhard, 2000; Jaber & Kher, 2002; Nembhard & Osothsilp, 2002; Jaber & Kher, 2004; McCreery et al., 2004), interruptions in the work/learning cycle trigger a forgetting effect. Since training resources are likely to serve larger numbers of users sequentially rather than simultaneously, this effect will persist unless trainees have formed accurate mental models of the system.

The predominance of experiments in prior research (Sein & Bostrom, 1989; Bostrom et al., 1990; Davis & Davis, 1990; Santhanam & Sein, 1994; Satzinger & Olfman, 1998) has resulted in measures of mental models used as proxy variables for performance on the job. In other words, scores on near- and far-transfer tasks and time needed to accomplish tasks have usually been the outcome variables. This study confirms their roles as predictors of performance on the field (e.g., Sauer et al., 2000). Since the testing instrument included tasks that a trainee would be expected to perform in a live environment, super users who had mastered these tasks on the test were better able to use the application and assume their support function on the field. More important, the significant interaction between score on far-transfer tasks and time elapsed since training indicated that better performance was sustained over the long run even in the absence of practice between system training and system use, thereby supporting the insulating properties of mental models (e.g., Towse et al., 2005). The ability of far-transfer tasks aptitude to counteract time-related knowledge decay underscores its status as a superior indicator of mental model accuracy (Mayer, 1981; Bayman & Mayer, 1984; Borgman, 1986; Sein & Bostrom, 1989). Since the system had somewhat evolved (refinements) between training and final implementation in the facility where the study was conducted, a solid grasp on the system’s functions and their interrelationships was more critical than knowledge of simple commands.

It was thus no surprise that performance in far-transfer tasks partially mediated the relationship between the RO–AE mode and performance on the field. In other words, active experimenters tended to be more proficient users and problem solvers, and some of that proficiency was channeled through a superior performance on fartransfer tasks. Performance on near-transfer tasks was also a mediator but to a lesser extent. Taken together, these results substantiate that mental models act as intervening mechanisms between the learning experience and performance (Sauer et al., 2000). Learning modes shape this learning experience.

Corroborating prior experimental research, the results confirmed that AE had a significant impact on the acquisition of a more accurate mental model. Mental models are largely derived from empirical experience or hands-on interactions (Payne, 1991), which makes active experimenters especially well equipped to learn a new computer system. Since hands-on practice is a requirement for effective learning in this field, active experimenters’ proclivity to learning by doing works to their advantage, whereas the reflective observers’ more careful and detached approach may impede their progress. The large number of general users to be trained precluded the training programme from exceeding 8 hours for super users. This short time frame favoured individuals who were willing to take the risk of making mistakes and quickly start interacting with the system. These individuals had more opportunities to explore multiple facets of the system and their interrelationships. On the other hand, those who preferred to wait and gain a deeper understanding of the system before trying it out may have been hindered by the time limitations and, consequently, could not develop as coherent a model of the system in a relatively short time frame. Knowing that these individuals are probably at greater risk for timeinduced knowledge decay, trainers should schedule their training accordingly.

Contrary to expectations, the influence of AC on accurate mental model development was not significant. In prior experimental research, abstract thinkers’ ‘superiority’ had been established in conjunction with the use of abstract models as training aids (Bostrom et al., 1988, 1990; Sein & Bostrom, 1989). Our field study did not permit to tailor training programmes to specific users. As is common in practice (Sadler-Smith, 1996), a ‘mixed bag’ approach consisting of Intranet presentations, instructorled lectures, handouts, and on-hands lab sessions was used in the present training programme. It is possible that this training programme was deficient in the use of effective, abstract conceptual models, thereby handicapping abstract learners. When asked to evaluate the usefulness of the various teaching media, abstract thinkers’ ratings of the teaching media were consistently lower than those of the concrete learners. The difference was significant for lectures and approaching significance for handouts. Since lectures and handouts were the two main vehicles through which abstract, conceptual information was disseminated, the results lead us to question their content in terms of its ability to meet abstract thinkers’ needs.

Another reason may be the nature of the application itself. Although we previously postulated that concrete learners could not pursue their preferred strategy of learning by analogy since the target system shared very little resemblance with the old one, we could have overestimated its degree of newness. The new system was different in terms of interface (graphical vs text), the quantity and quality of information available, the time span of historical data, the establishment of standardised mnemonics for ordering tests, etc. Nevertheless, both the legacy and target client–server systems were patient care management systems and therefore shared common principles. This similarity may have leveled the field between abstract and concrete learners.

In summary, the results of this study cement the importance of effective training to build sound, coherent, and resilient mental models of the system. Prior research has shown that providing training aids meeting a diversity of learning needs ensures the quality of individual knowledge bases. Our findings indicate that proper scheduling of training can slow down their collective erosion.

Like any study, this one also has its limitations. The results could be company-specific since the study was conducted in a single organisation. Field performance was measured by the number of calls, that is, number of problem referrals, to the help desk. As mentioned earlier, this performance measure may have questionable psychometric properties. Its validity rests on the assumptions that all calls were logged properly. Its reliability is uncertain since we could not collect multiple measures of field performance. The study focused on super users and therefore user support functions. Its generalisation to other users cannot be ascertained unequivocally. The super users’ more intensive training must have shaped the development of a better mental model. Users receiving less training may thus not attain a level of comprehension that protects their knowledge base against the effects of long interruption intervals. In their case, the timing of training could be even more crucial. The fact that most hypotheses with strong theoretical underpinnings were supported somewhat alleviates the above concerns, but the legitimacy of the MMR model ultimately depends on a replication of the study and its findings in a different organisation(s), with other types of users, and with a more robust performance measure.

The study’s model omitted other important characteristics that influence learning and retention. Prior knowledge, aptitude, self-efficacy, training expectations, duration of training programme, and job characteristics also shape learning and retention. Including these variables in the model would provide a much richer and complete perspective on mental model construction and retention of knowledge and would render justice to the complexity of the learning phenomenon. We therefore recommend an interdisciplinary perspective focusing on multiple dimensions. Collaboration across disciplines is indeed necessary to adopt a comprehensive view of IS training rather than following the typical piecemeal approach. Such an undertaking would constitute a leap forward in meaningful theory development. It would also attract research sponsors frustrated by the proliferation of eclectic training solutions emerging from various academic disciplines.

## Conclusions

The research presented in this paper examined the impact of mental model accuracy on field performance both as a moderator and a mediator. It also analysed the role of learning modes as potential criteria to schedule training sessions when there is a time lag between training and system implementation. In doing so, the present study ventured into unexplored territory and provided very valuable insights for IS trainers.

Implementing enterprise-wide IS is replete with risks. Building and maintaining a solid knowledge base greatly influence the success of such implementations. To this end, one approach focuses on the design of training programmes facilitating the acquisition of decay-resistant mental models. Another approach involves the scheduling of training according to abilities. A final approach addresses overlearning and its timing prior to system implementation. The concern for retention undergirds all three methods.

Research has shown that the development of training aids matching individual characteristics promotes the acquisition of accurate mental models (e.g., Frese et al., 1988; Sein & Bostrom, 1989; Bostrom et al., 1990; Davis & Davis, 1990). Accordingly, a variety of training aids were used to accommodate users’ diverse learning needs in the present study. Nevertheless, performance disparities among individuals with different learning styles emerged. With no manipulation of the training aids’ format, the implications of the above findings would be purely conjectural. More useful is the corroboration of previous claims that performance on far-transfer tasks is a strong indicator of superior comprehension. Therefore, we recommend that training programmes and testing emphasise the ability to perform far-transfer tasks. In the present case, only 13 of the 35 exam questions referred to far-transfer tasks. This could be due to training programme deficiencies in far-transfer content or to the appeal of easier design of online test items involving near-transfer tasks. Both shortcuts result in elusive cost savings. Training and test design costs are aimed at achieving good quality through prevention and appraisal. Conversely, the costs associated with failure on the field (due to insufficient training or inadequate assessment) can be more substantial since they directly affect response time and customer service (Russell & Taylor, 2006). In summary, trainers should prepare users for changing situations and environments. This recommendation is hardly a pedagogical breakthrough, but the popularity of easier, ‘feel good’ educational programmes and assessment tools suggests that reinforcing its importance is still very much relevant.

In an ideal situation, employees should be trained just before system rollout. Unfortunately, in large enterprisewide system rollout scenarios, training sessions start months before the anticipated ‘go-live’ date to cover a large number of users’ educational needs. IS practitioners know that the go-live date is usually a moving target, especially in a crash conversion situation. With multiple unforeseen circumstances, the anticipated go-live date is usually postponed, and novices have ample opportunities to forget what they have just learnt. With novices trained on company time, training is extremely costly. It is therefore imperative that training schedules be designed to minimise the forgetting effect. When using an instrument as simple as Kolb’s (1999) LSI, organisations can pre-select active experimenters for the first rounds of training since they seem to forget at a lesser rate. In fact, this pre-selection process may also prove valuable in identifying good internal beta testers of the system.

This process may be appropriate for super users who are by definition pre-selected. However, it may not be practical or even feasible for all general users in an organisation. This is especially relevant in organisations where unions have considerable bargaining power. Fortunately, there are other means of maintaining know ledge integrity after training. Hurlock & Montague (1982) insisted that ‘yany variable that leads to high initial levels of learning, such as high ability or frequent practice, will facilitate skill retention’ (p. 5). Learning modes are associated with the notion of high ability. Frequent practice, on the other hand, evokes overlearning. Overlearning is a popular and proven method of strengthening learning (Farr, 1987) and enhancing the quality of working mental models (de Kleer & Brown, 1983; Norman, 1983). It is usually expressed in terms of extra practice or trials beyond mastery. Its implementation would involve longer training sessions (especially if far-transfer tasks are emphasised) and/or refresher training sessions before system implementation. Incidentally, overlearning presents additional scheduling challenges and possible trade-offs. With no increase in training resources, longer training sessions will produce even more substantial interruption intervals for the first groups of trainees. As for refresher training sessions, their timing also seems to affect their effectiveness. How often should they be offered? Research suggests that longer intervals between refresher sessions yield the best results. In an experiment designed to test the effects of spaced learning trials, Bahrick (1979) found that the interval between refresher training sessions should be commensurate with the time elapsed between the last refresher session and the measurement of retention. Although his experiment involved perfect spacing of sessions to a single group, the implications for our field study are important. The time elapsed between the last round of training and system implementation was 30 days. The training staff could have offered short, refresher training sessions or re-administered the test during these remaining 30 days, starting with the first batch of trainees (who had completed their initial training 58 days before system implementation). This midway refresher would have constituted overlearning and promoted retention. In the hospital where the study was conducted, no refresher sessions were offered to the super users because the educators were still training the general users. However, the training system was available in the classrooms after classes. Interested employees could have maintained or even sharpened their skills by going to such classrooms after work. With access limited to certain blocks of time, it is not surprising that hardly any trainees took advantage of this opportunity in their free time. In retrospect, there could have been a conscientious effort to target at least the super users for refresher courses. Furthermore, this ‘review’ could have been conducted with larger groups of trainees, thereby minimising the demands on the training staff. There is no need for refresher courses to be a massive, costly undertaking. Even short sessions could be beneficial, but they should be scheduled on company time and spaced for minimum knowledge decay.

In short, IS trainers should emphasise the acquisition of knowledge and skills necessary for performing far-transfer tasks, use reliable methods for selecting highly capable super users, and encourage if not require super-user participation in well timed refresher sessions. A concern for limited resources underlies these recommendations since they focus more on training investment quality than on quantity.

Despite a general lack of transferability between laboratory findings and field research settings (Chapanis, 1967; Freedman et al., 1974; Kochan, 1980; Gordon et al., 1984), our results reaffirm the usefulness of learning modes as a cognitive variable in the IS training paradigm. The functionality of this variable is also expanded. Already established as critical factors in designing training methods, an individual’s learning modes now emerge as potential tools to schedule training.

The status of performance on far-transfer tasks is confirmed as the best indicator of mental model accuracy and therefore of performance on the field. More important, its contribution as a catalyst to neutralise the effects of interruption intervals in the learning cycle is uncovered. Consistent with memory research that stresses the importance of accurate mental models in influencing forgetting rates (Berry, 1988; Towse et al., 2005), the study shows that better comprehension fosters better retention even in the absence of practice.

By the same token, the applicability of research on learning and forgetting – most prominent in manufacturing and military environments – is extended to the field of IS. In demonstrating that interruptions in the learning cycle impact performance on an IS negatively, the study validates the existence of learning and forgetting phenomena across tasks and disciplines.
