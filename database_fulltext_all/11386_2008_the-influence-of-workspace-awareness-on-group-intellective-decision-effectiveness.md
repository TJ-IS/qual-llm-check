# The Influence of workspace awareness on group intellective decision effectiveness

Randolph B. Cooper<sup>1</sup>, Russell Haines<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Bauer College of Business Administration, University of Houston, Houston, TX, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Technology and Decision Sciences, College of Business and Public Administration, Old Dominion University, Norfolk, VA, U.S.A.

## Abstract

Workspace awareness is an understanding of members’ interactions within a shared workspace, and has been a fundamental concern to researchers examining how groups using group support systems can be more effective as they design and develop physical artifacts. We provide theoretical and empirical evidence that workspace awareness can be a significant factor influencing the effectiveness of groups employing group support systems for the development of intangible artifacts, such as decision quality and consensus. The workspace awareness literature typically does not examine causal relationships within awareness. We develop a theoretical model that divides workspace awareness into three elements (presence, behavior, and insight awareness), which are important for group decision tasks, and provide empirical evidence that: (1) understanding the reasons behind group member behaviors (insight awareness) is key to increasing decision quality and consensus; (2) greater insight awareness can be obtained when an individual is better able to track and characterize others’ behaviors (behavior awareness); and (3) behavior awareness depends on an individual’s ability to identify and distinguish among the different individuals within the group (presence awareness). Empirical support is derived from a laboratory experiment. European Journal of Information Systems (2008) 17, 631–648.

Keywords: workspace awareness; decision effectiveness; CSCW; collaboration

## Introduction

A shared workspace is a meeting area in which group members engage in collaborative activities (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002). Workspace awareness is an understanding of members’ interactions within a shared workspace. Maintaining such awareness is fundamental to the coordination of effort and sharing of information during collaboration (Dourish & Bellotti, 1992). Individuals collaborating in physical workspaces maintain this awareness through normal perceptual abilities, by seeing and hearing what actions they and other members are taking (Gutwin & Greenberg, 1996). However, group support systems (GSS) limit awareness in virtual workspaces by reducing individuals’ ‘visual fieldy to a computer screen, remov(ing) characteristic motions and sounds from actions, and complicat(ing) verbal and visual communication’ (Gutwin & Greenberg, 1996, p. 208).

There is therefore an increasing amount of research aimed at enhancing workspace awareness in GSS contexts. Most of this research has been applied to groups using GSS in the design and development of physical artifacts such as documents (e.g., Mendoza-Chapa et al., 2000; Diamadis & Polyzos, 2004), computer systems (e.g., Nutter & Boldyreff, 2003; Sarma et al., 2003), and other objects (e.g., Liu et al., 2001; Wu & De Silveira Jr.,

2002). However, recent work indicates the potential for workspace awareness to affect group learning (Romero-Salcedo et al., 2004), and suggests that workspace awareness may also be beneficial in group decision tasks (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002). Therefore, our research question concerns whether and how workspace awareness influences the effectiveness of groups employing GSS for decision tasks.

Although there are many different kinds of decision tasks, we focus on intellective tasks, which are those that have objectively correct answers (Laughlin, 1980; McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). This is reasonable because intellective tasks are ‘extremely common’ in management (Bonner et al., 2006, p. 618). In addition, the evaluation of group decision effectiveness by researchers is made easier because higher quality can be determined by how close decisions are to the correct decision (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). In order to determine group decision effectiveness, we are also concerned with consensus among group members regarding the decision (DeLone & McLean, 2003); this is important because consensus is a key factor in the implementation of group decisions (Castore & Murnighan, 1978; Donellon et al., 1986; Postmes & Lea, 2000).

The theoretical background and research model are described next, followed by the model’s test in a laboratory experiment. Implications for research and practice end the paper.

## Theoretical background

Awareness can influence the effectiveness of group tasks (Drury & Williams, 2002). Various authors have described different kinds of awareness, including activity awareness (knowledge of the past and present actions of group members (Steinfield et al., 1999)), availability awareness (knowledge of whether another is available for interaction (Steinfield et al., 1999)), concept/task awareness (knowledge of how tasks are to be completed (Gutwin et al., 1995)), conversational awareness (knowledge of who is communicating with whom (Vertegaal et al., 1997)), environmental awareness (knowledge of how events occurring outside of the immediate workspace have implications for group activity (Steinfield et al., 1999)), process awareness (knowledge of where particular activities fit into the overall process (Steinfield et al., 1999)), perspective awareness (knowledge of other members’ beliefs and knowledge in order to help understand their perspectives (Steinfield et al., 1999)), social awareness (knowledge of roles and social relationships (Gutwin et al., 1995)), and peripheral awareness (knowledge of the location of individuals relative to the artifact (Baecker et al., 1993)). Workspace awareness combines and adds to elements of activity, availability, and perspective awareness in order to focus on knowledge of the interaction of individuals with each other and with the artifacts in their shared workspace (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002). Specific elements of workspace awareness that can be important in GSS contexts include up to the minute understandings of member presence (who is in the workspace), behavior (what they are doing and working on), and insight (what are the reasons for members’ behaviors) (Zander, 1982; Gutwin et al., 1995; Drury & Williams, 2002).

For example, with a group intellective task that uses a GSS in order to create a solution to a problem, the artifact is the solution, and workspace awareness would include being able to (1) identify the presence of different members (e.g., there are four members in the group), (2) track and characterize individual members’ behaviors (e.g., the argument of member ‘X’ is providing support for the solution under discussion), and (3) develop insight regarding why individual members are behaving in a particular way (e.g., member ‘Y’ appears to be confused regarding the solution under discussion). In subsequent discussions, we will refer to these elements as presence awareness, behavior awareness, and insight awareness, with the understanding that they are components of workspace awareness.

With face-to-face groups working on a physical object, presence, behavior, and insight awareness can be provided through consequential communication (communication among group members by seeing or hearing their activities during the task (Segal, 1994)), feedthrough (communication occurring when the changed state of an artifact provides information (Dix et al., 1993)), and intentional communication (communication occurring via conversation (Heath & Luff, 1992; Clark, 1996)). However, with groups using a GSS for decision tasks, workspace awareness is gained mainly from intentional communication, that is, from communication that is specifically directed from one individual to one or more others, and which may also be received by those not directly involved in the conversation.

Such communication and resulting presence, behavior, and insight awareness can help manage and coordinate activities, provide a context for each individual’s activities, provide opportunities for synergistic group behavior, and provide for fluid and more natural interaction among individuals (Dourish & Bellotti, 1992; Mendoza-Chapa et al., 2000; Ferscha, 2001).

## Technology and task

As noted earlier, GSS typically limit group members’ visual field to the text on their computer screen. Early GSS researchers believed that the loss of awareness resulting from such GSS could enhance group decision making by, for example, decreasing social influence and thereby increasing participation and leading to more creative solutions as well as more objective evaluations of those solutions (Connolly et al., 1990; Valacich et al., 1992, 1994; Shepherd et al., 1996). However, after many equivocal results (Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998), GSS researchers now suggest that participants can gain a significant degree of awareness of each other and of their group as a whole strictly via their messages (Chidambaram, 1996; Gopal & Prasad, 2000).

McGrath & Hollingshead (1994) divide group tasks into eight different categories that depend on the degree to which the tasks involve collaboration vs conflict resolution and the degree to which they are more cognitive vs behavioral. A review by Fjermestad & Hiltz (1998) found that 40% of GSS studies focused on decision-making tasks, which involve deciding issues that do not have right answers; 30% focused on creativity tasks, which involve the generation of ideas; and 24% focused on intellective tasks, which involve solving problems that have correct answers. As described earlier, we focus on intellective tasks because they are extremely common in management (Bonner et al., 2006), and, as noted by Fjermestad and Hiltz, have been the subject of many GSS studies.

Based on work by Campbell (1988), Zigurs & Buckland (1998) describe GSS tasks in terms of four complexity components: (1) outcome multiplicity, (2) solution scheme multiplicity, (3) conflicting interdependence, and (4) solution scheme/outcome uncertainty. The task upon which we focus can be categorized as not very complex because (1) there is only one desired outcome; (2) although there are multiple potential solution schemes (or paths), only two (described later) have the potential to result in the optimal outcome; (3) there is little conflicting interdependence among solution schemes in that, for example, one scheme may be replaced by another early on in the task without a significant penalty; and (4) given the intellective nature of the decision, there is little uncertainty regarding whether a proposed solution will lead to the appropriate outcome. Appropriate GSS support for this type of task focuses mainly on communication by enabling simultaneous input, anonymous input, input feedback, and group display (Zigurs & Buckland, 1998). As described in detail later, the GSS employed in this study provides these communication functionalities, and therefore is a good fit for the group task.

## Decision effectiveness

We focus on decision quality and consensus among decision makers as the key goals of collaborative decisionmaking (DeLone & McLean, 2003). We are specifically interested in decisions that provide the group with a strategy regarding how it has to perform a subsequent task. These decisions are intellective since, as described later, there are clearly better and worse strategies, with better strategies representing higher decision quality (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). Consensus is important for the implementation of those decisions, because consensus reflects members’ support for the decisions and their willingness to faithfully implement them (Castore & Murnighan, 1978; Donellon et al., 1986; Postmes & Lea, 2000). Decision effectiveness in terms of subsequent task performance therefore depends on both decision quality and consensus. And, as described in detail next, workspace awareness can play an important role in determining the degrees of decision quality and consensus.

## Research model

The following discussions extend prior research by examining how different elements of workspace awareness (that pertaining to member presence, behavior, and insight) are related to each other and to decision effectiveness (quality and consensus). For completeness, we also examine relationships between decision effectiveness and task performance. The model described below is illustrated in Figure 1.

## Decision effectiveness and task performance

Decision effectiveness depends on the quality of the decisions made (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). When deciding on a strategy for future action, and when such strategies are intellective, the closer one comes to the optimal decision (ceteris paribus), the better the decision is. One of the many factors associated with the ceteris paribus condition is consensus regarding the decision. This consensus indicates that members are in accord with the decisions made, and reflects members’ support for the decisions and their willingness to faithfully implement them (Castore & Murnighan, 1978; Donellon et al., 1986; Postmes & Lea, 2000). Therefore, with regard to intellective decision effectiveness, decision quality and consensus can interact (Huber, 1990). With low-quality decisions, the degree of consensus is superfluous; that is, with bad decisions, whether the decision gets faithfully implemented should not have a systematic effect on performance. In contrast, when decision quality is high, the degree to which it is faithfully implemented should positively affect task performance. Therefore, the level of consensus (and therefore the likelihood of faithful implementation) should have a positive impact on the ability of decision quality to affect task performance. This results in the following hypotheses:

## H1 Decision quality will increase task performance.

H2 Consensus with regard to the decision will positively influence the effect of decision quality on task performance.

## Insight awareness and decision quality

Insight awareness reflects an individual’s understanding of the reasons for other group members’ behaviors. Insight awareness has the potential to increase intellective task quality in the following ways:

\- Coupling. Coupling is the degree to which individuals work together rather than independently (Salvador et al., 1996). Individuals within groups have been observed to iterate opportunistically between working alone and working together (e.g., Gaver, 1991; Dourish & Bellotti, 1992; Salvador et al., 1996), where working together includes ‘discussion, instruction, action, information, or consultation with another member’ (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002, p. 426). In the context of decision tasks using GSS, coupling largely reflects the degree of member participation. Insight awareness can help individuals promote the participation of others. That is, understanding the reason why an individual is not participating would help other members determine the best way to encourage the individual’s participation. For example, if it is understood that an individual’s decreased participation was due to him/ her being uncomfortable because he/she holds a minority view, then other members could encourage his/her participation by indicating their interest in and acceptance of minority opinions.

\- Coordination. Coordination refers to the management of individuals’ behaviors. Throughout a task, group members implicitly reorganize their roles and activities, and the effectiveness of these reorganizations can depend on insight awareness (Robinson, 1992; Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002). For example, depending on an individual’s understanding of the current degree of other members’ agreement/disagreement with the solution under discussion, the individual may suggest a new solution, help others understand the current solution’s strengths and weaknesses, or summarize the current state of discussions.

\- Assistance. Assistance involves providing help and support to individuals, and awareness can facilitate the assistance process (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002). An important element of this awareness is an understanding of what is driving others’ behaviors, that is, insight awareness (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002).

For example, an individual could provide effective assistance to another member (e.g., by helping the member understand a solution currently under discussion) only if the individual understands the reasons for the other member’s behavior (e.g., that the other member’s comments are a result of that member being confused).

In general, the more those members understand the reasons behind others’ actions (i.e., insight awareness), the more effective their coupling, coordination, and assistance can be. And more effective coupling, coordination, and assistance can, in turn, increase decision quality. This increase in decision quality comes from the potential for more equal participation (DeSanctis & Gallupe, 1987; Rao & Jarvenpaa, 1991) along with an increase in members’ mutual understandings of the task and its potential solutions, which can provide for more effective synergy (Dourish & Bellotti, 1992; Dennis, 1996). This leads to the following hypothesis:

## H3 Insight awareness will increase decision quality.

## Insight awareness and consensus

Group consensus regarding a decision reflects members support for the decision and their willingness to follow it during subsequent task execution (Carmines & Zeller, 1979; McLeod, 1997; DeLone & McLean, 2003). Consensus indicates that members are in accord with the decision made, and can come from the fact that the decision adequately reflects members’ views (Huber, 1990; Falk & Miller, 1992). As described above, insight awareness can help group members provide appropriate feedback and assistance to other members. For example, when it becomes evident that a member does not understand a proposed solution, other members can provide feedback to that member in order to help him/ her understand. This increases members’ mutual understanding of the task and its potential solutions, and thereby members’ perceptions that the decision adequately reflects their views (Kahai & Cooper, 1999). Such perceptions, in turn, increase consensus regarding the decision (Kahai & Cooper, 1999). This leads to the following hypothesis:

H4 Insight awareness will increase group consensus with a decision.

## Relationships among workspace awareness elements: presence, behavior, and insight

Workspace awareness models usually describe the process of awareness at a relatively macro level. For example, based on Neisser’s (1976) perception–action cycle, Gutwin & Greenberg (2002) propose that workspace awareness is derived from a perceptual exploration within a situation. And that this awareness leads to action, which changes the situation, and thereby results in changed awareness. However, we are interested in a more micro view that can provide an understanding of the causal relationships among the different elements of workspace awareness. It would seem, for example, that information enabling one to identify the presence of individuals in the workspace is a necessary condition for tracking and characterizing the behavior associated with each individual. Further, it would seem reasonable that being able to track and characterize each individual’s behavior would help provide insight into the reasons behind that behavior (cf. Markus, 1994).

Research that provides such a micro view is attribution theory. Attribution theory is based in part on the notion that individuals search for order, meaning, and control by attempting to understand the causes of others’ behaviors (Mizerski et al., 1979; Harvey & Weary, 1984). Because individuals usually do not know why others behave the way they do (Kwon & Weingart, 2004), they are forced to infer others’ motivations from their actions (Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1967). These inferences are based on naı¨ve or implicit theories regarding causal relationships among people and their situations (Lord & Smith, 1983; Bazerman & Carroll, 1987).

Although there are many competing and complementary attribution theoretic models (Lord & Smith, 1983), the following multi-stage model is common in the attribution literature (e.g., Lord & Smith, 1983; Bassili & Racine, 1990; Erickson & Krull, 1999), and describes how a perceiver might arrive at judgments concerning the cause(s) of an individual’s behavior. The perceiver: (stage 1) determines what the individual is doing by interpreting and/or categorizing his/her behavior (e.g., Bob is communicating in an anxious manner); (stage 2) makes a dispositional judgment regarding traits that are implied by the behavior (e.g., Bob is an anxious person), that may be revised based on situational information (e.g., because Bob is waiting to see the dentist, perhaps he is not such an anxious person); and (stage 3) uses this revised dispositional judgment along with the situational information to form a judgment concerning the cause of behavior (e.g., Bob is anxious because he is waiting to see the dentist, whom he fears).

Support for such a model comes in a variety of forms. Stage 1 appears to be necessary for stage 2, with stage 2 occurring spontaneously or automatically at the end of, or concurrently with, stage 1 (Smith & Miller, 1983; Bassili, 1993; Carlston & Skowronski, 1994). The separation of dispositional judgments (stage 2) from causal judgments (stage 3) is supported by the fact that they seem to be produced by different mechanisms. For example, Hilton et al. (1995) found that perceivers employed a method of agreement for dispositional judgments and a method of difference for causal judgments. That causal judgments come after dispositional judgments is further supported by the finding that while dispositional judgments sometimes occur spontaneously with the characterization of behavior (see references above), causal judgments typically occur later, especially when the behavior or events are unexpected or puzzling (Clary & Tesser, 1983; Hastie, 1984; Kanazawa, 1992).

Presence, behavior, and insight awareness can be mapped into these attribution theoretic stages in the following way. Implicit, though not directly addressed, in attribution theory is the ability to distinguish among individuals in a situation, which is necessary for appropriate attribution to occur. For example, it is assumed that jurors making attributions of blame in civil court cases can distinguish between the plaintiff and the defendant (e.g., Lupfer et al., 1985), and that managers making attributions concerning employee success or failure can distinguish among his/her employees (e.g., Dubinsky et al., 1989). Therefore, the ability to identify and distinguish among the different members in one’s group (i.e., presence awareness) will enable (stage 1) behavioral tracking and characterizations to occur. This leads to the following hypothesis:

## H5 Presence awareness will increase behavioral awareness.

The link between insight and behavioral awareness follows directly from the attribution model’s dependence of stages 2 and 3 on stage 1. That is, insight regarding the cause(s) of behavior, including an individual’s internal causes (e.g., traits identified in stage 2) and/or contextual causes (e.g., environmental influences identified in stage 3) are inferred from characterizations of that behavior. For example, an individual’s behavior may lead others to believe that he is confused about a solution currently under discussion (stage 1). This characterization is then employed by the others to determine if the individual’s personal traits may be causing this confusion; they may conclude, for example, that the individual is not very bright (stage 2). This dispositional evaluation may then be employed, along with contextual information, in order to determine if factors other than, or in addition to, the individual’s lack of brilliance could be causing his/her behavior. The result may be that inconsistencies within the proposed solution are largely to blame, and that this is the cause of the individual’s confusion (stage 3). Insight awareness reflects an understanding of such dispositional and/or contextual causes of behavior. This leads to the following hypothesis:

## H6 Behavioral awareness will increase insight awareness.

## Treatment

As described in more detail later, group members participating in this study communicated anonymously with each other within a chat room using the names of fruit (apple, pear, grape, and plum) as aliases. An altered chat room appearance was the treatment. Both the control and treatment chat rooms provided messages in chronological order, identified with each sender’s alias (see Figures 2 and 3). The chronological ordering in both chat rooms was maintained by starting a new row each time a message was received. In addition, the treatment chat room (Figure 3) provided feedback from group members in the upper right that indicated, for each member, whether he/she did not understand the technique under consideration, or if he/she did understand, whether he/she agreed with it or not.

The number of members and their presence in the chat room is specifically noted by the nominal labels (apple, pear, grape, and plum) in the upper right corner of the screen for subjects receiving the treatment. This should increase presence awareness, which involves determining who is in the workspace, and leads to the following hypothesis:

## H7 Treatment chat rooms will increase presence awareness.

In addition, the indications of members’ current understandings and (dis)agreements with the technique under discussion are included in the upper right corner of the screen for subjects receiving the treatment. In Figure 3, both Grape and Plum have indicated that they understand and agree that the group is ready to go on to the second task. It is not clear that Pear believes this is the case. And, Apple has indicated that he/she does not understand the technique. This should provide insight regarding the causes of members’ behaviors. For example, knowing that a member does not understand the technique under discussion facilitates other members’ inferences regarding the causes of that member’s messages. This leads to the following hypothesis:

## H8 Treatment chat rooms will increase insight awareness.

## The task and task context

Experimental tasks There were two tasks for the groups. In the first (decision) task, each group was given a description of the second (execution) task, and had to decide upon a technique that would be used to coordinate their communications during the second task. Together, these two tasks reflect the basic objectives of task-oriented groups: to get organized and complete their task (Guetzkow & Simon, 1955).

During this first (decision) task, messages were exchanged among group members via a web-based chat application. This first task was intellective (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994) in that there were clearly better coordination techniques for completing the second task (as described below). The first task ended when group members indicated that they were ready to begin the second (execution) task. There were no limits or incentives offered concerning the time taken or the amount of messages exchanged during this first task. Groups were not provided guidance pertaining to technique options. Although the experimental tasks took place during one normal class session, groups finished both tasks well before the class session ended; the maximum time allowed for the tasks was thus not a constraining factor. It is the first task and its coordination technique decision that is the focus of this study, providing data for all but the task performance construct.

The transition from the first to the second task occurred without any administrator intervention. During the second task, members used the same computer system employed in the first task, but communicated using an e-mail application. When the second task began, each member in a group was given a list of four out of five possible colors. Each member’s colors were selected such that only one color was common to the list of every group member. In order to complete the second task, members had to exchange e-mail messages with each other until every member of the group indicated the correct common color to the administrator. There was no time limit, and the task did not end until each member of the group correctly identified the common color.

The top-performing group in each session was that which communicated the fewest messages during the second task. Protocols for experiments call for monetary rewards geared to performance (e.g., Smith, 1982). However, a number of experiments have shown that decision-making rationality is not necessarily improved and may be hindered by higher reward levels (e.g., Grether & Plott, 1979; Slovic & Lichtenstein, 1983). Our prior experience with this task suggests that larger rewards (i.e., \$20 per group member) lead groups to attempt risky ‘all or nothing’ schemes for accomplishing the task (cf. Tversky and Kahneman, 1981). Therefore, they were told that the three top-performing groups would win a cash prize of \$18, with deductions of 10 cents for each message sent; the net prize was divided among team members.

This second task is based on that used by Leavitt (1951) and Guetzkow & Simon (1955), and focuses on the structuring of communication among group members. The task has been characterized as simple yet elegant in its ability to permit observation of group processes without removing the essence of group interaction (Freeman et al., 1979). Similar information-sharing tasks have been used in GSS studies to examine the effects of communication medium on information processing (Dennis, 1996).

As indicated above, the focus of this study is on the first task. The decision in the first task was intellective, as some techniques were better for completing the second task than others. An example of a better technique is a two-tier hierarchy where members send their colors to one member, who then determines the common color by looking at the other members’ lists, and sends the common color back to the other members. With no miscommunication in a group of four members, this results in 10 messages: 3 messages from members to the one member, 3 messages from the one member to the other members, and 4 messages from the members to the administrator. An example of a poor technique is where each member sends every other member all of his/ her colors; all members then determine the common color for themselves. With no miscommunication in a group of four members, this results in 16 messages: 3 messages from each member to the other group members (resulting in a total of 12) and 4 messages from the members to the administrator.

GSS technology The technology employed for the first task (the decision task) would be categorized by Fjermestad & Hiltz (1998–1999) as a level 1 GSS, since it provided broadcast electronic messaging, computer terminals for each group member, a public screen at each group member’s terminal, the anonymous display of member ideas, and a voting tool (the ‘‘we are ready’’ check box). Systems providing such functionality have also been described as GSS with a communications focus (Zigurs & Buckland, 1998). Data regarding the treatment, workspace awareness, and decision quality and consensus were all related to this task and this technology. More details on the technology and its relationships with computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) and computer-mediated communication (CMC) systems are provided later.

The technology employed for the second task (the execution task) was a simple e-mail system that was restricted to one-to-one communication with no public screen. The only data captured when groups were using this technology was related to the task performance construct.

We are ready vs consensus with decision Each group could not move from the first to the second task until all of its members indicated that their group was ready (see Figures 2 and 3). Such indications do not necessarily reflect consensus regarding the coordination decision made by the group. For example, members could acquiesce in spite of holding reservations about the decision (Kahai & Cooper, 1999) because they do not believe that any more discussion would be fruitful or because of compliance with group norms (Spears & Lea, 1992). Therefore, although all group members indicated that their group was ready for the second task, this did not necessarily reduce the variance associated with the consensus with decision construct. (In fact. as described later, consensus with decision had the largest variance of all constructs in the model.)

Subjects Two hundred and forty undergraduate students enrolled in upper division business courses at a public university in the United States participated in the laboratory experiment as part of a classroom exercise. As described earlier, they were motivated by the potential to win a monetary prize. Thirty-nine percent of the students were females and the average age was 23. All participants had completed an introductory computer applications course and were proficient at operating a personal computer. Participants were divided into 59 groups of mixed gender. There were three experimental sessions. Owing to session size restrictions, this resulted in four groups formed of five members. However, these group size differences had no significant effect on the results.

Task process The experiment took place during a single session, and subjects were told that it was an online collaboration exercise. To control for any confounding of treatment effects by session due to differences in instruction administration or cohorts, treatments were intermingled within each experimental session, and all sessions received the same, scripted instructions.

After completing an informed consent document and a demographic questionnaire, each subject was randomly assigned to a group and then randomly assigned an alias (e.g., ‘apple’). Groups were randomly assigned to treatments within sessions. Subjects were first given instructions about the second task, its objectives, and how to send and read e-mail messages. They were then given instructions on how to use the chat screen to discuss the group’s technique for determining the common color. The first (decision) task then began, and ended when all group members had checked a box marked ‘We are Ready’ (see Figures 2 and 3). At the end of this task, the questionnaire (see Table 1) came up on students’ screens for them to answer. When all students in a group finished the questionnaire, the second task automatically began.

Decision quality High-quality decisions made during the first task involve communication techniques that potentially enabled color determination with the least amount of communication. There were two such communication techniques. The first is the two-tier hierarchy described earlier that could result in 10 messages. With the second technique (the circle), one group member would send all of his/her colors to a second member, the second member would send only the three colors that he/she and the first member had in common to a third member, the third member then would send the two colors that they had in common to the fourth. The fourth member would send the single common color to all other members, and all members then would send the answer to the experimental administrator. This also would result in 10 messages: 3 messages during the initial determination of the common color, 3 messages from the last member to all other members revealing the common color, and 4 messages from the members to the experimental administrator.

A dummy indicator was employed to represent decision quality, with a value of 1 indicating that the group decided to employ either of these two potentially optimal techniques, and a value of 0 indicating that the group had chosen a less effective technique or had not made a decision (i.e., had chosen to proceed to the second task without determining a technique). Thus, decision quality represents the potential for a group’s chosen technique to minimize messages. This should not be confused with actual performance on the second task, which could vary according to the degree to which group members understood the technique and appropriately implemented their responsibilities in completing the task.

Task performance There was no time limit for the execution task. Groups finished their task when each member sent a message to the experimental administrator that correctly indicated the common color. Task performance for a group was measured by the number of messages communicated among members and from members to the administrator: more messages represented decreased task performance.

Operationalization of the moderation of decision quality by consensus The moderation of decision quality by consensus was formed in the suggested manner by Chin et al. (2003). In order to include a moderation effect, the main effects of decision quality and consensus must be included in the model. Decision quality was already part of our research model with a path to task performance. Therefore, a path to task performance was also included from the consensus construct.

When a construct is reflective, these paths are only from other constructs. In the PLS model described below, all constructs are reflective, with no more than two incoming paths. Our dataset exceeds the 10 data points required by these incoming paths.

PLS enables the assessment of a research model’s measurement components by providing principal components factor loadings of indicators. ## Discussion

## Implications for research

Decision effectiveness The workspace awareness literature focuses on groups developing tangible products rather than group decision tasks. We have provided theoretical and empirical support for the influence of workspace awareness on group decision task quality and thereby on task performance. Our results indicated that insight awareness increased the likelihood that a group would choose a potentially optimal technique, and that this choice positively influenced task performance. Although not measured here, the workspace awareness literature suggests that this occurred due to the ability of group members with increased awareness to more appropriately couple (discuss, instruct, or consult with other members), coordinate (reorganize group members’ roles and activities), and assist (help other members with their tasks). For example, based on an individual’s behavior (i.e., a specific sequence of messages), it may become evident that he/she is confusing the current technique under consideration with a technique that had been suggested earlier. Understanding this, other group members could provide appropriate assistance to that individual. Because the workspace awareness literature has typically focused on groups designing and developing physical artifacts, it would be useful to take a closer look at the link between insight awareness and decision quality in order to validate the applicability of coupling, coordination, and assistance explanations.

We have also provided theoretical and empirical support for the positive influence of insight awareness on consensus with the decision, and thereby on the ability of decision quality to affect task performance. Although consensus is not addressed by the workspace awareness literature, more appropriate coupling and assistance due to increased awareness provided a basis for our earlier proposals linking workspace awareness to consensus. We noted that consensus can be generated when group members feel the decision adequately reflects their views. Coupling and assistance resulting from insight awareness can increase members’ mutual understandings of the task, which can increase members’ perceptions that the decision adequately reflects their views. It would be useful to take a closer look at the link between insight awareness and consensus in order to validate the links among members’ mutual understanding and coupling and assistance.

Workspace awareness The workspace awareness literature has rarely examined the potential for causal relationships among the workspace awareness elements themselves. We provided evidence that insight awareness may fully mediate the influence of behavior awareness on consensus and decision quality. We have also provided evidence that both behavior and insight awareness may fully mediate the influence of presence awareness on consensus and decision quality. In addition, our results indicated that behavior awareness explained 64% of insight awareness, and that presence awareness explained 52% of behavior awareness. This path of constructs suggests the following: (1) insight awareness is a key to increasing decision quality and consensus, and that presence and behavior awareness have no influence other than through insight awareness; (2) a high level of insight awareness can be obtained by an individual to the degree that he/she is able to characterize others’ behaviors; (3) the ability to characterize others’ behaviors depends on an individual’s ability to identify and distinguish among the different individuals who are working on the task.

We employed attribution theory to help understand the relationships among workspace awareness elements. When applying workspace awareness to group decision tasks, attribution theory may provide additional support. For example, a fundamental attribution error (Ross, 1977) has been frequently observed, where individuals overattribute others’ behaviors to their internal dispositions rather than to situational factors (Bassili & Racine, 1990). This error comes from the fact that initial dispositional attributions are relatively easy to make, perhaps due to the ‘salience and vividness of behavior (that) tend to direct attention to the actor and raise the likelihood of attributions to personal causes’ (Bassili & Racine, 1990, p. 882). Attributions are thereby anchored to the individual and then may be corrected through a process that requires substantial cognitive resources. Therefore, dispositional attributions tend to predominate because (1) any adjustments must be made from this initial anchor point and (2) adjustments based on the consideration of situational factors are limited due individuals’ tendencies to make attributions with the least amount of information processing capacity (Lord & Smith, 1983). This can be important with group decision tasks when members are attempting to gain insight based on others’ behaviors. It suggests that such insights will tend to be wrong because members infer that causes for others’ behaviors are due to characteristics of the others rather than the others’ reactions to contextual factors (cf. Steinfield et al., 2001). For example, if an individual’s comments do not quite make sense, members will tend to believe that problems exist with the individual, when in fact these comments may reflect basic problems with the other members’ assumptions underlying the decision alternative being considered.

Workspace awareness and anonymity Since they both involve information about group members, our work on workspace awareness is most closely tied to the GSS anonymity research stream. However, workspace awareness and anonymity are quite different. Workspace awareness is ‘ylimited to events happening in the workspace – inside the temporal and physical bounds of the task that the group is carrying out.’ (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002, p. 417). Workspace awareness focuses on task-related group member identification, with task processes becoming more efficient and effective when members know more about each other’s task-related roles, activities, and intentions. For example, being able to easily identify the stream of comments coming from each individual member could enhance members’ abilities to determine if a specific member does not understand the current discussion. In contrast, anonymity involves organizationally related group member identification, with organizational influences that can affect task activities (e.g., the potential for reprisals) becoming more salient when members’ actions can be connected to who they are in the organization. For example, believing that a potential solution came from a manager in the organization can make subordinates less likely to provide honest evaluations of the solution (Pinsonneault & Heppel, 1997).

Since workspace awareness and anonymity are conceptually different, their levels can vary independently of each other. If group members have never met, know nothing about each other, are geographically separated, and employ a communication system that does not reveal any clues to their organizational identities, then anonymity is high, and thus fears of reprisal are low. Within this context, workspace awareness can be low, such as when comments cannot be identified as coming from different individuals. In contrast, workspace awareness can also be high, such as when artificial identities are created that can be observed and tracked by group members. For example, McLeod (2000) found that anonymous group members created identifying markers for themselves and each other by using epithets, comment line numbers, and physical location in the room. These artificial identifiers were used to request information about or from others and to manage the meeting process. Second Life (www.secondlife.com) is another example of a context where high anonymity and high workspace awareness can coexist. Individuals are anonymous (individuals typically have never met, know nothing about each other, are geographically separated, and employ a communication system that does not reveal any clues to their organizational/personal identities), yet their artificial personas interact with each other in computer-simulated work and play.

However, it is possible that a contextual or GSS change could result in having the effects of workspace awareness and anonymity confounding each other with regard to decision quality. For example, group members who have had significant interactions with each other prior to a group task will be better able to identify communication patterns as coming from a known individual. This can increase behavioral awareness by helping members track the communication behavior of that individual, and we have demonstrated that increased behavioral awareness can increase decision quality. However, this a priori knowledge could also decrease the level of anonymity, since members would be better able to identify the organizational identity of the individual, potentially decreasing participation and therefore decision quality (Pinsonneault & Heppel, 1997).

Therefore, anonymity was held constant in our experiment across both treatment conditions, and was achieved in the following ways: (1) each group member’s comments and e-mail messages were identified only using an alias (apple, pear, grape, and plum); (2) all communication was textual and mediated by the computer system; (3) all groups were ad hoc and groups and aliases were assigned randomly; and (4) although the members of each group were in the same room, they were not introduced to each other and were intermingled with members of other groups. In addition, when the model was run with an anonymity construct based on Pinsonneault & Heppel (1997) with paths to decision quality and consensus, the paths were not significant.

Implications for other computer-supported environments We have focused on workspace awareness in the GSS context. The GSS we employed in the first (decision) task did not include sophisticated task support or structuring tools, but rather provided an anonymous environment in which each group member could communicate synchronously and simultaneously with all other members and these communications were all displayed for each member. Although there was a rudimentary voting tool (members voted on whether they were ready to go on to the second task, with unanimity required for continu ing), this tool did not reach the level of sophistication offered by many GSS. Our GSS would therefore have been described as level 1 by Fjermestad & Hiltz (1998), and had much in common with 30% of the GSS that they identified.

However, the relative ubiquity of chat-room style communication technology in other distributed collaboration contexts suggests that our results with respect to presence, behavior, and insight awareness may be able to be extended to the CSCW and CMC research streams. For example, instant messaging systems typically include a means for participants to indicate their status (online, busy, away, etc.) (Smale & Greenberg, 2005; Riemer et al., 2007). The presence-based utility of this status display has been noted in medical contexts, where connected experts can be discovered and second opinions more easily sought (Bønes et al., 2007), and in collaborative research environments, where the morale of the group can be increased simply by feeling the presence of the group leader. Furthermore, by adapting these messages, the expertise and/or assistance of others can be solicited even when one is not actually online (Riemer et al., 2007).

Speaking more broadly, there is a long standing conflict in CMC research between those who feel that interaction processes are inherently changed by CMC enforcement of relative distance and anonymity (Postmes et al., 1998) and those who feel that individuals adapt their processes and accommodate for limitations such that CMC is experienced in much the same way as face-to-face interaction (Walther, 1996). One of the driving forces in making interaction processes different between CMC and face-to-face communication may be the lack of presence awareness at the time the conversation is initiated (Short et al., 1976; Oemig & Gross, 2007). As interaction proceeds via CMC, one may eventually feel that distinct others are present and identifiable through the contents of their messages (Hayne et al., 2003) or by injecting identifying information into otherwise anonymous communication (McLeod, 2000). Thus, presence, behavior, and insight awareness can be facilitated by CMC tool designers (Oemig & Gross, 2007) or can be signaled over time by users (Riemer et al., 2007) to the point where CMC and face-to-face communication are not experienced in materially different ways (Walther, 1992).

In terms of the application of CMC, presence awareness (i.e., ‘Is anyone out there?’) is noted to be a fundamental desire in asynchronous virtual team environments ( Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Steinfield et al., 1999), along with a desire to identify who made a past contribution to the group’s work, and whether and when they will make a contribution in the future (Huysman et al., 2003). Thus, our observed interrelationships among presence, behavior, and insight awareness may apply to virtual teams as well.

Finally, it is interesting to note that workspace awareness in GSS/CSCW/CMC may be significant for distance education. An increasingly important view of distance education involves constructivism-based (or problembased) learning, which stresses collaborative working and learning in order to develop higher order skills (Mayes, 2001; McLinden et al., 2006; Martens et al., 2007). A key aspect of such learning is to place students in challenging and realistic contexts, such as having them role-play in a virtual company. in which they collaborate via computer to solve organizational tasks (Martens et al., 2007). Greater learning effectiveness is achieved ‘when students collaborate and learn from each other through discussions that challenge ideas and create multiple perspectives’ (Lou et al., 2006, p. 144). However, there is very little in terms of clear design guidelines for creating such contexts, systems, and tasks (Brush, 1998; Martens et al., 2007). Workspace awareness may provide a basis for such guidelines, with its potential to offer direction in terms of ways to promote insight awareness, and thus more informed bases on which students can comment on and challenge the ideas of other group members.

## Implications for practice

We found that though the chat room treatment had a significant effect on presence awareness, it had no effect on insight awareness. To explore this lack of significance, we examined the logs of the experimental application and found that out of the 240 subjects, less than 10% indicated that they either did not understand or that they disagreed with technique under discussion. Thus, it appears that the subjects did not use this chat room function, which was designed to provide group members with a greater understanding of the reasons behind other members’ messages. In hindsight this lack of use should have been expected, because it violates one of the workspace awareness guidelines that awareness information should be passively collected and distributed by the system rather than explicitly generated by the participants (Dourish & Bellotti, 1992; Steinfield et al., 1999; Wu & De Silveira Jr., 2002). Given this guideline, it is difficult to imagine how a GSS could directly provide insight awareness to group members. However, our experiment demonstrated that it is possible for a GSS to enable greater levels of presence awareness; in addition an example of how a GSS might be able to provide greater levels of behavior awareness is provided next.

Attribution theory suggests that in general, behavioral characterizations (that are part of behavior awareness) are made with the least amount of information processing capacity. Therefore, in order to overcome the fundamental attribution error described earlier, relevant information should be made easily available and in a form consistent with conscious, reflective thought (Lord & Smith, 1983). For example, if the chat room were divided into columns, with each column containing messages from only one group member (Figure 5), this should help members characterize the behavior of others because all the current and past behavior associated with one member can be easily seen merely by scanning down a single column. (Note that chronological ordering is maintained by starting each new message on a new row.) In contrast, the typical chat room requires much more information processing capacity because a member has to mentally disentangle the behavior of one member from that of all other members.

In addition to reducing the necessary information processing capacity, attribution theory suggests that more accurate behavioral and insight awareness can result from increased motivation. For example, if a group member anticipates future interactions with the other members or if he/she depends on the others for rewards, he/she will expend more information processing capacity to understand the others (Lord & Smith, 1983; Harvey & Weary, 1984). When these conditions do not exist, more effort should be aimed at creating a GSS interface (such as that described above) that reduces the information processing required by members in order to increase their behavioral and insight awareness.

## Limitations

Our subjects were involved in ad hoc student groups working on laboratory tasks. While the behaviors of students in such tasks are reasonably generalizable to behaviors in organizations (Roth, 1988; Bettenhausen, 1991), it would be good to replicate our study in a more natural environment. This environment would include natural groups in which individuals have a history of working together. With this history, the individuals would be more familiar with others’ knowledge, communication styles, etc., and workspace awareness may therefore depend less on the GSS design. In addition, the laboratory tasks were intellective, and as a result (as described earlier) there was little need for group support other than the facilitation of communication. In contrast to intellective tasks, judgment and mixed-motive decision-making tasks have no right answers (McGrath & Hollingshead, 1994). It would thus be good to examine workspace awareness issues in other decision contexts to see if the relationships we found hold when there is less problem structure and, perhaps, more GSS functionality.
