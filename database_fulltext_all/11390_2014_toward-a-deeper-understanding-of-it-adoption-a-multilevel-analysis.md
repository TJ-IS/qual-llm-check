## A B S T R A C T

This study adopts a multilevel perspective and uses cross-level modeling to explore how resistance to change (RTC) influences information technology (IT) adoption behaviors. RTC is conceptualized at two levels of analysis (personal and contextual) and with two levels of specificity (distal and proximal). Data were collected from 258 employees within 25 administrative work units undergoing a new IT initiative. The findings from this study reveal a ‘‘maverick effect’’ wherein pro-initiative employees react in contrast to their workplace contexts. They champion IT adoption when their work units resist the IT initiative and prolong IT adoption when their work units readily embrace the IT initiative. This study introduces a novel interactionist perspective on IT adoption that considers employee dispositions and perceptions, as well as workplace climate and culture, as enablers and inhibitors of IT adoption. Moreover, the study advocates for change management practices that account for multilevel interactions among the personal and contextual influences of IT adoption.

## 1. Introduction

The challenges associated with end-user adoption of information technology (IT) have been studied extensively within both the practitioner and academic communities. Motivated by the failure of many significant IT initiatives reported in the practitioner press [57], researchers have sought to elucidate issues associated with the adoption of newly implemented IT solutions to facilitate corporations’ ability to fully leverage their oftentimes substantial infrastructure investments [57]. Despite this focus on IT initiatives, obstacles to IT adoption persist when IT initiatives are deployed to end users. IT infrastructure investments are often expected to bring significant benefits, but unfortunately, these returns are rarely realized [9].

We posit that issues associated with IT adoption can be better understood by simultaneously taking into account the characteristics of both the institutional context and the end user, particularly as these characteristics relate to resistance to change (RTC). We are not alone in making this assessment. Although rapidly changing and complex technological systems are frequently blamed for these adoption failures, research suggests that technological problems account for less than 5% of project failures [8]. Conversely, psychological (as opposed to technological)

challenges associated with employee resistance to new IT adoption are often cited as fundamental barriers to successful IT adoption [70,74,75]. This paper explores the psychological influences, both personal and contextual, that shape employees’ decisions to behaviorally adopt newly implemented IT. By elucidating the personal and contextual factors that drive adoption and the interactions between them, this paper aims to increase the odds of success during risky and costly IT initiatives.

Previous research has identified RTC as a critical impediment to an organization’s ability to achieve the anticipated benefits from an organizational change [44,48,85]. Often conceptualized as a person-level personality trait, RTC has been defined in the literature as an individual’s dispositional inclination to resist changes [67]. RTC is thought to be a stable pattern of behavior associated with a tendency to avoid change and to find change aversive [67]. However, RTC can be viewed from three theoretical perspectives [44]: the person-oriented perspective, which focuses on resistance factors that are internal to the end user, the systemoriented perspective, which focuses on factors inherent in the IT system, and the interaction-oriented perspective, which examines the interactions between the user and the system. Although the person-oriented approach is the most commonly applied perspective for understanding RTC, it adopts a limited view that ignores the contextual environment within which IT adoption occurs. Moreover, the person-oriented approach often relies on intentions to adopt IT rather than actual IT adoption behaviors [75,78].

This paper addresses these limitations by drawing on a social constructivist belief that IT adoption behaviors are the product of personal cognitions (thoughts about the IT system or initiative) and contextual workplace influences [33,73]. From this position, we explore how RTC operates at two levels of cognitive specificity (distal and proximal) and two levels of analysis (personal and contextual). Combined into one cross-level measurement model, the direct and interaction effects associated with these multilevel RTC concepts are hypothesized to predict IT adoption behaviors. As Burton-Jones and Gallivan [15] suggest, multilevel perspectives can offer a more complete understanding of IT adoption behaviors.

One methodological and theoretical implication of this multilevel approach is that it allows for a novel interactionist perspective on IT adoption processes. Furthermore, from an applied point of view, an examination of these interactionist processes may reveal improved methods for anticipating the influence of the context on end user behavior and leverage points for better managing change during strategic initiatives.

## 1.1. Assessing extant models of IT adoption

Recent IT adoption research advocates for an interactive person-system perspective [44] that recognizes technology acceptance and resistance as two conceptually distinct (and not necessarily opposing) constructs and predictors [21,41,44,60]. Aligned with this research stream, the present study examines both positive (acceptance) factors that enhance IT adoption and negative (resistance) factors that inhibit IT adoption—a dual focus that has been absent in the literature [17].

Early IT adoption research established a series of models that integrate users’ perceptions of technology as a foundation for evaluating technology acceptance (e.g., technology acceptance model (TAM) [22]; the perceived characteristics of innovations (PCI) [64]; the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) [79]). More recently, acceptance researchers have begun to shift their focus away from end users’ perceptions to dispositions—a pattern of thoughts, beliefs, and/or behaviors that facilitates IT adoption [26]. Unlike perceptions, which are relatively narrowly targeted and fleeting cognitions (e.g., ‘‘I think this change initiative is a bad idea.’’), dispositions are more enduring and more broadly generalized cognitions (e.g., ‘‘I think change, in general, is a bad idea.’’). Therefore, dispositions are thought to be more stable and reliable for behavioral prediction.

For many years, psychologists have used dispositions to predict behavior (for a review, see [30]). Underpinnings of the theory of planned behavior and empirical meta-analytic findings indicate that significant relationships exist between people’s attitudes and their behaviors [4,6,35]. Studies that use dispositions to explain IT adoption behaviors are only just beginning to emerge [26,27,47,62,68]. For example, studies on computer self-efficacy (CSE) [20], personal innovativeness in the domain of IT (PIIT) [2], and cognitive absorption [1] provide evidence that dispositions can predict both positive and negative reactions to IT initiatives.

Therefore, an additional strength of this paper is that it also incorporates both perceptual and dispositional RTC measures to identify the unique cognitions that enhance and inhibit IT adoption [60]. Specifically, two conceptual distinctions of the RTC construct are explored. First, two degrees of cognitive specificity are examined and are aligned with the traditional conceptions of dispositions and perceptions. Distal RTC is characterized as generalized and stable dispositional beliefs about change, whereas Proximal RTC is a specific and dynamic perception about a given change event.

Second, in response to a growing body of literature that promotes multilevel research designs that include workplace structures (e.g., psychological culture and climate) [3,54], we posit that Distal and Proximal RTC can be conceptualized at two levels of analysis: the Person-level (Level 1) and the Context-level (Level 2). While RTC is commonly conceived of as a person-level phenomenon [67], we extend the construct to describe shared beliefs and attitudes that are held by employees within a work unit. Isomorphically, the Distal RTC variable conceptualized at the context-level reflects a work unit’s generalized belief and orientation toward change (i.e., Adaptability Culture; ‘‘In general, my work unit thinks change is a good idea.’’). Likewise, the Proximal RTC variable conceptualized at the context-level embodies a work unit’s fleeting perception about a specific change event (i.e., Acceptance Climate; ‘‘My work unit thinks this IT initiative is a good idea.’’). A summary of the conceptual and operational definitions of the RTC variables can be found in Appendix A.

Because IT adoption naturally occurs in an interactive psychosocial environment, our multilevel research design examines the influences of IT adoption at multiple levels of analysis (person and context levels) as well as the interactions among these influences (in cross-level measurement models) [55]. To the best of our knowledge, only one multilevel study has examined IT adoption intentions [55], and no cross-level or multilevel studies of IT adoption behavior have been undertaken. This sophisticated analysis of field-based data from actual organizations not only is novel but also provides a deeper, more naturalistic, and externally valid understanding of the IT adoption phenomenon [15,81].

We postulate that contextual workplace influences, such work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate, will affect the rates at which employees adopt new IT above and beyond what can be explained by person-level dispositions and perceptions, namely, Distal RTC and Proximal RTC, respectively. This finding would suggest that despite an individual’s inclination toward or against change, the workplace context affects the individual’s behavioral IT adoption. Support for this hypothesis would imply a need for organizations to simultaneously weigh both personal and contextual factors when planning and managing strategic change initiatives. Moreover, this multifaceted and multilevel investigation of RTC may help to explain ‘‘how organizations influence the behavior, attitudes, and well-being of members, [and] why some organizations are more innovative and quicker to adopt new technologies’’ [37].

## 1.2. Variables and hypothesis rationale

## 1.2.1. IT adoption time lag

The dependent variable of interest in this study is the duration of time between when an employee gains access to new IT (IT implementation) and when s/he reportedly begins to use the new system (IT adoption). IT adoption time lag is operationally defined as:

## IT Implementation - IT Adoption ¼ IT Adoption Time Lag

IT adoption time lag is critical to IT initiative success, symptomatic of IT initiative failure, and indicative of technology resistance [56,70].

## 1.2.2. Resistance to change (RTC)

IT initiatives bring with them an array of changes that affect communication and workflow processes, knowledge and skill needs, and work attitudes and behaviors [39], When examined in combination, Distal and Proximal RTC are thought to provide clues that explain when and how employees adopt new IT and constitute important indicators for distinguishing change innovators from change laggards within an organization [60,71]. Employees who are open to change and who are accepting of new initiatives may serve as ‘‘change agents’’ (i.e., low RTC role models) who organizational leaders may rely upon to help build support for IT initiatives.

Distal RTC can influence the amount of flexibility and tolerance that employees exhibit as they adapt to new job roles and novel situations in the work environment [14,45,46,82], and high Distal RTC has been associated with negative reactions to organizational change initiatives [67]. Therefore, Distal RTC is hypothesized to be positively associated with IT adoption delays. In comparison, Proximal RTC characterizes perceptions about specific workplace change events. Drawing on the theory of planned behavior, Proximal RTC is expected to be associated with lower intention to use new IT [4] and therefore to result in delayed new IT adoption.

## Hypothesis 1 (:).

Distal and Proximal RTC will be positively associated with IT adoption time lag.

As context-level variables, Distal and Proximal RTC are associated with work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate, respectively. Similarities and differences between workplace culture and climate have been long debated [24,72], and the general distinction between the concepts is that culture reflects stable and foundational values whereas climate reflects dynamic and specific attitudes. This study adopts the Glisson and James [37] view that the two concepts are distinct and responds to the need for multilevel research that examines the two concepts in combination—here by exploring the differential impacts of RTC context on IT adoption time lag [37].

Although the IT adoption literature has ‘‘typically ignored the innovation context—that is, the nature of the new technology being introduced into a specific organizational setting’’ [57], the workplace culture and climate literature has a long history [5] and thus provides the motivation for extending our focus to contextlevel RTC in order to predict IT adoption time lag. Just as personlevel RTC reflects the personality of an individual, Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate embody the ‘‘personality’’ of a work unit [37,80]. These contextual characteristics shape normative work behaviors [72] and are vital for helping organizations to succeed when implementing strategic initiatives [7,23]. Workplaces that generally value flexibility and change (high Adaptability Culture) are more responsive to the emergent challenges that are posed by new initiatives [38,46,77]. Adaptability Culture reflects a work unit’s ability to mobilize the business environment into action [24,25], to accommodate shifts in planning for newly implemented IT [38,84], and to anticipate IT implementation needs [53].

Similarly, workplaces that hold positive attitudes toward a specific IT initiative (high Acceptance Climate) are likely to be met with faster rates of adoption since ‘‘a strong implementation climate fosters innovation use. . .’’ [51]. ‘‘[When] a unit’s climate for innovation implementation is strong and positive, employees regard innovation use as a top priority, not as a distraction from or obstacle to the performance of their ‘real work’’’ [50]. While the importance of workplace climate and culture for change management is well researched, the cross-level interactive effects of these contextual influences on employee behaviors are less understood and, therefore, are the focus of the remaining study hypotheses.

## 1.2.3. Interactionist view of IT adoption

Trait theorists once argued that individual traits alone could explain differences in human behavior [65]. But, by the 1960s and 1970s, researchers began to question the utility of trait theory, stating that the situational context interacts with individual dispositions to alter behavior [63]. Since then, researchers have introduced individual traits and situational influences in integrated models that are now described as ‘interactionist’ because they depict combined influences [32,58]. Replicating these important streams of contemporary research [65], the following hypotheses suggest how person-level RTC may interact with context-level RTC to explain IT adoption behavior. This interactionist perspective leads us to posit that person-level RTC and IT adoption time lag may be moderated by the contextual influences of work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate. However, given the lack of an accepted cross-level design that takes into account these multilevel influences, the nature of the moderation could be explained by two opposing theoretical propositions.

First, the adoption enhancement hypothesis states that through social facilitation, Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate positively enhance Distal and Proximal RTC and thus facilitate employees’ new IT adoption. In contrast, drawing upon the theory of optimal distinctiveness, the Adoption Inhibition Hypothesis suggests that work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate have a negative and inhibiting influence that slows IT adoption and thus increases IT adoption time lag. The mechanisms for each hypothesis are explained below.

## 1.2.4. Adoption enhancement hypothesis

Social facilitation occurs when normative group influences have homogenizing effects on group beliefs and attitudes [83]. With respect to innovative social change, Rogers [71] notes that groups with accepting attitudes toward innovation (high Acceptance Climate) tend to facilitate and reinforce individuals’ acceptance and diffusion of innovation. Similarly, work units that value adaptability (high Adaptability Culture) are likely to view new initiatives as opportunities for improvement and to encourage proinitiative behaviors, such as the rapid adoption of a new IT. From an interactionist perspective, context-level influences that enhance IT adoption would be associated with negative moderation. That is, the negative relationship between an employee’s Distal and Proximal RTC in relation to IT adoption time lag would be weakened, causing rapid IT adoption when work units are adaptable and accepting of IT initiatives. As a result, among work units with a high Adaptability Culture and a high Acceptance Climate, person-level RTC would be associated with faster rates of IT adoption than those work units with a low Adaptability Culture and a low Acceptance Climate.

## Hypothesis 2a (:).

Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate will negatively interact with Distal and Proximal RTC to weaken the positive relationship between Distal and Proximal RTC and IT adoption time lag.

## 1.2.5. Adoption inhibition hypothesis

In contrast, the adoption inhibition hypothesis proposes that the work unit context has a lengthening-effect on IT adoption time lag. According to the theory of optimal distinctiveness [10,11], individuals strive to maintain self-identification that optimizes the need for assimilation and distinctiveness within a group. A delicate balance in self-identity is sought wherein identities that are too similar or too different from the group are rejected. In this sense, the distinctiveness of one’s identity in a group is driven by the context through cultural norms and group experiences [10]. As a result, how an employee self-identifies with respect to a workplace change event, such as a new IT initiative, may strengthen his/her compliance or defiance with a work unit’s prevailing culture or climate. In the context of IT implementation, work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate may motivate employees to self-identify in ways that differentiate themselves from others in opposition to the majority. According to the theory, low RTC employees may distinctively identify with a change initiative and therefore seek to differentiate themselves from the majority view that is held by others in the work unit. For example, an ‘‘IT change agent’’ (a person identified by his/her organization to exhibit support for an IT initiative and to serve as a role model for others [16]) may be inclined to ‘‘go against the grain’’ of his/her organization in reaction to an IT initiative in order to maintain an optimally distinct identity. Accordingly, he/she might adopt IT more slowly in low RTC work units that avidly support and adapt to new initiatives and might adopt IT more quickly in high RTC work units that are rigid and reluctant to adopt new changes. For a person who highly self-identifies with the IT initiative (i.e., selfidentifies as the local IT expert), a need for distinctiveness would drive him/her to second-guess, question, and/or even contradict the prevailing views and behaviors of the group in response to the initiative (i.e., ‘‘maverick effect’’). From an interactionist perspective, context-level influences would positively moderate the person-level attitude-behavior relationship and thus strengthen the positive relationship between employees’ Distal and Proximal RTC and IT adoption time lag. As a result, among work units with a high Adaptability Culture and a high Acceptance Climate, personlevel RTC would be associated with slower rates of IT adoption than those work units with a low Adaptability Culture and a low Acceptance Climate.

## Hypothesis 2b (:).

Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate will positively interact with Distal and Proximal RTC to strengthen the positive relationship between Distal and Proximal RTC and IT adoption time lag.

## 1.2.6. The study context: multiagency SAP procurement initiative

This study assesses the adoption behaviors of an IT system that is used for purchasing and requisitions in administrative work units (e.g., human resources, accounting, operations). The implementation of this system provides an ideal context for studying IT adoption time lag for three reasons. First, the IT system was broadly implemented across several work units and was considered by most work units (95% of the departments) to be a significant departure from the traditional (mostly paper-based) procurement method in that it increased processing speed and interagency communications. A second advantage of this research context relates to the levels of analysis that it allows. Eveland and Rogers [31] contend that implementation behaviors are not uniformly displayed throughout organizations and, therefore, that implementation behaviors should be analyzed at the context level with four or more persons representing at least two different status levels and in work units that are organized by output and workflow [28]. Satisfying these criteria, this study’s sample is divided into numerous similarly structured work units comprising entry-level agents and managing directors. Despite the operational and structural similarities, the work units are nested within government agencies that have different organizational missions, occupational compositions, and histories, thereby allowing for unique psychological climates and cultures to manifest across units. Third, although IT adoption was encouraged, it was implemented parallel to the legacy procurement system. Thus, the use of the new system was volitional and could be avoided or ignored. As a result, IT adoption time lag varied substantially across employees, making this an ideal context for examining factors that shape volitional IT adoption.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Sample

The sample comprises 258 purchasing agents and 25 administrative work units. These participants are nested within 18 government agencies from one northeastern state (one agency had three work units represented, and another two agencies each had two work units represented). The data collection coincided with the statewide implementation of a new enterprise resource system for managing government purchasing functions (SAP Procurement). On average, the purchasing agents had 15 years of job tenure and a slightly negatively skewed age distribution, which is typical for the government sector: 25 years and younger (3%), 26–35 years (10%), 36–45 years (31%), 46–55 years (45%), and 56 years and older (11%). The respondents were mostly female (82%) and Caucasian (93%), which is nationally representative of government procurement employees and of the state where the data collection occurred [13,59].

## 2.2. Procedure

Eighteen months after the statewide SAP implementation began, a state government electronic (email) listserv (the primary mode of interagency communication) was utilized to invite all procurement agents to complete the SAP Procurement Implementation survey. Paper-based questionnaires were made available upon request, but none were requested. The instrument contained 18 scales and an open-ended section for general comments. It took approximately 15 min to complete and could be submitted electronically to the research administrator devoid of participant names and email addresses. Participation was voluntary and yielded a response rate of 38%. No compensation was provided; however, modal themes from the data analysis were provided to the participating work units.

## 2.3. Measures

The main dependent variable in this study, IT adoption time lag, was measured as the number of months between the documented date when the SAP procurement system was introduced into each work unit and the self-reported month and year when each participant started using the system. The average IT adoption time lag was two months, with 68% of participants adopting the system within the first month; only 10% took six months or more. Archival and self-report data were used in combination to reduce common method bias (CMB). In addition, the remaining self-report measures were gathered in varying response scale formats for similar CMB concerns.

The remaining variables were measured with a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘‘Strongly Disagree’’) to 5 (‘‘Strongly Agree’’). The person-level RTC measures assessed ‘‘an individual’s dispositional inclination to resist changes’’ ([67], p. 680); thus, high scores represent greater resistance. The Distal RTC scale contained fouritems adapted from Oreg [67]; the principal component factor loadings ranged from .62 to .82, with 52% of the construct variance being explained and an internal consistency reliability of a = .69. The Proximal RTC measure was adapted from Klein, Conn, and Sorra [49] with a six-item scale that referred to SAP procurement as the technology of interest—an adaptation that provides greater context specificity. The item loadings ranged from .77 to .83, with 63% of the construct variance explained and an internal consistency reliability of a = .88.

The context-level RTC variables, Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate, were measured on the basis of employee responses to workplace-referent items. Item responses were aggregated by the mean to represent the shared RTC attitudes and beliefs of each work unit. Unlike the person-level RTC measures, the context-level RTC measures were scaled so that higher scores represent greater adaptability to and acceptance of change. Adaptability Culture was measured on a four-item scale that used aggregated individual responses to assess the workplace’s ability to translate the demands of the business environment into action [24,25]. The scale internal consistency for this measure was a = .88, with factor loadings that ranged from .79 to .89, with 68% of the construct variance explained. The Acceptance Climate measure was adapted from Klein and colleagues [49] and contained six items that were aggregated by work unit mean to assess the workplace’s inclination to accept the SAP procurement initiative. The internal consistency reliability estimate for the scale was $\alpha = . 9 0 ,$ , with factor loadings that ranged from .70 to .88 and 66% of the construct variance explained. Scale descriptive statistics and reliability estimates for all the variables in the study are presented in Table 1. All scale items are listed in Appendix A.

To support the aggregation of person data to the context level, intraclass correlations (ICC1) were calculated to estimate homogeneity within the work units [43]. Adaptability Culture displayed a significant ICC1 value of 0.07 $( F = 1 . 8 0 , \ p < . 0 5 ) ;$ ; Acceptance Climate exhibited an ICC1 value of 0.34 $( F = 1 . 4 4 , p = . 0 8 )$ . Although the climate indices are above the median range reported in the literature (.12; [42]), in a one-way ANOVA, the work unit effect was marginal. To provide additional justification for a work unit effect, rwg values were calculated to estimate the degree of interchangeability of the scale ratings within groups [43]. All values for culture and climate were above 0.93, which is well above the suggested cut-off of .70 for establishing a work unit effect [34].

## 2.4. Analytic strategy

Advancements in multilevel modeling techniques, such as hierarchical linear modeling (HLM), have only just begun to allow researchers to measure interactionist models. This paper applied HLM techniques to estimate cross-level interactions among the personal and contextual influences of IT adoption time lag [15,19,55,81]. This technique is preferred for a number of reasons. Previous attempts to measure cross-level interactions have often relied upon ordinary least squares regression techniques that fail to control for violated assumptions of sample independence [76]. The employees in the current sample are nested within work units; therefore, the data observations are not independent: two individuals from the same work unit are likely to hold more similar attitudes than individuals from different work units owing to their shared exposure to contextual influences. HLM accounts for these independence issues in the estimation of cross-level effects.

The hypotheses were tested by using HLM with listwise deletion to handle the minimal occurrences of missing item-level data and grand-mean centering to account for normality and multicollinearity concerns [69]. The person-level (Level 1) analysis was used to determine the relationship between the predictor variables (Distal and Proximal RTC) and the focal dependent variable (IT adoption time lag) within each work unit. Estimates of the intercept and the slope parameters obtained from the Level 1 analysis served as the dependent variables in the equations that were used for the cross-level (Level 2) analysis of the context variables. The focal predictors in Hypothesis 2, namely, work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate, were used to predict the hypothesized variances in the intercept terms from the Level 2 regressions. Statistical significance of the Level 2 parameter estimates provides evidence of the context effects from work unit Adaptability Culture and Acceptance Climate.

## 4. Discussion

Our findings indicate that contextual factors, such as work unit culture and climate, affect the adoption behaviors employees that engage when they are faced with a new IT initiative. While extant research has long recognized the impact of organizational climate as a ‘‘facilitating condition’’ of work unit IT adoption [51], few empirical studies have investigated the cross-level impact of context-level influences on individual IT adoption by taking both person-level dispositions and person-level perceptions into account. From a theoretical perspective, the current study advances the literature by promoting an interactionist view to explain how person- and context-level RTC factors enhance and inhibit IT adoption behaviors. The differential effects demonstrated by both Proximal and Distal RTC emphasize the need for a dual focus that extends beyond perceptions alone to encompass a broader, more complex set of explanatory variables in the prediction of IT adoption behaviors [75]. Specifically, our results suggest that general dispositional values and beliefs about change outweigh more temporal and proximal views of a given change initiative. Moreover, regardless of one’s general disposition toward change, employees who support IT initiatives may react against the majority view of their work units in an effort to maintain an optimally distinct identity. This ‘‘maverick effect’’ may be characterized by a person’s drive for distinctiveness that leads the individual to defy the predominant attitudes and behavior of the work unit—readily adopting IT when his/her workplace is perceived to be laggardly resisting a change initiative and prolonging adoption when the work unit is viewed to be adopting and accepting the initiative too hastily.

From an applied perspective, organizational managers who enlist change agents to champion workplace initiatives but who overlook the workplace context may inadvertently create a paradoxical circumstance in which pro-initiative employees respond as work unit mavericks who behaviorally resist change. The findings from this study emphasize the need for cognizance and consideration of the context as well as interactions between context-level and person-level factors. Managers should cautiously assess work unit culture and climate, as these factors may influence employees in surprising ways, with self-identified ‘‘IT experts’’ (a likely change agent candidate) delaying IT adoption if they feel that the work unit is inclined to respond too hastily (or too slowly) to the IT initiative. Managers should give such employees opportunities to voice their concerns and to participate in the initiative planning. The ability for a pro-initiative employee to become informed about and engaged in the implementation process may facilitate such an employee’s optimally self-identification with the IT initiative and volitionally (behaviorally) support of the process. These tactics may garner confidence among the employees whose support and compliance are most needed to champion a successful IT initiative [52] and thus to foster a positive workplace culture that embraces change.

## 4.1. Research limitations and future research directions

The findings from this study provide preliminary support for the Adoption Inhibition Hypothesis and contribute to recent developments in the literature regarding both enablers and inhibitors of IT adoption [17,18]. Given the ‘pro-innovation’ bias in IT adoption work [71], we encourage researchers to move beyond the study of factors that drive IT adoption to also include factors that inhibit it. Moreover, as our study demonstrates, inhibiting influences can be conceptualized not only as opposing perceptions (e.g., difficulty versus ease of use) but also as orthogonally distinct concepts from ‘‘enhancers.’

This paper responds to recent calls in the literature [15] to approach IT adoption from a multilevel perspective in order to elucidate how work unit, organization, and industry influences shape IT adoption decisions in naturalistic work settings. While this study applies robust modeling techniques to multilevel data that were difficult to acquire, it nevertheless has methodological limitations. First, this study’s small sample size may have limited its ability to detect statistically significant effects [12,69]. However, considering this study’s multilevel analytical strategy and the difficulty of detecting even marginally significant results by utilizing these complex techniques in field settings, the findings from this study contribute valuable and unique insights into an important and complex workplace phenomenon. Future researchers should seek to replicate this research by using larger, multiorganizational samples from different work sectors and industries (e.g., private sector; nonprofit sector). In addition, replication may be worthwhile to examine the role of selfidentification under conditions of mandated IT adoption (as opposed to optional, as in the context of this study) to determine whether the drive for distinctiveness diminishes with control over when (and whether) one adopts new IT. In addition, future research may explore factors that contribute to adoption time lags that are outside the control of the end user, such as timely training and competing organizational priorities.

Finally, the internal consistency reliability estimate for the Distal RTC scale fell below conventional standards in the literature (a = .69). A lack of scale internal consistency leads to an underestimation of the covariance between variables and increases the likelihood of Type II errors that makes significant relationship patterns more difficult to detect. This lack of internal consistency for the Distal RTC scale may have limited the influence of Distal RTC in the measurement models. Future researchers might strengthen the stability and reliability of the Distal RTC variable by increasing the number of scale items that are used to measure the construct [66].

## 4.2. Concluding thoughts

While a broad number of theories have been proposed to elucidate IT adoption behaviors, substantial work is still needed to increase our knowledge of how organizational managers and change management specialists can better leverage their IT investments to achieve greater benefits to businesses and individuals. As a step in this direction, this study explores RTC as a means to explain lags in IT adoption and offers theory-driven suggestions for managing multilevel interactions among workplace influences. In addition, we provide suggestions for other researchers aiming to replicate and advance this line of inquiry in order to better facilitate efficient IT adoption behavior in workplace settings.
