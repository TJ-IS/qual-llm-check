# TIME WILL TELL: THE CASE FOR AN IDIOGRAPHIC APPROACH TO BEHAVIORAL CYBERSECURITY RESEARCH<sup>1</sup>

W. Alec Cram School of Accounting & Finance, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, ON CANADA {wacram@uwaterloo.ca}

John D’Arcy Lerner College of Business & Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE, U.S.A {jdarcy@udel.edu}

Alexander Benlian Information Systems & E-Services, Technical University of Darmstadt, Darmstadt, GERMANY {benlian@ise.tu-darmstadt.de}

Many of the theories used in behavioral cybersecurity research have been applied with a nomothetic approach, which is characterized by cross-sectional data (e.g., one-time surveys) that identify patterns across a population of individuals. Although this can provide valuable between-person, point-in-time insights (e.g., employees who use neutralization techniques, such as denying responsibility for cybersecurity policy violations, tend to comply less), it is unable to reveal within-person patterns that account for varying experiences and situations over time. This paper articulates why an idiographic approach, which undertakes a within-person analysis of longitudinal data, can: (1) help validate widely used theories in behavioral cybersecurity research that imply patterns of behavior within a given person over time and (2) provide distinct theoretical insights on behavioral cybersecurity phenomena by accounting for such within-person patterns. To these ends, we apply an idiographic approach to an established theory in behavioral cybersecurity research—neutralization theory—and empirically test a within-person variant of this theory using a four-week experience sampling study. Our results support a more granular application of neutralization theory in the cybersecurity context that considers the behavior of a given person over time. We conclude the paper by highlighting the contexts and theories that provide the most promising opportunities for future behavioral cybersecurity research using an idiographic approach.

Keywords: Cybersecurity, information security, idiographic, nomothetic, longitudinal, within-person, between-person, compliance, policy

## Introduction

Maisie and Isaac both work for Pay-All, a payroll processing company. Due to recent cyberattacks at several competitors, Pay-All’s IT department sends an email to all employees requesting that their passwords be reset and satisfy a minimum length requirement of ten characters. Maisie follows the advice and resets her password right away. Isaac ignores the suggestion and deletes the email.

Joanna is also employed by Pay-All. In January, the IT department implements a new initiative requiring staff to lock their computer screens when away from their desk. Joanna believes the policy makes sense and diligently locks her computer throughout the month. However, as time passes, her workload steadily increases, and she starts to tire of the extra effort the screen-locking takes. By the end of February, she rarely complies with the rule and leaves her computer unlocked throughout the day.

Employee behavior is well-established as a fundamental component of organizational cybersecurity safeguards (D’Arcy et al., 2009; Hsu et al., 2015; Spears & Barki, 2010). Past practitioner reports have highlighted the frequency of accidental and intentional employee actions leading to cybersecurity breaches (Deloitte, 2017; Kaspersky Lab, 2017; Ponemon Institute, 2020; Verizon, 2020), and the academic literature has delved deeply into the factors associated with cybersecurity policy compliance and violation (e.g., Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Herath & Rao, 2009b; Straub, 1990; Vance et al., 2012). A variety of factors influence an employee’s cybersecurity<sup>2</sup> behavior, including those typically related to individual (e.g., attitude, moral beliefs) and organizational characteristics (e.g., top management support) (Moody et al., 2018).

To date, the scholarly information systems (IS) literature has overwhelmingly investigated behavioral cybersecurity issues using cross-sectional data and between-person analysis (collectively referred to as a nomothetic approach<sup>3</sup>), which has uncovered valuable insights regarding employee patterns of behavior (Cram et al., 2019). The typical approach (see Appendix A) used is a “single-shot” survey design, in which all study constructs are measured using a single survey instrument that is completed by one person. The resultant between-person analysis identifies relationships between variables across a population of participants at a particular point in time (Hamaker, 2012; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009). However, research using a nomothetic approach aims to identify patterns of behavior across a population of individuals and cannot be generalized to any one individual or point in time (Conner et al., 2009). To derive such insights, research requires an idiographic approach that utilizes multiple points of measurement over time and analysis of within-person (or, intraindividual) variation (Curran & Bauer, 2011; Hoffman & Stawski, 2009; Molenaar, 2004; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009; Wang & Maxwell, 2015). This is a critical point because although most theories used in behavioral cybersecurity research have been tested using a between-person approach, researchers often either implicitly or explicitly draw inferences regarding within-person relationships that are expected to persist over time. At the very least, most of these studies offer theoretical and/or practical conclusions that speak to within-person processes over time, as shown in Appendix A.

Despite repeated calls for more longitudinal research in this realm (e.g., Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Lowry & Moody, 2015; Ormond et al., 2019; Siponen & Vance, 2010), very few studies have actually undertaken investigations of how cybersecurity constructs and relationships evolve over time, especially through an idiographic approach. Drawing on this motivation, our objectives in this paper are to articulate how an ideographic approach (1) can help validate widely used theories in behavioral cybersecurity research that imply patterns of behavior within a given person over time and (2) how this approach can provide distinct theoretical insights on behavioral cybersecurity phenomena by accounting for such within-person patterns. By doing so, we also aim to highlight the contexts and theories that represent the most promising opportunities to reveal novel behavioral cybersecurity insights through the use of an idiographic approach.

To illustrate the value of an idiographic approach for behavioral cybersecurity research, both in terms of validating established theory and generating distinct theoretical insights, we provide a series of conceptual arguments followed by an empirical study. In the study, we apply an idiographic approach to an established theory in behavioral cybersecurity research—neutralization theory—and test a within-person variant of the theory using a four-week experience sampling design. We incorporate a temporal perspective into the theorizing by drawing on the concept of ego depletion, alongside evidence of the perceived inconvenient nature of organizational cybersecurity policy requirements. Our results suggest that neutralization techniques are more consequential for cybersecurity policy compliance as one’s self-regulatory resources become depleted over the course of a workweek. Accordingly, we find that the negative relationship between neutralization and cybersecurity policy compliance intensifies as the workweek progresses. Our theoretical explanation and results support a more granular application of neutralization theory in the cybersecurity context that considers the behavior of a given person over time.

Collectively, our logical arguments and empirical results suggest that the increased use of an idiographic approach, which enables within-person analysis of longitudinal data, will aid behavioral cybersecurity research by enabling more comprehensive insights about construct relationships over time. In addition, our approach enables an assessment of if and when proposed theoretical relationships in behavioral cybersecurity models hold over time and support a person-specific interpretation, rather than one that applies to a population at a point in time.

From a practical perspective, person-specific findings and interpretations can assist organizations in understanding employee deviations from their “typical” cybersecurity behavioral patterns, which is valuable considering that even a single noncompliance incident can have severe consequences (IBM Security, 2021; Ponemon Institute, 2020). One explanation for these continued problems is that employee vulnerability is not set in stone but changes over time because people get distracted, tired, emotional, are subject to biases and temptations, are curious, and lack sustained discipline, such as in the second introductory vignette. These vulnerabilities represent a fundamental target of cybersecurity attacks (e.g., social engineering, deep fake scripts) that can be defended by countermeasures at some points in time but not others. The insights derived from an idiographic approach can aid organizations in designing and monitoring cybersecurity initiatives by directing more attention to changes in employee behavior over time, thus facilitating more effective and targeted cybersecurity training, communications, and compliance monitoring.

## Conceptual Background

Behavioral cybersecurity can be described as “the complexes of human action that influence the availability, confidentiality, and integrity of information systems” (Stanton et al., 2003, p. 3). There is a rich history of behavioral cybersecurity research in the IS literature, including seminal works by Straub (1990) and others (e.g., Loch & Conger, 1996; Siponen, 2001; Thomson & von Solms, 1998) and a more recent surge of work over the past fifteen years or so. Reviews of this literature highlight the many theories used, which encompass a large number of theoretical constructs that predict and explain cybersecurity-related behaviors (Chen et al., 2021; Cram et al., 2019; Cram et al., 2017; Moody et al., 2018). Our goal here is not to rehash this body of work but, instead, to make the point that most behavioral cybersecurity studies utilize a nomothetic approach (i.e., cross-sectional data and between-person analysis). For example, Cram et al. (2019) found that 90 of the 95 papers included in their meta-analysis of empirical cybersecurity policy compliance (and noncompliance) studies used such an approach. We added to the list used by Cram et al. (now comprising 125 papers—see Appendix A) and found that all but seven<sup>4</sup> used a cross-sectional, between-person approach (94%).

Many of the theories used in these papers either implicitly or explicitly focus on within-person processes. This is evident in several of the theoretical and practical implications derived from these studies. Consider, for example, the Boss et al. (2009) paper listed in Appendix A. The authors used a nomothetic approach in finding a significant between-person relationship between employees’ computer self-efficacy and precautionary computing behavior. In discussing the managerial implications of this finding, the authors state: “As individuals feel more confident in using the computer to complete their work, they will be more likely to take precautions with the computer” (p. 160). What might logically be deduced from this statement is that, as a particular employee increases their computer self-efficacy their precautionary computing behavior also increases. Yet such within-person patterns can only be conclusively validated through an idiographic approach.

From a research perspective, employing an idiographic approach can help validate widely used theories in behavioral cybersecurity research that imply stable patterns of behavior within a person. Specifically, by complementing the appropriate between-person conclusion (i.e., a relationship holds in a population at a point in time) with a new within-person conclusion (i.e., a relationship holds for an individual over multiple points in time), we can further evaluate the robustness of a theory. This is known as demonstrating the homology of a theory across the levels of analysis (Gabriel et al., 2019; Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). Where differences are present (e.g., a positive betweenperson relationship and a negative or null within-person relationship), researchers are at risk of misleading theorizing and practitioners are at risk of implementing countermeasures that have little long-term chances of success. Related to this point, perhaps the strongest motivation for using an idiographic approach in behavioral cybersecurity research is theory advancement. Many established theoretical relationships in this literature seemingly have elements of dynamics and change in them (e.g., employees may not react to deterrence-based sanctions consistently over time), although such temporal patterns are often neglected in the applications of theory. An idiographic approach provides a means to understand when such relationships differ and how they might evolve over time.

For example, a study based on the first introductory vignette could point to the role of cognitive rationalizations of insecure behavior, known as neutralization techniques, as a key factor associated with cybersecurity policy compliance. Research might find that Maisie (who complied with the password policy) did not apply neutralization techniques, while Isaac (who failed to comply) did. This between-person analysis can lead to a conclusion that one group of employees—those who more strongly apply neutralization techniques—differ in their cybersecurity policy compliance when compared to employees who neutralize less. Indeed, extant studies that have applied neutralization theory to the cybersecurity context (e.g., Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen & Vance, 2010) confirm this relationship using cross-sectional data. In comparison, when juxtaposed with the second introductory vignette, Joanna’s initial compliance with the cybersecurity guideline during January erodes into a case of noncompliance in February, as she “tires of the extra effort.” We believe that it is important to acknowledge that such within-person changes in cybersecurity behavior do take place and that this reality be accounted for in the practice and study of behavioral cybersecurity issues.

As our analysis in Appendix A uncovered, most of the theoretical models used in behavioral cybersecurity research have not been evaluated in terms of within-person variation over time, which presents an opportunity to evaluate their robustness using an idiographic approach. It seems that numerous authors are aware of the general concern that their models may not hold over time, as per the frequent practice of calling for longitudinal assessments of research models in the discussion sections of papers. On this point, of the 118 papers that were cross-sectional in the analysis noted above, 40 of them (34%) explicitly called for more longitudinal studies. In this paper, we seek to articulate why an ideographic approach is potentially valuable, beyond the seemingly perfunctory “future research should see if our results hold over time” statement that is included in many empirical papers in the behavioral cybersecurity literature.

Although the cross-sectional, between-person studies that have dominated the behavioral cybersecurity literature to date have uncovered a wide range of valuable insights, it remains unclear if the identified relationships remain the same or change when examined in a within-person context. In other words, their homology has not been tested. Clarifying these potential differences is critical from a theory perspective to determine if relationships between constructs are sufficiently robust over time in the presence of potentially confounding effects (e.g., organizational changes, training), as well as situational and contextual factors (e.g., transient attitudes, moods, energy levels). Although it could well be expected that many betweenperson and within-person relations are similar (e.g., both a positive between- and within-person relationship between security attitudes and cybersecurity policy compliance), making such inferences is valid only in very limited circumstances, such as when the data have invariant statistical characteristics across time (Molenaar, 2004; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009). As we elaborate in the next section, there is support for the changing nature of certain cybersecurity phenomena over time, which opens the door for conflicting results across levels of analysis and more nuanced theorizing that accounts for time-dependent variation within a single individual.

## Impetus for an Idiographic Approach in Behavioral Cybersecurity Research

So why should we focus on within-person change over time in the cybersecurity context? We point to four conceptual explanations for within-person changes in employees’ behavior that motivate increased attention in this area: (1) transient factors, such as affective-emotional reactions or energy levels; (2) situational tensions between an employee and the organization (e.g., individual work goals vs. the organization’s goal of protecting its information assets); (3) context-specific circumstances; and (4) control-based orientation toward employee behavior.

To begin with, the cybersecurity context is one that is often subject to emotions (particularly negative), and emotions are known to be highly transient. Such transiency can be expected to relate to instability in relationships involving cybersecurity behaviors (i.e., how I feel at a particular moment likely influences whether I comply with a cybersecurity policy), similar to findings that have linked changes in emotions to changes in day-to-day work behaviors (e.g., Rodell & Judge, 2009). On the role of emotions in the cybersecurity context, practitioner accounts and academic research show that many employees find cybersecurity requirements, such as long and complex passwords and restrictions on cloud-based storage services (e.g., Dropbox), to be a nuisance and at times a detriment to productivity. Such reactions are ripe for negative emotions, as D’Arcy and Teh (2019) found in linking perceived stressful cybersecurity requirements to feelings of frustration and fatigue. Similarly, neutralization theory’s cognitive rationalization techniques seemingly include emotion-driven elements that could fluctuate over time, including the denial of responsibility (i.e., rationalizing that an act is beyond one’s control) and the defense of necessity (i.e., putting aside feelings of guilt because an action is deemed necessary) (Siponen & Vance, 2010). Moreover, many of the cybersecurity educational tools that are used to elicit protective and/or compliant behaviors rely on fear-based messaging (i.e., fear appeals). Fear is a short-term emotion that may result in a one-off action. But, as Renaud (2020) contends, “after the initial surge, fear will wear off … which makes people unlikely to commit to frequent actions such as choosing strong passwords” (p. 1).

The nature of such behavioral changes associated with transient emotions is obscured by the nomothetic approach that draws inferences based only on a snapshot of participant data. For example, as another application of theory to our introductory vignettes, researchers could apply deterrence theory to these contexts and undertake a cross-sectional data collection of the three employees’ perceptions of sanction severity, as well as their level of cybersecurity policy compliance (see Table 1). The data would suggest a strong correlation between the two constructs, indicating that employees with a strong perception of sanction severity tend to comply with policies more than employees with a weak perception of sanction severity. However, if the researchers complemented this line of inquiry with an idiographic approach, consisting of longitudinal data collection and within-person analysis (see Table 2), they might find a different explanation for employee compliance. In the illustrative dataset, the participant shows a strong correlation between sanction severity and compliance at Time 1 (and consistent with the data in Table 1), but as time passes, sanction severity remains stable and compliance decreases. Based on the second vignette, we might conclude that certain transient factors (e.g., cognitive resource depletion) related to Joanna’s increasing workload and growing fatigue from adhering to cybersecurity guidelines are plausible explanations for her declining compliance levels at Times 2 and 3, with these factors overriding the influence of sanction severity as time passes. In this vein, an idiographic approach provides researchers with the capability to uncover how transient factors such as emotions and energy levels can influence cybersecurity behavior changes.

Employees may also experience adverse emotional reactions due to the inherent tensions involved in cybersecurity policy compliance decisions, which are also likely contributors to instability in certain relationships involving antecedents to cybersecurity policy compliance. As an illustration of this tension, Karjalainen et al. (2019) described how some employees felt they were signaling distrust to their colleagues when adhering to a policy that mandated the locking of PCs when leaving one’s workstation. Such tension is likely to make some employees “feel bad” for following cybersecurity policies. More broadly, there are inherent tensions between the individual and institution in the cybersecurity context, in that the employee is often guided by their own immediate work goals and interests (e.g., getting a task done as quickly and efficiently as possible) while at the same time needing to consider the organization’s more stable goal of protecting its information assets (Karjalainen et al., 2019). These situational tensions associated with cybersecurity policy requirements likely fluctuate within employees over time, depending on the nature and/or urgency of the task being performed. Such tensions could induce an emotional reaction, depending on the employee’s state of mind. For example, an employee may be accepting of cybersecurity policy requirements under normal circumstances but perceive those same requirements to be particularly inconvenient when they are undertaking a high-priority activity for an important customer. Hence, how the individual is feeling on a particular day or in a particular context, combined with the situational tensions described above, should influence cybersecurity policy compliance decisions at that time.

Take the example of notifying IT personnel when a phishing email is received. Some cybersecurity policies mandate such behavior, and most organizations at least encourage it. Reporting a phishing email, however, takes extra effort (e.g., forwarding the phishing email to a specified email address), which is likely affected by how one is feeling at that moment. Indeed, past research has highlighted the variability of employee behavior and motivation, depending on an individual’s level of energy at a particular point in time (e.g., Kahn, 1990; Muraven & Baumeister, 2000; Quinn et al., 2012). In designing studies to better understand a phenomenon such as the propensity to report a phishing email, researchers could, on the one hand, use a nomothetic approach to provide useful insights into the groups of employees who tend to report phishing (e.g., those with strong personal ethics) versus those that do not (e.g., those working in organizations with weak managerial support for cybersecurity). On the other hand, relying exclusively on such an approach risks overlooking the individual-level fluctuations (e.g., mood, energy levels) that some employees might experience. We posit that the use of an idiographic approach can reveal a powerful—yet complementary—view into individual employee emotions and tensions that temporarily influence their cybersecurity-related behavior.

Related to the idea of emotions in the cybersecurity context, scholars have argued that certain cybersecurity decisions are not based on a fully rational decision process but are instead controlled by the instant and reactive cognitive process known as System 1 (nonconscious, automatic) thinking. For example, Dennis and Minas (2018) suggest that the decision to click on a phishing email and/or enter credentials into a phishing site is often made in a reactive and non-effortful way. Such System 1 decisions are influenced by cognitive heuristics and the current situation one is experiencing. Consequently, decisions produced by System 1 thinking are unstable, and the fact that many cybersecurity behaviors are strongly influenced by System 1 thinking makes the behaviors inherently difficult to predict.

For this reason, Dennis and Minas (2018) argue for a personcontext approach to understanding the drivers of cybersecurity behavior. As these authors describe, “change in the person or the context, and System 1 can produce different results” (Dennis & Minas, 2018, p. 17). Likewise, Karjalainen et al. (2019) encourage a stronger emphasis on the changing context-specific circumstances (e.g., shifting job responsibilities, newly implemented policies or procedures, or greater awareness of cybersecurity threats through updated training) that can impact a given individual’s cybersecurity behavior, based on their qualitative findings of differing explanations for why individuals chose to comply or not comply with cybersecurity policies over time. These perspectives align with the idiographic approach that we advocate in this paper.

Yet another area of behavioral cybersecurity research that suits an idiographic approach is research that takes a controlbased orientation toward employee behavior. The behavioral cybersecurity literature contains a plethora of studies that support the efficacy of countermeasures (e.g., cybersecurity policies, employee monitoring, cybersecurity education and awareness programs) for invoking compliant behavior and/or deterring illicit behavior—again, these studies are mostly cross-sectional. The foundation of this work is often deterrence theory, which is a control-based perspective and predicts that when individuals perceive sanctions for their illicit actions, they will be less likely to engage in such actions (or, on the flip side, be more likely to engage in desirable behaviors). The IS literature shows that certain control-based approaches, such as that for thwarting employees’ internet abuse, can lose their effectiveness over time (Shepherd & Mejias, 2016), perhaps as employees become apathetic toward them or the saliency of the controls becomes diminished in the minds of employees. More broadly, organizational research indicates that employee reactions to certain policies or initiatives can shift over time (e.g., Jansen et al., 2016). That is, some employees who initially resist the policy/initiative may later become supportive, whereas some employees who are initially supportive may become resistant over time. Similar shifts in employee perceptions about cybersecurity countermeasures seem likely in that these control-oriented mechanisms will be perceived as more or less reasonable depending on the employee’s state of mind and/or immediate contextual circumstances. It follows that the control-oriented theoretical frameworks in behavioral cybersecurity studies may be producing faulty or at least incomplete inferences because they are being tested in a cross-sectional manner. Our contention is that control-based theoretical perspectives for studying cybersecurity behavior can be sharpened with an idiographic approach that evaluates the efficacy of theoretical models over time.

## Empirical Study

In order to make our discussion more concrete, we designed an empirical study pursuant to our objectives of articulating how an idiographic approach can: (1) help validate widely used theories in behavioral cybersecurity research that imply patterns of behavior within a given person over time and (2) provide distinct theoretical insights on behavioral cybersecurity phenomena by accounting for such withinperson patterns. The study focuses on an established theoretical relationship in the behavioral cybersecurity literature—the link between neutralization and compliance behavior—which we investigate in a within-person manner over time. As we describe, this relationship has the potential to be influenced by time-dependent characteristics, like those noted in the previous section (e.g., transient emotions, fluctuating energy levels), and thereby lends itself to an idiographic approach and the inclusion of a temporal theoretical perspective. In the following sections, we develop the theoretical underpinnings of the study and its main hypothesis, then detail the study itself, followed by a discussion of the results, implications, and limitations.

## Neutralization and Cybersecurity Policy Compliance: A Temporal Perspective

Neutralization theory proposes types of rationalizations, called neutralization techniques, which individuals can invoke to justify their illicit behavior (Sykes & Matza, 1957). Common neutralization techniques described in the criminological literature and applied in behavioral cybersecurity studies are denial of responsibility, denial of injury, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties, defense of necessity, and metaphor of the ledger (Sykes & Matza, 1957; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen & Vance, 2010). Through such cognitive rationalization methods, individuals can “neutralize” the internal standards and values that would normally prohibit them from engaging in illicit behaviors. For example, employees may reason that committing a cybersecurity policy violation is justified on the basis that the action will not cause any real harm to the organization (i.e., denial of injury). Neutralization theory has been applied to several behavioral cybersecurity studies that take a between-person, cross-sectional approach, and the results support associations between increased neutralization and more cybersecurity policy noncompliance / less cybersecurity policy compliance (e.g., Barlow et al., 2018; Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Gwebu et al., 2020; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Vance et al., 2020). Based on the nomothetic study designs, it could be reasonably concluded that these results apply in an aggregate sense to a population of individuals at a point in time. That is, on average, people who more strongly apply neutralization techniques regarding cybersecurity policy compliance are less likely to comply.

However, there is evidence that neutralization is not an entirely stable construct, and therefore drawing inferences from between-person results to explain individual behaviors over time might be misleading. Criminological research describes how individuals can drift into and out of a state in which they use neutralization techniques (Maruna & Copes, 2005). Organizational research similarly explains how specific circumstances or contextual factors can trigger employee rationalizations of unethical behavior (Moore et al., 2012). The idea of neutralization having a degree of instability aligns with our conceptual explanations for within-person employee change noted above (e.g., affective-emotional reactions or energy levels; situational tensions between an employee and the organization). In cybersecurity contexts, neutralization has been shown to vary within individuals from day to day, as has cybersecurity policy compliance (D’Arcy & Lowry, 2019; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). Along with our prior conceptual arguments, the within-person variation in these two constructs provides an impetus for an idiographic approach to the application of neutralization theory to the cybersecurity context. To be more specific, because both neutralization and cybersecurity policy compliance are not entirely stable phenomena, it follows that the strength of the relationship between these two constructs may shift within a person from one point in time to another (i.e., within-person change over time).

To support such a temporal perspective for the application of neutralization theory to the cybersecurity context, we draw on ego depletion theory. Ego depletion theory proposes that individuals have a finite pool of self-regulatory resources, which are inner resources akin to energy or strength (Baumeister & Vohs, 2016). When these resources are sufficiently diminished, individuals are ego-depleted, which is a state of reduced willpower, often manifested in poor selfcontrol and impaired decision-making (Muraven, 2012). Adopting a temporal perspective, organizational research has used ego depletion theory to explain employees’ reduced selfcontrol/willpower as the workweek progresses (Dust et al., 2022; Pindek et al., 2021; Weigelt et al., 2021). The rationale is that certain work tasks and activities (e.g., abiding by organizational protocols) consume self-regulatory resources (Baumeister & Vohs, 2016; Muraven, 2012), and since there are few opportunities for resource recovery during the traditional five-day workweek (i.e., no weekend days off), these resources are at their lowest on Fridays (Fritz et al., 2010).<sup>5</sup> Employees are therefore more susceptible to deleterious and otherwise self-serving work behaviors, such as noncompliance, as the workweek progresses.<sup>6</sup>

We posit that relative self-regulatory resource depletion makes employees more susceptible to the effects of neutralization as the workweek progresses, thus enabling the negative neutralization-compliance relationship to become stronger over this period. General support for this prediction comes from studies in both laboratory and work settings that have found that mentally depleted individuals are more prone to justifications and rationalizations of illicit acts, thus increasing their chances of engaging in such acts (Bazerman & Tenbrunsel, 2011; Rosen et al., 2016). Research has likewise shown that ego-depleted individuals are less able to feel guilt for their illicit behaviors (Xu et al., 2012) and can be more easily swayed by external factors (Baumeister & Vohs, 2016), such as contextual workplace circumstances. Hence, insofar as employees experience diminished selfregulatory resources as the workweek progresses (i.e., become more ego-depleted), it should be more difficult for them to ward off neutralization techniques as drivers of their cybersecurity policy compliance behavior.

The nature of cybersecurity policy compliance lends further credence to this prediction. Cybersecurity policy compliance is a mandated behavior, and ego-depletion theory states that rulefollowing behaviors are particularly resource-depleting (Baumeister & Vohs, 2016). Moreover, as described, many cybersecurity policy requirements (e.g., creating and maintaining complex passwords for multiple systems; using a virtual private network [VPN]; restrictions on transporting files across home and work devices) are perceived by employees as frustrating and barriers to productivity (Bulgurcu et al., 2010; D’Arcy et al., 2014; Posey et al., 2014; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). Such negative reactions can be mentally taxing, particularly over consecutive days. From a workweek perspective, cybersecurity policy requirements are recurrent demands that likely contribute to cumulative self-regulatory resource depletion. The idea of cumulative resource depletion stemming from cybersecurity policy requirements aligns with empirical evidence that workplace factors that repeatedly impede employees’ goal progress (e.g., ongoing personal conflicts or unfair treatment at work) accelerate their accumulation of fatigue across the workweek (Elfering et al., 2021). It logically follows that employees would have increased sensitivity to the effects of neutralization later in the week, when they have diminished self-regulatory capacities (due to the joint influences of the workweek progression in general and repeated exposure to cybersecurity policy requirements in particular), thus enabling neutralization to translate to reduced cybersecurity policy compliance behavior more easily at this point. Stated differently, the negative relationship between neutralization and cybersecurity policy compliance will be stronger later in the week. It is important to highlight that, as per the idiographic approach, we consider daily instances of both neutralization and cybersecurity policy compliance in predicting their within-person relationships across the week. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H1: The within-person negative relationship between daily neutralization and daily cybersecurity policy compliance behavior will be stronger as the workweek progresses.

## Methodology

The idiographic approach we used to test our hypothesis is a daily-level experience sampling method (ESM), which captures constructs of interest on multiple days over the course of multiple weeks with the objective of measuring fluctuations in these constructs over time (Fisher & To, 2012). Such ESM designs are suited to understanding how phenomena of interest change from one day to another (Beal & Gabriel, 2019).

Participants in our study first completed a one-time online survey that contained measures for demographic variables. About a week later, participants started receiving online surveys on a Monday-Wednesday-Friday (MWF) schedule for a four-week period (maximum of 12 surveys). Each of these daily surveys contained measures for neutralization, cybersecurity policy compliance behavior, and positive and negative affect. The latter two constructs were used as control variables. The four-week time frame exceeds the recommended two-week threshold for ESM studies, which is considered a valid representation of one’s work life (Fisher & To, 2012). We used the every-other-day pattern (MWF) instead of consecutive days to create temporal separation from the previous survey responses and to reduce participant burden. As we later describe, we also used techniques to statistically control for potential carryover effects and time-based trends in our data, following best practices for ESM studies.

Because ESM studies require significant time and commitment from participants, researchers recommend using personal connections for recruitment (Gabriel et al., 2019). In this vein, we leveraged a university alumni database from one of the author’s research partner organizations to recruit participants. The database contained business school graduates from a large university in Malaysia for a 20-year period. We sent study invitation emails to 500 randomly selected individuals from this database, of which 210 agreed to participate by accepting the consent form and completing the one-time survey. The consent form detailed the study requirements and eligibility criteria and emphasized the time commitment involved. Notably, the consent form stressed that participants must complete the entire study to receive any compensation (as an aside, we believe this helped in obtaining all 12 completed daily surveys for those in our final sample). Those who completed the entire study as instructed were given the equivalent of 30 USD.

Thirty-two participants failed to complete at least three daily surveys, which is needed for appropriate statistical modeling (Gabriel et al., 2019); thus, their responses were excluded from any analyses and they received no compensation. Seventy additional participants were removed from any analyses for one or more of the following reasons, which we deemed as indicative of obvious non-conscientious responding: response set bias (i.e., answers exhibiting certain unlikely patterns, such as all 5s or alternating 4s and 5s across the entire survey for at least one daily survey), at least one daily survey completed in an unreasonably short time, or at least one daily survey completed on the wrong day (e.g., a Wednesday survey not completed until Friday). The remaining 108 participants completed all 12 daily surveys (some after receiving email reminders for a particular survey), which resulted in 1296 day-level observations. We further checked time stamps to ensure that these participants completed only one survey per workday and that the completed survey was for the correct workday. We also compared the excluded participants to those in the final sample, considering various demographic characteristics, and found no significant differences. The demographic profile of the final sample is in Appendix B.

## Measures

The daily (MWF) surveys consisted of previously validated scales that we adapted for our daily-level ESM context (e.g., “Today at work, I complied with the requirements of the cybersecurity policy”). Appendix B contains the survey items and the accompanying instructions.

Cybersecurity policy compliance (COMP) behavior was measured with four items adapted from Bulgurcu et al. (2010) and Son (2011). This is a self-report measure, which researchers have argued is valid for ESM studies because “behaviors are often captured most accurately by directly asking people experiencing them day-to-day” (Gabriel et al., 2019, p. 991).<sup>7</sup> For cybersecurity policy compliance in particular, focal participants (as opposed to supervisors or co-workers, for example) are likely the most privy to shortterm changes in their own behavior. Further, by asking participants to report on their compliance behavior for that same day, retrospective biases that might produce a gap between one’s stated and actual behaviors are less of a concern (Beal & Gabriel, 2019). The rationale behind our focus on cybersecurity policy compliance as the behavior of interest, as opposed to policy noncompliance or violation, is in part grounded in the utilization of a selfreport approach. Research indicates that people are more honest when responding to questions about their compliant behaviors (Trevino, 1992), and there is evidence of this phenomenon in cybersecurity contexts (Mou et al., 2022). We wanted to guard against socially desirable responses that may have been induced by repeatedly asking participants over multiple surveys about their engagement in noncompliant behavior.

Neutralization (NEUT) was measured with six items taken from Siponen and Vance’s (2010) neutralization scale. Each item captures one of the six neutralization techniques identified in the criminological literature and used by Siponen and Vance (2010) in their adaptation of neutralization theory to the behavioral cybersecurity context: denial of responsibility, denial of injury, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties, defense of necessity, and metaphor of the ledger. Siponen and Vance’s (2010) scale consists of three items for each dimension. Our shortened, six-item scale follows the ESM convention of using short-form versions of established measures to minimize participant burden from taking surveys repeatedly (Gabriel et al., 2019). Similar shortened neutralization scales have also been used in behavioral cybersecurity studies (e.g., Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; D’Arcy & Teh, 2019; Gwebu et al., 2020) and in other IS studies (Lowry et al., 2016). Our shortened neutralization scale maintains the full content coverage of Siponen and Vance’s (2010) multidimensional scale (i.e., all six neutralization techniques are captured), thereby aligning with ESM best practice of ensuring that a shortened measure is not deficient in terms of content validity (Gabriel et al., 2019).

Positive and negative affect (PA and NA) were measured using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988). Since research has linked daily PA and NA to several daily work behaviors (e.g., Koopman et al., 2016), we included these affective states to help distinguish the influence of neutralization on compliance in our withinperson (Level 1) analyses. We also included age, gender, education, and work experience (i.e., number of years working at the current organization) as between-person (Level 2) control variables.<sup>8</sup> For the between-person (Level 2) variables, their relationships are with average daily cybersecurity policy compliance (i.e., its grand mean across all participants) (see Hofmann et al., 2000 for a statistical explanation). As such, these are between-person relationships and thus had no bearing on our within-person (Level 1) analyses. We included them for completeness, to be consistent with prior behavioral cybersecurity studies, and because our daily compliance measurement and fourweek study time frame offer a somewhat different perspective for the influence of these control variables, as compared to prior between-person findings. Appendix B provides the descriptive statistics and correlations among the study variables and the psychometric test results, which demonstrate the convergent and discriminant validity of the construct measures.

## Analysis

Given the multilevel structure of our data (days, at Level 1, nested within persons, at Level 2), we used hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) for the analyses, which accounts for the nonindependence of the observations. To elaborate on the reasoning for HLM, although our hypothesis considers only a single level (i.e., within-person, or Level 1), participants in our study completed multiple surveys, which yielded dependencies in the data. Multilevel analyses, such as HLM, “explicitly estimate and model the degree of relatedness of observations within the same cluster, thereby correctly estimating the standard errors and eliminating the problem of inflated Type 1 error rates” (McCoach et al., 2018, p. 3). We used the HLM 8.2 software, due to its ease of use for simple multilevel regression models (as opposed to more elaborate multilevel models with mediated relationships, for example), such as our regressions of cybersecurity policy compliance on neutralization. We used HLM 8.2’s default estimator, the restricted maximum likelihood estimation, for the analyses, but note that the results were qualitatively unchanged when we used full maximum likelihood estimation.

Before testing our hypothesis, we examined whether a multilevel analysis was appropriate by partitioning each daylevel (Level 1) variable into its within- and between-person variance components. As shown in Table 3, the percentage of within-person variance is sizeable and in line with many other ESM studies in the organizational and IS literature (e.g., Benlian, 2020; Koopman et al., 2016; McClean et al., 2021; Scott & Barnes, 2011), thus justifying a multilevel modeling approach and a focus on within-person change.

We adopted several best practices for ESM studies (e.g., Beal & Gabriel, 2019; Benlian, 2022; Gabriel et al., 2019; Hofmann et al., 2000) for the HLM regressions of cybersecurity policy compliance on neutralization that we used to test the hypothesis. Namely, we included a lagged compliance score (i.e., the compliance score on the previous MWF survey) to account for serial dependence that may be present in the data. Indeed, because the time intervals between measurement periods in our study (every other workday) are relatively small, it is likely that a prior study day’s compliance measurement influenced a current measurement. This is typical of endogenous variables in daily-level ESM designs like ours (Beal & Weiss, 2003). Accounting for carryover effects also aligns with theory and evidence that cybersecurity policy compliance has a degree of instability over short periods but also has a relatively stable component (i.e., compliance tendencies) (D’Arcy &

Lowry, 2019). In terms of testing the hypothesis, including the lagged compliance score excludes its prior level as an alternative explanation and reduces reverse-causality concerns. Notably, omitting the lagged compliance variable did not affect the results, but we included it in all analyses to provide more robust testing of the hypothesis.

We person-mean centered the Level 1 predictor variables, thus making the variables represent changes in each variable relative to the participant’s average.<sup>9</sup> Person-mean centering is considered “the most appropriate form of centering in situations in which the primary substantive interest involves a Level 1 predictor” (Enders & Tofighi, 2007, p. 128), such as with our hypothesis. The rationale for this position is that person-mean centering removes any between-person (Level 2) variation in the Level 1 predictor, thus yielding an unbiased estimate of its within-person relationship (i.e., Level 1 regression coefficient) with the outcome variable. Our analyses therefore provide a “pure” within-person assessment of the relationship between neutralization and compliance that is unconfounded by between-person factors. In this way, the relationship is not confounded by individual differences such as response tendencies or personality traits, typical culprits of common method variance (CMV) in between-person studies (Gabriel et al., 2019). Since affective states are also sources of CMV (Podsakoff et al., 2003), our inclusion of daily PA and NA as within-person (Level 1) control variables further ameliorate this concern.

Lastly, for the HLM regressions, we modeled all within-person (Level 1) predictors as fixed slopes, meaning that their relationships with compliance are assumed as uniform (nonvarying) across the participants in our sample (i.e., average slope across the sample). Fixed slopes are a standard convention for any non-hypothesized Level 1 variables to reduce model complexity (Gabriel et al., 2019). For neutralization, modeling it as a fixed slope was guided by two factors. First, a fixed slope fits with our hypothesis because we predict the overall withinperson relationship between neutralization and compliance (at different points in the week, as later shown in the analysis) and do not explicitly predict any variance that would exist in the overall trend. Second, we empirically verified that the amount of variance in the within-person relationship between neutralization and compliance was significant at the p < 0.05 level in only one of the three workday subsamples (Wednesday), thus implying that a fixed slope was warranted for assessing this relationship across the workweek. We later revisit this modeling choice to explain the interpretation of our results. The HLM regression results are presented in Table 4.

## Results and Discussion of Empirical Study

Although not hypothesized, we start by assessing the overall within-person relationship between neutralization and compliance that does not account for any progression of this relationship across the workweek. Because this analysis spans the full workweek, we followed ESM best practice and accounted for potential weekly cyclical trends in the outcome variable by including sine and cosine variables as additional

Level 1 control variables.<sup>10</sup> As shown in the full sample results in Table 4, the within-person relationship between neutralization and compliance is negative and significant (b = - 0.11, p < 0.01) after controlling for lagged compliance and positive and negative affect on that particular day and the sine and cosine variables. This finding extends prior cross-sectional, between-person results (e.g., Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Moody et al., 2018; Siponen & Vance, 2010) to within-person patterns over time, suggesting that a positive change in neutralization relative to one’s own daily average is negatively associated with their compliance behavior on that particular day over the four-week period of our study. This person-specific finding is unique compared to prior between-person findings; again, the between-person findings would suggest that, in general, people who neutralize more tend to comply less. In terms of fulfilling one of the objectives of this paper, the full sample analysis results demonstrate how an idiographic approach can help validate a widely used theory (neutralization theory) in behavioral cybersecurity research that implies patterns of behavior within a given person over time.

While supporting the homology of neutralization theory across between- and within-person levels is a valuable robustness exercise, we acknowledge that evidence of the theory holding in this manner is not a major theoretical development. Moreover, past work has found a similar within-person relationship between neutralization and cybersecurity policy noncompliance using a sample of employees from the United States (D’Arcy & Teh, 2019). To differentiate our work and build toward a more substantive theoretical contribution, as per our hypothesis, we further leveraged the time-based aspect of our ESM study. Specifically, to test the hypothesis, we partitioned the data based on the day of the week (MWF) and conducted separate HLM regressions for each day. We conducted the analysis in this multigroup manner to provide a more granular assessment, based on the specific workday, as opposed to testing for an overall weekly trend with a moderation analysis.<sup>11</sup> As shown in the MWF subsamples in Table 4, the magnitude of the negative relationship between neutralization and compliance varies based on workday and intensifies as the workweek progresses (from -0.05 to -0.12 to - 0.21), thus supporting our hypothesis. To elaborate on this support, we found evidence of the stronger negative withinperson relationship between neutralization and compliance as the workweek progresses based on multiple criteria. First, the absolute value of the t-ratio for neutralization increases from

1.00 to 2.52 to 3.06 over the MWF period, thus indicating a stronger effect of neutralization as the workweek progresses. A second way to assess effect size is through standardized regression coefficients (Kelley & Preacher, 2012). In multilevel modeling, the standardized regression coefficient provides information about the magnitude of a relationship between two variables while controlling for other Level 1 variables and the Level 2 nesting (Lorah, 2018). Since HLM does not provide standardized regression coefficients, we manually calculated them. These results support a progressive increase in effect size for neutralization over the workweek (β = -0.06 for Monday; β = -0.14 for Wednesday; β = -0. 25 for Friday).<sup>12</sup>

Returning to our fixed effect modeling choice for the slope of neutralization in the HLM regressions, the interpretation of the results is that, on average across the people in our sample, the negative within-person relationship between neutralization and compliance becomes stronger over the course of the workweek. In other words, the temporal (workweek progression) effect of the neutralization-compliance relationship does not vary across individuals. Importantly, even if we assume the temporal effect does not vary across individuals, our theorizing and analyses are specific to the individual and the individual’s patterns over time, thus preserving the goal of an idiographic approach.<sup>13</sup>

Although we provide evidence to support our hypothesis, it is important to evaluate the results and the conclusions in light of the study’s limitations. For one, we did not capture daily resource depletion or a related measure and therefore rely on theory and past empirical findings to support the idea of diminished self-regulatory resources over the workweek. We believe this is a plausible explanation for our study context because employees typically view cybersecurity policy requirements in a negative light. Hence, the self-regulatory resources needed for cybersecurity policy compliance in particular should be diminished over the course of the workweek. As an intent of this paper is to demonstrate the theoretical insight that can be gained by applying an idiographic approach to behavioral cybersecurity research—in this case, providing a more granular, time-based explanation of the influence of neutralization in this context—we view our empirical study as a call for future studies that test the veracity of our theoretical explanation, rather than as definitive evidence of the phenomenon.

A further limitation is that our analyses examine concurrent relationships between constructs based on a single survey collected each day. Although an idiographic approach can be helpful in unraveling causality questions, the study design characteristics play a key role in determining the extent of the insights that can be gleaned (Mitchell & James, 2001; Polyhart & Vandenberg, 2010). We provide some evidence for our proposed causal directions by using lagged compliance variables in the HLM regressions.<sup>14</sup> However, the ESM design we used provides only a partial view of the causal ordering we hypothesize. This is a limitation of many daily-level ESM studies (Beal & Gabriel, 2019). Idiographic research can support alternate research designs (e.g., participants taking multiple surveys a day, each survey at different times and containing different construct measures) that establish temporal separation between predictor and outcome variables and thus enable stronger assessments of causality and help to ameliorate CMV concerns. Regarding CMV, it is a known issue in behavioral cybersecurity research (Cram et al., 2019), and in terms of our ESM design, a potential concern is that capturing the independent and dependent variables on the same survey instrument repeatedly over time could worsen CMV. ESM designs such as ours, however, allow researchers to capture transient factors, such as moods or emotions, that are known culprits of CMV. CMV can therefore be controlled for, to an extent, on a particular day or at a particular time, as we did with our daily PA and NA control variables. And again, the personmean centering that we used removes some traditional sources of CMV, such as response tendencies or personality traits, that typically plague single-source research designs. We encourage researchers to employ such techniques, whenever feasible, when adopting an ideographic approach, especially in cases where all the data come from the same source.

Additionally, we did not explicitly consider stable differences that might modulate the within-person relationships we found regarding neutralization and compliance. Multilevel analysis is ideal for examining such cross-level relations and can also offer increased theoretical nuance. For example, future research could consider whether stable characteristics shared by certain types of people (e.g., personality differences, different job positions, responsibilities, characteristics of the organization)

correspond with an increased or decreased neutralizationcompliance relationship. Importantly, from a statistical perspective, the examination of such cross-level relations involving individual differences would only be applicable if the neutralization-compliance relationship varies among individuals, as we found for our Wednesday subsample. More broadly, as a further test of the homology of neutralization theory in the cybersecurity context, future research could explore potential moderators of the within-person neutralization to compliance relationship without accounting for workweek progression.

Future studies could also employ alternative designs that draw on longer-term datasets—perhaps over months or years—to capture more slowly evolving aspects of behavioral security relationships, such as organizational leadership changes and updated training programs, which would be valuable to consider as a supplement to the shorter-term relationship changes captured in our dataset. Such a study would be another instantiation of the idiographic approach to behavioral cybersecurity research that we advocate in this paper.

Finally, the data in our empirical study was based on selfreports provided by individuals who willingly opted to continue participating, which introduces the potential for bias (e.g., retrospective bias, social desirability) in our results. Although we undertook procedural steps to address these concerns (e.g., participants reported their compliance behavior on the same day, thus assuaging retrospective bias concerns; we measured compliance as opposed to noncompliance or violation to reduce socially desirable responding), collecting additional data, such as objective behaviors, independent managerial corroborations, and/or digital trace evidence, could further add to the robustness of the results.

## Idiographic Behavioral Cybersecurity Research: What Context and Theories?

Although we provide the preceding empirical study as an illustration of the benefits of an idiographic approach to behavioral cybersecurity research, an important question remains: What contexts and theories represent the most promising opportunities to reveal novel behavioral cybersecurity insights through the use of an idiographic approach? In this section, we turn our attention to a discussion of the considerations for researchers who choose to pursue this line of inquiry.

In general, we view an idiographic approach as a complement to a nomothetic approach in behavioral cybersecurity research rather than an alternative or replacement. Just as a nomothetic approach may be suitable for determining patterns of behavior across a population of individuals at a point in time, we posit that an idiographic approach has the potential (as illustrated in our empirical example) to expand the field’s theoretical insights and reveal novel practical implications. Although a fundamental aspect of this approach is collecting longitudinal data and using alternative analysis techniques, we suggest that an even more refined focus can be developed that directs behavioral cybersecurity research toward contexts where an idiographic approach shows the most promise. By expanding the conceptual “toolkit” of behavioral cybersecurity research, novel insights and enhanced theory development can result. Based on the insights that emerged from our empirical example, as well as existing research outside of IS that advocates for an idiographic approach, we suggest that the following three contexts are particularly ripe opportunities for continuing the direction we propose in this study (refer to Table 5 for a summary).

## Context #1: Where (Real)Time Matters

An idiographic approach is optimally employed in circumstances where research constructs are subject to the potential for variance over time. As such, predictors that are unlikely to change substantially over the course of a study, such as education level or topics that are of a historical nature (e.g., have you ever violated a cybersecurity policy), are not generally suited to an idiographic approach (Hoffman & Stawski, 2009). When considering behavioral cybersecurity research, constructs such as employee attitudes, energy levels, affect, and emotions would appear to be an ideal focus of an idiographic approach, since these commonly fluctuate (e.g., I was in a relaxed mood this morning, but now I feel anxious). Although past research using a nomothetic approach has uncovered group differences (e.g., employees with a positive cybersecurity attitude tend to comply with policies more than those with a negative attitude—Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Guo et al., 2011), there has been little investigation (one exception is work by D’Arcy and Teh (2019) into how the changing emotions of an individual might alter their policy compliance over time (e.g., when Jane is relaxed, she tends to comply with cybersecurity policies, but when she is anxious, she tends to violate policies).

Importantly, methodologists have acknowledged the discrepancies that exist between self-reported measurements of an individual’s emotion at (or shortly after) the point when it occurs (e.g., I am currently under stress in my job), compared to emotion data collected on a retrospective basis (e.g., over the past year, I found my job to be stressful) (Robinson & Clore, 2002). That is, emotions such as anxiety or anger can be fleeting, and measurements are best made as they occur or closely thereafter (Schwarz, 2012). If a study were to investigate the connection between employee emotions and policy compliance, it would be critical to undertake measurements of participant emotions at various points in time (and thus, potentially, in different emotional states) to account for the changing nature of the predictor variables. Similarly, in understanding the consequences on employee behavior, research that focuses on a real-time, “what is happening now” perspective could potentially be more insightful (and reliable) than studying how participants behaved in the past or how participants anticipate they may behave in the future (Schwarz, 2012). Thus, where time itself can bring about change in cybersecurity variables of interest, an idiographic approach may be able to deconstruct the underlying elements of those variables to provide more theoretical clarity. Data collection methods such as the ESM approach used in our empirical study would be well suited to this context since multiple measurements are collected from participants over a short period of time.

Another consideration related to time is the potential for a static cybersecurity initiative to decline in effectiveness over time. Past research outside of cybersecurity suggests that maintaining behavior changes in the days and weeks following a managerial intervention may be subject to a variety of factors, including the building of habits, the perceived cost of the behavior change, and ongoing reinforcement (Carden & Wood, 2018; Frey & Rogers, 2014; Schubert, 2017). For example, at the point in time when new sanctions are implemented for employees who are caught violating a cybersecurity policy, the sanctions may be found to be effective at encouraging compliance. However, as employees have more time to reappraise the situational factors that surround them (e.g., I am not likely to get caught for a violation), it is plausible that the influence of perceived sanctions becomes weaker as time passes. Even though the cybersecurity controls remain unchanged, it may be that employee emotions and affective factors become stronger predictors as time elapses. As such, drawing on an idiographic approach that considers individual change over time (e.g., repeated measure surveys) may provide a longerterm behavioral view that can complement an initial, pointin-time perspective. Practically, the results of such research can provide important insights to managers seeking to harness the benefits of those cybersecurity initiatives that have a long-lasting influence on employee behavior while avoiding initiatives that may quickly fade in effectiveness.

We also note that a promising opportunity to employ an idiographic approach concerns the idea of System 1 (nonconscious, automatic) and System 2 (deliberate, rational) thinking. Since many cybersecurity breaches stem from unintentional, nonmalicious employee actions, Dennis and Minas (2018) suggest that attaining a more robust understanding of System 1 thinking is necessary. However, they argue that System 1 thinking tends to be downplayed in cybersecurity research, despite being fundamental in understanding how specific individuals respond to changing contexts and stimuli (Kahneman, 2011). In studies where participants are characterized by a range of different background experiences (e.g., mixed levels of familiarity with phishing emails) and changing contexts (e.g., some employees are more susceptible to clicking on phishing emails depending on the time of day) that could plausibly lead to different cybersecurity behavior, an idiographic approach may lend itself to unique System 1 insights. That is, by collecting repeated measures data, researchers could more effectively account for the different contexts that could otherwise confound the results of a cross-sectional study. As a result, exclusive reliance on a nomothetic approach may inadvertently overemphasize the importance of System 2 theory constructs and underestimate the importance of System 1 thinking in cross-sectional surveys. To better explain employee behavior over time, researchers should consider an idiographic approach that contains more time-variant factors.

From a theoretical perspective, this real-time context can enable a within-person, longitudinal validation of the most prominent foundations used in behavioral cybersecurity research, such as we did with neutralization theory. Other candidates are the theory of interpersonal behavior and the theory of reasoned action, which are recognized as being among the foremost foundations for behavioral cybersecurity research (Cram et al., 2019; Moody et al., 2018) and seemingly have elements of dynamics and change. The theory of interpersonal behavior (Triandis, 1977) examines how emotions impact behavior (e.g., Moody & Siponen, 2013), and the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) assesses how attitudes drive behavior (e.g., Guo et al., 2011; Siponen et al., 2014). Taken together, the use of an idiographic approach could reveal unique insights in research that draw on these theoretical foundations or others that place importance on fluctuating employee attitudes, energy levels, and emotions. Practically, insights from the idiographic work in this context can aid organizations by providing insights into the impact that fluctuating employee emotions can have on the effectiveness of cybersecurity initiatives. For example, managers who are aware that employee energy levels decline as the workweek progresses may choose to run cybersecurity training programs at the beginning of the week rather than at the end, when staff are increasingly tired. Likewise, if employee attitudes toward a cybersecurity initiative tend to hold steady for a month after implementation but begin to decline after two months, the increased risk to the organization could provide an impetus for managers to circulate reminders to reiterate the importance of compliance at that point.

## Context #2: Where the Individual Is the Focus

In addition to the real-time context described above, an idiographic approach in behavioral cybersecurity research primarily applies in the context of individual-level phenomena (e.g., why does a particular employee comply with a policy?) rather than those pertaining to groups (e.g., why does the marketing department comply with a policy, but the finance department does not?) or organizations (e.g., how can the organization drive better overall policy compliance?). Although individual-level phenomena can be studied using a nomothetic approach, again, doing so can typically only provide insights into between-person relationships (Molenaar & Campbell, 2009). That is, where a person is the context, a within-person (idiographic) approach can provide distinct insights, whereas, in group contexts, a between-person (nomothetic) approach is typically more suitable (Hoffman & Stawski, 2009; Molenaar & Campbell, 2009).

When considering behavioral cybersecurity research, several of the prominent theories arise from psychology (Moody et al., 2018) and focus on individual-level effects, even though the vast majority of research uses cross-sectional data. This is consistent with past commentaries outside of IS that suggest the majority of psychological theories posit within-person effects (suggesting a fit with an idiographic approach). However, research tends to utilize a nomothetic approach, which “can result in errors of inference (e.g., results obtained from individual data are used to make group-level inferences, or vice versa)” (Curran & Bauer, 2011, p. 587). This is not to say that the existing research is poorly designed, but that the use of an idiographic approach can provide a supplementary perspective not only into how employees behave at a point in time but how they behave over time. Future use of repeated measure surveys, longitudinal field experiments, and latent growth models (Zheng et al., 2014) would be appropriate methods to apply in this context.

For example, protection motivation theory (Rogers, 1975) considers the extent to which individuals respond to a fear appeal (i.e., perceived severity, vulnerability, response efficacy, and self-efficacy) when complying with cybersecurity policies (Moody et al., 2018). Most existing cybersecurity research drawing on protection motivation theory utilizes a nomothetic approach, with conclusions such as: Employees with a higher level of self-efficacy toward cybersecurity are more likely to comply with cybersecurity policies than employees with lower self-efficacy (Herath & Rao, 2009b; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Siponen et al., 2014). However, this between-person result does not reveal whether an individual who experiences a change in selfefficacy (e.g., from low to high due to a helpful training program or from high to low due to a new, confusing software program) will experience a corresponding change in behavior or behavioral intent. In fact, research outside of IS has found that general self-efficacy can have a negative relationship with performance when evaluated in a within-person manner over time, whereas this relationship is typically positive in between-person analyses (Vancouver et al., 2002). Only an idiographic approach can reveal such important insights, which can be of practical use to managers by contributing to the improvement of the design of cybersecurity initiatives (e.g., training) that directly consider how individuals respond to change.

## Context #3: Where Variability Can Come from Different Sources

An idiographic approach can also provide complementary insights to a nomothetic approach in cases where withinperson variations are theoretically distinct from the variations associated with between-person theories. As suggested by Zaheer et al. (1999), “just as it is true that a theory that applies at the industry level may not hold at the firm level of analysis, so it is true that theory that is valid for one time interval may not be valid for another” (p. 725). This context is particularly relevant when importance is placed on understanding how much variability in a cybersecurity behavior is driven by between-person versus within-person factors.

As a case in point, consider the idea of cybersecurity fatigue, which is a new topic in behavioral cybersecurity research (Cram et al., 2021). Cybersecurity fatigue refers to employees who become tired of and disillusioned with the cybersecurity activities within their organizations due to factors such as policy inconvenience (i.e., the extent that a policy impedes an employee’s work) and cybersecurity policy illegitimacy (i.e., the extent that a policy is viewed as unfair). In the theoretical model of Cram et al. (2021), policy inconvenience and illegitimacy positively relate to cybersecurity fatigue at the between-person level. One possibility is that these between-person relationships are distinct from their within-person relationships. This line of reasoning suggests that “what applies in aggregate is not necessarily informative on what is true in general, where the latter implies it is true for each and every individual in the population” (Hamaker, 2012, p. 43). For example, a positive between-person relationship may be found across a population (e.g., employees who perceive cybersecurity activities as illegitimate tend to have a higher level of cybersecurity fatigue). Based on this result, we might expect this positive relationship to hold at a within-person level, such that as an individual’s perceptions of legitimacy increase over time, so will their fatigue.

However, drawing within-person conclusions based on between-person data is only valid under very strict circumstances and is “very unlikely to hold in psychological practice” (Hamaker, 2012, p. 48). As such, it remains possible that, while still satisfying the positive between-person relationship, we could simultaneously find a null or a negative within-person effect. For example, cybersecurity illegitimacy could be somewhat of a “hygiene factor” at an individual level. That is, employees reach a tipping point at which the relationship between illegitimate cybersecurity activities and fatigue is established. Subsequent variations in their level of illegitimacy may rise and fall, but as long as the level remains above the minimum, fatigue-inducing threshold, the changes do not directly translate to more fatigue. In a theoretical sense, it may be that employees adapt to the perceived illegitimacy of cybersecurity requirements over time in that they learn to “deal with them” so that their fatigue is less impacted. This theoretical reasoning resonates with the idea of an accommodation effect (Bleich et al., 2003), in which individuals learn to cope with adverse circumstances in their environment over time (e.g., threat of terrorism).

Another explanation for conflicting between- and withinperson results is dependent on the relative contributions of the stable, trait-like factors that distinguish one group of people from another (e.g., gender, department, age, work experience) and the transient, state-like explanations for individual changes over time (e.g., moods, emotions) (Hoffman & Stawski, 2009). For example, in a crosssectional study, participant responses might suggest that both employee age (a stable trait) and attitude (an unstable state) positively relate to policy compliance. In the scenario where both age and attitude have roughly the same relationship in terms of correlation and variance with compliance, the between-person effect would typically hold at a within-person level (Geiser et al., 2015; Hamaker, 2012; Steyer et al., 1999). This is because the longitudinal nature of within-person research would be oriented around the potential variance of the unstable state variable (attitude). However, in an alternative scenario where age has a negative correlation with compliance and attitude has a positive correlation, if the cross-sectional result is dominated by much larger between-person variances, it could mask the contradictory result of the within-person data (Hamaker, 2012). Techniques such as random intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM)<sup>15</sup> (Hamaker et al., 2015), which account for both stable and transient constructs, could be applied in this context. Likewise, simulations could be used to evaluate the existence of biases that may be present in a particular method (e.g., Newman, 2003; Saravakos & Sirakoulis, 2014).

However, such contradictory results could also suggest the existence of a confounding variable that influences a betweenperson relationship when compared to a within-person relationship. For example, using a cross-sectional, experimental design, Jenkins et al. (2021) found that the required effort of the employee moderated the relationship between intentions and cybersecurity policy compliance. That is, individuals with a high required effort were characterized by a weaker relationship between intentions and compliance when compared to individuals with a low level of required effort. The presence of this moderator in some individuals— but not others—could lead to inconsistent within-person results. An idiographic approach that uses a multilevel design can uncover potential cross-level moderating influences that might be masked by nomothetic approaches. In particular, as we advocated for in extending our empirical study, multilevel statistical methods (e.g., HLM) can test for such cross-level relationships, such as where a stable factor (e.g., age, personality, cybersecurity culture) influences the strength of a within-person relationship.

Overall, we suggest that more broadly considering the sources of variations in cybersecurity behavior is important, particularly when that variation may differ depending on the adoption of nomothetic or idiographic approaches. This awareness could lead to additional attention being paid to investigating potential moderators of some of the wellaccepted cybersecurity construct relationships. From a practical perspective, such insights could aid managers in tailoring cybersecurity initiatives that work not only in the short term but have a greater chance of success over time. Finally, although beyond the scope of our paper, we believe that our overall observations on the value of an idiographic approach and recommendations for its adoption—in contexts where (real) time matters, where the individual is the focus, and where variability can come from different sources—may generalize across other domains of IS research. We encourage future studies to consider how the principles we discuss in this paper may help validate and extend theories used in other domains of IS research.

## Conclusion

Despite the valuable insights derived from the existing behavioral cybersecurity research, which relies almost exclusively on a nomothetic approach, this paper seeks to highlight the value that idiographic research could reveal in this realm through a series of conceptual arguments and an empirical example. We tested an established theory (neutralization) using a longitudinal research design (ESM) and a within-person analysis. Our theoretical explanation and results support a more granular application of neutralization theory in the cybersecurity context afforded by an idiographic approach. More broadly, we encourage researchers to not only avoid inferring that cross-sectional relationships will hold over time but also to draw on an idiographic approach to determine the novel insights that can be derived from the core theories used in behavioral cybersecurity research through within-person, longitudinal techniques. For practice, our findings provide an explanation for why cybersecurity countermeasures that seem promising at implementation can show inconsistent effectiveness over time, which managers can leverage to implement more effective cybersecurity initiatives that account for within-person change. In setting the stage for future studies that draw on an idiographic approach, we suggest that researchers pay particular attention to situations where (real) time matters (e.g., fluctuating employee emotions), where the individual is the focus (e.g., individual behavioral changes are of theoretical or practical interest) and where variability can come from different sources (e.g., between-person theoretical explanations differ from within-person theoretical explanations).
