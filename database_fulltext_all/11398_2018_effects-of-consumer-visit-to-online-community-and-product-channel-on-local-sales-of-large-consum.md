## A B S T R A C T

Online intermediaries have become important information sources for consumers' purchase decision making. However, the economic values of consumer visits to two online intermediaries, namely, online community and product channel, have not been well studied. By collecting a dataset on consumer visits from a large real estate website to match with local offline housing sales data, we empirically explore the respective, relative, and interaction effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods. We control for relevant factors, account for potential endogeneity issues, and perform various robustness checks to validate the consistency of our findings. Our results show that consumer visits to online communities have a more significant effect than those to product channels in driving sales. However, the interaction effect of consumer visits to these two online intermediaries on sales is negative. We also find that consumer website-related experience has a significant moderating effect on the relationship between consumer visits to product channels and sales. Our findings provide important theoretical contributions and managerial implications.

## Introduction

Online intermediaries have become popular in recent years. We distinguish two types of online intermediaries, i.e., online community and product channel. Online communities are defined as websites containing and sharing communication contents, including product reviews and discussions from consumers (e.g., shopping experiences, individual preferences, as well as questions and answers). Product channels are defined as websites showing and exhibiting product information, which embed product descriptions and marketing activities by marketers (e.g., key attributes of products, promotional activities and persuasive advertising). Online communities and product channels have two major differences. First, for consumers, online communities serve as a venue for free communications with marketers and other members to become better acquainted with the products, whereas product channels do not interact with consumers. Hence, they are only used to obtain objective product information. Second, for marketers, online communities provide approaches to execute mutual communications with consumers, whereas product channel is a tool for them to convey information unidirectionally to consumers. Given these functionalities, user interaction contents in online communities usually supplement product descriptive information from marketers in product channels. Thus, firms seek to set up both online communities and product channels, typically on one online platform, which can provide two information sources that allow consumers to obtain more comprehensive information that reduces uncertainties in their purchase decisions. This study is motivated by the lack of empirical research that investigate the effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on the sales of large consumer goods particularly on three essential aspects, despite consumers and marketers' prevalent use of online intermediaries.

First, we attempt to determine whether consumer visits to online intermediaries will drive offline sales. Previous studies have focused on the effects of visiting and browsing behaviors on repeated visits (Chatterjee et al., 2003; Moe and Fader, 2004a), purchase conversion rates (Moe and Fader, 2004b), and depth of search (Johnson et al., 2004). However, studies that focus on the influence of consumer visits on offline sales is scarce. Additionally, few previous studies have emphasized on unraveling the relative and potential interaction effects between online communities and product channels. Therefore, we focus on consumer visits to these two different types of online intermediaries on offline sales.

Second, we aim to fill the research gap by understanding the unexplored effect of online communities on the sales of large consumer goods. Prior studies have examined mainly the economic influences of product reviews and online discussions on small consumer goods (Chintagunta et al., 2010; Dewan and Ramaprasad, 2014; Dinner et al., 2014; Moe and Trusov, 2011; Zhu and Zhang, 2010). They have shown that marketer-generated information in product channels may increase consumers' purchase intentions and drive sales further (Goh et al., 2013; Holzwarth et al., 2006; Lee and Kim, 2010). However, most studies have overlooked large consumer goods, which are also an important product type in the consumer market. Large consumer goods are expensive special products that cannot be purchased repeatedly in the short term. Because of its high prices, consumers' search intensity increases (Richardson and Zumpano, 2012) and the time spent before deciding on the purchase of large consumer goods (which are more expensive) lengthens, requiring additional complete information and careful consideration compared with small ones (which are cheaper). Large consumer goods require more complex procedures, additional time, and energy for consumers to accomplish the transaction processes. Thus, our second focus is to unravel the unclear effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on the sales of large consumer goods.

Third, we assume that consumer characteristics (i.e., consumer website-related experience) may have a moderating effect on the sales effect of consumer visits to online communities and product channels in three aspects. First, large consumer goods are costly; thus, purchasing these goods requires more product comparisons and considerations. Consumers with website-related experience are more familiar with the information and members on the platform. They can easily locate valuable information. Specifically, information in online communities and product channels has a greater value for website-related experienced consumers, and hence, has a more significant effect on them as compared with novices who are inexperienced with the website. Second, consumers with website-related experience will probably use the Internet as their primary information source, and thus, are expected to be influenced by information from online intermediaries. Third, consumers with website-related experience visit the online intermediaries frequently, which indicate their interest in the website and may have stronger purchase intentions. Thus, they will be more affected by the website's contents. To examine our above assumptions, we aim to explore whether consumer characteristics (i.e., consumer website-related experience) will moderate the effect of consumer visits to online intermediaries on sales.

Based on the aforementioned research gaps, we seek to answer three research questions: (1) How do consumer visits to online communities and product channels affect the sales of large consumer goods? (2) What are the relative and interaction effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods? (3) How do consumer characteristics moderate the effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales?

To answer our research questions, we collect data on consumer visits to online communities and product channels from a large real estate website in China to match with local offline sales of new houses from the real estate trading center. Our econometric specification represents the sales area of new houses as a function of consumer visits to the new house online community and the new house product channel. In particular, we use instrumental variables to address endogeneity concerns. We also perform various robustness checks to validate the consistency of our findings.

Our study has several notable findings. First, consumer visits to both online intermediaries positively affect the sales of large consumer goods. The elasticities of sales with respect to visits to online communities and product channels are estimated to be 1.620 and 1.458, respectively. Second, consumer visits to online communities are more effective than those to product channels in driving sales. Third, although consumer visits to both online communities and product channels lead to an increase in sales. However, visits to these two online intermediaries will decrease each other's positive sales effect. In other words, consumer visits to online communities and product channels have a substitute relationship. Finally, consumer characteristics have no moderating effect on the relationship between consumer visits to online communities and sales, whereas consumer characteristics have a positive moderating effect on the sales effect of consumer visits to product channels, which implies that visits of experienced consumers to product channels have a significant effect on sales relative to those of novices.

Overall, our study has several considerable contributions. First, our study contributes to the online intermediary literature by distinguishing between two different sources of online information (i.e., online communities and product channels), and exploring empirically the sales effect of consumer visiting behaviors to the two online intermediaries. We also propose a more integrative understanding regarding the relative and interaction effect of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on offline sales. Second, our research contributes to the extant literature by investigating the effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods. Third, our research is a leading effort in the investigation of the moderating effect of consumer characteristics on the sales effect of consumer visits to online intermediaries. We also provide critical managerial implications for firms to develop marketing strategies for different types of online intermediaries. Lastly, we offer some discussions on potential policy implications.

## Literature review

## Effects of visits to online intermediaries

Studies on website visiting and browsing behaviors have explored repeated visits (Chatterjee et al., 2003; Moe and Fader, 2004a), purchase conversion rates (Moe and Fader, 2004b), and the depth of search (Johnson et al., 2004), whereas others have investigated factors affecting website visit duration (Bucklin and Sismeiro, 2003; Danaher et al., 2006). Moe and Fader (2004b) showed that frequent consumer visits will lead to high probability of eventual purchase. Montgomery et al. (2004) suggested that visiting paths may reflect consumer's goals, which can be useful in predicting future movements on a website.

Other studies have focused on the effect of visits to advertisements in the online context. The three major categories of online advertising include display advertising (Danaher, 2007; Manchanda et al., 2006), paid search advertising (Rutz and Bucklin, 2011), and email advertising (Bonfrer and Drèze, 2009). Display advertising is the most similar one to online information in product channels in our setting. When a user is exposed to a display-type advertisement, a click-through typically takes the consumer to another site by presenting additional detailed information on the product or service (Chatterjee et al., 2003). Manchanda et al. (2006) matched individual-level consumer visits to the display advertisement to their purchase transactions and found that display advertising accelerates the timing of purchase behaviors made by existing consumers. In addition to within-website visits, other studies have investigated browsing behaviors across multiple websites. Park and Fader (2004) developed a stochastic timing model between two competing sites and concluded that leveraging information from one site can help explain consumer behaviors at another through cross-site visiting behaviors. Other researchers have reported an opposite view that overall extant of cross-site searching is low (Johnson et al., 2004).

Recently, researchers have placed more emphasis on consumers' participation and engagement to further explore the effects of online intermediaries. Rishika et al. (2013) examined empirically the effect of consumers' social media participation on consumer visit frequency and profitability. They found that consumer participation effect is significant with high activity level on the social media site. Ray et al. (2014) showed that a user's revisit to an online community indicates the sense of engagement, which is positively affected by his knowledge self-efficacy, self-identity verification, and community identification.

## Effects of online communities

With the rapid development of information technology, the Internet serves as a venue for interactions among consumers and between consumers and the firm (Trusov et al., 2009). Online communities contain consumer discussions on products or brands. Other consumers visit online communities and user-generated contents may influence their purchase decisions.

Our research focuses on a critical question that interests both researchers and practitioners. A large number of studies have examined how the contents of online communities affect product sales, such as books (Chevalier and Mayzlin, 2006), movies (Chintagunta et al., 2010; Liu, 2006; Moon et al., 2010), music (Dewan and Ramaprasad, 2014; Dhar and Chang, 2009), video games (Zhu and Zhang, 2010), beer (Clemons et al., 2006), apparels (Dinner et al., 2014; Goh et al., 2013), and beauty products (Moe and Trusov, 2011). Generally, they have indicated how consumer reviews and discussions lead to sales. However, prior empirical research has focused on the sales effect on small consumer goods. Large consumer goods are expensive special products that cannot be purchased repeatedly in the short term. As discussed earlier, consumer purchases of large consumer goods require more comprehensive information and consideration. Therefore, we expect potential differences in the effect of consumer visits to online communities on sales between small and large consumer goods.

## Effects of product channels

Internet advertising and online product exhibitions have become an important part of the marketing mix of most companies. Product channels are one kind of online intermediaries, which maintain detailed descriptions and related information on products or services. Most marketers and researchers seek to determine whether these online information offering pages will persuade potential consumers to make the final purchase decisions (Russo and Chaxel, 2010; Wu et al., 2009).

Some studies have centered on the economic effects of product descriptions and marketing activities from online marketers. Online firms usually play the role of intermediary by bringing content creators, consumers, and marketers together (Albuquerque et al., 2012). Specifically, Holzwarth et al. (2006) indicated that marketer-generated avatar sales agent leads to higher user satisfaction with the firm and their products, thereby leading to higher purchase intentions. Other marketers send emails that contain descriptive and promotional information on products to consumers to encourage them to become better acquainted with the products and drive sales further (Lee and Kim, 2010).

Other studies have focused on the relative and interaction effects of communication contents in online communities and descriptive information in product channels on economic outcomes. For instance, Chen and Xie (2008) revealed that a sufficiently informative review information will connect two types of information on products, reviews, and product descriptions. Trusov et al. (2009) concluded that word-of-mouth (WOM) elasticity is about 20 times higher than the elasticity of marketing events, and 30 times that of media appearances. Goh et al. (2013) found that consumer-generated reviews information richness and valence have a larger effect than those of marketer-generated descriptions information in driving consumers' purchase expenditure. Lu et al. (2013) found a substitute relationship between WOM volume and coupon offerings and a complementary relationship between WOM volume and keyword advertising.

## Moderators in effects of online intermediaries

Prior studies have reported several moderators in the effect of online intermediaries. For instance, some have examined the moderating effect of product type. Zhu and Zhang (2010) indicated that online reviews are more influential for less popular games. Phang et al. (2013) investigated how patterns of social interaction among consumers discussing niche products enhance consumption intentions of products. Dewan and Ramaprasad (2014) indicated that the negative relationship between song buzz and sales is stronger for niche music relative to mainstream music, and Adjei et al. (2010) examined the valence of information exchanged as a moderating factor.

Other studies have focused on the moderating effect of consumer characteristics. Some studies have investigated expert-novice differences in online information search (Jaillet, 2003) or decision aids in online search (Hubl and Trifts, 2000), including the use of intermediaries and shopping bots (Rowley, 2000). User experience is also a critical moderator that interests researchers. Users with task experience have more efficient and analytical search strategies (Fenichel, 1981; Kulviwat et al., 2004). Experience allows consumers to identify particular elements of a task that make them more selective in their search behaviors (Ellis and Haugan, 1997). Consumers with additional experience are more familiar with the usefulness of information available on the Internet compared with novices (Hammond et al., 1998), and more proficient in locating websites during search tasks than novice ones (Lazonder et al., 2000). Users with more significant Internet experience conduct a large proportion of their search on the Internet (Klein and Ford, 2003) and will probably be influenced by online reviews (Zhu and Zhang, 2010).

## Research model and hypotheses

Our research model in Fig. 1 focuses on the sales effect of online intermediaries, including respective, relative, and interaction effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels, as well as the moderating effect of consumer characteristics.

## Effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods

Online community is a communication medium that allows consumers to generate topics and ask questions on products or services. An increasing number of members participate in providing posts that discuss and answer questions on topics based on their own preferences and prior usage experiences. Visits to online communities enable the observation of shopping experiences and evaluations posted by other consumers. Marketers provide basic descriptions (e.g., appearance information, such as shapes and colors) and detailed information (e.g., usage instructions and announcements) on products in product channels, which are available for consumers to search online. Consumers visit online communities and product channels to gain related information on goods. Several visits and comparisons may reduce consumers' uncertainties and could determine their final purchase decisions.

Large expensive consumer goods cannot be purchased repeatedly in the short term, and thus, consumers must have additional information for consideration and assessment. Consumers with additional visits to online communities and product channels would have acquired additional information that would lead to a higher probability of purchases (Algesheimer et al., 2010) and additional spending (Manchanda et al., 2015; Phang et al., 2013), which will eventually drive product sales. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H1. Consumer visits to online communities have a positive effect on sales of large consumer goods.

H2. Consumer visits to product channels have a positive effect on sales of large consumer goods.

Relative and interaction effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods

We posit that the effect of consumer visits to online communities is stronger than the effect of consumer visits to product channels. Two sets of arguments support our hypothesis. First, marketers' provisions of information in product channels only exhibit basic descriptive information or promotions that persuade consumers to purchase (Goh et al., 2013; Russo and Chaxel, 2010). The strict paradigm always limits the contents of product channels, which sometimes would seem to be excessively simple and prim. Therefore, consumers often acquire incomplete product information (Kivetz and Simonson, 2000) and have to make purchases despite uncertainties (Narayanan et al., 2007). Generally speaking, consumers are averse to losses (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) and will prefer additional information to reduce uncertainties. Normally, platform operators have little control over the display format of communication contents (Benlian et al., 2012) and allow consumers to post product evaluations based on their own preferences. Specifically, members in online communities can use flexible text lengths and contents to meet different needs of a border group of consumers (Chen and Xie, 2008). Therefore, communication contents in online communities, which include rich information, such as usage experiences and discussions, are more informative compared with descriptive information in product channels. Hence, visits to online communities will probably allow consumers to identify the advantages and disadvantages of products, which will increase their confidence to purchase. Second, messages in online communities can be updated constantly over time. However, the relatively stable product descriptive information in product channels cannot be changed frequently. Therefore, visits to online communities can satisfy consumers' additional requirements than those to product channels. Additionally, large consumer goods are an expensive kind of special product that needs to be considered thoroughly. Thus, to reduce consumer uncertainties and assist in consumers' purchase decision making, consumer purchases of large consumer goods require more informative and adaptable messages. Considering the differences between the two information sources, we posit that consumer visits to online communities may have a stronger effect on sales of large consumer goods than those to product channels. Thus, we arrive at the following hypothesis:

H3. Consumer visits to online communities have a stronger effect on sales of large consumer goods relative to those to product channels.

As discussed above, consumer visits to both online intermediaries may positively affect sales. Online communities and product channels function in different ways in driving sales. Contents in online communities share mainly usage experiences on products, whereas messages in product channels allow marketers to exhibit advertisements and promotions. They offer complementary and corporately different information for consumers. Thus, when consumers feel that the information on a product in an online community (a product channel) is insufficient, they will prefer searching for additional information in another intermediary. Observing information from two different sources will largely reduce consumers' uncertainties (Narayanan et al., 2007) and stimulate additional purchases. In summary, we expect a positive interaction effect of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on product sales.

H4. A positive interaction effect exists between consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods.

## Moderating effect of consumer characteristics

Our research focuses on the moderating effect of consumer characteristics of whether the consumer has website-related experience. First, the sales effect of consumer visits to online intermediaries varies according to users' familiarity level (Balasubramanian et al., 2005). Consumers with website-related experience are familiar with the website and other users, and they identify themselves as organizational insiders with their regular visits to online intermediaries (Bhattacharya and Sankar, 2003). Thus, they clearly know how to obtain useful and valuable information in online communities and product channels compared with novices. Therefore, experienced consumers may obtain more adaptable and complete messages that will stimulate their final purchase decisions. Specifically, information in online communities and product channels is valued by consumers with website-related experience. Second, some studies confirm that website-related experience is positively correlated with the frequency in the use of the Internet for information (Palmquist and Kim, 2000) and search tasks (Lazonder et al., 2000). Given that consumers with website-related experience prefer using the Internet as their primary information source (Bart et al., 2005; Zhu and Zhang, 2010), they will probably be influenced by the information in online intermediaries. Information from online intermediaries will increase their purchase intentions resulting in high product sales. Third, frequent visits by consumers with website-related experience imply consumers' interest in the website and its products, which may have stronger purchase intention compared with novices. Novices have no strong desire or determination to purchase; therefore, their first visit aims to understand the basic functions of the online intermediary. Summing above, we hypothesize the following:

H5. Consumer website-related experience has a moderating effect, such that an increase in visits to online communities results in higher sales of large consumer goods.

H6. Consumer website-related experience has a moderating effect, such that an increase in visits to product channels results in higher sales of large consumer goods.

## Empirical methodology and analysis

## Data description

We collect our dataset from a real estate Internet marketing service platform, a leading online intermediary that displays mainly real estate-related information on houses in local markets across over 60 major cities in China. For each city, this website provides four online intermediaries: (1) online community for new houses, (2) product channel for new houses, (3) online community for second-hand houses, and (4) product channel for second-hand houses. For online communities (1) and (3), any consumers can participate in these communities for discussions. For product channels (2) and (4), any property developers can display their property information in these channels to convey information to consumers. Based on our research questions and data availability, we concentrate on the effect of consumer visits to new house online intermediaries on sales of new houses in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province. Different houses have different sizes (in terms of area), and hence, adopting sales area rather than sales units of new houses as our dependent variable in the main model is more appropriate. $^{1}$ We obtain the daily data on unique visits of online consumers from the new house online community and the new house product channel from March 19, 2013 to February 28, 2014 to match with local offline daily sales area data from real estate trading center. We also collect average housing prices from Fangjia.com $^{2}$ and daily offline marketing advertisements data extracted from four famous local newspapers. Moreover, we account for time fixed effects. Notably, we obtain additional data from Baidu search index $^{3}$ as our instrumental variables to address potential endogeneity issue to support our empirical validation.

## Empirical model

We operationalize our variables at the daily level. Specifically, let subscript t denote each time period (daily). The dependent variable is daily local offline sales of new houses, $area\_new_{t}$ , measured as the total area of new houses sold on day t. Our independent variables include daily consumer visits to the new house online community and new house product channel, as well as the interaction of daily consumer visits to these two online intermediaries (to investigate the interaction effect): (1) $com\_new_{t}$ , measured as the total number of consumer unique visits to the new house online community on day t; (2) $prod\_new_{t}$ , measured as the total number of consumer unique visits to the new house product channel on day t; and (3) $com\_new_{t}*prod\_new_{t}$ . Our control variables are collected from available information online, which include (1) housing prices, $price_{t}$ , measured as the average housing prices on day t; and (2) offline marketing activities, $ad_{t}$ . We collect new house marketing advertisements published in four famous newspapers, i.e., Jinling Evening News, Yangzi Evening News, Nanjing Daily News, and Modern Express. If a newspaper has ads on that day, we use a dummy with value 1 to indicate, otherwise with value 0, finally $ad_{t}$ is measured as the average of the four dummies on day t; (3)–(4) are time fixed effects – $weekend_{t}$ and $holiday_{t}$ . If day t is a weekend or holiday, we assign 1 (otherwise 0); (5) sales of second-hand houses, $area\_sec_{t}$ , measured as the total area of second-hand houses sold on day t.

Theoretically, a time lag exists between seeking for related information on products and making the final decision. Analyzing cars and major household appliances' duration of the purchase decision process, previous research found that half of the buyers have their decision processes last for two weeks or less (Newman and Staelin, 1971). In our context, a down payment is first made (with temporary agreement) to acquire a real estate. Then, a formal agreement will be completed at a later date. With this contextual understanding of real estate business, the local financial regulation and legal transaction processes, the time lag between searching for information and completing the final transaction is about 40–45 days. Thus, we choose 42 days (i.e., six weeks) as the lag level in our model. We specify the dependent variable in logarithmic form. $^{4}$ The empirical model is specified in Eq. (1):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln (a r e a n e w _ {t}) = \beta_ {1} * c o m n e w _ {t - 4 2} + \beta_ {2} * p r o d n e w _ {t - 4 2} + \beta_ {3} * c o m n e w _ {t - 4 2} * p r o d n e w _ {t - 4 2} + \beta_ {4} * p r i c e _ {t} + \beta_ {5} * a d _ {t} + \beta_ {6} * w e e k e n d _ {t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {7} * h o l i d a y _ {t} + \beta_ {8} * a r e a s e c _ {t} + \alpha + \varepsilon_ {t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha$ captures unobserved specific effects and $\varepsilon_{t}$ indicates the error term.

## Empirical analysis and results

We estimate the model of new house sales area on all control variables recognized as important factors affecting sales. As shown in Table 3, Column (1), all control variables except offline advertisements have significant relationships with the sales of new houses. Specifically, high price may impede sales, and price exhibits a negative effect. Weekend and holiday represent the off days of the real estate trading center; thus, weekends and holidays have no sales, which should have negative effects. High sales area of second-hand houses means that real estate transactions are active on that day, area\_sec may be perceived as a popular indicator of the local real estate market, making a positive effect likely.

Based on these control variables, we further estimate the sales effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels and their interaction. As reported in Table 3, Column (2), consumer visits to both online communities and product channels have positive effects on sales. Specifically, the coefficients of com\_new and prod\_new are both positive and statistically significant. However, the coefficient of com\_new\*prod\_new is negative and significant, thereby suggesting that the interaction of consumer visits to these two online intermediaries has an unexpected negative effect.

To establish the robustness of our findings across different time lag levels, we estimate another three models using different lag levels, including 40 days, 45 days, and 49 days (seven weeks), and summarize the results in Table 4, Columns (2)–(4). Table 4, Column (1) presents the baseline results from Table 3, Column (2). Evidently, all estimates remain similar.

## Identification

The above estimates have shown significant sales effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels; however, one may be concerned with the potential endogeneity issues. We eliminate the potential simultaneity bias in the main model using time lags and another common issue is the omitted variable bias. ## Discussion

## Main findings

Our study of consumer visits to online intermediaries on the effect of sales of large consumer goods has four notable findings. First, consumer visits to both online communities and product channels have positive effects on sales of large consumer goods. The elasticity of sales with respect to consumer visits to online communities is 1.620. Moreover, the elasticity of sales with respect to consumer visits to product channels is 1.458. Generally, these findings are consistent with previous research conclusions about the positive impacts of consumer visits to online intermediaries on the sales of regular consumer goods. But differently, we further provide a quantification of these impacts.

Second, by examining the relative effect of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales, we find that consumer visits to online communities are more influential compared with those to product channels in driving sales. According to elasticities, the effect of consumer visits to online communities on sales (i.e., 1.620) is 1.11 times the effect of those to product channels (i.e., 1.458). This relationship was not explored in past literature and thus we also provide a new finding to the literature.

Third, although consumer visits to online communities and product channels both lead to an increase in sales of large consumer goods, the interaction of consumer visits to these two online intermediaries will decrease each other's effectiveness. This also differs from prior research findings by further exploring the interaction effect.

Finally, we investigate the moderating effect of consumer characteristics, which shows that consumer website-related experience has no significant moderating effect on the effect of consumer visits to online communities on sales. However, in the product channel, consumers with website-related experience visits have a greater effect on sales relative to those by novices. Again, this also differs from prior research findings by further investigating the moderating effect of consumer characteristics.

## Limitation and future research

Although this research has highlighted several notable findings, some limitations must still be acknowledged. First, in assessing the effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales of large consumer goods, we only have data at the aggregate level. Insufficient individual consumer level data limits our dataset. Second, our study is based on a secondary dataset. Thus, we are unable to address all potential sources of endogeneity biases.

This study can be extended in several ways in future research. Our dataset lacks individual consumer level data. Thus, the first important extension must collect a more comprehensive relevant dataset about individual consumer clickstreams. By using this dataset, future research can directly associate individual detailed visiting and browsing behaviors with purchase decisions for more insightful investigations. Second, to generalize our findings, future research can further investigate other large consumer goods, such as cars.

## Theoretical and practical contributions

Theoretically, this study offers important contributions in the following ways. First, we contribute to the online intermediary literature by distinguishing two different types of online intermediaries (i.e. online communities and product channels). On one hand, an online community is a type of communication medium where consumers can interact with firms and other consumers. Online community contains communication contents, such as product reviews and online discussions. Product channel, on the other hand, is a venue for marketers to convey information unidirectionally to consumers. Product channel is a website showing and exhibiting information, such as product descriptions and marketing activities. Our research distinguishes different online intermediaries and explores their effects on offline sales empirically.

Second, we also contribute to the literature through our investigations of the relative and interaction effects of consumer visits to online intermediaries. Our study advances the understanding regarding the distinct effects of consumer visits to online communities and product channels on sales. The results show that consumer visits to online communities have a stronger effect than those to product channels in driving sales. However, few studies have explored their interaction effect on sales, although the coexistence of online communities and product channels is becoming more prevalent (Albuquerque et al., 2012; Chen and Xie, 2008; Lu et al., 2013; Trusov et al., 2009). Our research is one of the rare studies to examine the interaction effect, and our findings show that the interaction of consumer visits to online communities and product channels negatively influences sales. This finding is an interesting theoretical insight that indicates that their integration may result in a decrease in sales instead, although individually, both visits to online communities and product channels are effective. Therefore, by showing the possibility that “additional information from different sources are not necessarily more effective in driving sales,” our results challenge conventional wisdom.

Third, most relevant prior studies have been conducted using small and regular consumer goods (Chintagunta et al., 2010; Dewan and Ramaprasad, 2014; Dinner et al., 2014; Goh et al., 2013; Moe and Trusov, 2011; Zhu and Zhang, 2010). These studies have concluded that reviews and discussions positively affect sales. Our empirical results also suggest that consumer visits to both online communities and product channels have significant positive effects on sales of large consumer goods (i.e. house sales). Thus, by exploring the effect of online intermediaries on the sales of large consumer goods empirically, we also add insights regarding large consumer goods to existing literature.

Fourth, prior research has examined the moderating effect of product type, such as popular and niche products (Dewan and Ramaprasad, 2014; Phang et al., 2013; Zhu and Zhang, 2010), information valence (Adjei et al., 2010), whereas others have focused on the moderating effect of consumer characteristics, such as experts and novices (Hubl and Trifts, 2000; Rowley, 2000), experienced and novice users (Hammond et al., 1998; Klein and Ford, 2003; Lazonder et al., 2000; Zhu and Zhang, 2010). Our research further enriches the literature by studying the moderating effect of consumer website-related experience on the effect of consumer visits to online intermediaries on sales of large consumer goods. Our results also indicate that website-related experience has no moderating effect on the effect of consumer visits to online communities on sales, whereas it has a moderating effect on the relationship between consumer visits to product channels and sales.

In a practical sense, marketers can drive sales of large consumer goods using online intermediaries with the three implications we offer. First, according to our findings, consumer visits to both online communities and product channels can produce additional sales of large consumer goods. Thus, firms selling these goods should consider establishing online intermediaries that provide communication contents and product descriptions to improve their performance. With the development of online intermediaries, an increasing number of real estate marketers have adopted online channels to offer information on their products. Furthermore, because consumer visits to online communities have a stronger effect than those to product channels, firms selling large consumer goods must exploit online communities to attract additional consumer visits strategically to help them achieve great performance efficiently. For instance, marketers can exert extra efforts and spend more budgets (e.g., using discount coupons or reward points for consumers' future purchases) to provide incentives to consumers that visit online communities.

Second, our results show that the interaction of consumer visits to online communities and product channels negatively affects the sales of large consumer goods. On one hand, consumer visits to online communities are less influential in driving sales with increased visits to product channels. On the other hand, the effect of consumer visits to product channels on sales of large consumer goods will diminish with increased visits to online communities. Therefore, marketers should focus on the substitute relationship and the strategic combination of online communities and product channels.

Third, according to our results, consumer website-related experience has no significant moderating effect on the sales effect of visits to online communities, but has a moderating effect on the sales effect of visits to product channels, i.e., experienced consumers have a greater positive effect on sales. Thus, a possible strategy for online marketers is to retain consumers with website-related experience. Specifically, marketers can develop various loyalty programs to retain consumers with website-related experience. For instance, they can implement different forms of reward mechanisms, such as badges and scores, to help consumers with website-related experience identify themselves as organizational insiders and develop a high level of engagement (McAlexander et al., 2002). Consequently, they may visit product channels frequently and enhance their purchase intentions, which will eventually generate high sales (Bhattacharya and Sankar, 2003).

Lastly, we provide a critical policy implication. Our findings show that visits to online communities have a stronger effect than those to product channels. Evaluation information and discussions in online communities may spur consumers to engage in additional purchases relative to product channels to possibly create herd (Lux, 1995) or cascade behavior (Grebe et al., 2008). In the context of China, the inevitable housing price hike will cause more people to rely on the bandwagon. In this case, most consumers may have a wait-and-see attitude and will encounter difficulties in making a final purchase decision. Thus, governments can make appropriate announcements to monitor the housing market and perhaps regulate online intermediaries, facilitate healthy economic development, and help consumers build confidence in the real estate market.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we focus on the effect of consumer visits to two types of online intermediaries on sales of large consumer goods. Our findings shed light on the effectiveness of consumer visits to online intermediaries with sales. Specifically, consumer visits to online communities (elasticity = 1.620) have a stronger effect on sales of large consumer goods relative to those to product channels (elasticity = 1.458). Our results suggest that consumer visits to both online communities and product channels have a significant negative effect on sales of large consumer goods. Finally, we investigate the moderating effect of consumer characteristics, and find that consumers with website-related experience visits have a more significant effect on sales relative to those by novices. These new findings provide important theoretical contributions and practical implications.
