# How <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions become a part of organizations: A study of enterprise architecture as a tool for institutional change

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Institutional theory Institutional change Enterprise architecture

## A B S T R A C T

Heeding recent calls for more studies on micro-practices and institutional change, this study reports a collaborative case study to elucidate the micro-practices and legitimating strategies that make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations. Building on institutional theories and using Enterprise Architecture (EA) as an example of <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions, the study explores how two distinct organizations adopted EA, the impediments they experienced, and the strategies used to make EA an acceptable part of organizations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest three change mechanisms that enact <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions in organizations: the creation of shared spaces for knowledge exchange between organization members, the importance of role shifting within shared spaces to e<sup>f</sup>ectively engage with other organization members, and the need for lexical shifts in commu nication to better resonate with users. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings deepen our understanding of institutional elements that impede change, as well as the legitimating strategies that enact <sup>fi</sup>eld-level in stitutions.

## 1. Introduction

An interesting question that pervades institutional research in information systems (IS) is how actors enact legitimating strategies to make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations (Currie & Swanson, 2009; Hultin & Mähring, 2014; Mignerat & Rivard, 2009; Oliver, 1991). By <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions, we mean the taken-for-granted meanings, practices, norms, and regulations that constitute the social structures within an organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld which often organizes around a recognized area of expertise or activity (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). Institutional research in IS has explored <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions in a couple of insightful ways. For example, in exploring the importance of information technology (IT) as a carrier of institutional logics that constrain and enable action (Gosain, 2004, p. 156; Davidson & Chismar, 2007), and in exploring how <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions exert pressure on human agency and impact decision-making (Hu, Hart, & Cooke, 2007; del Águila, Bruque, & Padilla, 2002; Ang & Cummings, 1997). However, IS research has not fully explored the micro-practices used to enact <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions, particularly those that stimulate shifts in cultural acceptance, normative habituation, and regulative policy to become interwoven in organizations (Avgerou, 2000; Hultin & Mähring, 2014; Micelotta, Lounsbury, & Greenwood, 2017; Mignerat & Rivard, 2009). Moreover, numerous analyses of IS institutional research reference the need to better understand these micro-practices (Currie & Swanson, 2009) and the strategies for implementing institutions (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009). Accordingly, this paper responds to these requests by depicting the results of a collaborative case study (George & Bennett, 2004) to elucidate the micro-practices and legitimating strategies that make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations.

We present <sup>fi</sup>ndings from a collaborative study on Enterprise Architecture (EA). EA is an IT planning discipline (Jonkers et al., 2006). Its goal is to produce documentation, which (a) acts as a blueprint for an organization's current or future IT environment (Tamm, Seddon, Shanks, & Reynolds, 2011), and (b) provides the management roadmap to move from a current to a future state (Ross, Weill, & Robertson, 2006). In IS research, EA is discussed in numerous capacities. For example, as a tool for strategic change (Ahlemann, Stettiner, Messerschmidt, & Legner, 2012; Op't Land, Proper, Waage, Cloo, & Steghuis, 2008; Ross et al., 2006), an approach to complex issues in IS/IT Management (Lindström, Johnson, Johansson, Ekstedt, & Simonsson, 2006; Luftman et al., 2012), an organizing logic for linking business processes and IT (Ahlemann et al., 2012; Ross et al., 2006), a method for structuring decisions for IS development through embedded management and governance arrangements (Gregor, Hart, & Martin, 2007; Op't Land et al., 2008), and a coherent whole of principles, methods, and models used in the design and realization of an enterprise's organizational structure, business processes, information systems, and infrastructure (Jonkers et al., 2006). However, little is known about how EA is successfully implemented (Foorthuis et al., 2016; Aier, 2014; GAO, 2006; Tamm et al., 2011), and thus, we contend more research is needed to understand how EA becomes a part of organizations

EA is also a classic example of a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution (Lawrence, Suddaby, & Leca, 2009). It consists of several well-established frameworks that have been developed by numerous organizations,<sup>1</sup> and these frameworks are inextricably responsible for shaping patterns of EA use and interaction within and across fields, and within organizations (Lawrence. Hardy. & Phillips. 2002). Thus, for the purpose of our collaborative study, we utilize institutional theory to elucidate the dominant regulative, normative, and culturalcognitive elements (Scott, 2014) of two EA Programs, and the strategies and micro-practices that made EA a part of these organizations. Accordingly, we ask the following research question: How do <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions make their way into organizations? Two sub-questions guided our collaborative study:

How does EA manifest itself in organizations?

• What are the micro-practices Enterprise Architects use to make EA a part of organizations?

Our study makes three contributions: (1) We elucidate deeper understanding of the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive characteristics that impede institutional change in IS contexts. (2) We elucidate the micro-practices that actors use as part of legitimating strategies to make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations. (3) We develop a theory for design and action (Gregor, 2006), giving explicit prescriptions of how organizations can engage in shifting established structures and activities to incorporate new ones, when they often do not command adequate legitimacy. These contributions relate directly to (1) generating understanding of how <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions are implemented in organizations to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the design and use of IT within and across organizations (Barley & Tolbert, 1997; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001); (2) applying insight from the practice turn in IS and Organization research which has inspired scholars to go beyond the IT artifact as the central unit of analysis (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Kellogg, 2009; Orlikowski, 2007; Zilber, 2002); and (3) illustrating how a combination of micro-practices and legitimating strategies can help change agents overcome impediments to change.

The following section gives background to the analytical lens used in the study. We follow this with a detailing of our research methods and research settings for each of the two cases. Next, we detail the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our collaborative study and the theoretica contributions of our research. Lastly, we conclude this article by building the case for research that looks at the nascent stages of IS implementations in the face of extant institutions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Institutional perspectives in IS research

Based on previous research (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009; Nielsen, Mathiassen, & Newell, 2014), we roughly divide IS innovation research that uses institutional theory into three key perspectives: (1) the artifact-centric perspective where IT innovations are seen as carriers of institutional logics, (2) the decision-making perspective that investigates the role institutions play in IT/IS-related decisions, and (3) the lesser-explored perspective that investigates how micro-practices and legitimating strategies engender institutional change introduced by IS innovations. The <sup>fi</sup>rst perspective centers on IT as a unit of analysis in organizational contexts. In particular, the degree to which IT can enable or constrain extant institutions irrespective of e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness, and what can happen when IT, and IT implementation processes, run counter to the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive pillars of extant institutions (Cousins & Robey, 2005; Davidson & Chismar, 2007; Gosain, 2004; Nielsen et al., 2014; Pearson & Keller, 2009; Srivastava, Teo, & Subramanian, 2009). The second perspective centers on how institutional e<sup>f</sup>ects a<sup>f</sup>ect human agency in IS decision making, emphasizing how cultural-cognitive, normative, and regulatory aspects in<sup>fl</sup>uence decisions in strategic IS contexts (Ang & Cummings, 1997; Hu et al., 2007; Miranda & Kim, 2006; Wang & Swanson, 2007). We contribute to research on the third perspective, which focuses on how IS innovations become legitimized to reinforce or change existing institutions.

Like studies in the institutionalization process perspective (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009), research in this area investigates the di<sup>f</sup>erent legitimating strategies and micro-practices that infuse new IS in existing institutions. For example, in a historical study of the Mexican oil company Pemex, Avgerou (2000) investigated the IS-related processes that engendered innovation, and how they became part of

Pemex culture. Backhouse, Hsu, and Silva (2006) investigated the role of power and politics in introducing new IS security standards and, in particular, the “power mechanisms” (Backhouse et al., 2006, p. 414) that legitimated their implementation. Recently, studies have explored how adaptive changes by end-users of IT systems enabled the acceptance of new systems. For example, Berente and Yoo (2012) reported four forms of loosely coupling practices to deal with con<sup>fl</sup>icts between individual use and ERP standard processes. Similarly, Leonardi, Bailey, Diniz, Sholler, and Nardi (2016) showed an even more complex set of adaptive practices in which di<sup>f</sup>erent users adapted their use of a banking system di<sup>f</sup>erently–its technical, social, or policy elements–depending on di<sup>f</sup>erent business settings. And Jensen and Aanestad (2007) posited that the mutual co-evolution of users and practice would eventually determine the success of ERP adoption. In addition, Nielsen et al. (2014) summarized the rise of studies on discursive strategies that legitimize IS adoption. For instance, research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a variety of strategies such as identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, di<sup>f</sup>erentiation, reframing, containment, enhancement, and substitution (Cheney, Christensen, Conrad, & Lair, 2004), using institutional vocabularies and theorization of changes (Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005), or employing rhetoric for persuasion (Erkama & Vaara, 2010). Others went deeper, scrutinizing individual activities that constitute an emerging institution through episodes of technology uses. For instance, Jarzabkowski and Kaplan (2015) provided vignettes of how use of a strategic tool allowed a managerial team to converge on new strategic initiatives. And Arvidsson, Holmström, and Lyytinen (2014) showed how socially situated practices shaped the implementation and use of a production management system in a Swedish paper mill

While all three perspectives provide valuable insight to our understanding of IS innovation implementation and use, IS scholars have called for an expansion of research in the third perspective. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, to move outside the artifact-centric view, and towards micro-level studies that investigate institutional processes in IS contexts (Currie & Swanson, 2009, p. 285), to provide a better understanding of the legitimating strategies and micro-practices enacted in organizations, to go beyond acquiescence as a strategic response (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009, p. 375), and to understand the di<sup>f</sup>erent ways legitimacy is obtained (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009; Mignerat & Rivard, 2012). In answering this call, recent research has explored the micro-practices by end-users (e.g., loose coupling, adaptation, co-evolution) (Berente & Yoo, 2012; Jensen & Aanestad, 2007; Leonardi et al., 2016), the discursive strategies by change agents (Nielsen et al., 2014; Erkama & Vaara, 2010), and the emergent process of institution change through individual activities (Arvidsson et al., 2014; Jarzabkowski & Kaplan, 2015). However, there is a paucity of research on the micro-practices across multiple areas (e.g., individual responses, structural changes, communication, and management) and how they work in conjunction rather than isolation to enable institutional change (Arvidsson et al., 2014). This issue also aligns with the rising interests within the EA community for studies that unveil the variety of operational and managerial practices to legitimize and enable EA success (Ahlemann et al., 2012; Simon, Fischbach, & Schoder, 2013). The chief reason is that “there are two sides to an enterprise architecture” (Bui, 2015, p. 169): the conceptual side which works on paper, and the execution side which requires clever maneuvers to engage managers, educate users, and regulate work practices. Our research contributes to this area by reporting on a collaborative study that depicts how actors enact legitimating strategies through micro-practices that make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level EA institutions a part of organizations and in<sup>fl</sup>uence institutional change. In the next section, we develop our analytical lens based on extant research on institutional theory.

## 2.2. Institutional theory as analytical lens

Research on institutional theory presents a duality for both organization theory and IS research. The roots of institutional theory express institutions schemes, rules, norms, and routines as stable features of social life that become authoritative guidelines for social behavior (March & Olsen, 1983; Powell & DiMaggio, 2012). However, this stability also creates <sup>fi</sup>ssures between the everchanging nature of social activity and the institutions themselves, and thus, causes institutions to undergo constant challenge and change (Dacin, Goodstein, & Scott, 2002; Fiss & Zajac, 2004; Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Thornton & Ocasio, 1999). This dual nature <sup>fi</sup>ts well with our purpose: On one hand, EA can manifest itself as a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution that seeks to establish stability within disintegrated IT infrastructure and unstandardized IT processes (Ross et al., 2006), while on the other hand, the very introduction of EA causes an institutional change in the adopting organization, requiring well-thought strategies and practices to enable a successful EA implementation. Thus, we draw from research on institutions as forces of stability and change to develop our analytical lens.

As a force for stability, institutions are theorized to endure from selection processes that favor stable outcomes over unstable ones. From sociological perspectives, institutions are seen as having evolutionary qualities where high levels of structural inertia are seen as both preconditions for and consequences of selection, where in turn, institutional actors face constant pressure to favor stable systems over unstable ones (Hannan & Freeman, 1984; Scott & Davis, 2015). Building on this perspective, it is research on organization theory that is pertinent to our study. From the organization theory perspective, selection processes also favor stability but take into account how organizational norms, goals, size, complexity, and competitive environment can a<sup>f</sup>ect strategic responses. For example, Oliver (1991) theorized that depending on institutional constituencies, the congruence of institutional norms, and organizational goals, actors may strategically respond in a variety of ways ranging from passive compliance to active de<sup>fi</sup>ance. Goodstein (1994) theorized that cause, constituents, content, control, and context stimulate actors' selection processes dependent on an organization's size. And Ang and Cummings (1994) theorize that selection processes by actors are stimulated by environmental factors such as perceived gain in production economies, <sup>fi</sup>nancial capacity to resist institutional in<sup>fl</sup>uences, and transaction costs. This stream of research highlights two key points: (1) Actors are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by institutional logics–the practices, assumptions, values, beliefs, and rules that cause them to produce and reproduce material subsistence, organize time and space, and provide meaning to social reality (Almandoz, 2014; Thornton, 2002). (2) These institutional logics can manifest at multiple levels, for example, at the <sup>fi</sup>eld-level (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Friedland & Alford, 1991; Meyer & Rowan, 1977; North, 1990), within organizations (Kunda, 2009; Martin, 2002), within organizational subsystems (Barley, 1986; Heimer, 1999; Kellogg, 2011), within strategic practices (Reay et al., 2013; Smets, Morris, &

Greenwood, 2012; Suddaby, Seidl, & Lê, 2013), and through dyadic processes (Creed, DeJordy, & Lok, 2010; Hallett & Ventresca, 2006; Lawrence et al., 2009).

Research on institutions from the change perspective focuses on the impediments, triggers, and practices of institutional actors that cause institutional shifts to occur (Seo & Creed, 2002; Streeck & Thelen, 2009). This can be seen from regulative, normative, and cultural perspectives (Scott, 2014). From a regulative perspective, research has centered on the logics of fear and coercion that cause actors to obey, and how new regulatory frameworks and other regulatory change mechanisms can weaken extant institutions, trigger de-institutionalization, and create space for new institutional logics and new institutional forms (Lounsbury, 2002; North, 1990; Thornton, 2002). From a normative perspective, research has centered on the ties between duties, responsibilities, and organizational goals, the sheer di<sup>fi</sup>culty in shifting work routines, and how functional, political, and social sources shift the way actors view the utility of institutionalized practices (Goodin, 1998; Oliver, 1991). Lastly, from a cultural perspective, research has centered on the role of one's social identity within a cultural system, and how change can occur when new backgrounds and experiences bring di<sup>f</sup>erent interpretive frameworks and social de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of behavior to the organization, which can diminish consensus and un questioning adherence to taken-for-granted practices (Busenitz, Gomez, & Spencer, 2000; Zilber, 2002).

Underpinning the aforementioned regulative, normative, and cultural perspectives are the triggers that cause institutional change to occur. Research on triggers can be characterized along three key lines: (1) Institutional change can be triggered by exogenous shocks to an institutional environment that cause shifts in legitimacy-granting criteria, de-institutionalization, isomorphic adaption, and shifts in institutional logics that can cause the disappearance of organizations, the di<sup>f</sup>usion of new practices, or the restructuring of industries (Clemens & Cook, 1999; Greenwood, Raynard, Kodeih, Micelotta, & Lounsbury, 2011; Meyer, 1982; Thornton & Ocasio, 1999; Thornton, Ocasio, & Lounsbury, 2012). (2) Institutional change can be triggered by the actions of institutional entrepreneurs who craft non-disruptive strategies that change rhetoric, frames, or bricolage, or disruptive strategies that engender collective action and resource mobilization (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Rao, Monin, & Durand, 2005). (3) Pertinent to our research is the idea that institutional change can be triggered by micro-practices that shift institutional logics, deinstitutionalize existing practices, and change institutional environments (Barley, 2008; Gehman, Trevino, & Garud, 2013; Gray, Purdy, & Ansari, 2015) as institutional logics are adopted in organizations, and are capable of propagating (or damping) the initial impetus for change in meaning and practice (Vaccaro & Palazzo, 2015).

Notably, numerous studies in organization theory have called for additional research on the micro-practices that engender institutional change and lead to new regulations, new norms, and cultural shifts in organizations (Compagni, Mele, & Ravasi, 2015; Lounsbury & Crumley, 2007; Micelotta et al., 2017; Smets et al., 2012). Accordingly, we employ institutional theorists' conception of institutions as consisting of three pillars—regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive (Scott, 2014; Palthe, 2014; La<sup>f</sup>an, 2001; Ho<sup>f</sup>man, 1999)—as these pillars are theorized to (1) arise in interaction (Scott, 2008), and (2) both constrain and empower socia behavior (Scott, 1995, p. 235). The regulative pillar gives primacy to rules, policies, and guidelines that legitimize, constrain, and regularize actor behavior, where legitimacy is constituted by systems that coerce obligation. The normative pillar gives primacy to the work roles, habits, and norms of actors that drive organizational objectives and goals, where legitimacy is constituted by the moral and ethical systems that make certain behaviors expected over others. Lastly, the cultural-cognitive pillar gives primacy to the values, beliefs, and assumptions of actors, where legitimacy is constituted by shared conceptions of social reality and cognitive frames through which one acquires meaning (Berger & Kellner, 1981; D'Andrade, 1984; Scott, 2014; Thornton et al., 2012).

For the purposes of our study, we describe the pillars of institutions by their institutional elements, the elements' basis for legitimacy, how these elements impede change, and what mechanisms actors employ to engender change with respect to these elements (Fig. 1). Elements for regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive pillars can be rules, work roles, and values, respectively.

In turn, elements exist through bases for legitimacy, which, for each of the pillars, can be coercively sanctioned, normatively expected, or culturally supported. These give rise to impediments where, for each of the pillars, institutional change can be hampered by fear and coercion, duties and responsibilities, or social identity. Lastly. because we wish to understand the nature of the institution and what is done to engender change, we depict change mechanisms, which, for each of the pillars, can entail new legal or compliance requirements, moral obligations, or internalization of new value systems (Palthe, 2014; Hatch & Zilber, 2012, p. 95).

This lens serves to capture institutions in their duality and how, in IS contexts, the embedding of practices within structures can produce action to embed <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions in organizations (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Uzzi, 1996; Zilber, 2002). Understanding institutional stability is a key component when incongruity exists between social life and institutions, and in turn, the actions of institutional actors are telling in understanding institutional change. This, we theorize, is rooted in how micro-practices and strategies are used to legitimize institutional rules, in<sup>fl</sup>uence new norms, and shift shared beliefs. In sum, in building from the collective work of institutional theory, our analytical lens allows us to (1) depict the enduring features of organizations, which, in turn, lead to the mechanisms, which emanate as the micro-practices elucidated in the sections below, to make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations; (2) understand how outside institutions in<sup>fl</sup>uence IS organizations; and (3) understand the practical e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of micro-practices employed by actors to in<sup>fl</sup>uence change. ## 3. Research methods

To understand how <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions make their way into organizations, we report the results of a collaborative study on EA implementation. A collaborative study is a method of case comparison where even though individual cases are undertaken by di<sup>f</sup>erent scholars, a study can be performed to analyze results across cases (George & Bennett, 2004). According to George and Bennett, this must be done in a structured and focused manner. Structured in that researchers involved in comparing cases write general questions re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the objective of the collaborative study, and then these questions are asked of each case. Focused in that only aspects related to the research questions come under investigation (George & Bennett, 2005, p. 71). Numerous examples exist demonstrating the value of collaborative studies. In a study on the rati<sup>fi</sup>cation of arms control treaties in the U.S. Senate, Krepon and Caldwell (2016) structured their study by proposing a set of research questions to be asked across cases, and focused their comparison by carefully choosing a subclass of case research to put through a hypothesis-forming exercise reliant on process tracing (Krepon & Caldwell, 2016, p. 426). Similarly, in a collaborative study of teachers' understanding of philosophical shifts in how Master of Education (M.Ed.) programs are taught, Holt & Johnston, 1989 structured their study by proposing research questions targeted at how guiding <sup>fi</sup>eld-level philosophies for M.Ed programs were put into practice, and focused their study by selecting a subset of case interviews to answer the research questions. Both examples highlight how collaborative case studies can provide analytical value. With respect to our cases, each were of the same topic, EA implementation, but conducted in di<sup>f</sup>erent industry sectors. Case 1 investigated EA in a state government, case 2 in a private corporation. However, because years of discussion between the authors of this paper were replete with similar ideas, it seemed logical to provide some form of generalized contribution. Accordingly, our collaborative study is structured by the set of research questions in Section 1, and focused through a hermeneutic process (see Appendix 1, Fig. A) that extracted dominant characteristics to provide a subset of information on how both organizations used common tactics to enact a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution.

## 3.1. Data collection

Case 1 utilized two sources of data collection: (1) archives from public records, databases, and websites, and (2) interviews with EA Program and state leadership. Archives consisted of news articles, meeting notes, the state's EA framework, public reports on technology modernization e<sup>f</sup>orts, and white papers specifying the state's technology environment. In total, more than 80 di<sup>f</sup>erent documents totaling over 2000 pages were collected. After archival analysis, interviews were conducted with state leadership and the state's Enterprise Architects, between 2011 and 2012 at the state's Technology and Security O<sup>fi</sup>ce. Preliminary content analysis from archival data and insights from literature were used to guide the interviews. Interviews were purposive, as future informants came at the recommendation of those currently being interviewed. In total, seven one-hour, semi-structured, audio-recorded interviews were conducted.

## 3.2. Data analysis

Data were analyzed iteratively through a hermeneutic cycle (Appendix 1, Fig. A), providing a view of EA rooted in individual <sup>fi</sup>eld experiences (Denzin, 2001; Packer & Addison, 1989). We performed our analysis manually, as opposed to using qualitative tools, such as NVivo. We ventured to know our data intimately, relying on up-close and personal interactions with each other as researchers, and open-coding, to develop theories representative of collective wholes (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Each iteration consisted of the following steps: contextual understanding of case-speci<sup>fi</sup>cs, elucidating change impediments and change mechanisms, and merging common portrayals across cases (Paterson & Higgs, 2005, p. 345; Tesch, 1990).

Step 1: Contextual Understanding. We re-exposed ourselves to data in both cases by analyzing archival and interview data related to the research questions in Section 1. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed relevant interview transcripts and archives to extract text segments and develop themes in line with the research questions. In initial iterations, this step captured our initial thoughts related to the research questions. In later iterations, this step served to coalesce di<sup>f</sup>erent themes to develop shared meaning (Crist & Tanner, 2003).

Step 2: Elucidating Change Impediments and Change Mechanisms. This step moved the researchers towards writing 1–3 page portrayals of the themes from Step 1 and developing timelines for each case. We then reviewed each other's data from both steps (chosen text segments, themes, timelines, and portrayals) to compare with our own interpretations. This was useful since it gave each case a second review. In this step, we also introduced the institutional lens to acclimate our data to institutional theory before developing shared meaning across cases. Fig. 1 was used to code the text segments in each portrayal relative to the pillars and characteristics.

Step 3: Merging Common Portrayals. In moving between text and context, the two researchers engaged in a process of moving back and forth from within- to between-case portrayals to identify similarities and merge them into portrayals oriented around change mechanisms, which are central to the research questions. The portrayals that were <sup>fi</sup>rst developed within cases were then developed around change mechanisms and existing theory to elucidate the theoretical contributions in Section 6. Initial iterations entailed the writing of the portrayals, while subsequent iterations edited them with new information.

This 3-step process went through four rounds of iterations, and took over two years to reach a point of theoretical saturation (Bowen, 2008) where dominant themes were agreed upon for the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive pillars. For case 1 we targeted three key elements for regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive pillars, which had over 70 related themes related to bases for legitimacy, twelve themes related to EA impediments, and three themes related to change mechanisms. For case 2, there were over 60 themes related to elements' bases for legitimacy, 11 themes related to EA impediments, and six themes related to change mechanisms. Appendix 1 provides an example of the data analysis process.

## 4. Research settings

Case 1 investigated one of the very <sup>fi</sup>rst adopters of EA at a U.S. state government. In 1998, IT architecture policy was crafted to increase IT integration across agencies. However, <sup>fi</sup>ve years later an IT commission report (released in 2003) noted that IT integration had not proceeded as expected, and this was attributed to a lack of a formal EA Program, Shortly thereafter. the state appointed a new CIO, who then appointed the state's <sup>fi</sup>rst CTO, to create and sta<sup>f</sup> an EA Program. One year later (in 2004) the EA Program was created, which later that year issued its <sup>fi</sup>rst version of EA–the State Enterprise Architecture Framework (SEAF). From 2004 to 2006, SEAF was a welcome addition; however, informants also revealed that very few agencies actually used SEAF during this time (S-DCIO, S-CEA). In late 2006, the EA Program began making adjustments to SEAF to make it more usable, for example, changing existing reference models to incorporate technologies that were already widely used by agencies. However, this had little e<sup>f</sup>ect, and for the next three years SEAF continued to have very little use within state agencies. In 2009, however, EA began to gain momentum. The governor enacted several policies detailing IT consolidation, and EA was a known industry guideline used for this purpose. This appeared to turn agencies' attention towards EA; however, this newfound attention also re-surfaced old problems–how could the EA

The state was organized into several major agencies, divided along the lines of civic services such as public health, public safety, or public transportation. Agencies were federated (Handy, 1993) in that policy was determined at the state level and state agencies carried them out autonomously. EA was envisaged by state IT leadership to remedy the duplicative systems that were a result of this autonomy. It was seen as a way to “standardize information, data, applications, and infrastructure silos” (S-CTO), and optimize systems so that there would be a statewide Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) where services, not systems, could provide access to data and applications (Erl, 2005) to any agency that needed it. To move towards SOA, SEAF was architected to have reference models: component-driven, technical frameworks that categorized the standards and technologies that support and enable the delivery of service components and capabilities (Guijarro, 2007). Reference models were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed along six domains: (1) the access domain for information delivery, (2) the information domain for data standards and management, (3) the application domain for design and development of service-oriented applications, (4) the integration domain for the integration of data and services across state agencies, (5) the management domain for the management of traditional IT infrastructures and services, and (6) the service domain for the protection of information assets.

Case 2 was conducted within the EA Program at a large U.S.-based multinational technology conglomerate. The case was chosen because of the opportunity for one of the researchers to investigate EA at one of the world's largest software producers. EA was <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced in 2000 when a new CEO was appointed to deal with mounting market pressure from competitors who were moving to cloud-based applications while the company was still in the business of selling traditional software. EA was seen as the vehicle to move to the cloud, by porting existing applications to the web and enacting a “single customer experience” (P-CTO) that could provide a common sign-on for products and services. Six years later, the EA program was created with the task of standardizing data across business units, in particular, by developing Master Data Management (MDM) standards, a key component of EA, to “integrate data across 40 di<sup>f</sup>erent business units , reduce the technology variants that housed critical customer data , and standardize interfaces so business units could use another business unit's data sources” (P-PEA). However, this proved incredibly controversial. Although EA was seen as important by IT leadership, the EA Program was faced with integrating IT across a highly divisional and competitive corporate culture that made a point of “pushing autonomy to the businesses” (P-EA4). Thus, EA was not seen as strategically necessary by the business units. In contrast, it was seen as a euphemism for “control”, “enforcement”, and “dictatorship” (P-EA1), and consequently caused a large-scale resistance movement among business units. In 2008, the EA Program's funding was cut substantially, leaving them with a ‘skeleton crew’ of enterprise architects with very little responsibility. For two years, the EA Program barely survived. This, however, changed in 2010, as mounting market pressure and a new CIO gave the EA Program new life. MDM and the idea of a ‘single customer experience’ were still critical, and thus unifying data sources for this purpose were stil strategically important. However, with EA previously seen as a vehicle for authoritarianism, the central question for the EA Program became how to keep EA from again being perceived that way. At the time of our interviews. the EA Program appeared to be makins progress. In 2011, when interviews were conducted, it was collaborating with a few key business units, working principally with the business unit that handled marketing campaigns, and was beginning to work with other units to integrate EA into engineering decisions (Fig. 3).

The organizational structure could be best described as ‘divisional’ (Jones, 2010), as each business unit (BU) acted as its own company. ## 5. Findings

In this section, we report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our cases, structured by our analytical lens (Fig. 1). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each case, we report how the institutional elements were presented in the case, what the basis for legitimacy is, what the impediments for change are, and how institutional change was enabled in the case through practices that acted as mechanisms for change.

## 5.1. Case 1: regulative

The State CIO's goal was to use SEAF as the regulatory element to move the state closer to a Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA). SEAF set guidelines for six main areas of IT development: User Accessibility, Data Formats, Application Development, Service Integration, Service Management, and Security. For each area, speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines were provided that regulated how technologies were developed and deployed in the state. For example, in areas such as user accessibility, SEAF mandated all user interfaces must be X.509 compliant and support 128-bit encryption. For data formats, it mandated the use of Global Justice XML Data Model (Globa JXDM). For application development, it mandated the use of virtualization, as well as the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Process (UP) as a software development methodology.

As a basis for regulative legitimacy, SEAF had penalties attached, but also faced considerable legitimacy hurdles. On one hand, agencies would lose funding if new systems were found non-compliant. On the other hand, non-compliance was di<sup>fi</sup>cult to penalize as it was described by multiple informants as “di<sup>fi</sup>cult to follow” (S-CTO, S-DCIO). For one, it called for extensive use of virtualization when, many times, “legacy machines didn't have the computing resources to support more than one operating system” (S-CTO). In addition, it called for common development processes across agencies, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, using the “Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Process [UP] as a development methodology” (S-EA1) when “agency developers weren't trained in UP and didn't have the software to support it” (S-DCIO). The above <sup>fi</sup>ndings were substantiated when the State CIO was asked about how he enforced SEAF guidelines:

I would say they [the EA Program] were probably trying to propagate that policy and the practice at, but I wouldn't say in those years, 2004 to 2008, I wouldn't say it was totally successful, you know what I mean? I guess it's not a measure of success. I think it's a measure of adoption and compliance, okay? With anything you need to educate people, you need to give them the tools to do their jobs.

(S-CIO)

Thus, a key impediment to change appeared to be that regulative coercion only worked to the point that non-compliance was discoverable. For example, it was useful in getting agencies to use technologies associated with SOA, but of little use in espousing common development methodologies. Enterprise Architects described agency developers as having little issue with SOA protocols that “facilitate service integration”, but “considerable di<sup>fi</sup>culty with establishing RUP” (S-EA2) as a development methodology. This was substantiated when an Enterprise Architect was asked about how they thought agencies used SEAF:

We got them to go SOA but not to standardize. In developing any new technology that requires changes to occur in other areas, you'll have to negotiate with people that work there with the time, eforts, and resources to make those changes. So, it's not that people are resistant to change, but everybody is very busy.

(S-EA3)

One of the key change mechanisms utilized in case 1 was the practice of using governance groups as a shared space to facilitat collaboration and knowledge exchange. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in case 1, the EA Program worked through an IT governance board (ITGB) to engage agencies in EA development and use. The ITGB was established together with the EA Program in 2004 as part of the state legislature that aimed to strengthen IT governance across the state. Its membership includes CIOs, IT managers, and developers from agencies; and its goals are to “foster cross-agency collaboration”, creating a forum for agencies to share best practices and participate in strategic planning (State IT Strategic Plan, 2009). The ITGB met monthly to discuss various topics such as “strategic planning activities”, “state IT policies”, and “future years funding allocations” (S-CEA). In ITGB meetings, agency representatives discussed their biggest IT challenges and success stories, and through these discussions representatives from the EA Program had a chance to understand how EA can help address other agencies' urgent issues. In turn, the ITGB discussions allowed agency developers, the end users of EA, to participate in SEAF modi<sup>fi</sup>cations and o<sup>f</sup>er their suggestions and insights to make the EA Program more relevant to them. This was further evidenced when one of the Enterprise Architects was asked about his opinion of the ITGB:

You need to include those people in your standard setting process for your organization's architecture-reach goals…these people need to participate to say, ‘Yes’, that's something that I can see, or ‘No’, and here is why—because the actual business of my organization and the

mission of the organization are counter to that concept.

(S-EA1)

Our review of ITGB meeting notes showed that agency representatives found those discussions and shared spaces for information exchange “useful” (ITGB meeting notes). As a result of the ITGB, the State CIO described agencies as becoming more proactive in discussing “data ownership”, “integration”, “service interfaces”, and “moving beyond di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies and di<sup>f</sup>erent codes between agencies” (S-CIO). In 2009 when the state discussed priorities for a statewide consolidation project, SOA and enterprise policies and standards were voted as one of the seven key initiatives to create a shared SOA infrastructure. However, while the evidence indicated that ITGB meetings were helpful in increasing EA awareness and engagement, it was not su<sup>fi</sup>cient to encourage statewide compliance, and the state faced the problem of “[uneven] compliance with standards … resulting in [a] highly complex IT environment” (State ITGB Assessment 2008). To assist agencies in their compliance e<sup>f</sup>orts, State Enterprise Architects needed to engage a di<sup>f</sup>erent kind of strategy to change normative behaviors.

## 5.2. Case 1: normative

An investigation of normative elements revealed two di<sup>f</sup>erent roles for Enterprise Architects and agency developers. The Enterprise Architect's role was to “develop SEAF and enforce it” (S-DCIO), while agency developers' role was to develop IT in support of agencyspeci<sup>fi</sup>c civic services. For example, the Homeland Security and Emergency Management agencies' role was to ensure IT infrastructure can withstand, respond to, and recover from all types of emergencies and disasters (State Website), while the Department of Motor Vehicles was responsible for IT services that allow motor vehicles registration and licensed operation. This was reinforced when the Deputy CIO was asked about the role of the EA Program:

It's the EA group that establishes the parameters for IT procurement, development, and maintenance across agencies which has never been done before. Everyone worries about themselves and not cross-integration. The agency IT folks hardly work with other agencies and they have no idea how they may be impacting integrated services, and this has made it hard to get the agency folks to change their habits and using the EA group.

(S-DCIO)

The above contrast caused EA's basis for normative legitimacy to come under <sup>fi</sup>re, particularly because Enterprise Architects expected agency developers to shift roles to “incorporate SEAF guidelines into daily work” (S-EA1) even though Enterprise Architects and agency developers were on di<sup>f</sup>erent pages about SEAF (S-EA1). From our perspective, normative expectations diverged due to competing moral systems. The Enterprise Architects were bound to the inherent goodness in moving to an SOA, while agency developers were bound to the mission of their supporting agency. This was further substantiated when the Chief Enterprise Architect was asked about how agency developers saw their responsibility to the EA Program:

Everyone is way too busy. When they saw the low-hanging fruit they went for it, and said, hey, I'm compliant! They integrated SOAP and WSDL because it was quick and made their jobs easier…but to get on something [that] supports services across agencies is another matter.

(S-CEA)

This di<sup>f</sup>erence in the duties and responsibilities of the EA Program and agency developers manifested as an impediment to change. Enterprise Architects were <sup>fi</sup>rmly entrenched in duties that supported the proliferation of SEAF while agency developers were <sup>fi</sup>rmly entrenched in duties that supported the mission of their agency. Adding to this was the notion that SEAF was “delivered from the top down” (S-EA1) and that Enterprise Architects had little jurisdiction in making EA a responsibility (paraphrased from S-EA1). This was further substantiated when the State CTO was asked about the role of the ITGB in getting agency developers to feel responsible if the did not comply with SEAF:

The technology board is great but it only serves as a venue and information forum and not so much as a forum that would ensure that whatever standards that we want to be set are gonna be set so I'm not sure that answers your question but my bottom line is the board no way changes who is responsible to who.

(S-CTO)

To provoke normative change, the enterprise architects shifted their roles to what we describe as being agency consultants. Several Enterprise Architects consulted agencies on funding proposals to better align with the EA Program. For example, Enterprise Architects engaged in “writing proposals to align with our policies [SEAF]” (S-EA3), “advising on the most appropriate technologies and standards that could be used in procurement choices” (S-EA2), and “assisting with implementation processes such as inputs on the composition of service interfaces to facilitate inter-agency integration” (S-EA2). The researcher <sup>fi</sup>rst found out about the consulting activity when the state CTO (S-CTO) was asked about how they attempted to in<sup>fl</sup>uence agency norms:

We are popping up in the forums and in the agencies, working with other agencies, and acting as consultants to try and give them sound advice. Some of us have been working in the agencies and communicating back to the central team. We have a virtual team to make sure we are connected to the center.

(S-CTO)

In most cases, Enterprise Architects participated in the proposal-writing process of agencies and sat in on their meetings to provide advice on technology choices. The following quote was received when an Enterprise Architect (S-EA1) was asked how consulting architects impacted agency developers' compliance with the EA Program:

We help you with selecting products that are needed in the standards or how we can help to put the pieces together that integrate with the existing pieces that you want to retain, or we often help write RFP, Request for Proposal, to procure new components or new systems. So, it's part of drafting the RFP. I participate in RFP drafting with these agencies to procure the system components that are compliant [with SEAF].

(S-EA1)

One feature that made the practice of role shifting vis-à-vis the engagement of Enterprise Architects at the agency-level activities an impactful mechanism for change was the increasing regulatory EA requirements for project funding. Our review of the state's project funding guidelines showed that while in 2004 EA compliance was only one of the “suggested standards” for project proposals, in 2006, new projects were expected to show evidence of their EA compliance, and the 2010 requirements explicitly requested EA compliance for successful funding proposals. Facing these requirements, agencies appreciated the consulting services from Enterprise Architects because many of them lacked the knowledge to comply with EA standards, as the State CTO commented:

Any one of these agencies, they haven't implemented a system in many years, so when they get funding to implement a system, they have no internal experience or exposure with how to implement the systems, the skillsets that are associated with it.

(State CTO)

At the time of our inquiries, the practice of using architects had been widely accepted and normalized by agencies, and in many cases, Enterprise Architects told us that agencies actively requested help from the EA Program in supporting their agency compliance.

…we do have an architect aside to review most or all of the projects as far as I know. But in addition to have an architect aside, often time, even if we don't have an architect, the agency CIOs will come to our CTO and ask specifically for somebody for short-term loan, you know, maybe a one or two days meeting or design lesson. It could be a couple of weeks, so it really varies. They often come to us and ask for a person to participate if we have time.

(S-EA3)

## 5.3. Case 1: cultural-cognitive

EA elements also caused a cultural-cognitive conflict in case 1. They were valued by agency developers from a standardization perspective, while valued by Enterprise Architects for their cost-cutting and optimization potential. From the perspective of the Deputy CIO, agencies valued EA as a guideline for “standardization” and “standardizing agency IT” (S-DCIO). In contrast, Enterprise Architects valued EA for its ability to “optimize IT” (S-EA1), “condensing [of] agency IT” (S-EA1), and “alleviating budgeting concerns” (S-EA3). This was discussed in depth when the Deputy CIO was probed about who the stakeholders of EA were, and how they valued EA:

The agencies couldn't get past the idea that EA was all about standardization and I can't blame them since everything you read from OMB [US Ofice of Management and Budget] is all about standard IT for budgeting and procurement. But that isn't how you cut costs…it's about optimization and process and it seems like more work to them but it's really not.

(S-DCIO)

The reason for this con<sup>fl</sup>ict was that EA elements and their legitimacy were simultaneously supported and undermined by a system intended to preserve agency autonomy. This system reinforced the legitimating aspects of strong agencies, but undermined perceived legitimacy of the EA Program and SEAF. As an example, implementing SOA protocols and technologies posed no perceivable threat to autonomy. However, requesting agencies to optimize assets was another matter as it (1) decreased the sheer number of things IT agencies were responsible for, and (2) meant agencies would have to use the systems of other agencies to provide civic services. This was reinforced when an Enterprise Architect was asked about how agency culture threatened the values inherent to the EA Program:

The state strongly believes in agencies ability to self-govern…like, we can get them to implement SOA but no way are they going to borrow services or change to Rational [UP]. The developers love implementing SOA since it gets them away from the mainframe world but changing methods takes away freedom.

(S-EA2)

The aforementioned allegiance to state agencies was pre<sup>fi</sup>gured by identity formations that impeded cultural-cognitive change (Ashforth & Mael, 1989). Research reinforces this idea, in particular, the idea that situated and deep structure social identities can undermine exchange-based and value-based commitments (Meyer, Becker, & Van Dick, 2006). This was also reinforced by Enterprise Architects who described agency developers as “wedded to their agency” (S-EA1), and “working on the same systems for 20 years” (S EA2). In addition, this was reinforced when the State CIO discussed in-depth how agency sovereignty a<sup>f</sup>ects the job of the EA Program:

The engineers work in their agencies and sovereign agencies waste taxpayer dollars. We need to be process-based so good resources aren't just agency property. What's also is that I don't need 7 accounting systems for each agency and other accounting systems embedded inside. I need agencies to work together and trim the fat. That's why they brought me here…to change strong agency culture that costs the taxpayer and the citizen.

(S-CIO)

We interpreted the EA Program's practice of using metaphors as a change mechanism. In particular, in creating shared lexicons through which the architects could change existing culture and attempt to get agency developers to internalize the values of the EA Program. For example, the metaphor of “moving to service-based agencies” (S-CEA) was a form of shared lexicon used to quell the idea that a strong agency culture would su<sup>f</sup>er from adopting the values of the EA Program. “Service reusability” (S-CIO) was used to acclimate agency developers to agency-speci<sup>fi</sup>c services that could be used across the state. “Utility services” (S-CEA) was used to acclimate agency developers to the idea that enterprise-level services could provide greater value to both agency developers and citizens. And “services as platforms” (S-CTO) was used to acclimate agency developers to the idea that services could be integrated to provide new civic services. As a form of shared lexicon, metaphors were <sup>fi</sup>rst revealed when the Chief Enterprise Architect was asked about what techniques the EA Program used to persuade agencies to use SEAF:

We often derive metaphors from concrete examples of saying that their current systems are either broken or have very limiting features or features that should be extended […] So we focus on things specific to their business that would have measureable impact if they would make them: design in smaller bits and more loosely coupled with the eco-systems around them so that they could change more rapidly with the changing business conditions and business requirements.

(S-CEA)

In another instance, a review of the ITGB meeting notes showed how carefully state agencies and architects worked together to frame new changes in positive lights and not as a disruptive force to existing culture. In this example, the shared lexicon regarding consolidation, a move that strengthens EA institutions in the state, was adjusted to minimize the negative impacts of consolidation while highlighting the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Agencies' concerns such as job elimination, lack of prior experience, or lack of careful planning were addressed <sup>fi</sup>rst, followed by discussion on how consolidation was a suitable solution for cost savings in government:

Consolidation—this will move the State towards a standard, eficient and cost-efective environment…This efort is not designed to eliminate jobs. We will plan carefully and thoughtfully; we can learn from other states. Government was not organized in a haphazard way, but technology has driven us that way. Now that the technology exists to consolidate, we need to do it for cost savings and to provide a secure environment. We need to be able to meet growing needs with the resources we have.

(ITGB meeting note 2008)

It is not easy to assess how e<sup>f</sup>ective these strategies were, but our inquiries in 2012 and subsequent revisits to state websites in the following years showed strong evidence of a stable and well-supported EA Program in the state. In 2009, EA was listed as one of the key initiatives for the statewide consolidation project; in 2012, EA standards enabled the state to quickly develop the health information exchange (S-EA3); and at the time of writing, EA compliance remains a requirement for state project proposals. Thus, it seems that EA institutions have been legitimized and accepted in the state as part of its operations.

## 5.4. Case 2: regulative

In contrast to case 1, the EA Program in case 2 lacked regulatory elements as it had neither an EA framework nor explicit rules, policies, or authoritative guidelines that could coerce business units to use EA. Moreover, when they had previously developed EA artifacts and attempted to distribute them, it caused a resistance movement among the business units. The EA Program found itself in a position where it had little hope of regulative enforcement, and rather dim hopes of being in<sup>fl</sup>uential. This was substantiated when the CTO was asked how he intended to get the business units to use EA:

![](/api/attachments/8K7KVBKF/fulltext/images/b8e0e339b9e89bb87abae2c05ff9c36d803f6923584033d5dbe6baaf89ca6488.jpg)

EA was attempted a few years ago…we set up as a TOGAF shop and marched along the ADM to develop artifacts everyone tells you to do in EA. What they didn't tell was how hard it would be to get people to use them. They threw it right back in our face and said we never approved this so why would we use it…I hope we can show them we are the smartest people in the room and hope they listen.

(P-CTO)

A lack of regulative elements undermined regulative legitimacy, and thus, at the time of our interviewing, the EA Program was more focused on how to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential. In what we perceived as a surrogate for regulative legitimacy, legitimacy gains were, instead, based on “in<sup>fl</sup>uence without authority” (P-CTO), and “<sup>fi</sup>nding ways to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential” (P-PEA). However, this was di<sup>fi</sup>cult considering EA was previously viewed by the business units as “a way to enforce standards” (P-EA1) and “a dictatorship” (P-CTO). The idea of in<sup>fl</sup>uence without authority was further substantiated when the Lead Enterprise Architect was asked what it would take for the EA Program to be seen as a legitimate source of technical knowledge:

We have to show that we are not some dictatorship trying to take over IT this is what they thought we were trying to do. All we want is to be influential in the way the company standardizes and integrate data sources …right now I have no ability to do that…all I can do is work with them and hope they realize we know what we are talking about

(P-LEA)

We interpreted the company's divisional structure to represent the most prominent impediment to change that undermined regulative legitimacy gains by the EA Program–a centrally-located organization charged with integrating IT across business units. This divisional structure goes back to the company's roots, where the founder believed pushing autonomy down to the business unit leve was the best way to foster innovation. Furthermore, this was enacted as policy in that promotion and <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives were solely determined within divisions. Evidence of this was revealed when one of the Enterprise Architects was asked about how EA, a corporate-level organization, contrasted with the company's divisional structure:

What you see on the outside is [Company] as a single big corporation. What we see on the inside is 40 diferent businesses sharing cafeteria services. EA is simply orthogonal to how we run as a company…the company is a bunch of self-reliant businesses and here we come trying to reach across them all.

(P-EA4)

To overcome these impediments, the EA Program began working with business-unit management to make EA a part of <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentivization and promotion. The change mechanism was the practice of creating a shared space through which Enterprise Architects and business units could negotiate a common ground. By creating this shared space, Enterprise Architects negotiated with businessunit managers to integrate the goals of the EA Program into employees' yearly commitments as a way to increase employees' participation in EA development. Through these negotiations, architects and business-unit managers were able to share and exchange concerns, understand each other's needs, and bridge divisions. This strategy was motivated by the fact that the company's culture was “heavily commitment focused” (P-EA3), and thus, it was imperative for the EA Program to get on the yearly commitment list to have business units commit their “time” and “resources" (P-IA2). From our perspective, the shared space that facilitated tying EA goals to incentivization appeared as an e<sup>f</sup>ective attempt to establish regulative structure. We <sup>fi</sup>rst found out about this when the Lead Information Architect was asked about how the EA Program was making inroads into the business units:

We are getting there but the biggest pushback I am having right now is the project I am working on started mid-year and it is not locked into people's commitments…if people don't have it on their list of commitments than it just won't happen. [Company] is very commitment focused they are what you get reviewed on and employees are locked into their commitment. We got EA on that yearly commitment list in marketing and is it working well for us

(P-LIA)

Once the EA Program focused on being in<sup>fl</sup>uential through changing employees' yearly commitment list, the architects received a lot more responses and collaboration from business units. For example, an IT Engineer described how his annual evaluation determined his commitment to develop standard data de<sup>fi</sup>nitions for the marketing unit, and to have them used company-wide (paraphrased from P-LIA). Additional evidence was shown through our conversations with other Enterprise Architects and employees as they commented on how this strategy was key to their EA involvement:

…in my own [yearly] commitment it says we have to get alignment with a number of programs. In the form of their roadmap aligning with my roadmap. So that's part of my own personal success.

(P-IA3)

To solve that cross-pollination and collaboration that is definitely one thing, if you can drive that collaboration to happen you've got a lot of impact. Unfortunately that is very hard, for diferent reasons. There are a lot of political hurdles, who is going to take the lead, who is going to take the credit in the end. People are very resource constrained, so to have team collaboration is sometimes very time consuming. You can put people together as a virtual team, and people start talking, but when you say who is going to take action for specific things then the interesting part will happen because you know, nobody really wants to take action for doing the real work. It's just natural, it's not in people's [yearly] commitment.

(P-IA2)

## 5.5. Case 2: normative

Getting into the yearly commitment list had a regulative e<sup>f</sup>ect on business units, but it was not enough to ensure normative changes. In case 2, normative elements were seen in the opposing work roles of Enterprise Architects and business unit engineers. Prior to the resistance movement that sparked the EA Program's downsizing, the EA Program consisted of mainly technical personnel who saw their role as “writing EA documentation” (P-EA2), while business unit engineers' roles were described as “purely interested in working for their unit” (P-EA1). After the revitalization of the EA Program, however, the Enterprise Architects described their roles as “collaborating with the businesses to create usable EA” (P-LEA), but still were unable to shift the work roles of business unit engineers. This was substantiated when the Lead Enterprise Architect was asked if the current EA Program was having an e<sup>f</sup>ect on the roles of those in the business units despite the changes to the yearly commitment list:

Without really tangible constraints it's just hard to get the IT folks to do anything about it…We got into their yearly commitment and it has changed how we do business with each other but it's gonna be hard to sustain those roles.

(P-LEA)

This divergence in roles manifested as a deeper divergence from normative expectations that hindered the EA Program in gaining normative legitimacy. We interpreted business unit engineers as being tied to moral and ethical systems that kept them working on projects that directly bene<sup>fi</sup>tted their business unit. In turn, we interpreted Enterprise Architects as morally tied to the moral and ethical systems of EA–producing the artifacts that standardize data and data sources, and applications and infrastructure (TOGAF, 2009). This was further substantiated when the CTO was asked about how he expected the EA Program to change the nature of work roles in the company:

Governance alone doesn't work…we thought we could get people to come along because it was supposed to be good for business but we found out we had no support and a lot of outright bickering…now they know we are supposed to help them but they don't necessarily feel committed to help us.

(P-CTO)

The company's divisional structure was the chief impediment that gave rise to the divergence in work roles and subsequent expectations that kept the duties and responsibilities of both parties misaligned. We initially discovered how the company's divisional structure re<sup>fl</sup>ected work roles and expectations in interviews with the CTO, who described working with the business units on EA as “extremely complicated” (P-CTO), as well as the Enterprise Architect quoted above who described the company as “40 di<sup>f</sup>erent businesses sharing cafeteria services” (P-EA4). Our conceptualization of this as the chief impediment was further substantiated when the Lead Information Architect was asked about how the divisional structure of the company limits the EA Program's potential:

Giving the businesses ultimate autonomy and control was how [founder] made this place successful but we don't sell code anymore we sell services and they have to be better process-oriented across the businesses…the hardest thing has been getting them [the business units] to see that and do something about it. Until then [until they do] we are just paper tigers.

(P-LIA)

As a mechanism for change to overcome the impediments of divisional structure, the EA Program engaged in role-shifting practices when they began dispatching Enterprise Architects to engineers who had EA on their yearly commitments to “supply business units with shortage expertise” (P-CTO). For example, the marketing unit needed database expertise to integrate data sources for targeted marketing campaigns, and at the time of interviewing, there was reference to this happening in other business units. The most needed resource across the company was expertise in information architecture, and because the EA Program was charged with developing MDM, it had previously recruited several experts in this area. Thus, the EA program dispatched Information Architects to the business units to help with database integration, and to do so in line with the universal data de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, data governance plans, and data systems architecture concomitant with MDM. In turn, the Information Architects circulated knowledge about the business units to evolve EA:

I would say initially we gave them services for free. We were just happy to be given access. We were just glad we weren't being ‘ivory tower anymore and were trying to make a diference. We gave them universal data definitions, data governance plans, data systems architecture, and much more and eventually we got them to give us stuff about their work and think about our work and come to our meetings.

(P-EA6)

The dispatched architects understood that to change behaviors, they needed to work with business units at the operational leve rather than instructing them at the conceptual level. Several architects commented on the importance of “pick[ing] the right problem” (P-EA4), “understanding who your individual stakeholders are, not just their names, but also their motivation” (P-LIA), and asking for the problems that business units are trying to solve (P-EA7). Compared to the <sup>fi</sup>rst initiative, this strategy allowed the EA Program to “in<sup>fl</sup>uence without authority” (P-PEA), to communicate EA value and normalize its institutions right down at the practice level of business-unit operations. The impacts of dispatched architects were substantiated in the following comment by one of the architects who saw no need for a “sales job” when real problems were addressed:

…so if there's an owner that says my data is all mixed up, I need to fix this. So we dive in and fix that, no sales job needed! If you say your data is all messed up, maybe they don't feel that way, you baby is ugly, but if you have an owner they will usually tell you what they want and if they need changes and what the problems are they are facing. And they are diferent from what you might solve in looking at it from the outside. People do have priorities and are closer to the space and it is best to focus on real business problems and drive the change needed to solve those problems.

(P-EA3)

## 5.6. Case 2: cultural-cognitive

The cultural-cognitive elements in case 2 were re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the dynamics between EA's goals for better integration and the company's autonomous culture. As a global technology conglomerate, the company has a divisional structure that fostered a culture of autonomy among business units. This culture was central to the company's technological innovativeness and to the policymaking, cultural norms, and values of the business units. In contrast, the EA Program's intentions of integrating IT across business units was seen as a “threat [that] hinders what made us one of the world's largest software producers” (P-ITE). When asked how he views some of the central values of the company and how the EA Program <sup>fi</sup>ts into those values, the Lead Enterprise Architect replied with the following quote that illustrated the dominant institutional values, beliefs, and assumptions at the company:

The way we are divided by businesses gives people the freedom to innovate…this is, oh by the way, what made us a multi-billion-dollar company and gave us the wealth that created this mess we are trying to fix…I wish they saw it that way…they value autonomy so much its gonna hurt EA and hurt the company bottom line.

(P-LEA)

The company's divisional structure and accompanying culture of autonomy were also underpinned by an inter-business-unit competitive culture that hindered legitimacy gains by the EA Program. From the early days of when the company had limited resources, a culture of <sup>fi</sup>erce competition for human and IT resources was created to reward those who were the most innovative, productive, and pro<sup>fi</sup>t-yielding for the company. For the EA Program, this competitive culture appeared to run quite deep, as this was part of the founder's goal in pushing autonomy down to the business units. Eventually, this competitive culture made it di<sup>fi</sup>cult for the EA Program to commit business units' scarce time and resources from other priorities, threatening the EA Program's legitimacy as a useful initiative for business units. Indeed, this notion was exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed when one of the Enterprise Architects was asked about competition between business units:

We have had some of the best engineers in the world here for 20 plus years and they are driven to innovate from [Company]'s early start-up days when the businesses were competing for valuable resources just to survive. These folks are now icons in the industry and they set the tone for how the businesses work. Fierce competition between the businesses and the demand to be left alone so they can produce is our culture here…ego driven.

(P-EA3)

From the quote above, this culture of autonomy and competition was underpinned by deep-seated identity formations, which we perceived as the dominant cultural-cognitive impediment to institutional change. Compounding this was that the intentions of the EA Program to reach across business units directly contrasted with these identity formations. This was substantiated when the CTO spoke precisely about how many of the company's best engineers saw themselves and how that makes implementing EA on a company-wide scale di<sup>fi</sup>cult:

We are a company of 30,000+ engineers who are here because they produce the best software in the world…and they know it…this is their life some literally (laughs). They know we need [MDM] and they know we need to integrate so that we can know more about who purchases our software…but they also know that they are evaluated by their peers by building the applications that make the company money.

(P-CTO)

As a mechanism for change, the EA Program engaged in practices to facilitate the creation of shared lexicons to focus on the internalization of EA values to slowly overcome cultural-cognitive impediments, which mainly had to do with relinquishing control of key EA artifacts. In the words of one Enterprise Architect, it was so engineers could use their own terms to de<sup>fi</sup>ne data de<sup>fi</sup>nitions in their own way” (P-EA4), in hopes that “they could use their own words to de<sup>fi</sup>ne data <sup>fi</sup>elds the way they wanted” (P-EA6), and “get credit for working with us and own the space (P-LIA). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the EA Program relinquished control to marketing unit engineers to supervise the creation of several MDM components, and in turn, using the technique of dispatching, the Enterprise Architects worked with business unit engineers to act as advisors. This idea of relinquishing control was <sup>fi</sup>rst discovered when the Principle Enterprise Architect was asked about how the EA Program seeks to get others to internalize the values of the EA Program:

First and foremost is the bottom line that we have to show them we are not trying to alter their worlds with EA…so we gave them control of data definitions and a few other parts of MDM…the EAs in my group were trying to use their terms as opposed to the engineers and so what if it was more industry accepted…they needed to have their own verbiage in there so they didn't feel like they were being forced to re-learn everything

(P-PEA)

The concept of relinquishing control was also further substantiated through changes in the language used by Enterprise

Architects. Architects that we talked to felt it important to “translate business lingo into technology and vice versa” (P-EA1), to have “soft skills and [di<sup>f</sup>erent] views” (P-LIA), and to have “strong personal skills and interpersonal skills” (P-CTO). One architect provided a great example in which he had to adjust his lexicon when talking to di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders:

When you get into the strategy execution step, you are down a notch and you are with kind of the senior business leaders and business managers who care about they have a job to do and to get it done. Now this is the get it done people…for folks at that level you have to get out of the alignment conversation. Alignment has to happen fast and quietly. Because the moment you bring up alignment to them they're gonna go “your questioning the scope of my program”…For folks at that level you really have to bring it up to what are the key things that they want, need to know, and what they need to know how to do is reduce risk. So you have to illustrate risk and you have to illustrate the things you are going to do to reduce risk. Or suggest they do organizationally, process-wise or systematically to reduce risk.

(P-LEA)

The relinquishing of control helped the EA Program be better received by business units. At the time of inquiry, EA practices had increasingly been included in many business units' yearly commitment list, and the EA Program was well on its way to complete the MDM, the very <sup>fi</sup>rst artifact since the inception of the program. The perception of the EA Program has slowly changed as more people realize the importance of enterprise-wide standards. A few even actively sought out help from the EA Program, as told by one of the architects:

…when I joined [Company] back in 2006 I was of the idealistic nature. I said, this is, I was in the marketing group. And I said, I painted the future state of Architecture from marketing. I said, you need to have these and these and these things. And I got lots of resistance. The thought it is purely academic. I said, no, this is not academic and I promoted this in multiple organizations, but they were, they couldn't get it. And I was kind of tagged as a failure. But guess what? Last month lots of people started coming to me from marketing. They said, “Hey, [P-EA1], you know what? That thing that you presented six years ago, we are actually implementing it now. We need your help.” I said oh man, after six years.

(P-EA1)

## 6. Discussion

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our collaborative case study o<sup>f</sup>er three direct contributions to IS research: (1) We elucidate deeper understanding of how actors enact legitimating strategies that make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations, which has been cited as an underserved area of IS research (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009, p. 388). (2) We answer the call to move beyond the IT artifact as the central focus of institutional logics, where research on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of institutionalism have underplayed the importance of politically and socially constructed realities in shaping the processes and e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT (Currie, 2012; Currie & Swanson, 2009). Lastly, (3) we elucidate how micro-practices in<sup>fl</sup>uence the acceptance of <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions at the organizational level, which, in line with the need for more explicit prescriptions in theory for design and action (Gregor, 2006), helps us understand how organizations can foster acceptance of new institutions among extant institutional and organizational norms

EA is unequivocally a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution. It represents well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned views on how to organize IT infrastructure and IT activities (Ross et al., 2006) and has rigorous frameworks that are standardized by <sup>fi</sup>eld-level organizations, and these frameworks are used in organizations as a roadmap for IT architectural change (Uhl & Gollenia, 2016; Hoogervorst, 2004). However, the process of bringing <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions into existing practices is not unique to EA. It is an unavoidable aspect of implementing any IS design process (e.g. Agile, Kanban, Continuous Integration, DevOps, Evented IO, etc.), and we contend understanding of this type of institutional infusion warrants further attention

Our study focuses on change mechanisms, enacted as micro-practices, and how they arise from the enduring features of institutions. In case 1, the enduring features of state institutions were rooted in compliance, regulation, and a strong agency culture that made full acceptance of SEAF and the EA Program elusive. In case 2, enduring features of institutions in this private company were rooted in a divisional structure and its resulting norms and culture that rejected the very position of in<sup>fl</sup>uence the EA Program sought. However, from a change perspective, the cases revealed some interesting similarities. From a regulative perspective, both EA Programs created shared spaces (Nonaka & Konno, 1998; Pan & Leidner, 2003; Volko<sup>f</sup>, Elmes, & Strong, 2004; Wasko & Faraj, 2000) for Enterprise Architects to exchange knowledge or negotiate with their end-users. From a normative perspective, both EA programs combated unit-level autonomy and worked within shared spaces to shift roles of architects and engage in a form of resource currency exchange to in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT development. Lastly, from a cultural-cognitive perspective, both EA Programs shifted lexicons to better resonate with users (Cohendet & Steinmueller, 2000; Enns & McDonagh, 2012; Kashanchi & Toland, 2008; Reich & Benbasat, 2000).

These micro-practices that emerged in both cases provide additional insight to practice studies vis-à-vis institutional change. To date, most studies have focused on the interactions between end-users and technologies, particularly the adaptive maneuvers of endusers (Berente & Yoo, 2012; Leonardi et al., 2016); or have emphasized discursive strategies utilized by change champions to legitimize new technologies and gain end-users' buy-in (Cheney et al., 2004; Erkama & Vaara, 2010; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). Recent studies within the “practice turn” in strategy and IS research (Whittington, 2006) focused on short episodes of technology use through which new strategies were implemented and formulated at the same time (Jarzabkowski & Kaplan, 2015; Suddaby et al., 2013). Others like Smets et al. (2012) illustrated how practice-level activities transfer across boundaries and accumulate over time to become new institutions. To those studies, our micro-practices suggest that institutional change is also enabled through intervening tactics by change agents, not just through end-user activity; and these tactics were not used in isolation but rather in conjunction to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. As Arvidsson et al. (2014) posited, IS strategy implementation is a multi-dimensional issue that involves interrelated factors—artifacts, actors, structures, and processes. Through these micro-practices, one can observe combinations of tactics to address such a complexity and to make EA work: using institutional structures through shared spaces (Ahlemann et al., 2012), engagement practices through roles shifting (Hardy & Phillips, 1998; Tello-Rozas, Pozzebon, & Mailhot, 2015), and discursive strategies through lexical shifting (Kaganer et al., 2010; Suddaby & Greenwood, 2005). Together, they provided the necessar guidance and encouragement that broke down the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive impediments of existing institutions. Next, we discuss in detail the three micro-practices (Fig. 6).

## 6.1. Change mechanism 1: shared spaces for knowledge exchange

Much like the Japanese concept of Ba (Nonaka & Konno, 1998), our study revealed two EA Programs that found creative ways to obligate stakeholders to participate in shared spaces for knowledge exchange. In case 1, the EA Program utilized the ITGB, which was formed for state agency representatives to share knowledge through success stories. This governance body then became a shared space between the EA Program and agencies to engage in discussions on how EA can become more relevant to the agencies. In case 2, shared spaces were created when the EA Program negotiated through managerial discussions and persuaded business unit engineers to factor EA objectives into their yearly employee commitments, obligating them to engage in developing EA. For case 1, shared spaces proliferated use of SEAF as a regulative mechanism for IT decision making and development. For case 2, shared spaces were created by regulative mechanisms to incentivize participation in creating EA.

![](/api/attachments/8K7KVBKF/fulltext/images/dd6455399d6c049035bbf1ff4e10b3ded4dd82daca42ea1340b61161133069a4.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Change mechanisms.

The concept of shared spaces contributes to an understanding of how regulative mechanisms are implemented in IS contexts to change institutions. While researchers have studied shared spaces through communities of practice perspectives (Pan & Leidner, 2003; Volko<sup>f</sup> et al., 2004; Wasko & Faraj, 2000), much of this research centers on the use of IT artifacts as mechanisms for knowledge exchange, and does little to investigate institutional structures. In addition, in institutional IS research, a paucity of research investigates the underlying interpretive processes by which new institutions are enacted in existing practices (Hultin & Mähring, 2014). We <sup>fi</sup>nd this surprising given the calls by multiple scholars to: (1) move beyond institutional research that centers on the IT artifact (Currie & Swanson, 2009), and (2) further understand how legitimating strategies are enacted beyond acquiescent regulative mechanisms (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009, p. 388; Oliver, 1991). We answer this call by elucidating the regulative stimuli that created shared spaces to engender compromise, as opposed to acquiescence, in responding to institutional processes (Oliver, 1991). In our cases, the creation of shared spaces acted as a platform to reconcile expectations, accommodate unknown institutional elements, and allow two-way negotiation (Oliver, 1991, p. 154) where members shared, created, and exploited knowledge (Nonaka et al., 2000), and represented environments that shifted roles and internalized the values of a new institution. We contend that, when it comes to understanding the regulative mechanisms that contribute to implementing new institutions in existing practices, further theoretica and empirical attention is warranted. This is so particularly given that research has described shared spaces as incredibly dynamic, and sometimes con<sup>fl</sup>ict-ridden endeavors where existing institutional pillars clash head-on with new regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive frames (Pache & Santos, 2010; Sanders & Tuschke, 2007; To<sup>fl</sup>er, 1990). Indeed, for practitioners to navigate these environments, shared spaces must be utilized in creative ways to enable two-way knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ow between IS practitioners and endusers, allowing ideas and policies to be molded, evolved, and adjusted to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs of the local contexts (Ahlemann et al., 2012: Foorthuis. Van Steenbergen. Brinkkemper. & Bruls. 2016).

## 6.2. Change mechanism 2: role shifting within shared spaces

Both cases also revealed how Enterprise Architects engaged in acts that engendered role shifts within shared spaces. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Enterprise Architects shifted their roles as developers of EA artifacts towards roles associated with EA end-users, and in turn, shifted the roles, habits, and norms of end-users to work more with the EA Programs. In case 1, Enterprise Architects shifted their roles to act as consultants on agency projects and to provide agency developers assistance employing EA. They engaged mostly in project pro posal writing activities, helping agencies select the right technologies that comply with EA guidelines and state standards. In addition, they advised agencies on implementation processes as most agencies lacked the necessary know-how. In case 2, the EA Program dispatched Enterprise Architects to work directly as developers within the business units to assist with database integration, and in so doing, develop MDM in line with the objectives of the EA Program. The architects focused on working on problems that were relevant to the business units, thus making the EA artifacts more acceptable.

In both cases, we theorize that both EA Programs engaged in acts of currency exchange, which has been discussed as a reciprocity tactic when <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in situations where actors lack direct authority (Cohen & Bradford, 1989; Nelson & Cooprider, 1996; Yukl & Falbe, 1990). Both EA Programs exchanged currency by providing needed business and technical value to agencies and business units. In case 1, currency was the knowledge, expertise, and help that facilitated acquiring funding and making appropriate decisions regarding the implementation of new projects aligned with SEAF. In case 2, currency was the expertise needed to integrate databases, and do so in a way that aligned with the motives of the EA Program. Currency in both cases was primarily task related (Cohen & Bradford, 2007, p. 38): relating to a person's ability to perform his or her assigned tasks or to the satisfactions that arise from accomplishment. However, it was also inspiration-related (Cohen & Bradford, 2007, p. 36); as Enterprise Architects shifted roles to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the inspirational goals that provide meaning to their end users' work, and was position-related (case 2); and as EA was tied to the enhancement of a person's position in the organization and, thereby, aided the person's ability to accomplish tasks or advance a career (case 1).

The idea of currency exchange highlights the role of compromise as a strategic response to structural inertia (Oliver, 1991). Both cases engaged in acts of compromise as they balanced the desires of the EA Program with the needs of constituents, situated themselves within institutional elements, and negotiated with institutional stakeholders to appropriately place themselves in their environment (Oliver, 1991, p. 153). As previously noted, strategic responses other than acquiescence are seldom discussed in IS research (Mignerat & Rivard, 2009, p. 389; Standing et al., 2009). Instead, IT artifacts such as EIS are seen as carriers of institutional logics that influence users to make social structural changes (Davidson & Chismar, 2007: Gosain, 2004) and IT outsourcing decisions are seen as consequences of acquiescence to environmental norms (Ang & Cummings. 1997: Miranda & Kim. 2006). In contrast, we contend that di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of strategic responses are ripe for IS research, particularly since prior research reports that (1) top management does not just mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of normative pressures on system usage, it directly a<sup>f</sup>ects it (Liang, Saraf, Hu, & Xue, 2007); (2) early experiences with innovations in<sup>fl</sup>uence later adoptions and drive peripheral actors to engage in practices of discursive persuasion and skill reproduction (Compagni et al., 2015); and (3) structural inertia increases monotonically with age (Hannan & Freeman, 1984).

## 6.3. Change mechanism 3: lexical shifts to internalize values

Both cases revealed the importance of lexical shifts, or, ‘using the language of your users’, as a way to in<sup>fl</sup>uence internalization of values. Case 1 made heavy use of metaphors to link the concepts of SEAF and the EA Program to the work environments of agency developers. EA documents and meeting reports were framed from perspectives of agencies as to minimize the disruptive nature of EA institutions. Case 2 permitted the lexicon of business units to be directly included in MDM, and architects embraced the new lexicon in conversations with their stakeholders. Architects relied on social and interpersonal skills to “translate” technical problems into business problems that were familiar to their end-users. Research reinforces the importance of a lexicon as a tactic to align or internalize di<sup>f</sup>erent values, particularly in looking at language misalignment as a source of lost in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Enns & McDonagh, 2012; Reich & Benbasat, 2000), and in the use of lexicons to codify stakeholder knowledge (Cohendet & Steinmueller, 2000; Kashanchi & Toland, 2008). Thus, we theorize that both EA Programs utilized lexical shifts as a change mechanism to deepen their end-users conceptualization, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and internalization of EA as a new organizational form (Hsu & Hannan, 2005).

The use of lexical shifts as a tactic for values internalization also further deepens the importance of compromise as a strategic response to assimilate <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions into organizations. For case 1, the use of metaphors by Enterprise Architects was a balancing tactic to reconcile the motives of the EA Program with the motives of agency developers, which research theorizes as a way to mitigate con<sup>fl</sup>icting institutional demands and inconsistencies between constituents and those exhibiting a strategic response (Rowan, 1982). For case 2, the allowing of business unit engineers' lexicon by Enterprise Architects into MDM was a form of passive in<sup>fl</sup>uence to stimulate conformity in the liminal space between the expectations of business unit engineers and Enterprise Architects, particularly as a way to placate sources of resistance to the EA Program (Prasad & Prasad, 2000). This further emphasizes the importance of understanding strategic responses and how they manifest institutional change. Thus, institutional IS research would be well-served in investigating the toolbox of strategic responses actors incorporate as they enact <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions into their respective organizations.

## 7. Conclusion

This article contributes to an understanding of how actors enact legitimating strategies that make <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions a part of organizations. Like all research our study has several limitations. First, while comparative studies facilitate generalization to theory, in no way do they constitute evidence of a workable approach for a majority of organizations. Thus, further research is needed to uncover further variants of legitimating strategies, and how they may di<sup>f</sup>er in IS contexts versus traditional organizational contexts (Oliver, 1991). Moreover, meta-analyses of these approaches are needed to provide sound practitioner advice. Second, we chose an institutional lens to study two cases of EA, in addition to which there are innumerable other lenses researchers could use to investigate the work of Enterprise Architects. Further research on EA, and Enterprise Architects as actors in IS contexts, would be wel served by using a multitude of other lenses, such as actor-network theory or sense-making, to understand how EA manifests itself in organizations, and how a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution such as EA gains organizational acceptance. Third, our research is simply a snapshot of two EA Programs. In particular, it is a snapshot of a phase where Enterprise Architects are <sup>fi</sup>guring out how to introduce and implement EA in their respective organizations. Thus, in no way can our research depict EA as bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to these organization, nor does our study in any way depict cases of EA success. Further research on EA, using an institutional lens or otherwise, might consider investigating EA at di<sup>f</sup>erent phases of the institutional change process, for example, at phases of totality, contradiction, or praxis (Seo & Creed, 2002, p. 225) as EA becomes more established as an institution, as it evolves, or how inconsistencies manifest between work structure and work practice in EA environments (Meyer & Rowan, 1977; Seo & Creed, 2002).

Overall, our study answers questions about how a <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution can make its way into organizations. We investigated how EA was embedded into two organizations and revealed the common micro-practices and strategies used within them. Together, these micro-practices appeared to be e<sup>f</sup>ective in helping architects address the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive impediments of existing institutions. In addition to answering questions that have been called upon in previous IS research, our study also gives ris to future research opportunities, particularly in furthering understanding of the nascent, chaotic phases of institutional change. Our study provides a snapshot of how actors embed <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions into their organizations and stimulate shifts in regulative structures, normative roles, and cultural-cognitive values to manifest institutional change, Moreover, our study helps us understand how EA, an IS <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institution, manifests from the actions of Enterprise Architect, and the micro-practices used to make inroads. Accordingly, we contend investigation into these nascent and chaotic stages is ripe for further IS research. It could provide additional clarity into the constellations of activities in new IS practices–those just coming into existence and beginning to display signs of future potential–which research has described as rife with complex challenges as actors build new practices into existing organizations, and seek legitimacy for early-stage ideas (Kannan-Narasimhan & Lawrence, 2011). In addition, research in this area could reveal new techniques for how actors in these nascent phases, in their situated environments, engage in deep forms of organizational learning about environmental, organizational, and individual processes as they seek to turn novel, confusing, or equivocal situations into systems of action aligned with strategic value (Honig. 2001). Thus, for IS research, our research contributes to an understanding of how <sup>fi</sup>eld-level institutions become a part of IS organizations. However, we contend further exploration into the micro-practices and strategies, particularly in these early nascent phases, can help researchers uncover the markers for future practitioner success.
