## 1. Introduction

The rapid digitization of business processes over the last two decades has created a significant growth opportunity for IT service providers. The creation of a variety of digital technologies has led firms in most economic sectors to partner with IT service vendors for digitizing components of their business models and processes, as well as for seeking advice for competing in this environment (Kotlarsky et al. 2012, Mithas and Whitaker 2007). The IT services sectors are not only highly competitive with firms vying vigorously for market niches and human capital (NASSCOM-McKinsey 2005, Ranganathan and Kuruvilla 2008, Tymon et al. 2009), but these sectors are also highly institutionalized because of widely prevalent certification standards (Gopal and Gao 2009, Mehta and Mehta 2009), high degree of inter-firm mobility of employees (Audretsch and Feldman 1996, Ranganathan and Kuruvilla 2008) and multisourcing approaches followed by clients (Bapna et al. 2010). We argue that these factors, which tend to increase the transparency of IT vendors’ and establish industry norms, have a direct bearing on firms’ strategic postures, namely the strategy of differentiation and the strategy of imitation. On one hand, in a resource-scarce environment it is understood that success can be achieved through a novel recombination of existing or new capabilities (Ghemawat 1985, Kogut and Zander 1992), and thereby achieving a unique competitive position. This strategy of differentiation has traditionally been the focus of the literature on innovation (Pennings and Harianto 1992, Podolny et al. 1996), technology entrepreneurship (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2002, Rindova and

Kotha 2001, Youngjin et al. 2012), and technological discontinuities (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009, Suarez and Utterback 1995). On the other hand, the factors that foster an institutionalized environment can also lead IT vendors to pursue a strategy of imitation (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Lieberman and Asaba 2006, Ordanini et al. 2008), as is also suggested in the information systems (IS) literature. For example, imitation is viewed as a feature of the institutional context formed of various stakeholders such as IT vendors, IT buyers, and third parties (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, 2004); and recently, firms were found to enjoy performance benefits from firm-level imitation (Chellappa et al. 2010) and (less purposively) pursue fashionable IT trends (Wang 2010). This suggests that a study of firm-level imitation among IT vendors can better help us understand diffusion of innovative products and services and thereby complement the insights from prior literature.

Despite the centrality of the IT services sector to IS research, there is little empirical work that yields insights into how imitation occurs in these sectors. We set the stage by elaborating three research questions that, apart from proposing to apply existing imitation literature to study the IT services sector, also propose to produce unique insights that can stimulate future IS research. The first research question is based on the broader literature that identifies multiple rationales by which firms choose industry rivals whose strategic actions they imitate (these industry rivals are alternately referred to as imitation “referents”). In particular, we focus on the imitation of the serviceline decisions of IT vendors, which cumulatively indicate their market positioning. The research question is Which firms influence an IT service vendor’s choices of new service lines? However, not only are decisions about new service lines important but decisions about service-line pruning are just as crucial. In strategic management literature, product line pruning (Johnson and Myatt 2003, Kotler 1965) or product roll-overs (Koca et al. 2010, Saunders and Jobber 1994) is critical for optimizing resources. However, there is scant research that considers the broader imitation mechanisms underlying product line pruning, and none that examines both development and pruning of services in the same setting.<sup>1</sup> In our context, that of a fastpaced and institutionalized IT sector, we investigate how firm-level imitation affects both types of decisions by IT services vendors: Do the firms that influence an IT vendor’s decision to develop new service lines (referents) differ from those that influence withdrawal? The final research question considers that an

IT vendor’s decision to imitate the service line of an imitation target can differ according to the knowledge domain of that service line. IT vendors can be seen as embedded in three broadly distinct, “institutional spheres” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997), each corresponding to a somewhat distinct knowledge domain: (i) technical service lines are based on expertise on computing-related processes/technologies (e.g., computing/networking/database technologies, software engineering, security/privacy); (ii) functional service lines are created based on expertise for delivering the business process components of their services (e.g., accounting associations, human resource management institutes, and supply chain institutes); (iii) vertical-industry service lines are based on expertise about vertical industries including knowledge about regulations, labor laws, tax laws, trade and marketing practices, and other industry-specific processes. Because each knowledge domain is distinct, we explore whether the imitation of IT service lines by vendors occurs differently across the knowledge domains to which these services belong to. Thus, we ask: Does the imitative influence of referents vary across the knowledge domain of the service lines that are developed or withdrawn by a focal vendor?<sup>2</sup>

We test our research questions using a unique longitudinal data set of 116 Indian IT-enabled services vendors and their service-line development and withdrawal decisions. Table 1 illustrates the ownership change of a subsample of service lines in the Indian IT services industry between 2002 and 2005. It highlights that, even in the short time period, the IT service vendors have frequently made decisions to develop new service lines or withdraw existing ones. It is evident that some service lines are more frequently chosen than others, and vendors do not uniformly possess all or a subset of the service lines. This suggests that the time period may be sufficient to identify the firm-level imitation. We also address the empirical challenge of distinguishing imitation from the nonimitative case where similar firms respond independently to the same external shock (Lieberman and Asaba 2006, Manski 1973). To mitigate this potential misidentification, we use an instrumental variable analysis to achieve identification using the “friend-offriend” approach to create instruments (Shriver et al. 2013, Zeng and Wei 2013). Although this empirical technique is not novel, it has not yet been applied to the context of imitation among various referent groups.

The IT industry is an exemplar for competitive strategy and therefore insights from research grounded in this sector have significant implications for the wider strategy literature (Ferrier et al. 2010). In particular, the IT services sector is an institutional field where customers play a critical role in shaping of products and services, and where the technological trajectory is highly uncertain. These factors set the stage for imitation of service-line offerings by IT vendors. The IT services sector also has added significance because its member firms serve client organizations in all economic sectors. Additionally, as this sector is fast paced and knowledge intensive, the insights from research on this sector can be generalizable to other professional service sectors. Therefore, a deeper understanding of how vendors’ service offerings evolve in response to those of rivals’, has important implications for practitioners such as IT service vendors, clients, and policy makers. It also has important implications for future research in IS and research on similar industries where institutional factors are similarly salient.

Our analysis highlights that although imitation is highly prevalent in this sector, not all imitation mechanisms are equally so. First, the imitation mechanisms are active in different ways when firms choose to develop a new service line versus when they prune it. Next, imitation of the largest vendors and the most popular service lines, both indicative of industry bandwagons, are only selectively at play. Instead, imitation through homophily (peer imitation) is much more widespread in this sector. Our study contributes substantively to the IS literature by laying a basis for a variety of research directions about resource spillovers across IT vendors, vicarious learning, and human capital transfer.

2. Literature and Theory Development We use the literature review below to guide our empirical models in §3. First, we highlight IS literature that draws from institutional theory and competitive strategy in the IT sector as well as characterize the knowledge domain of an IT vendor’s service line in three dimensions—technical, functional, or vertical industry. In §2.2 we review the key rationales of firm-level imitation from strategy literature, which will guide our measures of imitation referents in the empirical models. Finally, §2.3 will explore the literature pertaining to capability (and product) development and withdrawal. It will also explain how understanding of imitation of service-line withdrawals by IT vendors can yield useful insights, apart from imitation of service-line developments.

2.1. The IT Industry and Its Institutional Context In recent years, scholars have contributed to a richer understanding of IT industries from a variety of theoretical perspectives. Extant literature focuses on the internal capabilities that enable firms to exhibit a wide competitive repertoire in high-velocity environments (Li et al. 2010, Taylor and Helfat 2009, Whitaker et al. 2010); the shaping of industry boundaries in emerging fields (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009); the impact of complementarity-based alliances of enterprise software vendors on their financial performance (Ceccagnoli et al. 2011, Chellappa and Saraf 2010, Gnyawali et al. 2010, Lee et al. 2010); firm differentiation through continuous morphing (Rindova et al. 2010); performance effects of mutual forbearance (Young et al. 2000); and exploitation of network externalities (Chellappa et al. 2010). Indeed, a defining feature of the IT industry is that of continuous striving by IT vendors for unique, inimitable innovations (essentially, products and services)— albeit, after accessing external resources through alliances and partnerships. IT vendors are viewed as entrepreneurial (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2002, Rindova and Kotha 2001, Youngjin et al. 2012), having stocks of unique, patentable knowledge (Podolny et al. 1996), and adept at constructing new markets (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009) or leading discontinuous change (Suarez and Utterback 1995).

Many IT vendors, especially those that cater to enterprise customers, often offer a combination of both products and services (Cusumano 2004). They strive to attain an optimal portfolio that constitutes their market position. Furthermore, given the significant role of customers in shaping IT services, multiple service lines are recombined to create actual IT products or services delivered to a specific client. For example, in order to attract clients from the automotive industries who need a human resources management (HRM) system to be installed and maintained, a vendor will need to position itself to offer software and services in HRM processes, automotive/manufacturing processes, and systems implementation. That is, the vendor’s service lines will pertain to strategic analysis and performance enhancement in the automotive industry, analyzing and reengineering HRM processes, and managing specific ERP configuration and implementation. It should be noted that IT service vendors also develop generic capabilities (for example, services delivery methodologies or project management) (Ethiraj et al. 2005, Levina and Ross 2003, Swanson 2010). However, most of these capabilities, though pertinent to the IT outsourcing industry, are also fairly general and apply to other service industries or to professional service firms. In contrast, by announcing its service lines the vendors claim to be equipped with the intermediate, knowledge-domain-specific skills that feed into the final and customized IT contracts for each client.

IS scholars are increasingly applying the institutional framework to understand strategic phenomena in IT sectors (Mignerat and Rivard 2009), especially IT outsourcing and enterprise IT services. As per this framework, IT adoption decisions by organizational clients are dynamic and emergent, and are the result of strategic interactions among multiple stakeholders such as IT vendors, clients, and other third-parties (Swanson and Ramiller 2004, Wang 2010, Wang and Ramiller 2009). Literature points to at least three defining features of the global IT services sectors that reflect the heightened competitiveness among IT vendors and, at the same time, create an institutionalized environment. First, client firms are increasingly multisourcing their needs, that is, they are partnering with multiple IT service providers, as much as to reduce vulnerability toward any one partner, as to access the best-in-class capabilities of each provider (Bapna et al. 2010). Consequently, the vendors’ relationships with overlapping sets of customers result in quicker leakage of product/service attributes and client-specific knowledge to aspiring imitators (Kapur et al. 2001, Levina and Su 2008, Ravindran et al. 2011). Second, the knowledge required to deliver IT services is often built around standardized technologies and skills, which induces imitation of expertise. This reduces the barriers for creating the highest quality of products and services (Gao et al. 2010). Third, the human capital intensiveness of IT services also leads to a global competition for crucial skill sets, thus creating high levels of inter-firm employee mobility (Mehta and Mehta 2009), evidenced by the growing importance of immigration programs run by many developed countries (Mithas and Lucas 2010).

Although our paper bases its empirical exploration of imitation among IT services vendors on the institutional perspective explained above, there is yet another nuance of the institutional environment. Each service line developed by IT service vendors belongs to a knowledge domain, that is institutional in nature because the knowledge resides within specific communities-of-practice (Swanson 2010). We identify three distinct institutional “spheres” or knowledge domains that can characterize an IT vendor’s service lines. First, technical service lines pertain to the construction and management of IT systems, implementation, and execution of IT processes within client firms (Gao et al. 2010, Pawlowski and Robey 2004, Shaft and Vessey 1995, Vitalari 1985). For example, the capability of delivering “data management and integration” services is distinct from “security and privacy” capability. Adding a new technical service line requires the vendors to acquire and combine capabilities pertaining to software development methodologies, software modules, with talent of programmers, business analysts, database administrators, and project managers (Arora et al. 2001, Heeks 1999, Lamin and Dunlap 2011). Therefore, IT vendors are often linked to technical communities through conferences, software engineering institutes, certification bodies, and technical standards consortiums. It is from venues such as these that IT vendors acquire or replenish their knowhow underlying their technical service lines. Second, functional service lines embody knowledge about standardized business processes that are to be automated, implemented, or reengineered at client firms. For example, the capability in delivering finance and banking solutions is distinct from that of human resource management solutions. The capability of IT service vendors to offer functional service lines is derived by IT vendors from their participation in professional communities of practice such as supply chain institutes, accounting associations, and other institutional bodies. Third, vertical industry service lines are focused on specific industries (Levina and Vaast 2008) because often vendors specialize in offering wide-ranging services within a select vertical industry. This requires deep knowledge of the industry context such as knowledge of regulations, taxation, international trade, competitive conditions, labor laws and practices, distinct marketing, production, and logistics practices, to name a few. Maintaining vertical service lines sometimes requires IT vendors to link with industry lobbies, associations such as logistics providers, and regulatory bodies, and assimilate industry-specific labor laws, tax regimes, and trade and marketing practices.<sup>3</sup>

Prior work also indicates that know-how for enterprise software and services is drawn from multiple knowledge domains. For example, IT vendors often diversify into one or more software component markets and vertical industry niches as they acquire capabilities in multiple dimensions (Chellappa et al. 2010, Tanriverdi and Chi-Hyon 2008). Similarly, other work examines the various technical capabilities (Garud et al. 2006, Lamin and Dunlap 2011) that are acquired by vendors and utilized for serving a variety of customer segments. Our distinction between service-line domains is somewhat akin to the distinction between technical and business knowledge required by IS professionals (Vitalari 1985). Thus, different from technical service lines, functional and vertical industry service lines require capabilities in business domains. This includes knowledge about process benchmarks in order to standardize performance of business processes (Garud et al. 2006, Levina and Ross 2003), development practices that would adhere to legalities of specific markets and industries (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2005), and expertise in the industryspecific software packages such as those from SAP or Oracle (Swanson 2010).

In summary, although the IS literature recognizes that institutional factors may be influencing decision making by enterprises, the literature is silent about the possibility that such institutionalization may create conditions for imitation among IT vendors. Furthermore, depending on the knowledge domain to which a service line belongs, the IT vendors are exposed to distinct institutional environment(s) (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, Swanson 2010).

As IT service vendors alter their IT service-line offering, they need to make continual efforts to draw from these knowledge domains. The multidimensionality of the knowledge domains of IT services suggests that imitation mechanisms can vary across these dimensions in terms of how strongly, and in what way vendors imitate the service-line decisions of others.

## 2.2. Imitation Mechanisms

Recent work has classified imitation mechanisms into two broad rationales: information-based imitation and rivalry-based imitation (Lieberman and Asaba 2006). First, facing environmental and outcome uncertainty, firms imitate to seek legitimacy for their actions (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Haunschild and Miner 1997). They may also consider the decisions made by imitation referents as information signals of market opportunities or threats, or technology trends (Palley 1995). At times, instead of identifying a subset of rivals to imitate, a firm may infer industry-wide popularity of certain strategic moves as an information signal and thereby imitate the broader trends (Haunschild and Miner 1997). Second, rivalry-based imitation involves imitating rivals’ decisions to deter strategic attacks such as price wars or new product launches (Jayachandran et al. 1999, Korn and Baum 1999). Thus, Multimarket contact (MMC) theory predicts imitation of market entry and exit as an effort by firms to pose a credible threat of retaliation to rivals by achieving competitive parity (Bernheim and Whinston 1990, Jayachandran et al. 1999, Ordanini et al. 2008). Another rivalry-based rationale is that firms are subject to the cognitive bias resulting from social comparison with equally ranked rivals (Garcia et al. 2006). This leads to rivalry at a relational (dyadic) level, an example of which are the one-on-one direct contests between prominent rivals.

Although the imitation literature is extensive, researchers face the theoretical challenge of attributing a distinct imitation rationale (information-based or rivalry-based) to an instance of imitation because the referent groups in both information- or rivalrybased imitation are often the same. Furthermore, instead of being exclusive, both motives may underlie imitation simultaneously (Lieberman and Asaba 2006). Despite attempts at more nuanced econometric analysis (Aral 2011, Manski 1973), the challenge of teasing out the precise rationale why each referent is imitated remains unresolved, as per our view. Notwithstanding the empirical reality that firms mimic other firms’ actions, we believe neither archival data nor survey data can capture the precise motivation (information-based or rivalry-based) in imitating a specific rival firm. Therefore, this study will explore the empirical significance of various imitation referent groups without labeling it exclusively as either information- or rivalry-based.

2.2.1. Imitation of Similar Rivals (Group Level). Group-level imitation occurs when a firm considers an entire group of rivals and imitates their aggregate behavior (McGee and Thomas 1986). The motivation could be either to reduce uncertainty or be competitive. Thus, group-level imitation referents are either an imitating firm’s strategic group (Fiegenbaum and Howard 1995) or a subset of the competitors most similar to them on traits such as size or product offerings (Haunschild and Miner 1997). A group of rivals, in the aggregate, is expected to have superior information about market conditions (Abrahamson 1996) and also poses a competitive threat to the imitating firm because it occupies the same market niche. The rationale of information-based rivalry echoes the strategic reference group theory (Fiegenbaum and Howard 1995), which proposes that membership in the same strategic group renders the firms with common beliefs about the trajectories of their specific products and markets—and greater vigilance about their competitive decisions. Managers of firms within a strategic group have a broad understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of other group members’ products and markets, norms about how resources can be deployed to enter new market segments, or how new resources can be generated by a recombination of existing capabilities (Porac and Thomas 1990).

This explanation can also apply to the imitation choices of IT services vendors. Since the IT services vendors serve global clients, there are a host of parameters about market trends and technologies of which service vendors need to be aware. Thus, imitating the capability among one’s rival group should reduce the outcome uncertainty of that strategic decision (Banerjee 1992). They also compete in order to be in the consideration sets of potential clients. Notwithstanding the possibility that sometimes the imitation of a group of industry participants can lead to inferior outcomes (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1993), the aggregate decisions of a focal IT vendor’s rival group is a significant predictor of imitation.

2.2.2. Imitation of Similar Rivals (Dyadic Level). Recent work recognizes the inordinate importance placed by firms on their direct (head-to-head) rivals; the rationale being that competitive actions by either one are a zero-sum game where a market share for one can only increase when there is a decrease for the other (Connelly et al. 2010, Ferrier and Hun 2002). Thus, at the dyadic level, an imitating firm closely observes its main industry rival and imitates its actions (Garcia et al. 2006, Sirmon et al. 2008). In a previous study, the most similar competitor was found to have been imitated to the exclusion of the rest of the competitors (Debruyne and Reibstein 2005). Apart from competitive motive, the cognitive competitive lens suggests that a focal firm engages in “social comparison” with a direct rival who is closely ranked, especially if both are top ranked (Kilduff et al. 2010) and highly similar (Chen 1996). Similarly, Sirmon et al. (2008) explain how firms benchmark their resources with those of direct rivals, confirming that firms closely examine their direct rivals even without the intention of necessarily imitating them.

The media literature is replete with stories of industry rivals such as the top Fortune 500 firms (Connelly et al. 2010), technology firm pairs such as Oracle-SAP, and Google-Microsoft, with one firm imitating the other in their online service offerings. Studies of technology standards examine rivalry between standards sponsored by rival pairs or between coalitions led by rivals, such as VHS and Betamax (David and Greenstein 1990) or IBM and AT&T (Axelrod et al. 1995). Consistent with the premise that direct, headto-head rivals feature prominently in a firm’s competitive frame of reference, we will empirically test whether the possession of a particular service line by a direct rival of an IT service vendor significantly increases the likelihood of the vendor firm developing the service line.

2.2.3. Imitation of Industry Leaders. In their pursuit of higher status, firms prefer to associate themselves in some ways with bigger industry members (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Imitating the practices and decisions of high status organizations is often an attempt to increase legitimacy because the decisions of higher status organizations often reflect deeper knowledge about the markets and technological trajectory of the industry in general. Larger organizations have deeper connections within the industry and even the customer markets (Haveman 1993a) and thus are viewed as opinion leaders. Many industry members view the largest in the industry to have superior information due to slack resources devoted to external research and market analysis, making them ideal referents to imitate, especially by small, financially constrained firms. The imitation of industry leaders is especially strong under conditions of market and technological uncertainty.

In the IT services sector, the larger players enjoy the advantage of bigger and repeat transactions with some of their customers (Ravindran et al. 2011), which gives them unparalleled visibility into the evolving market needs. At times, the larger players also consider other vendors as subcontractors (Van de Ven 2005), which makes it worthwhile for others to keep abreast by developing the same cutting-edge service lines that the largest players have acquired. In summary, literature points to a number of reasons why we would expect IT service vendors to imitate the service lines of the largest vendors in the industry.

2.2.4. Imitation of Popular Service Lines. Imitating the decisions made by several other industry rivals is often observed in practice because such decisions also have more legitimacy (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Meyer and Rowan 1977, Tolbert and Zucker 1983). Popularity of some courses of action has also been viewed as an information signal that can trigger imitation bandwagons, and sometimes, even to the detriment of the adopters (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1993, Banerjee 1992). More broadly, empirical evidence in a variety of contexts such as multidivisional form or investment banking (Haunschild and Miner 1997) has demonstrated the imitation of popular strategic choices. Some work also highlights how popular niches in software markets can benefit firms because of creation of network externalities (Chellappa et al. 2010, Farrell and Saloner 1985). This implies that under specific conditions, imitation of market entry decisions of industry rivals can also occur because of the potential for capturing such benefits.

Both the information- and legitimacy-based arguments can apply to imitation of popular service lines in our context. First, a greater number of IT vendors who offer a service line, is a valuable information signal to others that their customer markets have begun to value that service line. They also face the pressure to signal legitimacy to a customer base that knows little else about the unfamiliar vendors. In particular, the legitimacy they are seeking pertains to the ability of the IT vendors to satisfy the rapidly changing business needs of the customer base. Offering the most popular service lines increases the chances that they appear in the consideration set of potential customers. Adding a trendier service line also has long term benefits such as being perceived as cutting-edge, which affects future sales.

2.2.5. Imitation Through Multimarket Contact. MMC has frequently been examined as an explanation behind market entry and exit (Jayachandran et al. 1999). Though empirical work has been carried out mostly in the mature industries such as airlines (Evans and Kessides 1994, Korn and Baum 1999) or banking (Hannan and Prager 2004), recent work extends to the software industry (Chellappa et al. 2010). The MMC literature considers all industry players as competitors and predicts imitation of market entry as well as exit by a focal firm as resulting from its tendency to maximize MMC aggregated across all industry competitors (Gimeno et al. 2001, Jayachandran et al. 1999).<sup>4</sup> Greater MMC allows a firm to achieve competitive parity and thus pose a credible threat of retaliation in the event of rivalrous moves by competitors (Scott 1982). Mitigation of rivalry in industry segments through (intended or unintended) MMC has often been found to result in higher market prices because of tacit collusion (Gimeno 2002).

In summary, although there is evidence of significant imitation of similar firms in various strategic contexts, not many studies consider the key imitation mechanisms simultaneously—such as actions of industry leaders, industry trends, and choices of direct rivals or rival groups—all of which are indicated in imitation literature as referents.

## 2.3. Product Development and Withdrawal Decisions

Firms continually reconfigure their competitive positioning not only by developing new products or capabilities but also withdrawing them. Decisions pertaining to product line pruning (Johnson and Myatt 2003, Koca et al. 2010, Kotler 1965, Saunders and Jobber 1994) are considered as important as new product launches, and both of these decisions are critical junctures of a product life cycle (Klepper 1996). Although they may differ in fundamental ways from products, capabilities also have a life cycle beginning with capability development and ending with capability retirement<sup>5</sup> (Helfat and Lieberman 2002, Helfat and Peteraf 2003).

Although there is ample evidence of the salient internal and external factors for product launch decisions, studies have also identified factors that affect product elimination and capability divestment decisions. Thus, internal considerations such as firm size (Ingram and Roberts 1999), the number of own products in the same class (Chisholm and Norman 2006), and the method of market entry (as an entrepreneurial start-up or a lateral entry) (Khessina and Carroll 2008), are various reasons why firms eliminate product lines. Furthermore, external factors like the crowdedness of a market (de Figueiredo and Kyle 2006) and product competition (Greenstein and Wade 1998), both lead to product withdrawals. There is comparatively less literature concerned with capability divesting. The key motivation behind capability divestment is the improvement of internal production efficiencies (Helfat and Peteraf 2003) and corporate profitability (Makadok 2001).

Similarly, the applied economics literature has examined the influence of various internal and external factors for market entry and exit decisions in specific industry contexts (Chang 1996, Gil-Molto and

Piga 2008, Sinclair 1995). An established peer network has been found to decrease market entry and increase exit decisions in the airline industry (Morrison and Winston 1990). There is also evidence of vicarious learning between affiliated firms where firms of the same conglomerate exhibited slower speed of market exit than single entity firms (Disney et al. 2003). However, there are very few studies that have tested various rationales of firm-level imitation to understand entry and exit decisions within the same empirical setting—an exception being the well-established MMC literature (Jayachandran et al. 1999) that examines imitation resulting from tacit collusion (see discussion in §2.2). Furthermore, to our knowledge, no study examines the imitation of the various referent groups simultaneously for product launch (capability acquisition) and elimination (capability divesting) in the same setting.

In the IT services sector, imitation is an important mechanism by which IT vendors adjust their market positions, which include new IT service additions as well as withdrawal decisions. That is, not only are decisions about new service lines important, but decisions about service-line withdrawal are just as crucial. Therefore, the challenge for IT service vendors is to anticipate the value of a potential service offering, as well as to recognize the liabilities of older service lines to eliminate them selectively. This implies that firm-level imitation should be equally salient for service-line withdrawal as well as considering the resource constraints in the face of rapid and unpredictable evolution of customer needs and technological advances within the broader ecosystem. However, eliminating existing service lines is risky for IT vendors because it involves foregoing certain market opportunities and implies a commitment to acquire new resources to support the new service lines. Given that the clients’ needs are of primary importance, service-line expansion and contraction has to match the ebb and flow of industry demand, technological evolution, and competitive threats (Athreye 2005, Kotler 1965, Levina and Ross 2003). It is important for a vendor to avoid stretching itself too thin by withdrawing its service lines and to focus its resource commitments to fulfilling outsourcing contract deliverables. We suggest that these considerations can make IT vendors particularly prone to imitation of their rivals when seeking a favorable market positioning. A properly rationalized service line is an attempt at optimal market positioning so as to attract the desired clientele (de Figueiredo and Kyle 2006, Saunders and Jobber 1994).

In the empirical modeling presented in §3, we model service-line development as well as service-line withdrawal by IT services vendors in separate models. Furthermore, we also examine the significance of the various imitation mechanisms for service lines in technical, functional, and vertical industry domains separately.

## 3. Study Setting, Data and Analysis

Our study setting is the Indian IT service sector, which is an exemplar of IT outsourcing sectors worldwide. This sector has grown rapidly over the past decade from US\$330 million to US\$10 billion (1998 to 2010) with almost all revenue derived from IT-based services (Arora et al. 2001). The setting is appropriate for studying firm-level imitation because of various factors including the geographical clustering of the sector within a few large Indian cities (Khomiakova 2007), which adds to the pressure for homogenization of IT service vendors. This sector also continues to experience high employee mobility, high rates of certifications, and multisourcing strategies by clients (Bapna et al. 2010, Gao et al. 2010). In this context, our unit of analysis is an IT service vendor’s decision to acquire or withdraw a specific service line from its portfolio.

## 3.1. Data

The data were collected from the National Association of Software and Service Companies (NASSCOM) member directories and also from archival sources and websites of IT services companies. NASSCOM is a nonprofit organization and the apex industry association representing the software service vendors in India. The combined revenue of its member companies constitutes 95% of the revenue of the sector (NASSCOM 2003). NASSCOM is continually involved in policy-setting initiatives and therefore the data from NASSCOM member directories serves as an important point of reference for information on the IT sector. NASSCOM collects data from its members through self-reports, which include information about services offered, revenues, employee size, and company status (publicly listed or private).

The sample was drawn from the member directories for 2002, 2003, and 2005. Complete firm-level data for 116 firms in 2003 and 2005 and 103 of those same companies in 2002 were available for analysis. We extracted data about their service lines for each time period using a coding procedure (Appendix A). In addition to the NASSCOM data, the historical websites of the companies and archival data sources including the Centre for Monitoring of the Indian Economy (CMIE), Lexis-Nexis, as well as the Web archives were used to triangulate the information. Research assistants coded the qualitative data on each IT service vendor into the binary variables representing the service lines (see Appendix A for details) in each domain. The coding of quantitative and qualitative data resulted in a list of 29 service lines across the time frame for our sample of IT services vendors (see Table A.3 in Appendix A).

Since objective data from library sources in such detail is not readily available, one concern is whether the services declared by vendors through self-reports faithfully represent their existing capabilities. To a certain extent, inaccurate self-reports can jeopardize a vendor’s reputation (Banerjee and Duflo 2000) with the Western clients who rely on business directories to identify potential vendors in their consideration set. Furthermore, considering the complexity, cost, and criticality of most IT services (Ravindran et al. 2011), the client firms almost always engage in deeper assessment of the true capabilities of the potential vendors during the contracting process (Levina and Ross 2003). Therefore, it can be argued that the detailed contracting process mitigates the vendors’ incentive to misrepresent their capabilities, and thus alleviates to some extent the bias in our analysis arising from treating the “claimed” service lines as “actual” service lines.

## 3.2. Model

The model to be estimated is

$$
\begin{array}{l} S E R V I C E \_ C H A N G E _ {i j t} \\ = \beta_ {i} + X _ {i} \beta + R E F \_ G R O U P S _ {i j t - 1} \delta + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where i indicates the focal firm, j indicates service line, $X _ { i }$ is a vector of focal firm-specific control variables that includes a constant, $R \bar { E F } _ { - } G R O U P S _ { i j t - 1 }$ is a vector of five variables, each capturing the imitation referents discussed in §2.2 (direct rival, rival group, high popularity, largest firm, and MMC) and $\varepsilon _ { i j }$ is an error term.

Equation (1) is a general representation for both, adding or withdrawing service lines by imitation, and also, for each service domain separately. Therefore, the dependent variable $S E R V I C \bar { E } \_ C H A N G E _ { i j t }$ indicates whether the focal firm i has added or withdrawn service-line j between the time period t − 1 and t. We have vendor service lines measured at three points in time: 2002, 2003, and 2005. Since the model indicates a lag, $S E R V I C E \_ C H A N G E _ { i j t }$ is measured at 2003 with a one-year lag and 2005 with a two-year lag.

As the dependent variable is binary, a probit model will be used for estimations. We include random effects (intercept) for each focal firm in Equation (1) $( \beta _ { i }$ , which is distributed as $N ( \mu _ { i } , \sigma _ { i } ) )$ , as each firm has multiple observations to reflect its market positioning decisions. These random effects allow each firm’s imitation propensity to vary randomly from a normal distribution. We also conduct robustness tests in Appendix B that include random coefficients (Rabe-Hesketh et al. 2005) that allow each firm to vary in how they are influenced by the referent groups.

## 3.3. Variables

Dependent variables0 The model will be estimated with two different dependent variables: service-line development and service-line withdrawal. This is necessary empirically as we examine a binary model where the baseline status (i.e., the dependent variable is measured to be zero) is different in both cases. In our study, the baseline for the service-line development model is that the focal firm does not possess the service line at the beginning and end of the time interval, whereas for the withdrawal model, the baseline is that the focal firm possesses it at the beginning and end of the interval. These two situations are mutually exclusive. For the same reasons, market entry and exit has been examined separately in the airline industry (Gil-Molto and Piga 2008, Morrison and Winston 1990, Sinclair 1995) and the laser printer industry (de Figueiredo and Kyle 2006).

Service-line development. This dependent variable is the (binary) choice to announce a new service line and is measured as follows. At time t − 1, a focal firm is observed with k service lines from among a total of 29 service lines. At time $t ,$ it develops $k ^ { \prime }$ new service lines by selecting them from the remaining 29 − k service lines, some of which are possessed by industry rivals. Any service-line j that the focal firm did not possess at time $t - 1$ will constitute an observation for the dependent variable. For each observation, service-line development is $^ { \prime \prime } { 1 ^ { \prime \prime } }$ if the focal firm chose to develop a new service-line $j$ at time $t _ { \mathrm { ~ } } ( k ^ { \prime } { }$ observations) and $\prime \prime 0 \prime \prime$ if the firm chose not to $( 2 9 - k - k ^ { \prime }$ observations). There are 116 unique focal firms in our data set and each has a different number of new service lines it could potentially develop at time t. On average, each firm acquires $6 . 6$ new service lines and chooses to not acquire 17.3 during each time change period (see Table A.2 in Appendix A). This resulted in 3,404 observations for the development analyses aggregated over the two time change periods.

Service-line withdrawal. This dependent variable is measured in a similar fashion to development, however, the baseline observations are when the focal firm possesses service-line j in t − 1 and continues to possess it at time t. Service-line withdrawal is measured to be $" 1 \prime \prime$ when the focal firm then chooses to withdraw service-line j at time t. On average, each firm withdraws from 5.1 service lines and chooses to not withdraw from 7.4 during each time change period (see Table A.2 in Appendix A) resulting in 1,879 observations for the withdrawal analyses over the two time change periods.

Referent groups

Direct rival. We define the direct rival as the firm most similar in terms of service lines to the focal firm. The identification of direct rivals requires a method for comparing and ranking each industry rival to each other. Empirical work in literature emphasizes how competitive ranking and strategic similarity are important criteria for competitor identification (Clark and Montgomery 1999, Garcia et al. 2006, Haunschild and Miner 1997, Lieberman and Asaba 2006). Managers consider similarity with or differentiation from the “industry” or the “market” when identifying competitors (Porac and Thomas 1990). Semadeni (2006) treats service marks as a proxy for capabilities in the services-based consulting industry to understand how firms differentiate themselves from their competitors, and Debruyne and Reibstein (2005) operationalize similarity in size and resources to study imitation of direct rivals. In other work, size similarity is a form of social proximity (Lee and Pennings 2002, Walton 1986) and therefore, we use similarity in revenues and service lines in the previous time period, as the two criteria for identifying imitation referents.

To identify rivals, first, all firms were separated into revenue quartiles. Next, for each firm, i, all of its competitors within its revenue quartile were ranked according to their similarity (across all servicelines j) with i. Similarity between two firms i and i<sup>0</sup> is defined as <sup>6</sup>

$$
S I M _ {i i ^ {\prime}} = \frac {\sum_ {j} D _ {i j} D _ {i ^ {\prime} j}}{\sum_ {j} (D _ {i j} + D _ {i ^ {\prime} j} - D _ {i j} D _ {i ^ {\prime} j})}\tag{2}
$$

where $D _ { i j }$ is a dummy variable denoting whether company i possessed service-line j at time t − 1 (Debruyne and Reibstein 2005). In the development model, direct rival is an indicator variable where 1 indicates that the focal firm’s direct rival possesses the service line at time t − 1 (0 if it does not possess it at t − 1), whereas in the withdrawal model, it is measured as 1 if the direct rival does not possess the service line in time t − 1 (0 if not). A positive estimated coefficient for direct rival indicates that the direct rival of a focal firm positively influences its service-line decision.

Rival group. For a focal firm, we consider its rival group as comprised of the five most similar rivals excluding the direct rival (to avoid confounding this variable with direct rival). Thus, the rival group comprises the second to the sixth ranked most similar rivals. In the development model, we operationalize rival group as a continuous variable that measures the proportion of the focal firm’s rivals within its rival group that possess service-line j at time t − 1.<sup>7</sup> For example, if no one in the rival group has a serviceline j, rival group computes to zero. If three members (out of five) have the service-line j, rival group is 0.6. Thus, this measure aggregates the information from the choices made by the rival group (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1993). However, our measure differs somewhat from that used in the strategic group research (McGee and Thomas 1986). As our definition of rival group is asymmetric, firm A could be in firm B’s rival group but the converse need not be true; whereas all members within a strategic group are, symmetrically, considered as imitation referents of each other. Thus, the rival group defined in our study is unique to each firm.<sup>8</sup> Competitive asymmetry is recognized as typical to managers’ cognitive models of competition (Chen 1996), which thus justifies our approach. The measurement for the withdrawal model is identical, except we measure the proportion of group members without the service line. A positive estimated coefficient for rival group indicates that focal firms are positively influenced by their rival group to add or withdraw their service lines.

High popularity. Considering that highly popular service lines can potentially trigger a bandwagon, this indicator variable controls for whether the serviceline j is highly popular. In the development model, it is measured as 1 if popular for service-line j is above the 75th percentile where popular is the proportion of companies in the industry that have service-line j at time t − 1. In the withdrawal model, it is measured as 1 if popular for service-line j is below the 25th percentile. A positive estimate coefficient indicates that focal firms are influenced to develop highly popular service lines in the development regression and withdraw from the least popular service lines in the withdrawal regression.

Largest. This continuous variable reflects the influence that actions of large, prominent firms in the industry have on the development of new service lines (Angst et al. 2010, Haveman 1993b). It is measured as the proportion of the five largest companies in the industry that possess service-line j at time t − 1. A positive (negative) coefficient in the development (withdrawal) regression indicates that the focal firm imitates the service line of the largest firms in the industry.<sup>9</sup>

Firm in market. Considering a “market” as a service line, this variable measures the potential MMC that a focal firm would face if it develops a new service-line j (Baum and Korn 1996, Gimeno et al. 2001). MMC is measured according to Baum and Korn (1996):

$$
\begin{array}{r} M M C _ {i j t} = \frac {\sum_ {i \neq i ^ {'}} \sum_ {j} D _ {i j t} D _ {i ^ {'} j t}}{\sum_ {j} D _ {i j t} N _ {M M C _ {t}}}, \\ \mathrm{forall} i ^ {'} \sum_ {j} D _ {i j t} D _ {i ^ {'} j t} > 1, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $D _ { i j t }$ is an indicator variable equal to 1 if firm i possesses service-line j at time t, 0 otherwise, $D _ { i ^ { \prime } j t }$ is an indicator variable equal to 1 if firm i<sup>0</sup> possesses service-line j at time $t , \ 0$ otherwise, and $\mathsf { \bar { N } } _ { M M C _ { t } }$ is the number of firms i’ that overlap with the focal firm i in service-line j that are MMCs (i.e., that firm i encounters in at least one other service line other than j). Firms that acquire or withdraw service lines to increase their MMC with the rest of the industry by the greatest amount will be indicated by a positive estimated coefficient in the development regression and a negative estimated coefficient in the withdrawal regression.

## Focal-firm-specific control variables0<sup>10</sup>

Revenue. Firm revenue reflects firm size and is measured in logged millions of rupees and lagged by one year. Revenue is a more accurate proxy for size than assets in this human-talent-intensive industry as IT assets are often small relative to profits and also depreciate quickly.

Age. Firm age is measured in years as the difference between 2002 and the year of incorporation.

Private. This indicator variable is measured as one if the company is privately funded, zero if publicly funded.

Export experience. This indicator variable captures whether the company has international clients and is measured as one if the firm has had sales to international clients, zero otherwise.<sup>11</sup>

Geographic location. Following Chintagunta et al. (2010), we estimate the model with fixed effects that control for the geographic location of the focal firm’s corporate head-office (14 possible locations), although, in the interest of space, their estimates are suppressed from the results tables.<sup>12</sup> The fixed effects account for imitation resulting from colocation of firms contributing to common actions.

Data year. To control for differences between the two possible time change periods, we include a variable that is measured as 1 if the observations pertain to the second time change period (t = 2005) and 0 if for the first time change period (t = 2003). This control also addresses any concern with time-varying shocks that could influence all industry members to act in a particular way (Shriver et al. 2013).

Service lines in domain. Focal firms that already possess a large number of service lines in a particular domain (i.e., technical, functional, or industry) may be less inclined to develop more of them in the same domain. This control variable is measured as the number of service lines that the focal firm possesses in the same domain as service-line j at time t − 1.

Univariate statistics of the variables and their intercorrelations are displayed in Tables 2(a) and 2(b). The low correlations and variance inflation factors suggest that there should be little concern about multicollinearity biasing the results.

## 3.4. Endogeneity

Social influence studies are typically subject to significant identification problems that confound the rivallevel effect that we hypothesize in this paper (Manski 1973). Instead of service-line choice resulting from firm-level imitation, it is also possible that the choices of new capabilities by similar firms result from a tendency to react identically when faced with similar institutional factors (Sorensen 2006). To overcome this particular identification issue, we used instrumental variables for the two referent groups that are rivalbased: direct rival and rival group. The instruments use a “friend of friend” approach (Shriver et al. 2013, Zeng and Wei 2013). Because the direct rival variable reflects the service line of the focal firm’s top ranked rival, the instrument will be the service line of the first ranked rival of the focal firm’s direct rival. When the first ranked rival of the focal firm’s direct rival is the focal firm itself, the second ranked rival was substituted. This instrument should be correlated with the direct rival’s choice of the service line (direct rival) but not with the focal firm’s (service-line development). Similarly, the instrument for rival group is the proportion of the first ranked rivals of rival group members (excluding the focal firm or the direct rival or rival group members) that possess the service line for the development model or do not possess the service line for the withdrawal model. The strength of the endogeneity specification is indicated by Fisher’s Z transformed correlation (Roodman 2011, Wilde 2000). In all estimations including all robustness checks, there is significant evidence of endogeneity with the direct rival and rival group variables, justifying the inclusion of instrumental variables analysis. We present the base results with instrumental variables. The instruments themselves are found to be strongly correlated with their endogenous variables in their first-stage regressions and, in results not shown, are not significant when they were included in a stand-alone estimation (i.e., without instrumental variables) of Equation (1) as a test.<sup>13</sup>

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Service-Line Development

The estimations for the development model are displayed in Table 3.<sup>14</sup> The focal-firm-specific controls reveal that large firms (revenue) are significantly associated with new service-line development. Firms that already possessed a large number of service lines will be less inclined to develop a new service-line in the same domain (service-lines in domain). The estimated coefficient of direct rival is consistently positive and significant across the full sample regressions (first two columns), indicating that focal firms are likely to imitate their direct rival. The results also show that IT service vendors are likely to imitate their rival group.

To explore the possibility that imitation of referent groups varies according to service domain, Table 3 displays estimations of the development model for subsamples of the data set in the third through fifth columns. Consistently, we find that rival group is a significant referent group for development of new service lines, which is consistent with the results from the full data set. In contrast to the estimations on the full sample, direct rivals are not found to be imitation referents for new service-line development in any particular knowledge domain. Focal firms also are influenced to develop highly popular service lines in the functional domain. Firms will avoid developing technical and vertical-industry service lines where they will find increased MMC.

## 4.2. Service-Line Withdrawal

The estimations for the withdrawal model are displayed in Table 4. Older focal firms (age) are found to be more likely to withdraw existing service lines and, at least in the full sample estimation, those with a large number of service lines will be more likely to withdraw some of those lines in the same domain. Unlike in the development model, we find that the data year indicator (data year) is important in that focal firms are less likely to withdraw from service lines between 2003 and 2005 compared to between 2002 and 2003. This is surprising as the longer time frame between 2003 and 2005 would allow for more changes in service line. We find that, for the full sample, the two rival-based referent groups, direct rival and rival group, significantly influence serviceline withdrawal.

The estimations for service-line withdrawal for each service-line domain do not deviate much from those of the full sample. Direct rival and rival groups are consistently significant referent groups in all subsamples except the functional domain. Focal firms considering withdrawing a service line in the functional domain will only imitate the service-line development of their rival group. For service lines in the technical domain, aside from imitating their rival group and direct rival, focal firms also imitate the new service lines of the largest vendors (largest).

## 5. Discussion and Implications

This paper complements the entrepreneurial view of IT industry that focuses largely on internal innovation processes facilitating continuous innovation by vendors (Rindova and Kotha 2001) or on collaborations with clients leading to innovation (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2002). The focus of such research is often on IT vendors accumulating vast stocks of unique, patentable knowledge (Podolny et al. 1996) and becoming adept at constructing new markets (Santos and Eisenhardt 2009) or leading discontinuous change (Suarez and Utterback 1995). However, this literature has hitherto been silent on the strong imitative influences that can affect decisions by IT vendors because of institutional factors, although imitation of IT outsourcing decisions have been well recognized (Hu et al. 1997, Mann et al. 2011). We set the stage for our research by considering that the institutionalization in the Indian IT services sector was driven by interfirm spillovers in terms of employee mobility, customer overlap, geographical proximity, and industry certifications. Thus our research complements prior work by proposing a perspective that speaks of a more gradual change where IT vendors are influenced by rivals in deciding their strategic positioning.

## 5.2. Limitations and Future Research

The findings in our study are subject to the following limitations. The most significant limitation is that the identification of service lines was achieved by qualitative coding of textual data, and qualitatively verifying some portion of it with textual information from online sources including vendor websites and the industry media. The entire process involved significant subjectivity by the research assistants. Furthermore, the main corpus of the textual data is self-declared by the IT service vendors in the NASSCOM directories and therefore is not objectively captured. Over time, as algorithmic processing of textual data becomes more sophisticated (Netzer et al. 2012), this bias from existing methods will be mitigated. Future research could measure capabilities using the micro-level data on human capital spillovers and labor mobility and thus confirm our assumption that strategic imitation leads to employee flow, or vice versa. Second, our panel of IT services vendors spanned only three years and therefore does not allow us to fully observe if any service-line decisions were delayed but still resulted from imitation effects. Consequently, we have a “right-censored” observation window. Future work can address this using an extended panel and duration models (Strang and Tuma 1993). More data would also allow for examination of the effects of imitation on firm performance and survival. Furthermore, imitation is a mode of vicarious learning and if the imitation patterns in our results are indicative of learning, then further micro-level investigation using survey data or qualitative methods can shed light on how the learning is transmitted—by employee mobility, multisourcing clients, or standard certification.

Third, strict causality is hard to establish empirically in our case. Ours does not capture changing client preferences in selective vertical industries that may have solely led to certain IT service vendors developing or withdrawing service lines. Fourth, the analysis does not account for differing levels of scope economies between service lines, which if high, may jointly increase the likelihood of related service lines if any one of them is offered (Markides and Williamson 1994). Future research can either impute such scope economies from observed data or build such dependencies into the statistical model and estimation. Fifth, our industry context and country context is an exemplar of a turbulent IT services industry and therefore appropriate for studying imitation. However, before our findings are applied to other geographic areas and sectors globally, a qualitative assessment of the factors leading to an institutional environment in that particular sector may need to be completed.

## 5.3. Implications

Our study of the expansion and contraction of the service-line portfolios of IT service vendors resulting from imitation, has several implications for strategy on industry sectors that are highly institutionalized and also face significant market and technological turbulence. Therefore, some of our findings are not only generalizable to IT sectors but also to some knowledge-based service sectors. They are more directly applicable to those service sectors where vendors need to draw on knowledge from diverse institutional fields to create service bundles, so that constructing an attractive capability portfolio becomes strategically important. These preconditions are most likely satisfied in many professional service sectors such as legal or tax consulting. For example, in technology and legal consulting, service lines pertain to technology-centric domains such as biotechnology or computing, as well as to customer-centric domains such as country regimes and vertical industries.

The overall results have implications for the broader strategy literature in terms of the ease with which a strategy of imitation may be pursued over a strategy of differentiation (Porter 1980). In the literature, causal ambiguity is a significant barrier to imitation, which increases the preference for the strategy of imitation relative to the strategy of differentiation (Reed and DeFillippi 1990). Where causal ambiguity is lowered, imitation becomes a valuable lens to understand strategic decisions, as we suggest happens in the IT service sectors. That is, to the extent that IT vendors’ service lines also connote substantive capabilities, IT services vendors may not be able to engage in capability creation by internal experimentation and learning because various institutional factors reduce causal ambiguity (Ryall 2009). These industry conditions enhance the importance of applying imitation theories to our setting.

Our findings also have specific theoretical implications, all linked to our three research questions. The first is that rival imitation is the most dominant type of imitation during service-line portfolio expansion as well as contraction. This suggests that the industry is more likely to evolve in clusters where groups of similar players evolve their service portfolios in tandem. This probable evolutionary trajectory contrasts with a (hypothetical) industry-wide contagion where industry leaders and the popular service lines are most prominently imitated by others, indicative of a relatively centralized contagion process (Haveman 1993b). Although we do indeed find some evidence of industry-wide contagion, it appears to be far less salient, only being influential in the development of functional service lines. Similarly, industry leaders are imitated only in the withdrawal of technical service lines.

Second, the differences in the pattern of results between the empirical models corresponding to the technical, functional, and vertical-industry domains suggests a future potential for deeper and more nuanced theorizing of the institutional environments in the IT sectors. The findings point to a distinctive feature of the enterprise software and services industry where the software and related capabilities of the IT vendors is grounded in multiple distinct institutional spheres: the customer-centric institutions on one hand, which we operationalized as the functional and vertical-industry knowledge domains, and the technology-centric institutional field on the other, which we operationalized as the technical knowledge domain. Since IS researchers are increasingly interested in understanding bandwagon effects in the IT adoption resulting from institutional pressures (Marett et al. 2013, Ravichandran et al. 2009), our results suggest that demarcating the institutional field in a more nuanced way—in terms of technical- and customer-centric domains—would be a fruitful direction for future research.

Third, our results suggest that withdrawal of IT services can also be subject to firm-level imitation. Whereas most research has only attributed product and capability divestment to more rational, optimizing processes (de Figueiredo and Kyle 2006), our study suggests that IT vendors’ withdrawal from service lines can similarly result from their propensity to imitate industry rivals, mostly rival groups and, more selectively, the direct rivals. In particular, imitation of service-line elimination is somewhat stronger in the technical and vertical industry domains—because both the rival group and direct rivals are imitated by vendors. These findings have interesting implications for future IS research because of the rapidity with which technology cycles and associated competencies of the IT suppliers are phased in and out (Fenn and Raskino 2008). Contraction or withdrawal of IT service lines through firm-level imitation offers a far more complete picture than the one-sided perspective that focuses only on adoption of new IT products (Fenn and Raskino 2008, O’Leary 2008).

The results pertaining to the imitation rationale also have more nuanced interpretation. Rival-level imitation not only constrains the diffusion of innovation by one firm within a (homogenous) cluster, but it also limits myopia in the industry. Often, industry bandwagons set in motion by large players can result in misguided choices by the entire industry through firm imitation (Swanson and Ramiller 2004). This risk is mitigated in our context where similarity-based imitation predominates. This suggests that the IT services sector development of new service lines even by smaller companies is influential enough and quickly imitated by rivals—and need not be overshadowed by decisions of large players.

Our results suggest a more circumscribed imitation of large IT players. The evidence suggests that IT vendors identify themselves more strictly based on their technical service lines. Crossing these boundaries by developing new service lines from outside their rival group is much less common even if large IT players add these service lines. This indicates that the institutional field in which IT service vendors participate leads to relative stronger imitation of technical service lines over services in the functional and vertical industry domain. This has implications for how imitation results from managerial cognition of the competitive environment (Greve 1998) and for formation of organizational identity by the service vendors (Tripsas 2009). The practical implication of the pattern of imitation in Table 3 is that for a client in a new vertical industry, contracting with a large IT vendor will result in quicker imitation by the rest of the industry thereby augmenting the supply of these capabilities. In contrast, the development of technical service lines is not easily imitated by the rest of the industry even if they are popular or if the largest players acquire them.

Interestingly, however, the practical implication of our results from analyzing the withdrawal decisions differs from that of analyzing the service-line development decisions. As soon as a larger IT vendor withdraws from a technical capability, those possessing it throughout the industry are likely to follow suit. We tie this pattern to the notion of product obsolescence where larger players’ decisions of phasing-out some technical competencies are critical indicators of the end of the life cycle for a specific technology. Larger IT players are typically considered as industry bellwethers (Wang 2010), but our results, interestingly, suggest a more limited effect—only the withdrawal decisions pertaining to technical service of these large IT vendors are imitated strongly.

The results of our analysis suggest that an important structural feature of this industry might be driving the pattern of imitation we observed. It is that, in general, the IT services sectors are highly differentiated—which is reflected by the predominance of rival-based imitation across the various models we tested. It is possible that the results are a reflection that distinct clusters of most-similar firms are highly embedded in the institutional fields surrounding them, and often dominated by their large, industry-specific clients. The differentiation may arise not only because large clients need specific combinations of service lines (which we did not capture in our data) but also because there might be a qualitative difference between the economies of scope across the three knowledge domains. Our findings therefore should be generalizable to other IT sectors that have a similarly high level of differentiation. For example, in future research on a specific IT sector, the Gini coefficient can be used to measure market concentration across various service lines or niches. If specific IT service sectors are highly differentiated, as suggested by a low Gini coefficient, we anticipate that firm-level imitation will manifest mostly at the group level, as in our results. However, in contrast to highly differentiated sectors, highly concentrated sectors may witness greater overall barriers to imitation because of significant economies of scale for the service lines offered by the IT vendors.

This study can also stimulate future research on strategic positioning. Because the service-lines decisions of an IT service vendor in aggregate define its market positioning, future research can examine the industry life cycle stage of each service line chosen or withdrawn to determine an IT vendor’s strategic thrust (leader or follower, for example). If combined with a vendor’s resource additions over a period of time using other data sources, such analysis can be used to relate a vendor’s positioning strategy with its execution. Similarly, by surveying clients’ perceptions of IT vendors’ service capabilities we can better understand whether imitation does indeed benefit the imitator in terms of more favorable client perceptions.

Finally, we suggest caution in generalizing these results to more matured and consolidated industry sectors. As of the time of data collection, the Indian IT service sector was mostly emerging, geographically constrained to just a few Indian cities and highly oriented to clients in developed countries; all factors that we argued contributed to its institutionalization. Subsequently, it is possible that some factors since then may have diluted the level of institutionalization that creates the tendency of the vendors to imitate service lines, such as industry maturation to the point where revenue from software branded products has grown over revenue from services.

Our study also has important practical implications. It is important for managers and policy makers to have empirical evidence of imitation because it helps in policy-level intervention by industry associations and the government. For customers of the IT services vendors, it is useful to know which types of service lines are more easily imitated and thus likely to be available competitively in a shorter span of time. Our results point to a possibility of a bandwagon effect where popular service lines in the functional domain are likely to be imitated by the rest of the vendors.

## 5.4. Contribution

In summary, our work contributes toward an understanding of how evolution in the products and services portfolio within an industry can be facilitated through imitation by IT services vendors. Literature suggest that reconfiguration of resources, capabilities, and internal structures (D’Aveni et al. 2010, Gnyawali et al. 2010, Rindova and Kotha 2001) are important mechanisms of strategic differentiation by which product evolution can take place in an industry. Our work complements this perspective and examines how the development and withdrawal of service lines, and thus the continuous alteration of the market positioning by IT service vendors, also plays an important role in the industry evolution. In the IT services sector, which is largely driven by the needs of business customers, imitation can potentially spur productive innovation or consolidation. Imitation of another firm’s service line can also be beneficial for the industry itself as well as the client sector because of faster availability of the supplier base and more occasions for incremental improvement by the imitators. IT services vendors rarely offer direct “knockoffs” of the services offered by the originators, but create follow-on innovations, which advance the sector technologically and create greater diversity of IT services (Bessen and Maskin 2009, Garud and Kumaraswamy 2005, Levina and Ross 2003). In the same vein, selectively eliminating existing capabilities is equally important for vendors in their attempt to optimize resources, and imitation is an important mechanism by which such decisions are made.

## Matching Estimation

An important requirement for the estimation is that selfselection bias be mitigated (Morgan and Winship 2007). In our empirical context, selection bias can manifest when certain background characteristics expose only a subset of the focal firms in our sample to their direct rival or their rival group who have developed specific service lines. For example, it is likely that higher revenue firms are the only ones to participate in a subset of service lines and therefore are exposed to imitation. Thus, if the participants to a treatment (or, in our case, exposed to a referent group) differ from nonparticipants in observable or unobservable ways, the hypothesized causal link between the treatment and its outcome is undermined. Furthermore, regression models and its diagnostics do not account for any possible dissimilarity between the treated and control groups within the sample and therefore rely on inappropriate extrapolation (Stuart 2010).<sup>15</sup>

Matching estimators allow the estimation of a causal link between treatment and outcome in the presence of selection bias by identifying a control group as a subset of the entire sample, based on observable characteristics (Abadie and Imbens 2011). Thereby, matching estimators increase the quality of inferences as illustrated by recent studies<sup>16</sup> (Campbell and Frei 2010, Xue et al. 2011). Of particular importance to this approach is the method of identifying the matching (control) observations using relevant covariates. The STATA command−psmatch2- uses a propensity score method to identify the control observations for each treated one (Stuart 2010). This approach estimates the propensity to receive a treatment for each subject based on observed covariates. At least one control observation is matched with each treated one based on the closest propensity. We show results for one and two control matches. Any difference between the treatment and control group’s probability of acquiring a new service line (in our case) is assumed to be because of the treatment.

## Alternate Specifications

The model specification in Equation (1) is such that the coefficients of all of the variables, including the five referent groups, are constrained to be equal for all firms. This overlooks two possibilities. First, firms may differ in their ability or will to imitate the various referent groups. Second, each firm’s disposition to imitate a service line in a particular domain (technical, functional, or industry) may be greater than in another domain. Theoretically, such firm-level heterogeneity could result because of different strategic foci or also because of idiosyncratic factors. For example, some firms may be strategically motivated to diversify their service lines in the technical domain more than others, whereas other firms may be more inclined to diversify in the functional domain. Therefore, we tested for random coefficient models to address the first scenario above (Longford 1993) and random effects for each service domain for the second.

First, along with the random effects for each focal firm, we introduce a random coefficient for a referent group into the model

$$
