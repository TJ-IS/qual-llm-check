## Highlights

 We find a three-way interaction of social recommendation, shopping stage, and scarcity

 Weak-tie recommendations are more persuasive at initial stages when scarcity is low

 Weak-tie recommendations’ superiority weakens at initial stages when scarcity is high

 Strong-tie recommendations are more persuasive at later stages when scarcity is low

 Strong-tie recommendations’ superiority weakens at later stages when scarcity is high

## ABSTRACT

This paper examines the effects of recommendations from different social ties on online consumers’ purchase intentions and how such effects change as consumers proceed in the shopping process and are exposed to scarcity-related promotional information. Results from a laboratory experiment indicate that when the degree of deal scarcity is low, recommendations from weak ties are more persuasive than those from strong ties for consumers at the initial shopping stage, whereas the opposite occurs for consumers at the later shopping stage. At both shopping stages, the differences in the effects of social recommendations disappear when the deals are highly scarce.

Keywords: Social recommendations; Construal level theory; Shopping stage; Deal scarcity; Strong (Weak) ties

## 1. Introduction

One of the most important developments on e-commerce platforms over the past decade has been the explosive growth of social recommendations, that is, product recommendations from fellow consumers. For example, consumers’ “likes,” “pins,” and “shares” on websites such as TripAdvisor.com and Pinterest.com are among the most prevalent means of conveying one’s preferences on products. For other consumers, these actions serve as important and easy-to-observe social recommendations, which likely affect their decision-making [21,36]. Numerous past studies have shown that social recommendations (in the form of “likes” or “shares”) greatly shape consumers’ perception of product quality and their purchase decisions [36,38].

More recently, the increasing collaboration between e-commerce platforms and social networks enables consumers to access recommendations from different people in their social networks [11,44,59]. In particular, people in one’s social network can be broadly classified into two types according to the strength of social ties. Strong ties exist between close friends who interact frequently and whose social circles overlap, whereas weak ties are typified by distant social relationships and infrequent interactions, which are commonly observed between acquaintances or strangers [35]. For example, when consumers evaluate books on Amazon.com or hotels on TripAdvisor.com, they can access recommendations from unknown community users or online acquaintances (i.e., weak ties) but may also be notified of their Facebook friends (i.e., strong ties) who have liked or consumed the book or the hotel being evaluated.

This burgeoning phenomenon makes it important for both platform providers and online businesses to understand how users are affected by recommendation information from different ties. In particular, many studies have suggested that a piece of information from strong ties will be more persuasive than when it is provided by weak ties because of higher familiarity and perceived trustworthiness of the information source [7,24,44]. In other words, one’s strong ties are often considered more influential sources of recommendations than weak ties. Nonetheless, several other studies have suggested that people tend to perceive information from weak ties as novel and valuable, which often stimulates more creativity and exploration of new opportunities. Therefore, people are more likely to share or “retweet” information from weak ties than that from strong ties [59]. However, little research thus far has sought to explore when and how the effects of recommendations from different social ties may differ during an online shopping process. For example, will one always prefer a product more when the product is recommended by some close friends than when it is recommended by some acquaintances? It is thus meaningful to investigate the different effects of social recommendation information from strong versus weak ties.

Indeed, evidence suggests that consumers’ information needs may differ during their shopping process [32]. In particular, because online consumers are often unable to evaluate all available alternatives in depth prior to making their decisions because of their limited cognitive capacity, they will first browse and screen product offerings, trying to narrow down their choices. As their needs evolve and they are more certain about what they want [32], they will selectively attend to a limited set of alternatives pertaining to their needs and make their purchase decisions. The two-stage shopping model [32] describes the differences in these two phases of shopping in terms of consumers’ information needs and processing and suggests that consumers at the initial shopping stage do not have clear shopping goals and are thus open to any information that signals potentially attractive products. In contrast, consumers at the later shopping stage have constructed concrete shopping goals and tend to focus on attaining detailed information related to their target alternatives. In the context of online shopping, however, studies have generally overlooked this time dimension of information provision, e.g., it is unknown whether the effect of social recommendations on attracting users initial attention to products and on influencing their final purchases will differ [51]. In practice, because users’ initial screening of products helps them formulate their shopping goals and thus has an influence on their final choices, it is important for a business to not only influence consumers at the point of purchase but also attract their initial attention. Given the abundance of product information and the limited real estate on webpages, online businesses thus have to find out the right time to serve the right social recommendations. Accordingly, the first objective of this study is to investigate how the effects of recommendations (in the form of “likes”) from strong versus weak social ties differ depending on when the consumers are exposed to such information during the shopping process.

The limited understanding on when to present different social recommendations is aggravated by the fact that online consumers are not only exposed to information from fellow consumers but are also constantly affected by promotional information from marketers [29,55]. In particular, promotions using scarcity appeals in the form of quantity (e.g., “only 3 pieces left!”) or time constraints (e.g., “deals end today!”) have been widely observed on e-commerce platforms. Such scarcity-related promotional information often disrupts consumers’ shopping process and considerably affects both how they initially screen products and how they make purchase decisions [23,25,33]. In other words, consumers’ information foci and processing are determined by not only the phase of their shopping but also the presence of disruptive marketing information such as scarcity-related promotional cues. Therefore, the second objective of this study is to further examine how the effects of recommendations from different social ties during consumers’ shopping process differ depending on the level of product scarcity.

#

In sum, this study examines the persuasive effects of recommendations from different social ties and how such effects change as consumers proceed in their shopping process and are exposed to scarcity-related promotional information. In particular, “likes” for a product indicated by one’s strong or weak ties is the major form of social recommendations we investigate in this paper. This research offers several theoretical and practical contributions. First, by revealing the moderating role of shopping stages, our findings show that recommendations from weak ties may be as persuasive as or even more persuasive than those from strong ties depending on when people are exposed to the recommendation information. Second, previous studies have primarily examined the effect of either recommendations provided by fellow consumers or promotional information imposed by marketers [53]. This paper reveals the interplay between social recommendations and deal scarcity information during consumers’ online shopping process. Third, the findings of this study provide valuable guidance for website designers on how to deploy recommendation strategies according to the shopping stages of consumers and the presence of promotional information.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews the related theories and studies. Research hypotheses are then proposed, followed by the research methodology and data analysis. We conclude our paper by discussing its implications, limitations, and future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

This research investigates the persuasive effects of recommendations from different social ties during consumers’ online shopping process. We review the literature on persuasion and the construal level theory (CLT) to better understand how consumers mentally represent and process recommendation information [9].

## 2.1. Social information source and tie strength

The persuasion literature has widely demonstrated that information source may serve as an important cue that largely determines the persuasive effects of a piece of information [8,14]. The same piece of information may exert a stronger influence if it is from a more familiar or trustworthy source because the familiarity of the source may affect the perceived relevance of the information [48,50]. In particular, the relationship between the information source and recipient can be measured by tie strength, which represents the closeness and interaction frequency between the two parties [35]. Strong ties represent closer relationships and greater frequency of interaction than weak ties. On the basis of prior studies, recommendations from strong ties are expected to be more persuasive than those from weak ties as information transferred by strong ties is likely to be perceived as more relevant and reliable [7,24,49]. A few recent studies, however, have revealed that information from weak ties can be perceived as more novel and informative than that from strong ties and is hence more likely to attract attention and be widely spread [59]. A plausible reason is that users tend to believe that weak ties can bridge different social clusters in social networks [2,11,35]. Accordingly, recommendations from weak ties may actually be attractive to consumers as they deem these recommendations as potentially interesting and valuable discoveries.

#

This study intends to understand the relative effects of recommendations from different social sources from a fresh theoretical perspective for two reasons. First, as mentioned earlier, different social relationships reflect different degrees of interpersonal closeness and social distance. Close interactions between friends decrease their social distance, whereas the social distance between weak ties is more distal. Prior research has found that social distance, as a form of psychological distance, affects how people construe information from different social ties [57]. However, while prior studies have found that information from different social relationships will exert different effects on recipients, little research on persuasion has considered how people actually construe a particular piece of recommendation information differently when the social distance with the information source differs. Second, online consumers often go through a process where their shopping targets become clearer as they approach purchase. However, prior persuasion research seldom examines how information from different sources affects people’s product evaluations at different points in time during the shopping process. Indeed, a few research studies on psychological distance have identified that different dimensions of psychological distance, such as temporal and social dimensions, may interact with each other to affect people’s decisions [27,38]. This research thus investigates how consumers follow social recommendations at different timings in a shopping process from the theoretical lens of the congruency between psychological distances.

The CLT has been widely used to explain the relation between people’s processing of a piece of information and the psychological distance of the information based on its source (i.e., social distance) and the time of its provision (i.e., temporal distance) [56]. We believe that the CLT provides an important theoretical lens to understand how the same recommendation information may exert different persuasive effects depending on its information source (i.e., from a weak tie or strong tie) and the time it is presented during users’ shopping process.

## 2.2. CLT

The CLT contends that people may construe an event (e.g., an object or its behavior) at an abstract, higher level or at a concrete, lower level [57]. High-level construal reflects the general value and meaning of the event, whereas low-level construal reflects the details and specific conditions of the event. For example, the act of seeking for a new job can be perceived as having an opportunity for career advancement (high-level construal) or as undergoing a preparation procedure (low-level construal). The central idea of the CLT is that an increased psychological distance (i.e., temporal, social, or spatial distance) between a person and an event prompts the person to construe the event at a more abstract and higher level [56,57]. The CLT has been widely applied in various fields such as psychology [6], marketing [38], accounting [15], organizational science [20], and information systems (IS, e.g., [1,3,5]). In the domain of IS, for example, Phang et al. [5] have examined community users’ knowledge contribution behavior and argued that contributors have a lower psychological distance from the act of contribution and hence will perceive contribution activities at a more concrete level than mere observers.

## 2.2.1. Social distance and sources of information

#

Social distance, as one form of psychological distance, has attracted much research attention in recent years. In general, a person’s perceived social distance from strong ties is close, while that from weak ties is distal. When considering the behaviors or opinions of close social relationships, users are likely to form a low-level and detailed representation that includes specific attributes as they are familiar with the specific needs and actions of their strong ties [38,56]. Conversely, when considering the behaviors or opinions of distant social relationships, users tend to focus on the high-level and generic aspects [38,56] as it is often difficult for them to directly perceive the detailed considerations or specific actions of distant relationships, and hence, a process of abstraction is adopted [17,18].

For example, studies have found that the same action (e.g., making a list) would be construed as a low-level concrete behavior (e.g., writing things down) if it is performed by strong ties but as a high-level abstract behavior (e.g., getting organized) if performed by weak ties [13,34]. Zhao and Xie [38] have particularly mentioned that preference information from peers in the same university (i.e., close social distance) is construed at a lower level than that from peers in another university (i.e., distant social distance). In particular, preferences conveyed by close others are likely to be interpreted mainly on the basis of low-level, feasibility features of products, that is, the cost and specific conditions of obtaining products (e.g., the driving distance to a trip destination, ease of learning and using a new system [3]), whereas preferences conveyed by distant others are often interpreted on the basis of high-level, desirability features of products, that is, the general value and benefits of the products (e.g., the scenery of a trip destination, usefulness of a system [3]) [54].

## 2.2.2. CLT and two-stage shopping model

The CLT studies on temporal distance have similarly revealed that people tend to construe events in the distant future in terms of high-level and abstract features, whereas events in the near future are more likely to be represented in terms of low-level and concrete features [40,57]. For example, when seeking a new job next year (i.e., a distant future task), a person often perceives such a task as an opportunity for career advancement (i.e., a higher construal level), whereas when looking for a new job next week (i.e., a proximal future task), he/she often focuses on the preparation details (i.e., a lower construal level) [41,57]. Related to the concept of temporal distance, researchers have proposed that a shopping process can be divided into two stages where consumers’ shopping goals become more concrete as they approach the end of the shopping process [32].

Particularly, at the initial shopping stage where consumers are far from making purchase decisions, they are uncertain about what to buy and tend to process information in an abstract manner to define a desired outcome [32]. Consumers at this stage will focus more on the high-level primary features of products that define their general attractiveness (e.g., the scenery of a trip destination) because such information is more congruous with their abstract mental representation styles at the initial stage [12,38]. In contrast, at the later shopping stage where consumers are close to making purchases, they have established specific shopping goals and tend to process information in a concrete manner [32]. In this case, consumers tend to focus more on the low-level details and specifics of products (e.g., the driving distance to a trip destination) for making purchase decisions. Overall, consumers will process product information in different ways as they proceed in the shopping process.

## 2.3. Scarcity-related information

Prior research has shown that scarcity-related promotional information often affects how consumers construe product information and make decisions during the course of shopping [19,33]. On the one hand, some studies have argued that high deal scarcity signals a highly attractive offer and improves the perceived value of the deal because consumers uncritically apply the rule of thumb, “what is rare is good” [37,47]. Consequently, scarcity information may narrow down consumers’ scope of attention to highly scarce deals and prompt them to process these deals in a more concrete and detailed manner [46]. On the other hand, however, high scarcity may also serve as obstacles to hinder consumers from achieving their shopping goals, which will subsequently create a sense of “urgency.” This sense of urgency or threat will induce a motivational state that drives consumers to immediately engage in an appropriate behavior to prevent the feeling of regret when they lose the favorable deals [30]. In this case, high scarcity will interfere with the ability to process information in detail, leading to a suppressed cognitive process that focuses on higher-level abstract product information and simpler decision rules [4,19].

However, little research thus far has explored when scarcity information will spur different reactions. According to the literature on shopping stages and scarcity, it is plausible that how consumers are affected by scarcity information depends on when they are presented with such information. In particular, while we have argued that consumers tend to construe product information at different levels depending on the shopping stage (two-stage shopping model), product scarcity information may disrupt consumers’ shopping process and considerably affect both how they initially screen products and how they make purchase decisions. When consumers do not have specific shopping goals (i.e., at the initial shopping stage), scarcity information may serve as value signals to help narrow down consumers attention and prompt them to assess scarce deals at a more concrete level. When consumers have established specific shopping goals (i.e., at the later shopping stage), scarcity information may trigger a sense of urgency and induce them to process information at a higher level to accelerate decision-making. Hence, consumers’ information foci and construal are not only determined by the phase of their shopping but also the presence of disruptive marketing information such as scarcity-related promotional cues. Shopping stages and scarcity-related product information may together determine how consumers process product information and hence moderate the effect of recommendations from different social ties.

In summary, the present paper draws on the CLT and studies on tie strength, shopping stage, and scarcity effects to investigate how the effects of recommendations from different social ties differ depending on when consumers are exposed to the recommendations and the presence of scarcity information from marketers.

## 3. Hypothesis development

Hypotheses are formulated to investigate the effects of social recommendations on consumers’ product evaluation at different shopping stages and levels of deal scarcity. Figure 1 presents the research model of this study.

According to the CLT and related studies, actions from one’s strong ties (i.e., close social distance) tend to be processed at a lower level and in detail, whereas those from weak ties (i.e., distant social distance) are likely to be processed in a more abstract manner [38,56]. In particular, we argue that users tend to represent recommendations from strong ties in a low-level concrete manner as they are likely to perceive close others to consider the low-level details (i.e., the low-level process of obtaining or using the products) when giving them recommendations and likely to recommend products relevant to their personal interests. In contrast, users may represent recommendations from weak ties in an abstract and high-level way as they are likely to perceive that distant others tend to rely on generic and primary features (i.e., the high-level benefits of the products) when making recommendations.

Because consumers always place more weight on the type of information that matches their natural construal levels in a given situation [12], we expect that the effects of recommendations from strong versus weak ties may vary depending on how consumers construe information at different shopping stages. In particular, the two-stage shopping framework [32] proposes that consumers only have a broad idea of what they want at the initial shopping stage. At this stage, they tend to construe product information at a high level and focus on the abstract and general product information to narrow down the possible choices [22,43]. Accordingly, recommendations from weak ties, which are typically represented as abstract and generic information, are expected to be more persuasive than those from strong ties at the initial shopping stage. In contrast, consumers have specific goals of what they want to buy at the later shopping stage and they tend to process information at a low level and focus on product details to complete their target purchases [32]. Hence, consumers at this stage are more likely to be affected by lower-level and concrete information, such as recommendations from strong ties. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1a: At the initial shopping stage, recommendations from weak ties will induce higher purchase intentions than those from strong ties.

H1b: At the later shopping stage, recommendations from strong ties will induce higher purchase intentions than those from weak ties.

As previously mentioned, the information processing styles of consumers may change when they are exposed to scarcity-related promotional cues during the course of shopping. High scarcity information may be interpreted as a signal of valuable deals, narrowing down consumers’ attention to the details of scarce deals [46], or it may be processed as a potential threat to the achievement of shopping goals, which suppresses consumers’ cognitive capabilities [4]. We propose that how consumers process scarcity information depends on when they are exposed to such information.

In particular, consumers at the initial shopping stage do not have well-defined shopping targets; hence, deal scarcity information is likely to help them pay specific attention to valuable deals among an assortment of products. Consumers will then process the scarce offers in a more concrete way [46]. Accordingly, although weak-tie recommendations (often processed at a high level) are generally expected to exert a stronger influence at the initial shopping stage, the presence of high scarcity information shifts consumers’ information processing toward more concrete styles, which becomes more congruent with users perceptions of strong-tie recommendations (often processed at a low level). Consequently, the presence of high scarcity information may strengthen the effect of strong-tie recommendations and weaken the effect of weak-tie recommendations. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2a: At the initial shopping stage, the superiority of weak-tie recommendations will be weakened when the degree of deal scarcity is high.

In contrast, consumers at the later shopping stage have concrete preferences, and they tend to interpret high scarcity information as a potential threat to obtaining their preferred products. In particular, as the deals approach the end, consumers may feel impending regret about losing the benefits of purchasing their target products at discounted prices [26]. Such feeling may arouse consumers and suppress their cognitive ability to process information in detail [4,19]. Accordingly, consumers tend to adopt a more automatic and abstract information-processing mode that relies more on high-level primary product information [4]. In this case, weak-tie recommendations, which are often represented as high-level and abstract information, may serve as useful information for consumers to make decisions regarding the target deals. Hence, although strong-tie recommendations are generally expected to exert a stronger influence than weak-tie recommendations at the later shopping stage, the presence of high scarcity information may reduce the differences in their effects. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2b: At the later shopping stage, the superiority of strong-tie recommendations will be weakened when the degree of deal scarcity is high.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Pretest on the construal level of social recommendations

This study investigates the effects of recommendations from different social ties, in the form of “likes,” on consumers’ product evaluation. One’s action of liking a product represents his/her preference and recommendation of the product. A pretest was first conducted to reveal how users would construe such information from their strong/weak ties. From prior studies [41,54], we expect that users will interpret preferences of strong ties mainly on the basis of the attractiveness of low-level feasibility features, i.e., the cost and conditions of obtaining and using the products (e.g., the extent to which the price of a printer or driving distance of a trip destination is desirable), while they will interpret preferences of weak ties mainly based on the attractiveness of high-level desirability product features, i.e., the value and benefits of having the products (e.g., the extent to which the printing image quality of a printer or the scenery of a trip destination is desirable). In other words, users tend to construe recommendations from strong ties at a lower level and those from weak ties at a higher level.

A total of 43 students were recruited from a major university in China. The students were randomly divided into two groups. One group of subjects were asked to perceive which

#

option (among a pair of products in a given scenario) was likely to be preferred by a close friend (i.e., strong tie), whereas the other group was asked to perceive which option was likely to be preferred by a friend’s friend named “Geoffrey” (i.e., weak tie) [27]. Seven scenarios with exactly the same product descriptions were created for each group. For the two products in each scenario, the same features were described, and there was no difference in the details of feature information related to each option. However, for option A, the value of the high-level feature was desirable, whereas that of the low-level feature was undesirable (e.g., a printer that produced high-quality images but had a high price), while the reverse applied to option B (e.g., a printer that produced ordinary-quality images but had an acceptable price). Hence, subjects’ choices reflected how they construed social recommendations and, in particular, their foci of high-level or low-level aspects of the products, given the source of recommendations. The complete scenario information is presented in Appendix A.

Following Liviatan et al. [13], for all seven scenarios, option A was coded as 1 and option B was coded as 0. Scores were then summed for each subject, resulting in a 0–7 level of construal score [13]. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) results showed that subjects in the strong-tie condition had significantly lower construal scores $( \mathbf { M } = 3 . 2 7 , \mathbf { S D } = 1 . 0 8 )$ than those in the weak-tie condition $( \mathbf { M } = 4 . 7 1 , \mathbf { S } \mathbf { D } = 1 . 3 5 ; \mathrm { F } \left( 1 , 4 1 \right) = 1 5 . 0 9 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). In other words, consistent with our expectations, people tend to construe recommendations from strong ties at a lower level and those from weak ties at a higher level.

## 4.2. Main experiment

## 4.2.1. Experimental design

A laboratory experiment was conducted to test the hypotheses. A 2 (shopping stage: initial versus later stage) × 2 (social recommendation: strong-tie versus weak-tie recommendations) × 2 (deal scarcity: high versus low scarcity) between-subject factorial design was employed. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of eight conditions. We constructed a shopping website for the experiment, and a new domain name was used for the website. The subjects were told that this was a new shopping website that had been promoted to many college students recently. We ensured that the information presentation on the experimental pages resembled the presentation of such information in real e-commerce platforms.

Product selection. Badminton rackets were selected as the target product category because many universities offered badminton courses, which was one of the favorite elective courses among college students in China. Because badminton was a common sport for the majority of college students, browsing and inspecting badminton rackets represented a realistic scenario for the subjects. On the basis of a pretest, six badminton rackets at the same level of price and perceived attractiveness were selected. A hypothesized brand name was associated with these products to avoid the potential confounding effects of prior brand involvement. A target badminton racket was selected among the six, of which the social recommendation information and deal scarcity were manipulated.

Shopping stages. Two shopping stages with different levels of shopping goal concreteness were manipulated as follows. Subjects at the initial shopping stage were asked to visit the online store and freely browse a list of products for as long as they wanted. The subjects were

#

told that they did not have to make purchase decisions at the end of their visit; hence, they were sufficiently distant from making purchases. To ensure that the subjects did not have specific shopping goals during the task, they were informed that the products featured on the listing page (See Fig. B-1 in Appendix B) were randomly retrieved from a pool of products on sale. The information for each badminton racket on the product listing page included a picture, product name, social recommendations from different social ties, and the remaining quantity (i.e., deal scarcity) of the badminton racket. The position of the target badminton racket on the product-listing page was randomized. Only the social recommendation information and deal scarcity of the target badminton racket were manipulated. For the other five badminton rackets, we also displayed their social recommendation information and the remaining quantity, with low scarcity and social recommendations from unfamiliar others, and such information was kept the same across conditions.

Subjects at the later shopping stage were asked to visit a shopping website and specifically search for “badminton rackets” to purchase for the next semester. In particular, the same six badminton rackets that were listed at the initial shopping stage were retrieved during the search, but without social recommendation or scarcity information. The subjects were subsequently primed with a preference for the target badminton racket<sup>1</sup> and were directed to the detailed information page of the target badminton racket (See Fig. B-2 in Appendix B). The subjects were asked to make purchase decisions about the target badminton racket at the end of their visit. The social recommendation and scarcity information were manipulated for the target badminton racket on its detailed information page. In other words, the subjects were exposed to the manipulated social recommendation and scarcity information only after they had formed a specific shopping target (i.e., when they were evaluating the details of the target badminton racket for purchase decisions).

Social ties and deal scarcity. To present real social recommendation sources on the experimental website, we recruited subjects online a month before the experiment and collected their social tie information during the recruitment. In particular, the registrants were asked to answer a diverse range of questions, including listing five of their closest friends with whom they interacted frequently on their social network sites and five unfamiliar contacts (except famous people or expert users) with whom they had not communicated for at least 2 months. On the experimental website, the recommendations from strong (weak) ties were presented in the form of three close (distant) others “liking” the product, which were randomly selected from registrants’ answers during the recruitment. As mentioned earlier, the action of “like” represents one’s preference and recommendation of a product, and consumers tend to process this information at different levels based on the source of this recommendation.

The deal scarcity information was manipulated depending on the remaining quantity of the target product, with 4 units left representing high deal scarcity and 498 units left indicating low deal scarcity.

## 4.2.2. Experimental Procedures

#

A total of 134 undergraduate and graduate students were recruited from a major university in China. According to the power analysis for a between-subject design, this sample size (i.e., 16–19 subjects in each condition) assures a sufficient statistical power of 0.8 for a medium effect size (f = 0.25) [16].

The subjects were initially shown a welcome screen and subsequently instructed to perform a task, which was either to browse a list of products on sale without making purchase decisions (i.e., the initial stage) or to search and evaluate some badminton rackets to make a purchase (i.e., the later stage). The subjects were asked to complete a questionnaire after browsing all the webpages.

The questionnaire included measures of purchase intentions, manipulation check items for shopping stage (adapted from [22,43]), tie strength (adapted from [11,28]), and deal scarcity (self-created items based on [33]) and some demographic variables. Purchase intentions served as a proxy measure for one’s interest in a specific product, which was used as the dependent variable for both the initial and later shopping stages. Subjects at the initial shopping stage were asked to indicate their purchase intentions for several badminton rackets, including the target badminton racket on the listing page. The purpose was to make sure that the subjects were not aware of the target product and hence would report their true evaluation on it. The subjects at the later shopping stage were asked to indicate their purchase intentions for the target badminton racket after they browsed the detailed information page of the product. Purchase intentions were measured in a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 7 = “strongly agree”; likely to buy the rackets, look forward to buying the rackets, and will purchase the rackets next time when needed) (adapted from [58]).

Each experiment session lasted for roughly 20 minutes. A token payment of ￥30 (approximately \$5) was given to each subject upon completing the task.

## 6. Conclusion and discussion

This research examines how the persuasive effects of recommendations from different social ties vary depending on when social recommendations are presented during the shopping process and the level of scarcity of the recommended deals.

Our results reveal a three-way interaction effect among social recommendation, shopping stage, and deal scarcity. In particular, consumers are more likely to be affected by recommendations from weak ties than those from strong ties at the initial shopping stage if the degree of scarcity is low. This is because their processing of weak-tie recommendations is congruent with their natural high-level mindsets at this stage [38]. However, as their information processing becomes more concrete upon their exposure to high scarcity information, consumers become less reliant on abstract information for evaluating products, which dilutes the superiority of weak-tie recommendations. In contrast, at the later shopping stage, recommendations from strong ties are more persuasive than those from weak ties if the degree of scarcity is low because the mental representation of strong-tie recommendations is consistent with the low-level construal of consumers at this stage. However, when the deal scarcity is high, consumers will be prompted to think more abstractly when making purchase decisions [4]. Consequently, weak-tie recommendations, which are often construed as abstract information, may serve as a piece of useful information for consumers to assess their deals. Therefore, the presence of high scarcity information reduces the differences between the effects of strong-tie and weak-tie recommendations at this stage.

Overall, although previous studies have accumulated evidence about the effects of recommendations from strong or weak ties, our study demonstrates that the effects of such recommendations will vary depending on the shopping stage of consumers and the degree of deal scarcity provided by marketers.

## 6.1. Contributions and implications

This paper contributes to the literature in three aspects. First, the persuasion literature has long assumed that information from close relationships would always be more persuasive than that from distant others [24]. Granovetter [35] and subsequent studies, however, showed the importance of weak ties in information dissemination [10,11,39,45,52,59]. The current research extends the previous studies by investigating how the persuasive effects of recommendations from weak versus strong ties differ during the shopping process through the lens of the CLT. We find that information from weak ties may be as persuasive as or even more persuasive than that from strong ties in certain situations. In particular, we argue that consumers may construe recommendations from strong ties at a low level and those from weak ties at a high level. Accordingly, their product preferences will be considerably affected by weak-tie recommendations when they are under the mindset to form a high-level view, such as when they are browsing around without a concrete shopping goal. However, when consumers have developed specific shopping goals, they are more likely to be affected by low-level information such as recommendations from strong ties.

Second, this research distinguishes itself from the other IS studies by considering the time dimension of information provision in the context of online shopping. Although some studies have investigated how temporal distance affects the processing of information [6,

#

38,41], traditional temporal distance is relatively difficult to measure and manipulate on e-commerce platforms, hence limiting its application. The present study extends the current literature by drawing on the two-stage shopping framework, which reflects the evolution of shopping goals and browsing behaviors of consumers during their shopping process. In addition, while previous research has examined the nature of different shopping stages [32], few empirical studies have investigated the interaction between shopping stage and the type of information provided online. The present study examines the interplay between shopping stage and social recommendation and demonstrates that consumers will react to social recommendations from different social ties differently depending on when they are exposed to such information.

Third, this research explores the moderating role of marketer-provided deal scarcity information in the effects of social recommendations. Although several previous studies have demonstrated the positive effects of scarcity on consumption behaviors [25,37], the present study further suggests that scarcity may have different roles in the decision-making processes of consumers depending on when they are exposed to such information. In other words, scarcity may trigger different construal levels of consumers depending on the decision context. Scarcity may help narrow down consumers’ scope of attention and prompt them to thoroughly scrutinize the scarce offers among an assortment of products when they do not have concrete shopping targets [46]. Scarcity may also prevent consumers from thoroughly processing information of the scarce offers when they have formed clear preferences [4]. Our results reveal that the persuasive effect of weak-tie recommendations at the initial shopping stage is weakened in the presence of high scarcity information, but is strengthened at the later shopping stage when deal scarcity is high.

Practically, marketers are faced with increasing opportunities to use social networks for promoting their products online, such as highlighting “likes” from users’ Facebook friends on e-commerce platforms. However, when to serve such social information to effectively influence consumers is seldom investigated. Our research can guide website designers on how and when to present social recommendation information to induce more purchases. According to our findings, presenting recommendations from close friends is not always the most effective means of promoting products. For example, for consumers who are browsing around e-commerce websites without a particular target, providing weak-tie recommendations can more effectively trigger their interest in a product or even drive them to impulsively purchase the product as long as the deal is not scarce. In fact, online businesses have tried various ways to attract users’ attention to their products during the initial browsing and screening phase, such as displaying unique and attractive product images and highlighting large discounts or high popularity on product listing page. This is because this phase is key to consumers identification of more concrete shopping goals and has a direct impact on their final choices. When consumers are checking information of some target products in detail to make purchase decisions, however, presenting recommendations from strong ties is more effective than providing weak-tie recommendations in inducing purchase if the products are not scarce.

## 6.2. Limitations and future extensions

This research also has a few limitations. First, this study only considers one product type (i.e., badminton rackets). This may impede the generalizability of our findings. Social recommendations have been applied to various kinds of products nowadays, including both experience products and search products [42]. Consumers cannot fully evaluate experience products without actual consumption but can accurately evaluate search products before actual consumption. The product chosen in this study contains some experience attributes, such as the look and feel, and hence, the evaluation of this product usually requires seeking a second opinion from others. However, for some search products (e.g., hard drives), opinions from other consumers may not be as important. Future research can examine whether product type will moderate the effects of different social recommendations.

Second, the effects of social recommendations are manifested in this study through self-reported measures because of constrains in a laboratory setting. Future studies may examine the effects of field settings by collaborating with companies that feature social recommendation information and can capture consumers’ actual consumption behaviors.

Third, in terms of recommendation sources, our study merely considers the tie strength between a known recommendation source (i.e., acquaintances or close friends) and the recipient. Future studies can further validate our findings using unknown others (i.e., general community members) as weak ties. In addition, several other types of social relationships exist such as opinion leaders versus ordinary users. Future studies can explore whether and how recommendations from other types of social sources affect consumption behaviors in different situations.

Fourth, this study reveals that the effects of recommendations from different social ties are moderated by shopping stage and deal scarcity information. Future studies can extend this research by considering other moderating factors, such as network types. In particular, social networking sites are categorized into networking-oriented or knowledge-sharing-oriented sites [31]. On networking-oriented sites such as Facebook, MySpace, and LinkedIn, the formation and sustenance of social ties are the primary concerns, whereas information sharing is only a consequence of social network building. In knowledge-sharing-oriented sites such as Digg and YouTube, the creation and consumption of user-generated information are the primary concerns, and people form social ties only to facilitate their knowledge-sharing processes. Accordingly, people in networking-oriented networks tend to be more receptive to information from strong ties because the social ties in these networks are vital to the extent that social conformity and cohesion may increase the persuasiveness of strong ties. Conversely, information from weak ties may affect the awareness of consumers regarding generic information than that from strong ties in knowledge-sharing-oriented networks because people in these networks tend to seek high-level and generic information. Overall, future research has numerous opportunities to enhance our understanding of the effects of social information on e-commerce platforms.
