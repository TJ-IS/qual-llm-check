## Abstract

Research into the acceptance of technology is in need of an alternative approach. Despite repeated calls for research to guide our colleagues-in-practice in the deployment of systems that are accepted, there has yet to be systematic research into the relationship between the decisions made by individuals and the bottom-line impact that is derived from user acceptance. This study <sup>fi</sup>lls the gap in the literature by theorizing a process-based view of information technology (IT) acceptance. We report on a narrative study of 30 users of an ERP deployment and, analyzing this qualitative data through the lens of the <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions of acceptance proposed by Schwarz and Chin, propose a process model of IT acceptance. We conclude by theorizing on the relationship between these structures and the corresponding bottom-line impacts.

## Introduction

<sup>esearch</sup> <sup>into</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>acceptance</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>technology</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>in</sup> <sup>need</sup>of an alternative approach. Despite repeated calls for of an alternative approach. Despite repeated calls for research to guide our colleagues-in-practice in the deployment of systems that are adopted and utilized for Rperformance enhancement, there has yet to be systematic research into the relationship between cognitive processes and behaviors beyond usage. While work has previously studied information technology (IT)-enabled performance (Pentland, 1989; Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Lucas and Spitler, 1999; Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006; Webster and Ahuja, 2006; Barki et al., 2007), empirical evidence indicates that performance is not driven by the frequency and/or duration of systems use (Pentland, 1989; Jasperson et al., 2005; Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006), but rather through the usage behaviors of an individual adapting and incorporating IT usage into their routine work pattern (or routinization), and an individual maximizing the potential of the technology (or infusion) (Sundaram et al., 2007).

Within the academic community, there has been a considerable amount of focus upon the time period of postadoption, that is, the time after the IT has been deployed inside the organization. Within this time period, organizations are seeking for the implemented IT to become routinized and infused into the employee’s work<sup>fl</sup>ow, and therefore achieve the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IT-enabled performance. The need to understand the drivers of IT-enabled performance is not a new challenge within the academic community, but our ability to discover conclusive <sup>fi</sup>ndings remains an imperative for adoption researchers (Hirschheim, 2007; Lucas et al., 2007).

The need of our colleagues-in-practice to leverage the substantial investments made in IT and achieve higher levels of performance delineates our motivation. We posit that, despite the substantial body of work in this area, there needs to be a shift in research into post-adoption behavior that overcomes two major limitations of past work, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, (a) an over-focus upon perceptions as the most salient cognitive structures and (b) a lack of a process model to understand how routinization and infusion behavior is related to cognitive structures over time.

Early work on IT acceptance focused on the characteristics of the technology, with researchers developing models that focused on the ‘proxy view’ of the underlying IT (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001). Examples of these approaches include the

Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) and the Perceived Characteristics of Innovation (Moore and Benbasat, 1991). This ‘proxy-view’ is predicated on a paradigm that we term the ‘perception–behavior paradigm.’ The perception–behavior paradigm is derived from the Theory of Reasoned Action/ Theory of Planned Behavior, which suggests that perceptions drive behavior (Ajzen, 1985). The perception–behavior paradigm has been successful in explaining usage frequency behavior. However, given that the frequency of a usage behavior does not lead to IT-enabled performance, we propose that we need to move beyond the perception–behavior paradigm in order to better understand what drives performance (Sundaram et al., 2007).

From a theoretical perspective, we posit that behavior that occurs early in the post-adoption process is pre-conscious behavior, that is, behavior that requires a great deal of cognitive effort. If we consider the initial time period after an IT has been deployed, a user needs to exert a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of cognitive effort to recall how to use the IT in order to achieve a baseline of performance. However, over time, the behavior becomes routinized and moves from pre-conscious to post-conscious.

While psychology recognizes a variety of states of consciousness (e.g., Bargh, 1989 enumerates three main types), the distinction between pre- and post-conscious is distinguished by whether or not the ‘automatic processes requires recent conscious processing of the relevant stimulus’ (Bargh, 1989: 27). In the case of pre-conscious automaticity, the behavior is triggered by external stimuli, but the cognitive processes to engage in this behavior are made before awareness and they rely upon prior experience. In the context of user behavior, pre-conscious refers to a user who, when given a new IT, will use frames of reference that derive from prior uses of IT. However, over time, these frames of reference become less grounded in prior experience and more within the current IT. As this occurs, the user moves from pre- to post-consciousness, where the processing is ‘the nonconscious consequences of conscious thought’ (Bargh, 1989: 15). In this context, the user is not conscious of the actions that he/she is undertaking during the use of the IT.

Consider a parallel example of driving: a new driver must focus upon driving and the actions of driving, but over time, driving behavior becomes more routinized and requires less cognitive effort. We postulate that the same is true for IT usage behavior; the perception–behavior paradigm is useful in explaining pre-conscious behavior, but once it has crossed the threshold to post-conscious behavior, a different approach is needed – an approach that focuses on decisions. Therefore, we suggest that our limited <sup>fi</sup>ndings in understanding the drivers of the behavior of routinization and infusion can be attributed to our focus upon perceptions rather than the decisions that a user makes regarding the technology that was deployed.

The second limitation that we seek to address is the lack of process-based views of IT acceptance. While prior work has been longitudinal in nature (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000) and has found that perceptions and attitudes emerge and change over time, the nature of this interaction and the process through which these perceptions unfold lack a theoretical foundation. We therefore seek to create a model that articulates the cognitive process of users to describe the decisions made by users, how these decisions emerge over time, and the relationship between the decisions and the outcomes of routinization, infusion, and IT-enabled performance.

We are not alone in the view that IT acceptance research is in need of a new approach. As Benbasat and Barki (2007: 215) note, ‘multi-stage models are needed to better capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of salient belief variables’ as models up to this point have been static and do not re<sup>fl</sup>ect the dynamic emerging process of IT acceptance. In proposing a new approach, we seek to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the call of Benbasat and Barki (2007: 216) to ‘move outside (Technology Acceptance Model (TAM’s)) limited con<sup>fi</sup>nes’. We endeavor to <sup>fi</sup>ll the gap in the literature by proposing a process-oriented model of IT acceptance.

Our study builds upon the work of Schwarz and Chin (2007), who articulated <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions that users make during the acceptance process. Deriving their <sup>fi</sup>ndings from etymology, or the history of the meaning of words, Schwarz and Chin (2007) presented <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of acceptance, but they did not attempt to link these <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions to a process model. Drawing upon research into automaticity and utilizing these <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions as a guiding lens, we will analyze narrative, qualitative data collected by interviewing users following an Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) deployment. Our analyses will yield a process-based model of IT acceptance.

Our work will proceed as follows. First, we will discuss research into automaticity, highlighting how decisions unfold during post-conscious behavior. We will then integrate the <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions proposed by Schwarz and Chin (2007) into work on automaticity. Next, we will discuss our research approach (namely, narratives) that we used to generate our processbased model. We will then present our results and conclude by presenting our proposed process model.

## Theoretical approach

It has long been understood that the acceptance of an IT is a psychological decision – each individual decides whether or not to accept the IT. The Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (or UTAUT) concludes by claiming that ‘It is possible that we may be approaching the practical limits of our ability to explain individual acceptance and usage decisions’ (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000: 471) (emphasis added). However, the dominant approach within the acceptance research domain has been models and theories developed on the perception–behavior paradigm that was established by TAM and continued by UTAUT. The focus of the perception– behavior paradigm is upon the ‘proxy view’ of IT, where individuals assess the perception of the attributes of the IT. However, perceptions and attitudes toward IT do not constitute decisions – they are simply evaluations. We are not alone in noting that decision making is important during the postadoption period. Jasperson et al.(2005) note that ‘in voluntary and other mandatory use environments, however, such routinized behaviors are more likely to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the scripting of onceactive personal decision processes’ (emphasis added, 543). Given that we have not examined the decisions made by users during the post-adoption period, we postulate that a new theoretical lens is needed in order to understand this process. To understand this post-adoption time period, we have selected the lens of automaticity.

Automaticity refers to the ability of an individual to engage in a behavior that is outside of the conscious. As an individual engages in a behavior frequently, over time that behavior requires less cognitive effort and becomes more automatic to the individual (Langer, 1978; Langer and Piper, 1987; Bargh,

There is a considerable amount of extant work about decisions made in the post-conscious automaticity time period that provides support for the assertion that decisions (and not perceptions/attitude) are what drive behavior during this period, with the argument that the point of automaticity research should be the discovery of the causal mechanisms (i.e., determinants) of the reasoning processes (Hogarth, 1980; Nisbett and Ross, 1980; Holland and Holyoak, 1989; Plous, 1993; Baron, 1994). We assume the view of Bargh, who articulated that post-conscious automaticity should be viewed as an IF(X) THEN (Y) view. Thus, within our context, IF (decision is made to accept the IT) THEN (the individual will routinize the IT) (recognizing that this framework can be articulated for every decision and every behavior within the post-adoption time period).

Previous research has demonstrated the value of explaining the routinization of IT. While previous work has studied IT-enabled performance (e.g., Pentland, 1989; Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Lucas and Spitler, 1999; Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006; Webster and Ahuja, 2006; Barki et al., 2007), empirical evidence has suggested that performance is not driven by the frequency and/or duration of systems use (e.g., Pentland, 1989; Jasperson et al., 2005; Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006), but rather through the usage behaviors of an individual adapting and incorporating IT usage into their routine work pattern (or routinization), and an individual maximizing the potential of the technology (or infusion) (Sundaram et al., 2007). Nonetheless, parallel work has also demonstrated that our current approaches to adoption do not explain routinization or infusion (e.g., Sundraman, et al., 2007), and therefore we provide a new lens through which to explain the routinization of behavior (namely, automaticity) that can better explain acceptance and use decisions.

Shifting the view of post-adoption toward automaticity has two contributions: (a) conceptualizing post-adoptive behavior through the lens of automaticity and (b) arguing that we have under-theorized decisions at the expense of perceptions/ attitudes toward the IT. However, what decisions are made during the post-adoption time period? We have selected the lens of the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of Schwarz and Chin (2007) to inform the decisions made by users and will review these next.

## The <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions of IT acceptance

Relying upon etymology, Schwarz and Chin (2007) proposed that acceptance can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions. According to Schwarz and Chin (2007), previous work has discussed acceptance, but never theorized the constitution of this key term. To overcome this gap in the literature, Schwarz and Chin (2007) outlined these <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions as summarized in Table 1.

As Schwarz and Chin (2007) argue, each of these dimensions involves a psychological process whereby the individual begins with a frequentative action, which the individual repeatedly engages in the process. However, over time this frequentative action can turn into a state of being, whereby this psychological process has become automatic, thereby achieving the threshold of automaticity. This process occurs with the interaction with the technology and results in the psychological process being outside of the conscious cognition of the individual.

Nonetheless, while these dimensions provide a useful taxonomy, this approach does not articulate the type of cognitive structures that these dimensions constitute. While etymology itself does not explicitly align with psychological structures, the framework outlines dimensions that can then be theorized through the lens of automaticity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our analysis of these dimensions is that each dimension represents a speci<sup>fi</sup>c decision made by a user. For support, a textual analysis of the original Schwarz and Chin (2007) article reveals that the authors themselves refer to a series of questions asked by users; we conceptualize these questions as decisions. We have extended the original framework by articulating <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions made by users as they interact with the technology postadoption (Table 2).

The framework is therefore a starting point to understand decision making post-adoption. Furthermore, while this approach gives us a lens through which to interpret the psychological process, we have chosen to investigate how these decisions unfold over time into a process-based view of user acceptance. We posit that a user, upon initial introduction to and subsequent interaction with an individual system, experiences varying levels of acceptance within each of these dimensions (meaning that an individual accepts each technology with a different level of acceptance) and that different outcomes result depending upon the dimension being theoretically studied. This conceptualization of technology acceptance should be viewed as a process model (Markus and Robey, 1988). We will explore the data used to build this process model next.

## Methodological background

Our methodology assumes a positivist perspective, employing the <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions as a lens through which to understand user acceptance. As such, we utilize qualitative data for theorybuilding (Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007) to uncover the logical progression of a user through the process of acceptance. To build this model, we studied the deployment of an enterprise system within a single organization. All of the subjects participated in an interview lasting approximately 60 min, which consisted of a narrative interview with the user. This qualitative approach will be elaborated next.

## The qualitative approach: narratives

The qualitative methodology chosen for this study was the use of narratives. According to Bennett and Royle (1999), narratives are ‘a series of events in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c order – with a beginning, a middle, and an end,’ similar to the process of acceptance (Schwarz and Chin, 2007). Researchers have argued that we recount our lives as narratives (or stories), terming individuals homo narrans (Fisher, 1985) and claiming that humans are genetically programmed to understand the narrative form (Bruner, 2009). In addition, researchers have argued that the narrative is appropriate for building a process-oriented model (Pentland, 1999), which constitutes one of the contributions of this study.

Narratives have been utilized in the past within Information System (IS) literature. Examples of phenomena studied using a narrative approach include systems development (Boland and Day, 1982; Clausen, 1994); the role of politics in IT implementation (Brown, 1998); commitment in high-technology teams (Beech and McCalman, 1997); failures in the deployment of a system (Brown and Jones, 1998); and a series of studies from the IFIP 8.2 working group (Davidson, 1997). While not explicitly advocating the use of narratives, Boland (1985) argued that phenomenology, or in-depth studies of experiences using introspective methods, should be used more extensively within IS research. The use of narratives, while not novel, has yet to be applied in the context of IT acceptance, and represents a novel lens through which to view this phenomenon.

The creation of narratives does not occur through traditional approaches to interviewing. To facilitate the individuals in telling their story, they need to be guided in expressing their journey through the acceptance process. During the reporting, individuals must look into their own minds and report what they discover or introspectively examine their behavior. To develop this model of IT acceptance, we used a guided introspection narrative approach. This was selected to allow users to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon their experiences with using the IT system and freely detail their feelings and beliefs (Gould, 1995), bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that support our research purpose. According to Gould (1995: 719), the application of the guided introspection approach is appropriate for events that are ‘ego involving,’ since it allows the respondent to recount their ‘personal processes’. Given this study’s focus on determining the user acceptance process, the guided introspection approach was deemed to be the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for our research question. In this study, the researcher guided the individual’s discussion of the acceptance process as they introspectively examined their actions. From the analysis of the narrative, the process of acceptance was developed. For additional information on our interview and analysis approach, we point the reader to Appendix A.

## Pilot study

After the research methodology was designed, it was pretested using <sup>fi</sup>ve individuals in the target sample. The individuals were taken through the entire interview process (including a range of possible qualitative questions). In each step of the process, respondents were asked for their feedback regarding the instruments and the questions. On the basis of feedback from the end users, modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made to the interview approach. None of the responses from these interviews were included in the <sup>fi</sup>nal data set.

## Conducting the research study

The research site used for this research was Alpha University, a metropolitan university system located in the Southwestern United States. Alpha University has a number of degreegranting branch campuses across the metropolitan area, and this study was conducted at two of these sites: the main campus and one of the remote campuses. Citing major problems with their information systems, including a lack of integration between their legacy administration systems, some of which were no longer supported by the vendors, the university decided to form a committee to review their options for purchasing a new software package. After reviewing different vendors, in 1998, the university purchased ERP software for US\$41.5 million <sup>1</sup> and began implementation the following year. On the basis of business requirements, the Human Resources and Financial modules of the ERP solution were installed <sup>fi</sup>rst at these two campuses, with the Student modules to follow at a later point in time.

This study was conducted approximately 1 year after implementation, thus allowing some time for the users to become accustomed to using this mandated ERP system. At this time, disagreements had surfaced at the university over the ef<sup>fi</sup> ciency gains offered by the system and the value of this technology to the university. The project team believed that, in the long run, there would be considerable advantages to employing this IT solution and that as the university moved further away from the ‘go live’ date, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts would be evident. Thus, Alpha University represents an organization that can provide some interesting insights into how users feel about ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, advantages, and, ultimately, their own acceptance of the ERP.

To locate users of the ERP system in Phase 1 of the systems development process, the researcher sent out two e-mails to a listserv of the ERP users within the organization, soliciting volunteers. Following these general broadcasts, targeted messages were sent to key department heads within the organization, asking them to highlight end users who would be interested in speaking about their use of the ERP. End users contacted the researcher directly, and appointments were scheduled for participation in the research.

Thirty individuals were included in the research study, which represents a mid-range number of subjects (Polkinghorne, 1989). On average, the end users utilized the ERP system between 2 and 3 h/day. The end users who were interviewed had a variety of positions in the organization, ranging from division administrators to administrative assistants to assistant directors for major departments on campus (the positions of the users are presented in Table 3).<sup>2</sup> As such, the end users possessed a variety of experiences and uses of the ERP and thus met the requirements for good candidates for the narrative analysis, as our set of respondents had a variety of experiences with the ERP – a key requirement for a respondent in a narrative analysis.

## A process-based view of IT acceptance

After completing the data analysis, the process-based view of IT acceptance was developed. In short, the data indicates that IT acceptance is a four-stage process that begins before a user’s initial exposure to the technology (starting at the <sup>fi</sup>rst point when the user hears that a technology [although this is not necessarily the technology ultimately chosen] will be deployed) and continues through training and implementation. Within each of these stages, end users engage in activities and must ful<sup>fi</sup>ll certain conditions in order to move to the next stage.<sup>3</sup> In this section, we will present the process-based view, including a discussion of each stage, supporting each assertion with comments derived from the qualitative interviews.<sup>4</sup> We will structure our discussion by dividing each stage into two parts: Part 1 will present supporting evidence for the psychological state of the individual during the stage and Part 2 will present the mechanisms that enable an individual to move through the stage. These mechanisms were derived from an examination by the research ers of individuals who had progressed past the given stage in an effort to articulate the necessary conditions to achieve the stages. We therefore position our work as a longitudinal process model, which assumes that (a) causation consists of necessary conditions in sequence, with chance and random events playing a role and (b) outcomes may not occur (even when conditions are present) (Markus and Robey, 1988). Nonetheless, we will include a discussion of both the mechanisms and the transitions that a user makes when progressing through our process model.

## Acceptance stage 1: receive

Psychological state of an individual in Stage 1

During Stage 1 of the acceptance process, the end user attempts to make the decision: Am I willing to take this technology without question? The receive stage begins at the point when an end user initially hears that a new IT is coming. The end user in this stage is likely to be fearful and worried, and as a result has negative feelings toward the new IT. During this stage, one end user [126] recalls feeling miserable, knowing that something was going to happen, one way or another. This negative state then pervades the entire organization. An employee [110, Accounting Clerk] who joined the organization while the new IT was being implemented recalls:

The worst that I heard of it [the new information technology] was [from the former person] when she was training me … She used to say, ‘Glad I’m leaving. I don’t wanna be around when it changes.

Thus, an end user in this stage does not want to receive the IT; rather, the negative state leads end users to desire to reject the IT or send it back to those who provided it to them.

An end user [103, Administrative Assistant], still in the receiving stage recounted that if the information technology were taken away, it would help my job out. However, that will never happen. While some users do not use the word ‘reject’ or ‘want to return’ the IT, this is still the underlying desire behind comments such as [122, Museum Administrator]:

Was there apprehension at <sup>fi</sup>rst? Of course. There always is when somebody tells you it’s going to be better for you and you are hearing people saying, how could it be? We’re happy with what we have. I think it’s a comfort level. People are comfortable. You get comfortable with doing what you’re doing. It’s just like if somebody came in today and said – you are not going to be doing that anymore. You are going to be doing something that is totally different. You are like why? Seemingly, I am doing what I need to do. It’s working for me. If it wasn’t working and our records were bad and there was something that was really wrong about it, then I could see changing. But if the information technology is working, why change the information technology?

This apprehension, worry, and desire to reject the IT constitutes the essence of receiving – an underlying lack of willingness to take the technology without question. Thus, the end user does not want to receive the IT from those who bestowed it upon them; instead, he or she wants to return the IT and never be faced with it again. The end user will even justify their refusal to receive the software in two ways: by referencing others and/or by using alternative IT in the desire to displace the importance of the new IT within the organization.

First, users may justify their desire to not receive the new software by referencing ‘others’ who have had negative experiences with the technology. For example, one end user [120, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator], who has since moved beyond the receiving stage, referenced others (in this case, other universities) when she recalled that early on she thought:

… there’s a reason why so many other universities rejected this system. I can totally see that. There are six or seven universities that I know of that rejected the software and I totally see why.

During this time, end users feel frustration toward the new IT and the people who developed the IT. Users refer to how ‘they’ bought the IT, but not that the organization as a whole (‘we’) bought the IT. Individuals often isolate the source of their frustrations as those who developed or purchased the IT. For example, another end user [122, Museum Administrator] stated:

We went to CBMI [College Business Managers Institute] and people said – don’t get it, it’s horrible. We have it and hate it. Right away, we knew that we had no choice and that they had bought it. We didn’t have a say-so. We shouldn’t, but it was a done deal and it was funny because we were already getting negative feedback from other colleges that had it.

Second, users in this stage may utilize alternative IT in hopes of displacing the importance of the new IT within the organization. In referring to the prominence of the new IT within the organization, one end user [118, College Business

Administrator] stated, We all continue to run shadow systems. I will continue to run a shadow system and I will never give up that shadow system and I have told them that at the beginning. This end user’s desire to hold on to the legacy system translates to questioning the new IT, yet the user justi<sup>fi</sup>es this action by implicitly calling into question the reliability of the new IT.

Despite the justi<sup>fi</sup>cations employed in this stage, these feelings of worry and apprehension lead to fear and uncertainty associated with job security, perception of lack of management concern over the frustration, and a lack of satisfaction with the new organizational structure and associated tasks. Frustration may grow to become so intense that individuals consider leaving the organization during this time. One end user [127, Financial Assistant] recalled the early days:

I was so frustrated that I would just get sick. You really couldn’t eat, because you were busy all the time. You can’t take time out and eat because you are here late. I would come in Saturdays and Sundays to catch up. It was frustrating. I was like, you know what, no one took anybody’s input on this; they didn’t ask the people who had been here the longest what they liked. I thought I would take a job at Toys ‘R’ Us. I would rather deal with the children there than deal with this. People have been here a long time – why not take their input before we convert over and say this is what we are going to change to. I have never seen that.

The sentiment of wanting to leave the organization, due to a desire to reject the IT, remains beneath the surface for many users. As an individual end user analyzes whether he or she is willing to take the technology and not question it further, this user decides whether accepting the technology is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enough to justify learning the new software (which is Stage 2 of the acceptance process). The user will look at their perceived ability to move from the current organization to another company and decide whether the IT should be learned ‘for the sake of their job’ or whether he or she should move to another company where this change will not be required. The notion of exiting an organization due to a con<sup>fl</sup>ict is not necessarily new (Hirschman, 1970 discusses this as a question of whether to voice a concern to management or to exit); nonetheless, we see evidence that one speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of con<sup>fl</sup>ict that arises is over the role that IT will play in the organization. This transformation can be seen in the experience of an end user [132, Administrative Assistant] who recollected:

Some mornings, I would come in and close the door, sit down at the computer, and say – oh my God, please let me be able to get through this. I did that for an entire week. But, after that week, I thought, well, this is just a job and this is just a system. Let it go. If you learn it, you learn it. If you don’t, you don’t. You have to do the best you can. If you can’t, you just have to let it go. After that, I just took one thing at a time and did not get stressed out. I tried to come in early on days that I knew might be stressful or I might have trouble getting something out of the computer and let go of other duties so that I could put my attention toward that. I just began asking people. I thought, well, I am sure that people out there think that I am idiot, but I had to do it someway and I was not getting it the way I should have been getting it. A lot of it, again, was that I thought it was a bad decision to leave.

Thus, the user moved from desiring to reject the IT to deciding to learn the IT for the sake of their job. With this alteration in beliefs comes a shift from a negative affective state toward neutrality. This new sentiment can be characterized by an end user [118, College Business Administrator] who said, It’s [the new information technology is] fine with me. I’m not happy about it, but it’s no big deal to me. While the end user who has moved beyond this stage is not happy or positive, he or she does not view the IT as ‘miserable’ or desire that it be removed from the organization. An end user who exempli<sup>fi</sup>es moving beyond this stage is one who describes themselves in the following way [126, Accountant]:

I am somewhat ambivalent about the situation, since I know that these kinds of things need to move forward. We can’t stay with a system that is restrictive in its growth and still move forward as a business entity. But I’m also not very happy about it because it has created mountains of work.

Thus, there is ambivalence about the situation rather than the negative feelings evidenced by those still in this stage. At this point, an end user must deliberately make the decision to use the IT. This attitude is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by an end user who describes her [104, Secretary] perspective as follows: I can spend my time griping about it or learn to work it and figure out how to get things taken care of in the least amount of time because I am busy! Therefore, an end user can either remain in a negative state and focus on rejecting the IT or he or she can choose to move to Stage 2 and begin to learn how to use the software for their job.

## Enabling mechanisms to move an individual past Stage 1

## The role of computer self-efficacy and computer anxiety

The initial negative reaction toward the introduction of a new IT and the accompanying worry and concern can be reduced if the user demonstrates computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, or con<sup>fi</sup>dent judgment of their capabilities to use computers in diverse situations (Compeau and Higgins, 1995) and/or a lack of computer anxiety, which is a lack of apprehension, or even fear, when she/he is faced with the possibility of using computers (Venkatesh, 2000). The existence of these two moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uences will help an end user to experience a lack of negative emotion with regard to the new IT, which will then aid them in their progression through the acceptance process. One end user [119, Senior Secretary], when asked directly whether her positive attitude toward computers helped her to not be negative during the early stages of the introduction to the new IT, responded:

I think so, because I don’t mind going in there. At <sup>fi</sup>rst, when I started doing computers, I was afraid of them. I was afraid that I was going to mess them up. But I have learned that you can’t. You can go in there and do a couple of things and I haven’t done anything bad yet.

Another end user describes her views toward technology as [107, Financial Coordinator] follows:

I am very <sup>fl</sup>exible and very open to considering using software. It is kind of fun to learn how to use new software.

Thus, an end user who is <sup>fl</sup>exible and open to considering new software and has the associated con<sup>fi</sup>dence is more likely to progress quickly through the initial stage of receiving, while end users who are anxious or lack self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are more likely to be slower in their progression through the stage. It is likely that the users fear that they will be unable to learn the new software and will face the change with trepidation.

## The role of past experience

Another factor that affects end users during this stage is where he or she was in the acceptance process of the old IT. Similar to the user acceptance process for the new IT, the acceptance process of the old IT is not related to the length of tenure at the organization. Nonetheless, if a user has progressed through the acceptance process with the old IT, he or she will experience an increased desire to reject the new IT.

One end user [103, Administrative Assistant] who has been with the organization through changes in the past describes her feelings toward the old IT as follows: The old system was so much better. I am not trying to be resistant, but when given the choice, it was an easier system to learn and use. An end user [101] who has been with the organization for an equal amount of time agreed, stating, We knew it (the old information technology) was better – it was more effective. Both of these users had progressed in their acceptance of the old IT and found transitioning to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult.

Conversely, some users demonstrated that length of tenure does not matter. A user [139] that has been with the organization longer than the two end users above claims:

I think that it [the new ERP] is a really good system … You couldn’t do that [many things] with the old information technology. Sometimes when you are doing something for the whole department, it will take a while, but you will still get the job done

These examples demonstrate that the length of time an employee remains with an organization does not necessarily impact their acceptance of a new IT. Instead, we hypothesize that it is the stage of acceptance that the user does achieve in the old IT that shapes the extent to which the user will accept the new IT (we draw support for this assertion from the literature on technology frames (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994) and the discourse on sense-making (Weick, 1993)). If an end user has progressed further in their acceptance process of the old IT, he or she is more likely to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culties receiving the new IT. If the user was not convinced about the merits of the old IT, he or she is less likely to have the same problems.

## The role of user involvement and participation

User involvement is a key component of the user acceptance process during the receiving stage. Users who were involved in or participated during the system development process are less likely to have frustration toward the new organizational structure associated with the new IT. An end user [112, Asst Dir, Division of HR], recalling the early days with the new IT at other universities, stated:

I was also at several other campuses where they started this process and I was more involved at different levels at their institutions, so I had little bit of an idea that this was coming on, because everybody is going with this. I also had some negative experiences early on with the new system. Early on, I was ambivalent. I had positive feelings and negative feelings. At <sup>fi</sup>rst, I was ambivalent, had high frustration, but also high interest. But once I saw what it could do, it was complete buy-in

Thus, while the end user admits having negative experiences early on, an increase in their involvement led them to have complete buy-in to the IT, or not to continually question the merits of the IT within the organization. This experience is in contrast to those who had dif<sup>fi</sup>culties progressing through the receiving stage. Consider the following examples of end users who are either still in the receiving stage or had dif<sup>fi</sup>culties progressing through this stage:

● It is as though some man decided that this is the way it should be. And said, here it is and throw it to the wolves because he does not have to deal with it or use it every day. He does not have to spend the time on it because in his mind, it all works! [101]

● The users were never consulted. The (upper management) chose and paid for the new system without thinking of the real world. [103, Administrative Assistant]

● It also hurt that when they brought the new system in, they didn’t ask the little people what would be good or help them. It was a top decision and a lot of times, those people are good theoretically about what would help, but they do not know because they do not do it. They have not sat behind the desk and tried to manipulate it. [129, Program Coordinator]

In all three of these examples, the end users are either stuck in or are making a slow progression through the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of the acceptance process. As an alternative, consider the case of an end user [115, Compensation Analyst] who describes some dif<sup>fi</sup>culties with the IT department (a theme that will be explored later):

End user: All I am asking for is complete and total global access to my own system to do what I need to do and (someone in IT) sets up a roadblock every time I am trying to get somewhere. And it is not just me, and I know that. And it really and truly, from this angle, looks like a power hungry situation. For a while, I thought she was after my boss’s job.

Researcher: ‘It is interesting that you just said my system. Do you feel any sort of ownership over the system?

End user: ‘Oh yeah!’

## Researcher: ‘Why?’

End user: ‘I have been there since the beginning. When I say me, I mean my boss, myself, and my immediate coworkers are the people that are responsible for making sure that what is in the new system is correct and we can get it back out again in a recognizable format.

Researcher: ‘So you want to see this succeed?

End user: ‘Yes’

Researcher: ‘You do not want to see this fail?

End user: ‘No! If this fails, my life goes down the drain.

This end user exempli<sup>fi</sup>es an effective case of user involvement and participation. In this case, the end user was involved in <sup>fi</sup>tgap sessions that allowed her to develop a feeling of ownership with the technology. While the other cases demonstrate how a lack of user involvement can reduce a user’s ability to progress through this stage, this last example shows how user involvement can produce a positive side effect upon users: if there is a psychological feeling of involvement, the user will begin to feel ownership toward the technology and there will be no desire to reject the technology; instead, end users will naturally want to receive the technology.

An end user [116, HR Assistant], who ended up describing herself as ‘optimistic’ on the <sup>fi</sup>rst day that the new technology went live, described her experiences as follows:

I actually went to 3 or 4 classes pre-implementation. I had sandbox training, so that I could play around without breaking it.

This end user was able to bypass the negative feelings associated with the receive phase by her involvement pre-implementation. Her experience demonstrates the role that ‘sandbox training (or trialability) plays in perceptions of user involvement and the receive phase. Individuals who participate in the process are more likely to experience feelings of voluntariness, for the user believes that there is a choice in accepting the technology rather than it being forced upon them.

## The role of subjective norms

During the receive phase of acceptance, the presence of subjective norms, or the perception that most people who are important to the user think he/she should perform the behavior in question (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975), can overcome initial pockets of resistance. Users who see others who are important to them encouraging use of the technology will be more likely themselves to accept and use the new technology. Consider the following example of an end user [129, Program Coordinator]:

End user: ‘The business manager said that she knew it was not going to work. She didn’t like it.’

Researcher: ‘So she instantly disliked it?

End user: ‘Instantly. And still communicates that. But for a business manager to communicate that, even if she is uncomfortable with it, really sets a bad tone for the whole department.’

For this end user, the tone set by the business manager was important to her (and for the whole department). Yet, it is more than just the boss who is an important <sup>fi</sup>gure. An end user will look to fellow co-workers for support and for cues as to how to react to the introduction of the new technology. Users form a social group and exchange information about the new IT; it is these groups that are critical to individual responses to new technology. An example of how this materializes is found in an end user [130, Division Administrator] with a long history with the organization who admits to having many friends who she leaned upon during the introduction of the new technology:

Sometimes you are afraid to talk to people because they will tell you things that you have not <sup>fi</sup>gured out yet that are bad – oh no! I have that to look forward to!

Thus, the negative spirit of others in<sup>fl</sup>uenced her reaction and caused her to react negatively. By extension, subjective norms are closely linked to the characteristics of visibility and image associated with new information technologies. If an end user sees individuals who are important to them (image) using the IT (visibility), then the user will be less likely to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty progressing through the receive phase.

## The role of personality types

Users who do not have a stubborn personality or who are open to change are less likely to resist and feel the negative affective emotions that dominate this stage. While none of the end users interviewed during this study would describe themselves as not being open to change, many respondents described others who they knew who had a change-resistant personality. One end user reported the following about her boss [107, Financial Coordinator]: When I mentioned the word (ERP), he [my boss] rolled his eyes and said that other universities have had problems and that it was going to be a big mess. He knew nothing else about the software than that. Another end user [104, Secretary] depicted these types of users:

Going into it with a negative attitude is stupid. Generally, I am optimistic. We all have down days, but there are certain personality types where there is nothing you can do to please them. If you take it away, they will complain because it was not there. I think that they will have to have a negative attitude to focus on something negatively rather than to just let it go.

Statements such as this suggest that end users who would describe themselves as resistant to change or stubborn are more likely to attempt to reject the IT, for change’s sake. Personality types that are not stubborn or change averse would not reject the IT outright.

## Acceptance stage 2: grasp

## Psychological state of an individual in Stage 2

During Stage 2 of the acceptance process, the end user seeks to make the decision: Do I intellectually comprehend the intentionality (e.g., functionality and design) of the technology? While the end user is no longer in a negative affective state (they are in a neutral state at this time, neither negative nor positive toward the new IT), he or she is now motivated to understand the new IT and is making a movement toward change. During this stage the user begins to understand the intentionality of the new IT, including how to use the IT for their task needs, why the organization deployed the new IT, and their new role within the organization.

As the user goes through the training process in this stage, he or she attempts to understand the core requirements necessary to complete their job now that the new IT has been introduced. An end user [130, Division Administrator] at the beginning of this stage recalled the early days of training as follows:

Well, we started being given overviews. These overviews were like nothing we had ever seen. It was like nothing made any sense … At that point, it really caused me concern because it was so different then anything we had ever dealt with and it seemed so confusing. Of course, they told you that it would be easy, but I was thinking, ‘Oh my God, I am an idiot here,’ because it was always played up to be so simple and so <sup>fl</sup>exible, and yet everything you tried to sit and learn and take in was hard. And I am looking at this from my day-to-day perspective of what I needed it to do and I could not even <sup>fi</sup>t that in anywhere. It was like nothing made sense. It was as if my world was turned upside down.

The objective here is clear: to understand how to complete the day-to-day part of the job now that the new IT has been introduced into the user’s environment. Note that the user does not want to reject the software (and thus does not display the characteristics of Stage 1), but rather he or she desires to understand the new IT. Thus, end users are in the process of learning and turning their world ‘right side up.’ A user who is still in the process of moving through this stage admits [104, Secretary]:

I know such a little smidgeon of it. I’ve gained some con<sup>fi</sup>dence with it, but it is like doing it rote and being a monkey. If you deviate from it, you can go around and around … I have a hard time <sup>fi</sup>nding what I need. I do not know if that is because I need more training or if it is the complexity of the program. I am still learning.

Thus, the end user is still in the process of learning the key aspects of their job, associated with the new IT.

While learning takes place during the entire acceptance process, the understanding stage is the time during which the end user is attempting to understand not only the basic requirements for their job but also the rationality of how and why the IT works as it does. One end user [115, Compensation Analyst] provides a tangible example of this seeking:

There are some things that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand why a system this size would be as manual as it is. For instance, although I have not experienced it, I have been told that payroll, outside of the normal pay cycle, is completely manual – every step of the way.

Thus, end users need to understand the rationality for the IT. The frustration is also expressed in the following statement [118, College Business Administrator]:

There is much of the system that I do not understand. Even today, we have been live for a year and a half … (pause) … Some of it does not make sense. We were sold on an electronic system. We are still printing paper – it is not an electronic system. We do not understand the system, still. We understand bits and pieces here and here, but in the HR system, they are still trying to <sup>fi</sup>ne tune reports and come up with reports that look just like the ones we used in the old system.

This understanding that the end user seeks is theoretical, that is, why does the IT work like it does? However, a paucity of understanding of the rationality of the IT does not imply that the user is unable to complete their basic job. Rather, a lack of understanding represents a questioning of the underlying purpose of the IT. A user [120, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator] explains the frustration as follows:

I also noticed that it is inconstant and illogical in how you get to things … The logic part of it really throws me. I’m pretty reasonable, but it really throws me. You <sup>fi</sup>gure that if you go to one panel, you could get to similar reports. But no! It is four panels away. And they may not have anything to do with <sup>fi</sup>nance – it may be hidden somewhere else. And I just want to pull my hair out; I really do. I don’t get frustrated easy, but there have been a few times when I have gone home pretty frustrated.

Beyond this simple training for a utilitarian purpose, an end user seeks to understand why their organization made this change. During this time, stories circulate across the company as to why the organization chose the IT. These organizational stories are sometimes false, but provide the user with a means by which to deal with the organizational change. Taylor et al. (1993: 727) point out that organizational actors use stories as a point of comparison during times of change in order to ‘use their own coping styles more effectively, help reduce stress, and allow for a more accurate appraisal of the situation’. Consider the following examples when users were asked why the organization elected to use the IT (none of which are accurate reasons):

One of the things that started this whole thing is that the (upper management) wanted some screwy little report and they could not get it because of the systems we had. Maybe they can get that report now, I do not know. [118, College Business Administrator]

● My understanding was that they got this for free. But reading the (school newspaper), they didn’t get it for free. [127, Financial Assistant]

● You want the truth? It was because one of the vice presidents thought that we should have a new system. [132, Administrative Assistant]

Understanding, during this stage of the acceptance process, is an understanding of the underlying motivation behind the organization’s purposes for implementing the new IT (e.g., integration of all of the entities), while stories (e.g., we got the IS for free) are associated with an end user who does not understand why this IT was deployed in the organization. During this stage, there is a lack of understanding of the reason why the IT is in place.

An end user who does not achieve this understanding will be unable to obtain a broad perspective of where he or she <sup>fi</sup>ts vis-à-vis the new organizational structure. Consider the experience of the end user [124, Administrator] who claimed that the reason why the new IT was chosen was because it was the industry standard. Later, this end user recounted:

It [My frustration] is trying to <sup>fi</sup>gure out what does this mean, in terms of where does my job <sup>fi</sup>t into the process now? If everything needs to be done at the end user level and all of the reports are done at the end user level, what is my role? How do I make it to where I am still adding value to the process?

The interpretation is that the end user not only fails to understand the rationality of the IT to the organization,<sup>5</sup> but also the rationality for her job now within the new structure. One component of seeking the new role for a user’s job within an organization is the attempt to understand the new organizational processes. A user who fails in this aspect of understanding claims that the processes are so complicated [128, Financial Coordinator]. But those who succeed in understanding the underlying processes will admit, Now that I understand the process, the new system is no big deal. When I first started, I did not get the flow. It took me three months to get the flow of information [133, Executive Secretary]. The relationship between the elements of understanding is further exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the case of an end user [103, Administrative Assistant] who is struggling to do her job and also fails to understand why the IT is in place:

To do one report takes 2 to 3 hours, and I feel like an idiot. I do not end up getting any other work done. I know it's not their fault, but I do not understand why we are doing this or why I have to learn this.

This example highlights the linkage between the components of understanding – learning the core requirements for the job, why the organization deployed the IT, and the employee’s new place within the organization.

The need for understanding is one that end users admit. An end user [105, Research Clerk] who has not found the understanding admits, Maybe other people do not care or just do it, but I am a global thinker and I want to see how it fits together in the big picture. Likewise, an end user [119, Senior Secretary] who confesses to questioning others about the topic of understanding claims:

I have no idea why they quit one and started the new system. I don’t know. It was just ‘here it is, use it.’ I’m not sure if the other system wasn’t as good. I don’t know. I haven’t heard anybody say. I’ve actually been wondering why myself.

## Enabling mechanisms to move an individual past Stage 2

The role of perceived resource availability

While an end user is attempting to integrate their understanding of the IT holistically, resources must be available to allow them to complete this task successfully. While the presence of this factor does not necessarily impact the ability of an end user to pass through this stage quickly, it is the absence of resources that is crucial. An end user [127, Financial Assistant] still in the understanding phase explains:

Last year when they said it was here, so get started, you are going to training. All of that training was taking up your other time, so you ended up staying late to catch up on all of the work you missed from going to training.

The emphasis in this statement is that there is a lack of resources available to support the end user during the time required to understand the IT. While this respondent in particular (and others like her) noted that the boss was ‘supportive’ or ‘empathetic,’ there was still a perception that there were not enough resources available. Examples of other resources important to end users during this time are staff to aid them with the new organizational structure (if needed), the required technological infrastructure (e.g., hardware and software) resources, and resources to aid them in catching up on work, which would allow them to have a better experience within this stage. Some examples cited by users include:

● New staff: What I do here (at a branch campus) in a day, on the main campus, there are five or six people that do it, because they are specialized and that is all that they do. While I pay all of the PO vouchers, I deal with somebody on the main campus that only does the letter ‘C.’ I can’t fathom that. [110, Accounting Clerk, explaining how main campus has additional staff as support, when she does not]

● Infrastructure: My concerns will get resolved when we get our network upgraded. When that cost goes through, we will be fine. [131, Assistant Business Administrator]

● Resources to catch up on work: A year ago, I felt like I could catch up. Now I am cringing because I cannot do it all. [103, Administrative Assistant]

## The role of quality training

During the understanding phase, one source of information for end users is trainers. The quality of the training received at this time shapes an individual response to understanding the new technology. Here, an end user [103, Administrative Assistant] brings together the elements of understanding and links them to poor-quality training:

I hate the new information technology classes. They make me so angry! It might be a four hour class to learn a new form. There are 30 pages of instructions on how to complete one form. Plus, the instructors do not know a lot, they end up conjecturing about a lot of things that I need to know how to do for my job. I do not understand why it takes more work from me for the organization to be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. The end result is that my experiences are awful.

The link between a lack of understanding how to do the basic required job and why the organization went to the IT is impacted by a low quality of training that failed to communicate the essential elements. This is made clear by another end user [118, College Business Administrator] with a similar experience:

I do not think that their training was appropriate. Only toward the end of the initial start of the training did it get better, but we went to classes and thought, ‘what does this mean?’ I am not an idiot on a computer, but I wondered what everything had to do with me. They were talking about trees – what does a tree have to do with me? I need to be able to pay a bill. I want to know how to make a budget revision. I need to know how to create a budget. That is what I need to do. I need to know how to reconcile an account. But after getting into it for 9 or 10 months, only after employees asked how much was in their account and we told them that we could not <sup>fi</sup>gure it out or reconcile it because of the mistakes ... only then did training change.

Again, poor quality of training made it more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the user to move through the understanding phase. An end user [135, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator], who when asked directly whether she knew why the university went with this system answered, ‘No’ (and is therefore still in the understanding phase), demonstrates how quality of training can impact a user’s perspective of the ease of use of the IT:

When we did the training, they were pretty positive about the training. It looked fairly easy and I never really heard anything negative about it from the trainers. I do not know if that is their personal feeling or they were told to be positive.

This end user, despite her inability to see the big picture, does see that the IT is easy to use. Thus, as she seeks to understand the IT, ease of use is an important element during this stage, a perception linked to how well the training demonstrates this quality of the technology.

## Acceptance stage 3: assess

## Psychological state of an individual in Stage 3

During Stage 3 of the acceptance process, the end user seeks to make the decision: Do I assess the IT as being valuable to myself and to the organization? Although the user recognizes that the IT is theoretically important to the organization, he or she now begins to determine whether that expectation is realized in the new IT – are the promised bene<sup>fi</sup>ts being realized within the new IT?

At this stage, the user has achieved an understanding of the IT and is attempting to incorporate the technology into their work routines by utilizing the IT to complete tasks. Along with this integration, the user is assessing the worth of the IT to both themselves and the organization. An end user [124, Administrator] currently assessing the worth of the technology responds in a manner such as:

Yes. I think that there probably are a few things that the system can do that we were not able to do before, like being able to run reports where it is able to capture an entire department’s worth of reports and provide you with a fund equity report,<sup>6</sup> sorted by department, and you can get the whole division sorted by department. You could not do that in the old system. You could get the information probably just as quickly, but you would have to actually write it out, put it in Word – you would have to put the pieces together yourself. In the long run, that may be a big enough bene<sup>fi</sup>t. So far it is not.

The interpretation of this statement: the end user sees the potential value (i.e., the ability to gain information from the IS) and how this may be a big enough bene<sup>fi</sup>t, but so far it is not. Thus, the user is in the process of assessing whether the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts provided by the information system are valuable to both them and the organization, or, from a diffusion of innovation perspective, whether there is a relative advantage over the old IT and whether there is a demonstrable result.

There is a tension in the interpretation of the value proposition, that is, whether there is value to both the organization and the individual; nonetheless, it is the presence of these two assessments of the new IT that aids an end user in assessing the worth of the technology.

Nonetheless, not all end users are able to identify the speci<sup>fi</sup>c value that will be derived from the IS. As an example, an end user who had not yet arrived in Stage 3 [128, Financial Coordinator] when asked about the value of the new IS admitted, I do not know yet. It is still too soon to say, for me. Thus, the respondent is still assessing the worth of the technology, but has not yet made a <sup>fi</sup>nal declaration of the value of the IT.

An end user in this stage will typically admit that there is potential value in the new IT even if it has not yet been realized. In fact, 11 of our 30 users admitted that there is potential value. For example, one user [109, Administrative Assistant] admitted, ‘I think it could be (valuable) and I think it can be. I don’t think we are there yet.’ Consider the following interaction between the researcher and an end user [125, Financial Administrator]:

Researcher: ‘Do you think the university has seen the value from this?’

End user: ‘Maybe in another year or two.

Researcher: ‘But the value isn’t there yet?

End user: ‘I don’t think so. I really don’t.

The admission of the potential value of the IS can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users throughout all business units within an organization. One end user [126, Accountant] admitted that she would say that I am 99% sure that it [the technology] will be [valuable]. But it’s not yet, and another who did not see the value being currently realized stated [110, Accounting Clerk]:

I just think that there are very few people here that I think see that potential [value]. They know it’s there, but to really understand where it’s going and where it’s going to be. There are only a handful of people here who understand that.

As the end user discovers the realized value in the IT through the end of Stage 3, there comes the ability to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c examples of why the IT is valuable. Moreover, as an end user takes the steps to go through training and learning about the new technology, which is the stage of attempting to intellectually grasp the information system (Stage 2), that user will get more out of the IT (which had moved them into Stage 3). Thus, even in the minds of most end users, there is a natural progression between grasping (Stage 2) and the ability to see the value within a new IT (Stage 3).

## Enabling mechanisms to move an individual past Stage 3 The role of gaps

As an end user sees the value being realized in the IT, that is, the IT does deliver value, not just should deliver, a positive affective state will come as well. With this movement comes satisfaction. The same end user who articulated the value of production above [139] also noted:

I am also very satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. The panel allows for what it needs. All you are doing is data entry. Give it what it wants and you will get the report or queries that you need.

As previously discussed during the discussion of the construct and the linkage to satisfaction, one aspect of satisfaction is that the IT delivers what an end user needs. Thus, the end user noted that the IT delivered what was needed and as a result was satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. From a diffusion of innovation perspective, the user viewed the IT as being useful for the job.

Yet, when a respondent does not <sup>fi</sup>nd that a new IT delivers what is needed, a lack of satisfaction can be found as well. An end user [135, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator] who did not see the value in the IT also noted:

I have seen the panels and it looks like it holds a lot of information, but some of the panels, I do not need the information. It is not for me. Maybe in another department, but I need my information – not someone else’s information.

While this is an example of an end user complaining about information overload, the parallel remains clear: an end user in this stage is examining the IT for what he or she needs. If there is a gap between what the user needs and what is delivered, a lack of satisfaction is evident. Thus, to better help a user evaluate the technology positively, there should be small gaps between what the user desires and what the IT delivers, between what the user expects and what the IT delivers, and between the minimum amount that a user needs to do their job from the IT and what the IT delivers. In making this assessment, the gaps are dynamic, that is, what a user needs and expects changes as the user gains experience with using the technology, and satisfaction is the result of the alignment between expectations and performance (as espoused by Expectation Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation Theory, originally by Oliver (1980) and within the context of IT adoption by Bhattacherjee (2001)).

## The role of a responsive IT department

A responsive IT department that answers questions about the new IT can aid an end user in seeing the value and moderate the evaluating stage. Several end users caught in the evaluating stage expressed their frustration toward the development team. One end user [103, Administrative Assistant], clearly frustrated, claims, Sometimes it feels like all hell is breaking loose and no one cares. This expression of feeling toward the IT department re<sup>fl</sup>ects a non-responsive IT department, with the result being that this end user sees no value or worth in the IT.

As previously discussed, a key component of evaluating the worth is that there needs to be a reduction in the gaps. To help users through this stage, a responsive IT department can aid in the decrease in perceived gaps. One end user [115, Compensation Analyst] expressed the problems as follows:

What they are telling you when they say that is they do not want to make any adjustments at all to help the software work for you … That is a lot to do and the software is supposed to help us do that. And it can, but, now it does not help us do a lot of that.

Thus, the end user has minimum requirements, needs, and desires. A responsive IT department can aid in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling these perceived feelings and, ultimately aid IT in the marketing of the IT and its value.

## Acceptance stage 4: be given and submit

Before Stage 3, end users move through the stages in progression – the ability to enter Stage 2 depends upon the completion of Stage 1, and the ability to enter Stage 3 depends upon the completion of both Stages 1 and 2. However, at this point in the acceptance process, end users move to the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage in the process, where there are two psychological processes that are taking place: a willingness to adapt routines to what is required by the technology and surrendering to the technology. These two processes are independent of one another. The data collected from the study suggests that submission does not imply that routinization has occurred, nor does routinization require a user to have previously submitted. Rather, the two tasks represent the last stage in the acceptance process. Thus, for the sake of this discussion, while the two will be presented independently (as Stages 4a and 4b), both of these processes are occurring simultaneously within the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage of acceptance and there is no progression suggested by the designation as Stages a and b.

## Acceptance stage 4a: be given

During Stage 4a of the acceptance process, the end user is seeking to make the decision: Am I willing to fully adapt my routines to what is required by the technology? With the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts clear, the user is now determining whether he or she is willing to alter their work patterns to utilize the new IT. The user who is in the process of adapting their routines to what the IT requires does not complain or constantly look for new ways to get their job done. An end user [114, Employment Manager] who is unwilling to adapt their routines to the new IT will state something like the following:

I think that probably the new system was not the best choice, just because it requires you to work with their model and they have not adapted their model suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently to accommodate what (we) need.

The translation of this statement is that the end user is not willing to adapt their routines to the model that the IT required. After admitting her own struggle with altering her routines at work, an end user [126, Accountant] admitted:

This process requires us to change to it … (and) … I think this is a whole shift in emphasis and people need to shift their thought process and, for some people, this is a little dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, since they have been doing this for a long time and they have been doing it one way for a long time and don’t want to change how they work.

Yet, an end user who is in the process of changing (and expresses willingness to change) struggles with moving from their old conceptualizations toward new ones. Individual [131, Assistant Business Administrator] admit that they sort of forget the old IT, and this lack of resistance to change is evident in user behavior. After admitting that the new IT offers many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, an end user [117, Legal Secretary] recounts her own temptations regarding the new IT:

I still want to use sub-codes [codes in the old system] instead of account numbers [codes in the new system]. It is still hard for me. When I am talking to someone, like (a co-worker) in the business of<sup>fi</sup>ce, I will say sub-code, I mean account number. But, my brain still sees sub-code. See account number used to be number dash sub-code. Now, it is account number XXXXX. Plus all of the other codes. But I am getting used to it. It helps that I have the new system numbers under the sub-codes.

This end user has expressed her temptations with using the codes in the old IT, yet she remains willing to alter her behavior to refer to the codes used in the new IT. A requirement for adaptation is that the end user must perceive that the IT is compatible with their work:<sup>7</sup>

There were some fundamental kinds of business practices that we engage in that the new system could not manage … (and) … I just do not think that the new system is set up for the reality of (our organization).

## Acceptance stage 4b: submit

During Stage 4b of the acceptance process, the end user seeks to make the decision: Have I fully surrendered myself to the IT? This surrender to the technology means that an individual agrees with everything about the IT – they believe that it works properly and is satis<sup>fi</sup>cing<sup>8</sup> for the organization. The user has a positive affective state toward the IT and is constantly looking for new ways to use the IT within their job.

An end user who has not yet achieved this stage doubts the IT itself, with statements [104, Secretary] such as I think there are inherent problems with the program. End users who have not yet reached this stage do not believe that the IT is satisfactory, but instead feel that the IT has inherent problems (or have not yet seen the satis<sup>fi</sup>cing balance). This is in contrast to the experience of end users who are currently in this stage. These end users [116, HR Assistant] express sentiments such as:

I would not change the system at all. I am always <sup>fi</sup>nding new features that are great about the new system. One day, (a co-worker) went out. She had an emergency and did not leave her number. There was a place in the system for that, so I could get in touch with her when I needed to. That really surprised me.

In speaking with others, the experiences of users not in this stage vs those who are in the process of surrendering are different as well. Consider the following examples, both involving family members. An end user [135, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator] who is not in this stage recalls:

End user: ‘Basically, when my mother asked me about it, she said that, at her old company, she was going to a new company and they were considering buying the whole new system and I told her not to get it.’

Researcher: ‘Did they end up getting it?’

End user: ‘I do not know. She did not take the job offer, but I told her not to get it. All of my frustrations when I came home from (my previous job) and the time it took to do the payroll, I could have done it on paper a lot faster. I am hoping that they worked it out now. From what I understand it was because of the large number of employees. But still, that was in one district. This is a university. I am assuming that it is a lot larger, so I am pretty sure they have problems here.’

Therefore, an end user who is not in this stage encouraged her mother not to take a job simply because it would require using the new IT. While the individual who is not in this stage blames the software, the individuals who are in this stage hold the individual user responsible for failing to take the necessary steps to move through the acceptance process.

Any new discoveries made by users in this stage are further proof that the IT is the most appropriate IT for the organization. In addition, if any questions about the IT arise, then the user has become so submitted that he or she does not question the technology; instead, the individual questions either the users of the IT or the processes embedded in the IT. An individual [135, Of<sup>fi</sup>ce Coordinator] not in this stage would question another individual who has surrendered to the IT:

I would want to know their [people who have totally accepted the new system] experience and why they would consider accepting it 100%. If they have a good story, I want to hear it.

In contrast, an individual who is in the process of surrendering [112, Asst Dir, Division of HR] would respond by saying:

When people are complaining about it [the new system], I try to point out to them what about their job in the past that they liked and what did the old system do to help them. I’m not saying I’m evangelical, but I understand the change and the bigger picture, so I know we have to stop bitching and solve the problem and move on. If not, you have to leave. And if we continue to bitch and complain, we are never going to get higher performance. Either buy in or get out.

This quote demonstrates the balance between satisfaction and sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing, or the satis<sup>fi</sup>cing impact of the IT. A result of continued surrender is that the individual then sees the IT as a change agent within the organization, crediting the IT as the source of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts beyond what the IT can necessarily create. The same end user [112, Asst Dir, Division of HR] who pointed out to others about their old job (quoted above) also noted:

We are too complex of an organization, mainly because we make it so. It doesn’t have to be as complex as we make it, but it is. And I think that this new system will make us look at how we do things … So whenever you make a change like that, you have to look at your systems to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to improve your systems, and I think it makes us a better organization because of it.

This user’s surrender is to the point where the IT itself then becomes the perceived basis for other positive outcomes.

However, an individual who has not yet come to the point of surrender (and still resides in a previous stage of the acceptance process) will respond in a way such as:

I don’t like that [the new system] at all. And I don’t think that is going to change. It has probably tripled or more the amount of work necessary to get the job done. And I don’t see how any of those steps can be cut out.

An end user who has seen the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and has surrendered to the technology is so convinced about the positive aspects of the IT that the individual becomes an apostle to others, touting how wonderful the IT is within the organization and how everyone should love it. Individuals who are in the process of surrendering will say [112, Asst Dir, Division of HR], I’m not a new system person, but I tried to really sell the new system because I think it’s a great tool … it will make everybody’s life easier, while others who have not surrendered remark [132, Administrative Assistant], I know people though that … love it [the new system]. But that’s not me.

An end user who is in the process of surrendering knows how to use the IT beyond its intended use. These users have found new and innovative ways to use the IT, while others are still using the IT in a surface way. One end user, commenting on her use of the new IT, remarked [109, Administrative Assistant], The way I know is the way I was told. I am not able to manipulate it yet. End users who have not yet surrendered are still ‘manipulating’ the IT, while others have surrendered to the technology and are <sup>fi</sup>nding new features.

An end user who has not yet reached this stage will express how the process of their adaptation will occur simultaneously with the surrender to the technology. An end user [124, Administrator], commenting on her lack of adaptation to what was required by the new IT, stated:

So, there may come a point in which we are so well adapted to the day to day stuff that we are able to appreciate what the system is capable of doing when you can drill down into the more minute aspects of the system.

The implication: as I adapt my routines to the new technology, then I will simultaneously be able to deeply use the IT. This then serves as the link between Stages 4a and 4b. Through continued adaptation, end users are able to make the IT a normal and routine part of their lives. It is only as the individual is achieving this routinization that the user is able to see the deeper uses of the IT and continue surrendering to this new way of life within the organization. Yet, some users still resist the need to adapt to the new routines. An end user (in Stage 3), when asked whether she was willing to begin learning more ways to use the IT, remarked [110, Accounting Clerk]:

I have a problem with the wage and learning more here. I think if I get paid better for what I do. I am at a point now, where, for what I am making, I am doing all that I am going to do. Even though that is probably a bitter way of looking at it and I hear some things, I do not see the actions taking place.

The experience of this end user exempli<sup>fi</sup>es an end user who is not submitting, not in a positive affective state, not willing to be subjugated to the new way of organizational life, and unable to see the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that derive from use of the IT. However, if an end user is in this stage, the individual has now achieved all of the objectives of the IT and is faithfully using it, devoted to it, can see the value, is now satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, has routinized, and is using the IT deeply.

It is important to note that the submission outlined above does not involve an individual being subjugated. Instead, it is an individual’s choice to surrender. Thus, the user re-constitutes their environment, and the individual proactively embraces the technology to make the change happen.

The adapting and submitting stage is thus the <sup>fi</sup>nal stage in the acceptance process. This process-based view proposes that this stage is the optimal place for individuals to arrive and that there are no stages beyond this. Thus, users who continue to engage in actions consistent with the adapting and submitting stage are constantly looking for deeper ways to engage the IT within the organization. The more an individual submits to the IT, the deeper the IT becomes embedded into the work-<sup>fl</sup>ow of the organization and begins to control the actions that the organization will take.

## Stages, infusion, routinization, and impact

Our work was motivated by two gaps in the literature, namely, the lack of a process model of acceptance and a limited ability to explain the underlying psychological processes that occur as infusion and routinization unfold over time. Thus, we have provided empirical support for our stage model and will now turn to a discussion of how individuals in each stage differ in their own levels of infusion and routinization. In so doing, we will highlight how this new approach can provide the theoretical bridge to explain IT-enabled performance.

We began by providing the transcripts for all of the interviews to two independent judges – both MBA students enrolled in different universities in the Southeastern United States. Both judges were given the full transcripts for all of the interviews as well as Table 3, highlighting the predominant question, activities, and conditions listed above. Each judge independently reviewed the transcripts to determine the stage for each respondent in the study. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of review, the judges agreed on the stage for the respondent for 13 of the 30 respondents. The judges were then provided with both sets of reviews for the unresolved subjects and again independently reviewed the transcripts to determine the stage for each unresolved subject in the study. In the second round of review, the judges agreed on 7 of the 17. The judges then met to resolve the remaining 10 stage assessments.<sup>9</sup> The results of the assessments are shown in Table 5.

## Discussion

In this paper, we have outlined a new process model of IT acceptance and demonstrated how each stage impacts the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of the individual and impacts their overall job performance. We have examined this process through the lens of automaticity, arguing that each stage represents a decision made by the individual.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension, receive, refers to the instinctual response of end users when they <sup>fi</sup>rst hear about a new technology. This research posits that upon hearing about a new technology a user will instinctually desire to reject the new technology. Thus, following a user’s instinctual response to the new technology, the next cognitive structure that is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant is the affect component of attitude. The second stage, understand, involves the drive to comprehend the functionality of the IS. It represents the affect component of attitude. The affect component of attitude, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, is feelings, emotions, or drives associated with an attitude object. The next stage, assess, represents the evaluative component of attitude. The evaluative component of attitude, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, is judgments toward the object. This is an analog to the judgments implicit in the construct of assessing the IT.

## Evaluative Attitude Emergent belief

The process-based view assumes a positivist, rational, deterministic view of the individual. This is a technological deterministic view – in other words, that the individual must adapt to the technology. We believe that this view is limited, however, to organizational contexts within which the use of the technology is mandatory for the completion of work<sup>fl</sup>ow. Without empirical evidence, we cannot state that the same process would unfold for technologies that are within volitional, non-enterprise contexts. However, in situations where an individual is mandated to use an IT, we have discussed a process in which an individual moves from initial use of the technology to the adaptation of their psychological processes toward the ability of that user to achieve higher levels of productivity (along with the triggering mechanisms for encouraging the movement through the stages).

While we have demonstrated a relationship between the stages and three outcomes and the trend line associated with the relationship, we did have outliers with our users, that is, some had achieved the outcomes in earlier stages, while others lagged. This result is consistent with the assumptions of process models (Markus and Robey, 1988), namely, that outcomes may not appear (even when conditions are present). What this suggests to us is that there are further mechanisms that are operating beyond what we have found in our results. Our results point to this progression through stages, but there is more work to be done in this area.

What our results suggest is that (a) there are stages that a user progresses through when provided with a new IT and (b) there is a dynamic progression in place that can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by managerial levels and facilitating conditions. Our approach provided full support for (a), yet only partial support for (b). That is, the presence of the outliers is suggestive of additional areas for future researchers to build upon this model and identify other variables and management approaches that can be used to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the rate of progression and the outcomes that derive from the progression.

Furthermore, the current model operates outside of the economic rationality that underpins TAM. We have started from this position that automaticity plays a key role in the process of acceptance. Yet, the relationship between the automatic processes that we have examined and the TAM/ UTAUT constructs remains left open for future work. We suggest that this approach can be both a challenge to and a compliment to existing work in the adoption area.

We challenge others to examine the economic rationality of TAM within the context of time. While TAM has studied the aspect of time in the past, time was limited to a focus upon the time of the study relative to the time of the implementation of the new IT. We seek to challenge this notion of time by suggesting that the traditional TAM/UTAUT constructs might operate in a non-linear relationship that is predicated upon the user’s level of acceptance (which might or might not be related to the time relative to deployment). We further suggest that the traditional TAM/UTAUT constructs have reached the limits of their explanatory power in examining use. A new approach that can explain the psychological underpinnings of acceptance would be more useful in studying productivity outcomes.

As a complement, our work can provide a useful framework to understand the decision making of the user and, as such, provides a lens that can be used by future researchers to examine the relationship between the decisions and the perceptions. Is there a bi-lateral relationship between the two? Do the decisions in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions or do the perceptions in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decisions? It is the inter-relationship between the psychological decision making of automaticity and the economic rationality of TAM that provides an open forum for future work.

Furthermore, the examination of user behavior from the perspective of automaticity opens up future research as well. Our work provides support for the fact that, over time, the decisions made by end users change. Given the emergent focus upon habit and the role of habit, we urge researchers to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon the difference between habit and automaticity (we point readers to Schwarz and Chin, 2007 for a deeper discussion of the distinction) and to examine the cognitive effort that changes with deeper levels of acceptance. While concepts such as mindlessness and habit are similar in nature to automaticity, there are deeper psychological structures in place than our previous models have accounted for, and we urge others to continue with this line of work. We also recognize that a focus upon decision making comes at the expense of models of emotion, and we urge others to examine this relationship.

Finally, in integrating the organizational context as trigger ing mechanisms, we are providing the foundation for other researchers to begin examining how change management, project management, and risk management in<sup>fl</sup>uence how end users perceive technology over time. We encourage other researchers to move beyond this initial, rationalistic view of IT change to begin offering solutions on how to deal with the dysfunctional aspects of change and how to better deploy IT systems to move users systematically through the user accep tance process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, while we have articulated a number of enabling mechanisms that IT management can control, we urge other researchers to discover new approaches that can be used to enable individuals to progress through the model. In the years since the call for the IT artifact, there has been an increased focus upon studying how the characteristics of a technology in<sup>fl</sup>uence adoption; however, we posit that we have neglected to understand the impact of organizational aspects upon IT adoption.

Our work, however, does not come without limitations. First, we have collected data within one organization (speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally a university) that deployed an ERP. While narratives argue that generalizability is focused upon the experiences, we recognize that the context of the university and the IT (the ERP) may have introduced biases into our data. Second, we have viewed our data through the lens of automaticity and decision making. An alternative lens could similarly be applied to our data to uncover additional insights into the process of end user behavior. Third, the respondents who participated in the study were self-selected; this self-selection could have introduced a bias from the respondent in terms of their motivation for participation.

The process model proposed here is just the beginning. We are not arguing that this is a comprehensive, all-encompassing solution to the problems facing organizations in achieving IT value from deployed systems (Lucas et al., 2007). Rather, this is an initial starting point from which to build so that we can begin building theories that can better predict the individual and organizational level impacts that our colleagues-inpractice seek. However, by focusing upon a process-based view of acceptance, we highlight that this shift in thinking represents an opportunity to integrate other research domains into our current thinking about technology acceptance and usage.

For our colleagues-in-practice, we have structured a process model that depicts the changes in users over time. These changes highlight the need for different approaches depending upon the progression of the users, recognizing that the questions asked by users will change. For change management approaches, this means that the tactics and techniques must change with the alteration of the decisions of the users and must be inclusive of the <sup>fi</sup>ve questions asked by users. We argue that change management approaches that neglect to determine the underlying motivation for the change, the value of the change, an understanding of the implications of the change for the organization, and the corresponding resources and enabling mechanisms will struggle to achieve the value that is being sought with the deployment.

In conclusion, we have sought to overcome two limitations within the literature: (a) a lack of a model of acceptance that can explain IT impacts and (b) a lack of a process model of IT acceptance. Relying upon qualitative data, we have positioned the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of acceptance proposed by Schwarz and Chin (2007) in a process model that argues that end users <sup>fl</sup>ow through a cognitive process beginning when they <sup>fi</sup>rst hear about a technology. Moreover, we have demonstrated the impact of these acceptance stages on important impacts such as routinization, infusion, and overall job performance. Therefore, we have proposed an alternative lens with which to understand the phenomenon and outcomes of IT acceptance.

## Appendix A

This appendix serves as support for the narrative approach used for the study, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the guided introspection narrative approach. Utilizing the guided introspection narrative approach assumes that the researcher already possesses preconceptions about the phenomenon of study, whether through their own experiences or a literature review. However, pre-understanding ‘enables rather than constrains interpretation (Arnold and Fischer, 1994). These pre-understandings allow the researcher to offer suggestions to the subject to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon during the research conversation. The subject continues to offer insights and re<sup>fl</sup>ections until a shared understanding of the topic of study is achieved between the researcher and the subject (Stern et al., 1998). We will now discuss the considerations that should be made when using a narrative approach.

## Subjects in a narrative study

When participating in a narrative interview, the subjects themselves must have direct experience with the phenomenon being studied (in this case, a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology) and be able to express their feelings about this topic of study. Other considerations about the subjects include (Polkinghorne, 1989):

● Number of subjects: the appropriate number ranges from 3 (minimum) to 30 (mid-range) to 300 (maximum)

● There is no requirement for random selection; the subjects must have a variety of experiences, but not necessarily be randomly selected<sup>10</sup>

● For generalizability, the focus is not upon population characteristics but rather on the narrative itself. If others can relate to the story, then the sample is generalizable<sup>11</sup>

For this study, 30 individuals were interviewed, thus providing an adequate number of subjects.

## Interviews in a narrative study

Interviews conducted using the narrative approach are openended and focus on the experiences of the interviewee. In a narrative study, questions are generated during ‘self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the topic to be investigated (which) allow the interviewer to uncover prima facie dimensions for exploration’ (Colaizzi, 1978). The objective of these questions is to provide a structure through which to guide the respondent so as to guide the interviewee to provide their story. Thus, questions were created a priori using the <sup>fi</sup>ve decisions of acceptance for the subject to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon.<sup>12</sup>

The objective of the study was to develop an understanding of the process of acceptance for the users, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally related to their experience with the new ERP system. To uncover this information, the interview incorporated topics including the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions; job history and pro<sup>fi</sup>le; and perceptions, beliefs, and feelings toward the new ERP system. The interviews were completed in a semi-structured format, thus allowing the researcher to guide the subjects through their explanation of their experiences with the acceptance process.<sup>13</sup> Next, we will discuss the methods employed to analyze the data.

## Analyzing the narrative data

To prepare the data to be analyzed, the approximately 32 h of interviews were transcribed by the <sup>fi</sup>rst researcher. Following the transcription, the researcher utilized a methodology to analyze the data across the subjects and across the dimensions. We will now present a discussion of the steps undertaken to complete the analysis.

First, to create a process-based view of IT acceptance, a discussion of the levels of structure in narrative data must <sup>fi</sup>rst be considered. According to Pentland (1999), all narratives have four levels of structure. At the base level is the text itself, which includes the story told by the narrator during the interview. In this case, the base level would include the actual text of the interview conducted by the researcher. The next level above the base is the story. If the researcher’s questions are stripped from the text and the quotations are pieced together, then this becomes the narrator’s story from their point of view in their own words. The next level is the fabula, or in this case, a generic description of the user’s acceptance process. Therefore, if the story is taken out of quotations and is told recounting speci<sup>fi</sup>c events and perspectives, then this becomes the fabula. At the <sup>fi</sup>nal level is the generating mechanism, or the underlying structures. If all the users are analyzed across all subjects, then an overall story of the acceptance process can be formulated. The relationship between these four levels including relevant examples is shown in Figure A1.

In addition to the cross-subject analysis at the fabula level (in order to uncover the generating mechanisms), each of the dimensions was analyzed across subjects. This step was undertaken in order to validate the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions of Schwarz and Chin (2007) and ensure that what the researchers had proposed could be uncovered in empirical data. As recommended by Glaser and Strauss (1967), a three-step process was followed.

Step 1 – Initial analysis of transcripts: The transcript was carefully read by the <sup>fi</sup>rst researcher, and responses were pooled across subjects. Answers that contained multiple thoughts were separated into their component parts. After pooling the responses, the result was 4206 individual quotes that were used as the basis for Step 2.

Step 2 – Formal interpretation of transcripts: During this step, the interview transcripts for the subjects were read in a more structured fashion across subjects. The reading of the transcripts was intended to ensure that there would be a consistent interpretation both across and within subjects using a common set of de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the dimensions of acceptance (drawn from Schwarz and Chin, 2007) to categorize the quotes into their appropriate dimension.

Step 3 – Analysis of the formal interpretations: After studying the interviews, the researcher conducted a cross-subject analysis. The researcher looked across the subjects to ensure that there was a common interpretation. The researcher then looked within each subject to determine the number of dimensions that the individual subject exhibited, based on whether or not the individual demonstrated behavior consistent with each particular dimension.

Together, Steps 2 and 3 enabled the researcher to decontextualize interviewee comments and then recontextualize them (Tesch, 1990). In other words, these steps enabled the researcher to take the interviewee comments out of their original context (i.e., an interview) and present them in a different context. Then, the text level of data was analyzed across subjects to see whether common patterns emerged, e.g. If a respondent had two dimensions that were displayed, did this respondent exhibit the same patterns or perceptions as another individual who also demonstrated two state of being dimensions? The analysis at the text level revealed that individuals display common patterns of acceptance. Thus, the next step involved moving the data up to the story level.

At the story level, each of the individuals was pro<sup>fi</sup>led and, using the individual words of the respondent, stories were created across all subjects. Thus, the data was recontextualized into a process timeframe, so that the researcher could deter mine when speci<sup>fi</sup>c acceptance behavior emerged. Again, commonalities were found across the subjects, which allowed the researcher to move to the next level – fabula.

At the fabula level of analysis, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c quotations were utilized to develop generalized characteristics of individuals, similar to Weber’s concept of ideal types. These fabulas would not employ speci<sup>fi</sup>c words or phrases that the individual used to describe their behavior. Rather, generalizations were made to develop ideal types of individuals.

Finally, the fabulas were analyzed across all subjects, which produced a story of the process of IT acceptance. In the section below, the story of individual-level IT acceptance will be presented; this section will demonstrate the generating mechanism level of analysis, because it includes exemplar comments from many of the respondents to illuminate the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the text.

In Appendix B, we have outlined how the <sup>fi</sup>rst author analyzed the interviews from a cross-subject and crossdimension perspective.

Q: Do you see the value to the organization for this?

A: I don’t personally see it, because I don’t see the <sup>fi</sup>nance end of it on the auditing side. I’ve been told by the girls in <sup>fi</sup>nance that, on the auditing side, it really goes into detail and does better reports. Each transcription was coded by the <sup>fi</sup>rst researcher. Each individual thought in the interview was coded using the <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions using the three-step process outlined by Glaser and Strauss (1967).
