# Both global and local: ICTs and joint ventures in China

Chris Westrup & Wei Liu

Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, UK, email: Chris.Westrup@manchester.ac.uk

Abstract. The expansion of global firms, supported by extensive information and communication technology (ICT) systems, is frequently seen as leading to the spread of best practices to different regions of the world. Some argue that these practices are bound up with the national origins of these firms, while others propose that local circumstances in specific economies are of prime importance. According to the first perspective, global firms will use ICTs in China that reflect their national origins, but the second approach emphasizes the importance of the Chinese local context in how and what ICTs are used. Based on evidence from two case studies of global UK multinationals with Chinese joint ventures, this paper argues that the ICTs used by these companies do relate to their national roots and that China is seen by them as a very unusual setting that is treated as a special case. For example, one company was prepared to roll out a global enterprise planning system elsewhere but refused to implement it in China, while the second company cordoned off their ICT systems in China from their other, more global systems. Cultural frames of reference are advanced as an important explanation of differences in the usage of ICTs in China. Building on these ideas, this paper argues that how difference is recognized, accommodated and (re)affirmed is significant for the performance of formal business processes. Formal and ICTenabled systems ‘work’ by being embedded in changing Chinese cultural practices such as guanxi, though this is frequently not recognized by Western managers. While the global nature of global firms may be overplayed, the local arrangements of joint ventures are very important in how ICT systems are implemented in which the embedding and disembedding require an understanding of sited cultural practices that underpin the performance of formal systems. What is at stake is how global firms and their ICT systems are able to adapt to Chinese circumstances, and, in the longer term, how Chinese joint venture partners, often previously state-owned enterprises, adapt to different forms of control mediated by ICTs.

Keywords: China, ICTs, joint ventures, embedding, guanx

## INTRODUCTION

China has become the largest recipient of foreign direct investment (FDI) in the ‘developing world and has witnessed rapid growth of FDI over a number of years (UNCTAD, 2006). In 2001, China’s accession to the World Trade Organization (WTO) signified a move towards closer integration of China’s economy with business practices elsewhere. China, perhaps more than any other non-Western country, is heir to a rich, sophisticated and complex history whose material and cultural expressions have often surpassed those of the Wes (see Needham et al., 2000). Her recent past of central planning, Cultural Revolution and an opening up to Western trade while committed to communist governance makes China a very different environment for Western business (see Whitley, 1992). Some writers suggest that China is likely to provide new approaches to business practice and theory rather than simply be another example of how Western business practice is embedded in a foreign environment (see Tsui, 2006). For example, the Chinese use of information has been argued by Martinsons & Westwood (1997) to follow Chinese psychological preferences for harmony, order and authority drawn from a long tradition of Daoist and especially Confucian thought. Joint ventures (JVs) between Western and Chinese firms provide an interesting context in which these conceptions of business practice, information use and the role of information systems rub against each other. A body of literature suggests that Western designed information systems, such as enterprise planning (ERP) systems, embody Western business practice and a misfit between these systems and ‘traditional Chinese management systems is to be found (Soh et al., 2000; Martinsons, 2004, p. 68; cf. He, 2004). This paper seeks to contribute to this literature using two case studies of UK–Chinese JVs to show a complex and dynamic interaction between Western managers, their imposition of information systems and procedures, and Chinese managers and workers who retain a web of relationships while often seeking to use new information systems. In one example, this leads to Western managers denying access to ERP systems requested by Chinese managers. The paper uses the lens of situated business practice to show that Chinese culturally sited practices are necessary, yet often not recognized by Western managers as resources to enable formal business processes to be performed. How Western procedures can both be embedded within local business activities and, equally, disembedded from these practices, makes these systems ‘work’ and shows how a dynamic accommodation is taking place between Western and Chinese business practices.

The paper begins by discussing the contribution of cultural arguments on Chinese information systems and then considers the relevance of situated business practices and notions of embedding and disembedding in analysing changing information use. A review of the literature on managing JVs in China suggests the importance of information systems and other noncapital resources in these processes, though limited evidence exists on how such processes operate. This paves the way to a discussion of the research on two UK–Chinese JVs and the themes that can be drawn from these studies.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS AND CHINA

In discussions on globalization, an important literature identifies multinational companies as rational actors seeking to maximize profitability in markets driven by competition (see Dunning, 2001). This neo-liberal argument contends that the role of nation states is diminishing and that multinational companies are drivers creating a more homogeneous global economic system. Information systems, according to this analysis, are key components of globalization and their use in non-Western contexts leads to the adoption of global forms of work practice. A second strand of literature, drawing on insights from economic sociology and organization theory, proposes that economic activity is always embedded in social systems that enable them to work (see Whitley, 1992). It follows that divergent business systems are to be expected and are socially constructed across time and space. An important question arising from this approach is how different ways of managing and doing business are made to work in diverse places. Information systems in turn both reflect and reinforce different ways of managing, and a close analysis of how these activities are performed is central to understanding how the processes of globalization operate. This institutional turn has close associations with other forms of analysis that recognize and represent diversity of which perhaps the most influential explores the importance of culture in information systems development and use in both developed and developing countries (Walsham, 2001; Avgerou, 2002). This approach is widely used in analysing information systems in China.

How to identify a culture is an interesting question (see Kuper, 1999). One strand, exemplified by the work of Hofstede, uses responses to standard questionnaires. As is well known Hofstede identified four cultural dimensions – power distance, individualism, masculinity and uncertainty avoidance, and a fifth (long-term orientation) was added for East Asian countries drawing on the work of Bond et al. (Chinese Cultural Connection, 1987; Hofstede & Hofstede, 2005). The results for China show a high power distance score, low individualism, an average masculinity score, a low uncertainty avoidance score and a high long-term orientation score. Hofstede’s interpretation is that China tolerates high inequalities of power and wealth; reinforces reliance on family and extended networks; is able to accept change and risk; and values long-term commitment. A second strand focuses on the importance of traditional psychological characteristics of different societies, which often are seen to stem from indigenous belief systems. In China, stress is placed on the importance of Confucian or Daoist values in explaining behaviour (see Redding, 1990; Bond, 1991).

Drawing on these arguments, Martinsons & Westwood (1997) (see also Martinsons & Hempel. 1995) identify key Chinese cultural characteristics and their effects on the use of management information systems. The preference of Chinese managers for personal information leads to reliance on informal information; centralized decision-making reduces information exchange between managers; and high context communication; and intuitive decision-making reduces the need for encoded information. Martinsons & Westwood (1997) draw attention to ‘distinctively Chinese information management practices’ and argue for a more sophisticated and open-minded analysis of a Chinese approach to doing business and managing information. Other work builds on this approach (Davison & Martinsons, 2002;

Burrows et al., 2005; Davison et al., 2005; Martinsons, 2004; 2005) and has produced a variety of findings. Some implications from this work include the suggestion that technologies and social solutions must be developed together so as to respect cultural values and traditions (Davison & Martinsons, 2002); an awareness that knowledge management techniques are based more on relationships than on rules; on context rather than on processes (Burrows et al., 2005); how a neglect of cultural preferences in IT-enabled process-based change led to failure of a change effort in Hong Kong (Davison & Martinsons, 2002); and how the adoption of Western business practices are problematic in Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and will lead either to formalization and standardization or to continued reliance on persona relationships to conduct business (Martinsons, 2004).

Moving to other institutional accounts, an important literature identifies the distinctiveness of a Chinese business system (see Whitley, 1992; Boisot & Child, 1999). Boisot & Child, 1999, for example, characterize China as having a high cognitive complexity caused by ambiguity in where and how power relations are exercised, the presence of multiple governance systems and moderate levels of clan based relational complexity. These cultural and institutiona analyses of Chinese business and information system use identify frames of reference but they neither describe processes and practices directly nor describe how cultural and/or institutional differences are (re)produced through the continuing actions of people, technologies and their inter-relationships. Granovetter (1985), for example, argues that economic theory tends to ignore social influences and develops ‘undersocialized’ accounts while an opposite tendency constructs ‘oversocialized’ accounts in which human agency operates according to consensually developed norms and values which are internalized through socialization. The problem with the latter is that once we know an individual’s social class or culture ‘everything else in behaviour is automatic since they are so well socialized’ (ibid, p. 486). A salient issue becomes one of investigating how members and ongoing processes shape and are reshaped by culture (ibid). One important domain to investigate these activities is the use of information and communication technologies (ICTs) especially in diverse cultural settings.

Drawing on Granovetter (1985), economic activity is not an abstraction but works by being embedded in a social setting. With the advent of ICT systems designed to enact business processes, the coordination of spatially different sites is made easier by disembedding socia relations in one location and re-embedding them elsewhere (Giddens, 1991, pp. 20–21). These so-called ‘expert systems’ in which trust resides in the impersonal expertise of the system stretch social relations and rely on trust for their routine workings. Giddens focuses on what he calls late modernity and develops, in the context of this paper, an interesting characterization of how Western business processes can be conceptualized. What is less clear is how these Western processes are re-embedded in different social settings such as China and how they interact with existing, non-Western, social and cultural practices such as guanxi in which, by definition, they must be embedded if they are to operate successfully.

An important feature of Chinese business practice is the presence of business networks based on personal relationships or guanxi. Following Kipnis (1997) and Yang (2002), guanxi is seen as arising from the production and reproduction of human relations; as Yang (2002, p. 459) puts it, guanxi is ‘a repertoire of cultural patterns and resources which are continuously transformed in their adaptation, to, as well as shaping of, new social institutions and structures and by the particular Chinese experience of globalisation’ (emphasis added). Guanxi is to be found both in mainland China and more widely in countries in East Asia but is not restricted to the Chinese (Yeung, 2000). Put more generally, Yeung argues that cooperative relationships in Chinese business systems are largely embedded in personal business relationships. In contrast, cooperative relationships in the West are based on firm specific business strategies (ibid, p. 193). For example, Fukuyama (1995) identifies a difference between Western societies, which tend to have extensive sociability outside of the family, such as within institutions and a trust in what Giddens calls ‘expert systems’, and Chinese society, in which trust is limited to networks based on familial relationships. Of course, the existence of cooperative networks within companies has been recognized and exploited by Western companies seeking to develop business cultures (see, for example, Deal & Kennedy, 1982), as has the importance of relationships with others outside the company – for example, customers (see Treacy & Wiersema, 1995). The use of workarounds and other unexpected practices to enable formal information systems to work shows that cooperative relationships are necessary within Western companies (Knox et al., 2007). In China, guanxi is necessary in the absence of institutions that sanction or provide incentives for cooperative behaviour (see Kipnis, 1997). Therefore, for Western companies with JVs in China, key questions are how can they create systems that encourage cooperative behaviour, which enable measurement and control to be exercised, and how far are they prepared to recognize and seek to interact within personalized cooperative relationships characterized by guanxi?

## International JVs, control and information

There has been a rapid increase in FDI between the UK and China since 1979 and the beginning of the Chinese ‘open door’ policy. Most of this took the form of JVs: some 66% of FDI by 2000 (Li et al., 2005) with overall FDI investment in 2005 at US\$108 303 million (UNCTAD, 2006, p. 301). Dunning (2001) proposes there are four motivations for Western companies to engage in JVs: to increase market share, to improve efficiency, to obtain strategic assets and to gain resources. Li et al. (2005) argue that JV behaviour for UK/Chinese JVs has been strongly influenced by Chinese government regulations in a range of areas, though China’s entry into the WTO in 2001 is changing government policy (see, for example, Panitchpakdi & Clifford, 2002). How to control JVs in China is an important issue for UK companies according to our research informants. Drawing on the results of a survey, Child & Yan (2003) argue that strategic control of UK–Chinese JVs is obtained through a majority equity stake but that operational control is to be gained by investing in non-capital resources such as technical know-how, systems and training that create personal links and allow the parent company to learn from the JV. Subsequently, Yan & Child (2004, p. 168) propose that ‘[a] consistent and well-designed information reporting system operated through the JV management functions can facilitate low-cost knowledge transfer between partners . . .’ They suggest that Chinese management use data as a power resource rather than as a basis for information-led decisionmaking. Support for the importance of partner non-capital resources comes from a statistical study of US–Chinese JVs by Chalos & O’Connor (2004). They, however, reject the importance of a majority equity share as a determinant of control. Finally, Li et al. (2001, p. 128) propose that the level of technological resources in a JV is important and may be of greater relevance than culture. In their statistical comparison of JVs in China, they found less successfu outcomes for Western–Chinese JVs than Japanese–Chinese JVs. However, this difference disappeared when both samples were corrected to have similar technological resources.

In short, this literature is divided on the usefulness of equity in determining control in JVs in China and suggests that it is non-capital resources – such as the exchange of technologies, the development of information systems for reporting and local control, and personal relationships – that are important. The problem here is that because of reliance on statistically driven analysis, the evidence is lacking on how these processes take place and whether they are of significance in specific instances.

For Western companies in JVs in China, an important issue is how much they introduce emerging standard business practices based around business reporting requirements and enterprise systems such as ERPs, and how much they recognize that they have to embed the JV in Chinese business practices. Boisot & Child (1999) position this issue as a choice between reducing cognitive complexity through imposing routines and standards on JVs and absorbing complexity through entering into extensive relationships with other Chinese interests. JVs must exhibit both approaches: Chinese partners bring with them a mosaic of relationships, some of which may help embed the JV while foreign partners bring technologies, expertise and standardized business systems. However, as raised earlier, the interaction between standardized systems and local business practices also takes place within the JV as companies seek to embed standard systems and existing relationships adapt to a changing situation.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

The thrust of this paper is to investigate how formal business processes, often enacted with ICTs, ‘work’ and are accommodated by being embedded in a distinctively Chinese cultural and business environment. Observation and informal/formal interviewing are important features of this research. The theoretical stance is informed by an evolving literature on the relationship between the global and the local, which seeks to investigate processes of globalization though the development of standards, technologies and other techniques (see Burawoy et al., 2000; Dicken et al., 2001; Giddens, 1991 and Granovetter, 1985). The research reported here focuses on two UK-based multinational companies and their Chinese JVs, and was part of a wider study investigating three multinationals (see Liu, 2004). For both companies, contact was made by their chief accountant in response to a speculative letter sent to 50 companies with UK–Chinese JVs. In Recit, access was given with limited conditions, whereas in Flume, the chief accountant expressed concern about how Chinese culture and politics were affecting the productivity in their JVs in China and asked for guidance on these issues on completion of the research as a condition of access. After the companies gave their consent, interviews and site visits began in the UK over a period of 3 months in 2001. These were followed by a 3-month visit to China by one of the authors where he stayed for 4 weeks in two of Recit’s JVs in Southern China and for 1 week in Flume’s JV in Southwest China. In total, 58 formal interviews were conducted in mainland China and numerous informal discussions took place within the organization, and at other times, both with company members and with local people. Flume and Recit have regional offices in Hong Kong, which were also visited. The research took place in 2001 and 2002. Subsequently, a large study of the enterprise systems and organization in Flume in the UK and Europe was undertaken by the second author from 2003 to 2005 with a team of three other researchers (see Westrup et al., 2006) Repeat interviews of either the same individuals in the UK or their equivalent post-holder were undertaken. In the UK, interviews were tape-recorded, transcribed and analysed, but in China, extensive note taking was done instead because informants were unhappy about having their conversations recorded. One of the authors is a Chinese national and he was able to interview in Mandarin or English as was appropriate. The structure of interviews tended to be open ended, though questioning was guided and shaped by the researchers’ developing views on the research.

This body of evidence is best described as exploratory case studies, with some ethnographic elements in China, of the control and coordination of two UK–Chinese JVs that use ICTs and other approaches. It seeks to provide an analysis of processes and practices by which data and information is recognized as such, and how that information circulates and is transformed within the JVs and at these companies’ headquarters. Observation and simply spending time with people at and outside work were important in these cases as we sought to observe how things were done and tried to avoid some of problems identified by Tsui (2006, pp. 9–10) of traditional research instruments in a Chinese environment. The study is limited by the absence of a longitudinal engagement in China, though this is not the case of Flume in Europe. The next section discusses these two cases.

## Two JVs

## Flume

Flume is a glass manufacturer that started investing in China in 1983, establishing a JV in Shanghai with a 12.5% share of equity. This JV was profitable and in the mid-1990s, Flume invested in three new JVs: one in Jilin Province in Northern China (N), one in Hubei Province in Central China (C) and one in Guangxi Province in Southwest China (SW). Each JV had three partners; Flume (with a 40–60% stake), the Shanghai JV and a local partner. In each JV, major new production equipment was installed. For Flume, the main reasons for their presence in China were market seeking and building up a strategic asset rather than seeking efficiency (see Dunning, 2001). Each JV has a separate management board with representation from all partners. Many senior managers in Flume were surprised that their Chinese partners appeared to be satisfied with occasional visits to the JVs and did not require any formal reporting. Flume, on the other hand, sought a formal reporting structure from the outset, which was created by a combination of an expatriate manager in each JV for 2-3 vears and then a production manager and a management accountant shuttling between the three JVs on a monthly basis from their regional office in Hong Kong. The Chinese manager in SW said that ‘Flume wanted to manage the JV at a distance by setting up parameters and measuring performance’. Flume management in the UK also agreed with this view and wished to manage Flume at minima cost using global company standards without using their ERP systems. As a Western manager commented, ‘one reason that Western companies sometimes fail in China is that the cost o expatriates is so great, it’s an absolute swamp’. In SW, the Chinese workforce was placed on 1 year rolling contracts and they complained that they did not consider the company as theirs. As one worker commented, ‘employees do not want to give a lot of innovative ideas because they think that the company is not mine’. This grievance was compounded by the lack of provision of company housing. Flume introduced quality standards, such as IS9002, which required written records. Workers and managers alike have been criticized by Western managers for poor record keeping. For example, we were told that on one weekend there was a major problem with production but the workers neither entered in figures to show this had happened nor talked about it to the Western manager when he visited on the following Monday. When he asked if everything was okay, the reply was yes, though when he walked into the factory, he found that half the production had stopped. Our Western informan considered that this showed that workers did not want ‘to be the person to bring the bad news’. Our Chinese informant put it down to people just wanting to do their job in a company that was not theirs. Chinese managers complained that their Western managers were careless abou relationships and were not prepared to go and eat (and drink) with local government officials and others after work. This socializing is regarded as an important part of building up a relationship or guanxi that is not just confined to the work environment. This had two implications. First, it took time for Western managers to recognize that business relationships depended on what appeared to them as optional social activities. Even then, only one manager was recognized by our Chinese informants as having understood and developed these relationships. Second, it was difficult for Western managers to appreciate that the boundaries of companies were different: for example, Chinese managers were aware of the importance of local officials who needed to be socialized with if relationships were to develop and business be transacted. With one exception, none of the Western managers were prepared to meet with local officials except in Western-style business meetings.

Western managers were sometimes surprised at how difficult it was to order someone to do something. As one Western manager put it ‘if the Chinese worker considers you are not senior then they do not accept commands’. One Western accountant began to realize that she had to be more forthright in her demands rather than adopting an inclusive manner. She appreciated that she had to seek ‘the stories behind the numbers’ that were coming out of the JVs and very often she found that local accountants had a different sense of business practice tha caused problems. For example, JV N was regarded by its local management as very successful. It increased sales annually by 10%, but the accountant found that a major customer was not paying for the goods received and, moreover, was having their goods stored in a specia warehouse onsite. When questioned, the JV accountants could not see the difficulty as the customer was an important one and there was no danger of defaulting because both companies banked with the same local bank. However, it did very little for the JV’s profitability. In SW, the local managers wanted and had requested an ERP system to be installed to manage all aspects of their operation. The senior manager had experience of working in another Western company and saw the benefits of an ERP system to improve the production and accounting systems in SW. He said he found it difficult to control costs or plan production. As he commented ‘not all foreign things are right, but, first of all, copy everything, then conflicts appear and find ways to solve them’. Flume has a worldwide SAP ERP system in most countries, but they refused to install it in the JVs. When discussed with Flume, a senior manager explained that they found it difficult enough ensuring that their Chinese JVs provided the correct performance data and installing an ERP while standardizing with the rest of the company could lead to a leakage of information from the system and the Chinese workers could not be expected to enter the correct information. Instead, Flume’s JVs in China relied on limited local software systems, the collation of information by local accountants and the emailing of this information to the Hong Kong office, which in turn entered it onto the worldwide reporting system. Flume also was very wary and gave very limited information to JVs on comparative plants for benchmarking purposes despite repeated requests from local management. However, Western managers did try to make the three JVs compete by bringing the three Chinese factory managers to the Hong Kong office for a meeting, giving them some comparative information and proposing that they compete for contracts from the main company. In effect, this is what is done in the rest of the company using comparative statistics from the SAP ERP. As the Western manager related, it produced considerable ‘agitation’; however, our Chinese informants considered that they were not being given access to full information. Thus, we find that the Western company sought both to standardize and encourage the flow of information while constraining what management information is made available to Chinese managers even when they are being expected to compete with each other.

Flume approached their JVs by introducing a number of standardizing features common to other plants including a standard production process, a standard quality and reporting process, a contract of employment and early management by an expatriate manager. Their aim was to reproduce practices found elsewhere in Flume. The other partners made occasional visits but had little direct involvement in the running of the plant. Our Chinese informants considered that Western managers were careless with relationships and did not realize the importance of local contacts outside the company, where being on good relations with the local authorities was crucial to favourable treatment on regulation issues, and few Western managers had the time, energy or interest to attend to these relationships. Relationships with workers too were not cultivated in ways that were seen as satisfying to Chinese sensitivities. Gradually, some individual Western managers came to recognize the importance of relationships, but a turnove of managerial/expatriate personnel made this difficult to sustain.

Turning to the formal systems, these too were problematic. The workforce failed to exercise the workarounds necessary to keep these systems running smoothly, and the information to be entered on these systems was often misunderstood by local Chinese workers. A Western accountant related that 10 visits had been necessary to N to explain budgeting, but they were still making ‘mistakes’. Though these systems were used for control, the figures had to be regularly checked by Western accountants before entry onto worldwide systems, and the operations they represented had to be checked by frequent visits of the expatriate accountant. The Chinese management felt let down by the refusal to allow the company enterprise system to be installed in the JVs and for data to be shared from other plants outside China. Flume exemplifies the tensions between seeking to apply formal processes while often failing to acknowledge that how they are performed depends on an accommodation with existing cultural practices such as guanxi.

## Recit

Recit, a sugar manufacturer, has four JVs in China, two of which were visited. The first, JV1, was established in 1995 in a remote rural area in Guangxi Province and a second, JV2, was established in 1997 in another rural area in the same province. Both JVs are ex-SOEs with a minority equity held by the local administration. In the last 10 years, JV1 has become one of largest sugar refining plants in China. Both JVs have a Chinese management running them on a daily basis and occasional visits by expatriate managers especially in the sugar cane refining season. In both, workers and managers alike referred to the establishment of the JV as the end of the ‘iron rice bowl’, where their jobs and livelihoods were assured. Recit, just like Flume, quickly sought to establish new operating and accounting procedures. These were manually based at first, and then in 2000, they implemented Scala – a mid-range software suite – to collect financial and production data in JV2 followed by JV1 a year or so later. According to UK management, the main advantage of Scala is the ability to produce reports in Chinese and in English rather than as a cost-cutting measure. In JV2, there was considerable resistance to Scala from local accountants who thought their jobs would be at risk, and general lack of interest from senior management and from workers. Senior management included a number of local officials, appointed by the minority partner, who also held posts elsewhere. Scala was made to work by the local accountants who found it useful as a source of data to compile reports for Recit and for the local tax authorities though because of poor reporting formatting they also had to use Microsoft Excel and a software system called Crystal. To make sure the data were entered, they frequently had to cajole warehouse operators to enter data so that they completed reports on time. JV1, on the other hand, had a management who were enthusiastic about Scala and gave their support to its use. Scala in JV1 had fewer problems with data entry and though the hardware being used was quite poor, the system was used to produce accounting, tax and production information for Recit and the tax authorities. The ‘success’ of Scala in JV1 was linked to a charismatic Chinese manager who had worked in another JV prior to JV1 and said that he could see the benefits of a good computer system.

Recit’s JVs are the major employer for the local towns of JV1 and JV2 and the local authorities have considerable influence on farmers and how much sugar cane they plant. JV1’s and JV2’s Chinese management are very closely linked to the local authorities, though they argued that expatriates did not appreciate the importance of these links nor how the JV is par of the local economy. For example, in 2002, sugar cane farmers rioted in the local town of JV1 complaining about the poor sugar price.

Recit also introduced standardized procedures and information systems in their JVs though they did not install expatriate managers. The information systems used in JV1 and JV2 are technologically quite sophisticated, but they work only through the continuing intervention of accountants in JV2. The information from these systems is used to create reports sent to the Hong Kong office or occasionally directly to the UK. Recit does not share company operating information with either JV1 or JV2, and neither factory has access to the SAP ERP system used by Recit. In both JV1 and JV2, the boundary of the firm extends into the local authorities and the local farmers through economic and guanxi relationships, but these are not recognized as important by expatriate managers. Indeed, they are more often regarded as simply potential sources of corruption of company procedures. As one Western manager argued, ‘guanxi is sometimes equal to corruption’. Chinese managers, on the other hand, see these relationships as integral to their management and their more general social standing. For example, JV2 is the major employer in the area and the local officials are keen to have maximum employment in the factory. This has led, we were told, to the factory manager employing more people than necessary to accommodate local officials and in order to obtain favours that might be beneficia to the factory.

## DISCUSSION

Senior UK-based managers of both companies identified the key issues for their JVs as involving control and profitability. The initial phase of the JVs was marked by the introduction of new production plants or equipment in both companies’ JVs and the creation of operating procedures to be followed. The relationship of the employee to the company was defined or redefined as employment by competitive interview and employees were placed on short-term contracts. Promotion criteria were made explicit and performance based. In short. a determined effort was made to set up or change the organization to conform to standards and formal processes that were applied in UK operations. From these moves, forms of control based on the collection of data and its aggregation could then take place. This mode of organizing rested on collecting data on the performance of the financial and production systems and expecting that formal systems would provide coordination and control. The recognition by Western managers that these procedures have to be performed in accommodation with existing cultural practices was often limited. When it was identified, it was often seen as a problem that must be avoided.

Both Flume and Recit use SAP ERP systems in the majority of their sites throughout the world, but neither have put them into their Chinese JVs. Recit has put in a sophisticated stand alone system in their JVs and Flume has very limited software systems in place. Mediation between the JVs and the UK partner is through reports whose data comes from local accountants and production personnel but whose content is regularly checked and formatted by expatriate management based in regional offices in Hong Kong. Enterprise systems are recognized by senior management in both companies as a source of integration of business processes, which they are unwilling to share with their JVs even though local management in some JVs in both companies (SW in Flume and JV1 in Recit) are dynamic and keen to have and use latest technologies. When information systems are in place, Chinese workers and managers appear to link information to people rather than to processes. In JV2, even data entry has to be done as a favour for accountants, and UK expatriate managers find that their requests for information are responded to based on perceptions of their importance in the company hierarchy. The operation of the formal systems within the JVs depends, as studies in the UK also find, on a series of informal practices or workarounds (Knox et al., 2007). What appears to be different is that how these workarounds occur depends on perceived relationships between people. Guanxi, it appears, is important in maintaining formal systems within the JVs. As one Chinese manager commented, ‘there are no absolute friends and enemies; al cooperation is due to interests and splits are also due to interest. Fred [the English regional manager] is careless of relationships (guanxi) [but] the advantage is they would not commit a crime due to interests’.

An ongoing source of confusion for expatriate managers centres on what the data entered into formal systems sometimes represents. Chinese-trained accountants assign data to categories that need continued checking and rechecking by expatriate accountants. Some of this is the straightforward audit of transactions, but others point to a continuing misunderstanding of UK business requirements. For example, in Flume’s N JV, Flume thought the JV had bough the freehold of the factory site whereas they found out they had only bought the rights to use the site. Notions of ownership are often a problem. A less dramatic example is one of stock being produced for regular customers but not being paid for, which is seen by Chinese accountants as increased production but by expatriate accountants as a cash flow problem.

For the UK JV partners, both companies are using the presence of expatriate managers to mediate their formal reporting arrangements. They try to stabilize the performance of their formal systems by using skilled managers to monitor, adapt and intervene to control the relationship between these systems and local cultural practices. This leads to the apparently paradoxical situation, in which UK companies are also using personal relationships of trust with specific expatriate managers, in a somewhat analogous way to guanxi, to assure the data entry into their formal systems. In other words, formal systems that are expected to ensure trust are underpinned, for both Chinese and UK nationals, by the use of people-based relationships.

The two cases support the literature that UK JV partners do not rely on equity stakes in JVs and are using non-capital resources – the exchange of technology, the development o information systems for reporting and personal relations – to exercise control (Child & Yan, 2003; Chalos & O’Connor, 2004; Yan & Child, 2004). Using Boisot & Child’s (1999) terminology, UK companies sought to reduce cognitive complexity by imposing standards but failed to recognize the importance of absorbing complexity through understanding how relationships within China are conducted. In a dynamic situation, some Chinese managers are actively seeking to use sophisticated systems such as ERPs while others (as in JV2 in Recit) show little or no interest at all. In two of the JVs, Chinese management appear to be managing through a mix of formal and guanxi modes of organizing. With expatriates, they seek to build up relationships while recognizing that formal reporting mechanisms are seen as essential to Western companies. In managing within their organizations, they appear to use guanxi relationships both within and outside the organization boundaries as recognized by the UK partner. Chinese partners are much less visible in the management of these companies and prefer face-to-face contact rather than reports.

Though expatriate managers are seen as ‘careless’ in their performance of relationships (see also Goffman, 1959) by Chinese management and workers alike, it was unexpected that the formal systems reporting to the UK parent company would also be mediated through relationships. In this case, the company relies on the integrity and experience of their expa triate managers to check figures produced by formal systems in the JVs before they are entered into their global reporting systems.

These findings support the existing ICT literature on the importance and distinctiveness of Chinese information management practices and the problematic status of Western business practices in a Chinese business environment (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997; Davison & Martinsons, 2002; Martinsons, 2004). By focusing on how formal systems are made to ‘work’, this study shows that cultural frames of reference are significant and that dynamic accommodation is needed to perform and embed these formal processes. UK expatriate managers rely on extensive personal relationships within their companies and formal systems ‘work’ mediated by these relationships. Some Chinese managers realize the significance of formal systems and manage using combinations of guanxi and formal company systems. Similarly, a minority of UK managers began to realize the significance of personal relations in a Chinese setting and develop these relationships though their awareness of relying on and accommodating existing cultural practices when performing formal processes is more limited. It is likely that the rapic turnover of expatriate managers and the complexity and sophistication of Chinese cultural practices make this recognition difficult for UK managers who often regard existing cultural practices as simply pathological and leading to possible corruption. In short, a developing understanding of differing frames of reference by both Chinese and expatriate UK managers appears to be leading to some appreciation of unfamiliar cultural practices, of their centrality to control and coordination approaches, and perhaps to a more appropriate use of ICT systems.

## CONCLUSION

“The best charioteers do not rush ahead;

The best fighters do not make displays of wrath;

The greatest conqueror wins without joining issue;

The best user of men acts as though he were their inferior.”

(Tao Tê Ching, 1934, p. 227)

This paper argues that the distinctive Chinese business environment and use of ICT systems reported in the literature (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997; Boisot & Child, 1999; Davison & Martinsons, 2002) can be explored from a process-based perspective using UK–Chinese JVs as examples. By focusing on ongoing social relations in the embedding of business practices, the paper seeks to steer a path between under socialized (or economic) explanations and over socialized or deterministic arguments (see Granovetter, 1985). The specific frames of reference of Chinese business preferences and Western business processes (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997; Davison & Martinsons, 2002) are found in this study, but by investigating how business processes are created and made to ‘work’ in embedding in local cultural practices, other findings are observed. Formal systems are introduced into JVs by UK companies to create a recognizable business organization, though their operation frequently depends on workarounds, which, in China, are mediated through relationships or guanxi. A hybridization occurs where formal systems are (re)interpreted by Chinese managers and workers though cultural practices such as guanxi. However, guanxi is seen by Western management as a competing form of accountability and control and is often viewed as problematic in its effects. As a consequence, the outputs of formal information systems are mediated by persona relationships to avoid direct Chinese involvement before being entered into global reporting systems for UK partner companies. In this case, UK companies depend on the experience and integrity of expatriate managers to collect, investigate and assess the information provided by JV formal information systems. We find these UK companies are refusing to implement their global SAP enterprise systems in their JVs despite some Chinese management actively lobbying for their introduction. Some Chinese managers recognize the significance of using both formal and guanxi modes of accountability, but UK expatriate managers, perhaps due to their short stays in China, are seen as ‘careless’ with relationships and appear to be ignorant of the significance of guanxi in developing ongoing social relations.

In understanding this study, it is important to reiterate that the contractual relationships being applied by Western companies, and the guanxi practices encountered, especially in SOEs, are both changing forms of social practice (see Yang, 2002). Information management practices are being applied, revised and developed as Western companies seek to embed Western practices and technologies. We found some Chinese managers and workers keen to imitate Western practice and understand from the ‘conflicts’ that arise. In a sense there appears to be continuity with prior cultural practice as, for example, a Chinese manager commented, ‘Chairman Mao said “the most important thing for China is to educate the peasants.” Similarly, the most important issue for a factory is to educate the workers’. Perhaps Chinese cultura practices, as indicated by the quotation at the start of this section, support a willingness to learn, accommodate and change in ways that remain opaque to many expatriate Western managers on short rotations in China.

This study shows that the advent of JVs is leading to changing business practices that can be fruitfully investigated though detailed case studies and wider analysis. Though the paper uses the terms ‘Chinese’ and ‘Western’ in its descriptions, it is important to note the heterogeneity in China (for example, our informants in Guangxi province often commented on the business-like approach of Shanghai Chinese) and, of course, in the West. The approach adopted here is to seek to avoid oversocializing, to use Granovetter’s term, or essentializing either side in the encounter between Western and Chinese business practices and information management styles. This work gives another perspective on research using cultural frames of reference (see Martinsons & Hempel, 1995; Martinsons & Westwood, 1997) and perhaps can give a more socialized and contextualized understanding of ICT practices in China than questionnaire-based surveys (see He, 2004 and also Tsui, 2006). For example, our experience of the decision styles of Chinese management showed considerable variety. Some managers conformed to the traditional styles discussed in the literature (Martinsons & Westwood, 1997; Burrows et al., 2005) whereas others (such as the managers in JV1 and SW) used both more formal information systems and informal approaches depending on the audience for thei decisions. Clearly, there is scope for further, especially ethnographically informed, work to investigate what is still, to our minds, an early stage in the engagement of Western business practices, the use of ICTs and Chinese cultural preferences.

It does seem that the practice of guanxi is an important mode of interaction within Chinese business and elsewhere to such an extent that formal information systems introduced in JVs are mediated through guanxi with a reliance on personal relationships. In answer to Tsui’s (2006) call for more contextualization in Chinese management research, it appears that JVs show that both Chinese and Western approaches to control and information systems use come together in a complex and changing interaction (cf. Martinsons, 2004). We do not find that Western approaches simply subsume prior forms of business practice and indeed the evidence from the cases points to the continuing importance of changing cultural practices such as guanxi to enable formal systems to be embedded in JVs in China. Global forms of control, it appears, are (always) sited in local, highly contextual, practices that enable them to ‘work’.
