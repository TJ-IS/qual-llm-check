# An economic analysis of customer selection and leveraging strategies in a market where network externalities exist

Eunjin Kim, Byungtae Lee

Graduate School of Management, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), 207-43, Cheongryangri-dong, Dongdaemun-gu, Seoul, Republic of Korea

Received 14 October 2005; received in revised form 8 March 2007; accepted 19 March 2007 Available online 24 March 2007

## Abstract

When a firm implements analytical CRM, the value of a customer is assessed by profitability analysis. In a sense, a firm retains profitable customers but “fires” unprofitable ones. In this paper, we show that this typical customer selection strategy is not appropriate for a firm of goods and services that exhibit network externalities because of the strategic network value of unprofitable customers. In addition, we verify that this strategic value of customers also affects customer leveraging efforts through operational and collaborative CRM for such a firm. Under certain conditions, demarketing efforts based on simple profitability measures may prove counter-productive to the firm and even socially undesirable. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: CRM; Customer selection; Customer value; Network externalities

## 1. Introduction

Rapidly evolving information technologies give firms an array of tools to enhance their relationships with customers. A new marketing paradigm has arisen, customer relationship management (CRM), which is a management approach that enables organizations to identify, attract and increase retention of profitable customers, by managing relationships with them [1,36].

From its inception, implementation of CRM involved the deployment of CRM technologies [1,23]. These technologies are designed to automate sales and service functions, aggregate customer information into data warehouses, and manage collaboration with customers. These technologies are often formally classified into three parts of the application architecture of CRM systems: operational, analytical and collaborative CRM [33,34]. These three components of CRM systems are discrete, but interrelated in that they perform activities related to identifying, attracting and increasing retention of profitable customers.

Nowadays, many companies make use of CRM systems to serve customers selectively by optimizing their customer portfolios [35]. Based on sales and service data, firms can analyze customer profitability and determine profitability distribution within the customer base [25]. In measuring customer profitability, the lifetime value (LTV) analysis – the present value of the sum of the expected margins over time less the cost of serving the customer [8,28] – has typically been used. The activity-based costing (ABC) model is also frequently employed in measuring customer profitability with high levels of precision, providing a firm with a picture of the gross margins and cost-to-serve components that aggregate into individual customer profitability [14]. It is suggested that based on customer profitability analysis, firms need to select profitable customers and maintain long-term profitable relationships by imposing a return on investment (ROI) hurdle [36].

After selecting target customers, firms create loyalty among them through various CRM activities. Many studies report strict positive relationships between customer loyalty and firm profits [4,11,27,26]. For example, Reichheld [26] showed that a 5% increase in the customer retention rate results in an enormous 25% to 95% increase in profits. This is explained by the fact that retaining customers is less costly than acquiring new customers via advertising and price promotions [26]. In addition, loyal customers allow firms to charge higher premium prices for products or services since they are less sensitive due to customer loyalty [7,17,27]. Previous studies have verified that CRM efforts increase customer satisfaction and loyalty toward firms [10,22].

While firms with CRM systems retain profitable customers and attempt to create more profit from them, they try to reduce loss caused by unprofitable customers by “demarketing” [20,19] or “firing” [24,36] them. For example, with CRM systems, one major home shopping company detects unprofitable customers through analyzing their purchasing patterns, reasons for returning purchased items, and timing of returning items for refund. Given this information, the firm maintains a “black list” of those who abuse its “money back guarantee” policy. Its call center software directs calls from those on the list to specialized call center personnel to deal with these risky customers. Purchasing by highly unprofitable customers can even be blocked by these personnel. In addition, catalogs are not sent to those customers [21]. Similarly, online auction sites deprive users of membership rights if they tend not to purchase the objects that they knock down [21].

When firms cannot refuse to serve customers outright, either for legal reasons or fear of bad publicity, they search for other subtle or indirect means to discourage unwanted customers, such as price increases and decreases in product or service quality [19], which is termed “soft demarketing.” An often cited example of “soft demarketing” from enhanced knowledge on customer profitability is Progressive Insurance [9]. The company requires the installation of Autograph, which is a bundle containing a GPS device, a cellular phone and a crash data recorder, in the user's car. A recorder in the Autograph device tracks the car's movements. Then this data is collected once a month by the company's computer through wireless communications. This information is then used to set the premium for the month. By collecting the user's driving behavior in real-time, the company can estimate the user's profitability with unprecedented precision. With this information, the firm could develop a different pricing strategy for each customer. That is, users with much higher risk receive bills that charge them much higher premiums. This drives out unprofitable customers from the company. From previous studies on CRM, the key message of customer portfolio optimization is very clear: “Retain and leverage profitable customers, but fire unprofitable ones.”

On the other hand, the literature on network externalities has drawn seemingly contradictory conclusions regarding the size of the user base. That is, the greater the number of customers, the better in a network externality environment. There are many products or services for which the utility that a user derives from consumption of the goods or services increases with the number of other people also using them [16]. Economists say that these products or services exhibit “network externalities.” Katz and Shapiro [16] noted several possible sources of network externalities as follows:

1) Externalities may be generated through a direct physical effect of the number of purchasers on the quality of the product or service.

2) There may be indirect effects that give rise to network externalities when some second-round effect of an increasing user base increases the value of the product or service.

3) Positive network externalities arise for a durable good when the quality and availability of post-purchase service for the good depend on the experience and size of the service network.

Recent studies report that many industries in the digital economy show network externalities. They include IT products or services, and electronic commerce. For IT industries, direct network effects matter for communication technology companies. Indirect network effects arise in the relationship between hardware and its compatible software or between computer operating systems and compatible application software. Direct network effects are important to e-commerce companies such as eBay that provide transaction or communication services or platforms for consumers to interact with others. Indirect network effects arise from market interaction [30]. Schmitz and Latzer [30] note that the more individuals buy online, the more information is available about B2C e-commerce in general, and about the most popular sites, specifically. Individuals feel more confident about shopping at a site they know more about, especially concerning the quality of the complementary goods.

Previous studies show that the network externality is an interesting market characteristic that even makes seemingly financially unprofitable events strategically valuable to a firm if they result in growth in network size. Researchers verify that pirating activities can benefit a software firm because of enlarged network size [5,31,32]. In another related study, Conner [6] suggests that a manufacturer can be better off when lower quality clones are produced by its imitators. In conclusion, the underlying message in network externality literature is very clear. The larger your customer base is, the better it is to a firm.

From studies of CRM and of network externalities, we can get seemingly conflicting recommendations regarding customer acquisition and retention strategies. Should we maximize the size of the customer base or optimize it by firing unprofitable customers? The danger of applying only financial profitability measures to customer relationship management has not gone unnoticed. van Raaij [25] warns that such simple directives make firms fail to capture the “strategic value” of unprofitable customers, i.e. the indirect effects on firm profit. Despite this warning, to the best of our knowledge, there has been no theoretical analysis to resolve the aforementioned conflicting recommendations from the two schools of thought, or to verify the “strategic value” of unprofitable customers resulting from network externalities of a firm's products or services. Our study is an effort to fill this void.

CRM systems cannot only make a firm reconfigure its customer base (portfolio) using new customer selection and retention strategies, but they can also empower a firm to “leverage” selected customers in order to secure a larger wallet share from them with more precise knowledge on customers through analytical CRM and help from operational and collaborative CRM technology [23]. For example, Capital One uses the power of its extensive CRM information for customer leveraging [2]. Through Capital One's customer leveraging, 57% of its new customers buy another product within a year, and it retains 50–60% of its profitable customers who call attempting to cancel their accounts.

In our study, we deal with both aspects: customer retention and leveraging to increase customers' value. In both optimization problems, we especially focus on the strategic network value of customers who are classified as unprofitable according to strict financial measures. With analytical modeling, we show the effect of the strategic network value of unprofitable customers on a firm's customer retention strategy and customer leveraging effort in a network externality environment. In addition, we analyze the effect of this strategic value on consumer surplus and social welfare.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes our analytical model for customer base optimization. Results from this model are discussed in the ensuing section. In Section 4, we investigate a firm's customer leverage effort. Consumer surplus and social welfare are discussed in Section 5. In the final section, we conclude our study with a summary of possible contributions and limitations of our research.

## 2. Model of a firm's customer base optimization

First, we focus on the customer selection and retention strategy of a firm whose products or services exhibit network externalities. Analytical CRM technology endows the firm with more precise knowledge of its customers. Despite the richness and diversity of the information, we limit our attention to that of customers profit contribution to the firm, because customer base optimization strategy is often based on this knowledge.

## 2.1. Customer profitability

Customer profitability refers to the profitability in terms of the overall products or services of a firm during the lifetime of a customer. We assume the profitability of customer i as

$$
l _ {i} = R _ {i} - C _ {i}\tag{1}
$$

where $R _ { i }$ is the total revenue generated from customer i and $C _ { i }$ is the total cost incurred to the firm by customer i. Further, we define that the total revenue depends on a customer's individual valuation of the firm's products and services. Hence, the revenue function is defined as

$$
R _ {i} = f (w _ {i})\tag{2}
$$

where $w _ { i }$ represents customer $i \mathrm { { ^ { \circ } s } }$ valuation of using services or products of the firm. Here, $R _ { i }$ is an increasing and continuous function of $w _ { i } ,$ where $f { = } ( 0 ) { = } 0 , f ^ { \prime } ( w _ { i } ) >$ 0 and $f ^ { \prime } ( w _ { i } ) \leq w _ { i } .$ . The assumption $f ( w _ { i } ) \leq w _ { i }$ implies that a firm can extract some or at most all of customer $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ perceived value in using its services or products. Since only the customer who gets value from using its products or services purchases from the firm, $w _ { i }$ of an existing customer is greater than 0, i.e. $w _ { i } { > } 0$

Total cost incurred to the firm by customer i includes the cost for customer acquisition and retention, that of goods or services production, and other costs incurred by the customer [28]. Among those costs, the cost for goods/services is a function of the volume of purchases, which in turn depends on a customer's valuation of the firm's goods/service. Therefore, we can define the cost function as

$$
C _ {i} = g (w _ {i}) + e _ {i}\tag{3}
$$

where $g ( w _ { i } )$ is the total variable cost of products or services which are provided to customer $i ,$ and $e _ { i }$ is the cost of acquisition and retention, and customer-specific costs. Here, $g ( w _ { i } )$ is less than $f ( w _ { i } )$ in Eq. (2), since a firm can generate profit from customers when the typical cost of products and services is less than the price for them. We assume $g ( 0 ) { = } 0$ and $g ^ { \prime } ( w _ { i } ) { > } 0$

As shown in many previous studies [15,29], customer profitability usually distributes from negative to positive. Hence, the relative magnitudes of $C _ { i }$ and $R _ { i }$ determine whether customer i is profitable or not. For simplicity of analysis, we assume that the profitability of existing customers distributes uniformly from $l _ { \mathrm { L } }$ to $l _ { \mathrm { H } } ,$ i.e. $U [ l _ { \mathrm { L } } , l _ { \mathrm { H } } ]$ where $l _ { \mathrm { L } } { < } 0$ and $l _ { \mathrm { H } } { > } 0$ . Assuming a nonnegative profit, we define $l _ { \mathrm { H } } { > } - l _ { \mathrm { L } }$ . From now on, we denote $l _ { \mathrm { H } }$ as $l _ { \mathrm { L } } + a$ for simplicity of notation. Here, a represents the profitability gap between a customer of the highest profitability and that of the lowest profitability. For simplicity, we assume that the total number of customers, N, is normalized to 1. Therefore, the number of customers for each level of profitability is $1 / a .$

Profitability of customer $i , l _ { i }$ defined above, does not include the potential profitability of a customer i that can be developed by further means of a firm's customer leveraging efforts. Therefore, we identify $l _ { i }$ as the “initial profitability” of customer i.

## 2.2. Customer profitability improvement with a firm's customer leveraging effort

Of course, customer profitability can be significantly improved by diverse efforts of the firm, such as crosssales, up-sales and other efforts to enhance relationships with customers. This is one of the main purposes or promises that CRM investment offers. Often such effort is known as customer “leverage.”

In practice, the potential profitability enhancement varies among customers. However, for simplicity of analysis, we assume that the average increase in customer profitability is kq. Here, k is the coefficient of profitability increase, and $q \ ( 0 \leq q \leq 1 )$ is the degree of increase in profitability that is determined by the overall level of customer leveraging effort. In this model, for a given level of leveraging effort, we derive the customer retention strategy of a firm. We assume that unprofitable customers exist even after measures have been taken to increase their profitability, i.e. $k { < } - l _ { \mathrm { L } }$ . We make a simplified assumption. However, this assumption is consistent with the principle that potential profitability needs to be considered rather than the current level of profitability. Also, it allows us to verify the effect of network externalities of products or services of a firm on customer retention in a simplified context.

## 2.3. Network externalities

Network externalities which concern us are associated with the firm's products or services that all existing customers basically consume. Identifying the specific characteristics of network externalities, previous studies define a user's valuation of using products or services that exhibit network externalities as

$$
w _ {i} = v _ {\mathrm{i}} + \rho n\tag{4}
$$

where $\nu _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the intrinsic value of a firm's products or services, and $\rho n$ is the network value of a firm's products or services [6]. Here, n is the number of users who consume the same products or services, and $\rho$ is the coefficient that denotes the degree of effect of network externalities on a user's valuation of using a firm's products or services.

Based on the above definition, when a firm reduces the number of customers from N – which is normalized as $1 - \tan \ b _ { 1 }$ , a retained customer i's valuation of using a firm's products or services decreases by $\rho ( N { - } n _ { 1 } )$ . In this paper, we assume that the total revenue generated from customer i, $R _ { i } ( = f ( w _ { i } ) )$ , is an increasing function of customer $i ^ { \circ } \mathbf { s }$ valuation of using the firm's services and products, $w _ { i } .$ . Therefore, $R _ { i }$ decreases as $w _ { i }$ decreases. For simplicity of analysis, we assume that $R _ { i }$ is a linear function of w . Likewise, we assume that $g ( w _ { i } )$ in Eq. (3) is also a linear function of $w _ { i } .$

In our model, when $w _ { i }$ decreases by $\rho ( N { - } n _ { 1 } )$ , the profit that a firm can generate from customer i decreases by

$$
\begin{array}{c} (f (w _ {i}) - f (w _ {i} - \rho (N - n _ {1}))) \\ - (g (w _ {i}) - g (w _ {i} - \rho (N - n _ {1}))). \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

From the linearity of function $f ( w _ { i } )$ and $g ( w _ { i } )$ , we define the decrease in Eq. (5) as $\gamma ( N { - } n _ { 1 } )$ . Here $0 < \gamma < \rho ,$ since $0 { < } f ( w _ { i } ) { - } g ( w _ { i } ) { < } w _ { i }$ . In summary, when a firm reduces the number of customers from N to $n _ { 1 } ,$ , it also decreases the profit from retained customer i by $\gamma ( N { - } n _ { 1 } )$ If a firm retains customers whose initial profitability is equal to or larger than $l _ { \mathrm { F } } ,$ and fires the rest of the customers, the declined profit level, $\gamma ( N { - } n _ { 1 } )$ becomes $\gamma ( l _ { \mathrm { F } } - l _ { \mathrm { L } } ) / a ,$ , since the initial profitability of the existing customers is uniformly distributed from $l _ { \mathrm { L } }$ to $l _ { \mathrm { L } } { + a }$

When a firm decides to retain customers whose initial profitability is not less than $l _ { \mathrm { F } } ,$ and to fire the rest of the existing customers, the profit that a firm can generate from retained customers is

$$
\pi \left(l _ {\mathrm{F}}\right) = \frac {1}{a} \int_ {l _ {\mathrm{F}}} ^ {l _ {\mathrm{H}}} \left(l + k q - \gamma \frac {l _ {\mathrm{F}} - l _ {\mathrm{L}}}{a}\right) \mathrm{d} l.\tag{6}
$$

In our model, a firm determines the “firing point” of customer profitability level, $l _ { \mathrm { F } }$

## 3. Analysis of the customer base optimization model

To verify the effect on customer retention strategies of the strategic value of customers resulting from network externalities of a firm's products or services, we first investigate the optimal customer retention strategy of a firm whose products or services do not exhibit network externalities, i.e. $\gamma = 0$ . Then we compare this result with the customer retention strategy of a firm whose products or services do exhibit network externalities, i.e. $\gamma { > } 0$

When $\gamma = 0 ,$ , the optimal customer retention strategy is straightforward: the firm should keep only those customers whose profitability is not less than 0. Considering potential improvements in profitability due to a firm's customer leveraging efforts, kq, a firm needs to retain customers whose initial profitability is not less than $\hat { l }$ and fire the rest of its customers where $\hat { l } = - k q$ This result is very intuitive. We consider two factors in customer selection: customer profitability and network externalities of products or services. Therefore, if network externalities do not exist for a firm, only customer profitability matters. In this case, the CRM principle regarding customer retention – retaining profitable customers and firing unprofitable ones – is valid for the firm's retention strategy. For simplicity, we name customers whose initial profitability is less than $\hat { l }$ as “unprofitable customers.”

Under conditions of network externalities of products or services, firing unprofitable customers clearly decreases the firm's user base. From Eq. (4), this reduces the value that retained customers can get from business with the firm, making those customers less willing to spend on the firm's goods or services. Therefore, the profit from a retained customer decreases by the amount indicated in Eq. (5). We name this indirect effect of unprofitable customers on a firm's profit as the “strategic network value of unprofitable customers resulting from network externalities of the products or services of a firm.” The strategic network value of an unprofitable customer can be formally defined as the marginal indirect contribution of a customer to the firm's profit through his or her contributing network value. Mathematically, the strategic network value of an unprofitable customer at the firing point, $S _ { l _ { \mathrm { F } } } ,$ is defined as follows. (See Appendix A for detailed derivation.)

$$
S _ {l _ {\mathrm{F}}} = \frac {\partial}{\partial l _ {\mathrm{F}}} \left(\int_ {l _ {\mathrm{F}}} ^ {l _ {\mathrm{H}}} \gamma \frac {l _ {\mathrm{F}} - l _ {\mathrm{L}}}{a} \mathrm{d} l\right).
$$

In other words, if a firm retains this unprofitable customer, the firm expects the indirect contribution of the customer to the firm's profit through his or her contributing network value, $S _ { l _ { \mathrm { F } } } .$ However, the firm needs to abide the direct profit loss incurred by the customer.

Lemma 1. The strategic network value of an unprofitable customer whose initial profitability level is $l _ { F }$ and the direct profit loss due to this customer are derived as follows, respectively:

$$

The strategic network value $S _ { l _ { \mathrm { F } } }$ is decreasing in $l _ { \mathrm { F } }$ , i.e. $\partial S _ { l _ { \mathrm { e } } } / \partial l _ { \mathrm { F } } { < } 0$

A real world case of an online community site shows that failure to recognize the strategic network value of unprofitable customers can be very disastrous. The core service of online community sites is providing platforms for users to communicate and interact with each other. They are typical examples of a market with network externalities. In November 2002, under ever-increasing financial pressure after the dotcom crash, a leading online community site in Korea, Freechal.com, announced a change in its pricing strategy from free to fee, but providing more enhanced services, including larger storage capacity. This fee-based strategy aimed at securing a revenue source and demarketing unprofitable users [12]. However, even though it collected fees from the users who remained at that time, the company could not avoid the continuous exodus of users. The result was that only 0.4 million communities remained by May 2003, down from 1.1 million communities in September 2002 [12]. Users became less willing to remain at the site and to spend money on the service as the user base shrank. Due to the continued reduction of its user base and failure to attract new users, a seriously damaged Freechal.com restarted its free service, offering a feebased service as a premium service in June 2003 [12], but without recovering its lost customers.

## 4. Optimization of customer leveraging effort

Until now, we assumed that CRM's profitability enhancement of customers, $q ,$ is fixed. However, as explained earlier, one of the main goals of CRM is leveraging customers to improve their value with diverse efforts through operational and collaborative CRM, such as cross-selling and up-selling. Of course, such effort incurs costs to the firm. In this section, we derive the optimal overall level of a firm's customer leveraging effort and investigate whether and how much network externalities affect a firm's customer leveraging decision.

The level of a firm's customer leveraging effort is measured by the cost (investment) necessary to enhance customers' value to the firm. We assume that the leveraging cost $c ( q )$ is an increasing, continuous function of the degree of increase in profitability enhancement of customers, $q ,$ where $c ( 0 ) { = } 0 , c ^ { \prime } ( 0 ) { = } 0 , c ^ { \prime } ( q ) { > } 0 \forall q { > } 0$ $c ^ { \prime \prime } \left( q \right) { > } 0$ . For simplicity, we assume $c ( q ) = b q ^ { 2 }$ . Based on this assumption, we can derive the following.

Proposition 5. A firm with a higher level of network externalities of its products or services needs to increase its customer leveraging effort.

Proof. See Appendix C.

As shown in Proposition 4, a firm's ability to increase customer profitability allows it to explore further the strategic network value of unprofitable customers. Therefore, as this strategic value increases with higher levels of network externalities, a firm can explore more of this strategic value with enhancing its ability to increase customer profitability. This provides a firm an incentive to increase the customer leveraging effort.

In the real world, there is empirical evidence that CRM implementation is mostly active among companies with strong network externalities. According to a software market analysis of Knowledge Research Group in 2005, about 51% of all companies in Korea had adopted CRM systems [18]. However, the rate of adoption of CRM systems in the telecommunications industry in Korea is quite above average. Of nine telecommunication companies in Korea, eight have adopted CRM systems [18] resulting in the highest CRM adoption rate (89%) among all industries. Moreover, to enhance relationships with customers, the major players in this industry started to invest more in upgrading their CRM systems and redesigning their processes [3,13]. Our analysis partially explains why they are far more active in CRM investment.

In this section, we investigate how consumer surplus and social welfare can be affected by the strategic network value of unprofitable customers when a firm fires unprofitable customers. To verify this, we analyze two opposite benchmark cases. First, we investigate the case where every unprofitable customer is valueless with no strategic value. Then, we analyze the case where every unprofitable customer is valuable for the strategic network value.

For simplicity, we assume that it increases the perceived value and payment of customer i from $w _ { i }$ and $f ( w _ { i } )$ to $w _ { i } + z _ { i }$ and $f ( w _ { i } + z _ { i } )$ . Therefore, the surplus of customer i is $\mathrm { C S } _ { i } \mathrm { = }$ $( w _ { i } + z _ { i } ) - f ( w _ { i } + z _ { i } )$ . In this section, we only consider the case where $f ( w _ { i } + z _ { i } ) < ( w _ { i } + z _ { i } )$ , i.e. $\mathrm { C S } _ { i } { > } 0$

In the absence of network externalities of products or services, the surplus of retained customer i is not affected by a firm's firing unprofitable customers. Therefore, the surplus of profitable customers retained, $\mathrm { T C S } _ { \mathrm { P } }$ – the sum of the surplus of each profitable customer – is not changed by the event of firing unprofitable ones. ## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we show that the axiomatic CRM principle regarding customer retention – retaining profitable customers but firing unprofitable ones – is not appropriate for a firm whose products or services exhibit network externalities. It is because of the strategic network value of unprofitable customers resulting from network externalities of a firm's products or services. For a firm whose products or services exhibit higher levels of network externalities, unprofitable customers become more valuable for their contribution to the network value. In this case, a firm needs to retain more of them regardless of direct financial loss they may incur. A real world case of an online community site shows that failure to recognize the strategic network value of unprofitable customers in formulating a customer retention strategy can be very costly [12].

We also show that a firm's customer leveraging effort not only improves the direct profitability of customers, but also allows the firm to explore further the strategic network value of unprofitable customers. Therefore, it is encouraged that firms whose products or services exhibit network externalities to invest more in customer leveraging. This is also consistent with anecdotal evidence that CRM implementation is mostly active among companies in the telecommunications industry which is well-known as network externality business [18]. In addition, the strategic network value of unprofitable customers also affects consumer surplus and social welfare. In the presence of network externalities of products or services, firing unprofitable customers can even reduce the surplus of retained customers, and can be socially undesirable.

Further empirical studies providing methodological rigor rather than anecdotal cases are desired to enhance or complement our results.
