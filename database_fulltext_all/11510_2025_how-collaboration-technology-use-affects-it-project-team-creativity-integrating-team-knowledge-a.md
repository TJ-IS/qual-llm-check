# HOW COLLABORATION TECHNOLOGY USE AFFECTS IT PROJECT TEAM CREATIVITY: INTEGRATING TEAM KNOWLEDGE AND CREATIVE SYNTHESIS PERSPECTIVES<sup>1</sup>

Xiaojie Zhang College of Management & Innovation and Entrepreneurship Research Center, Ocean University of China, Qingdao, Shandong, CHINA {zhangxiaojie@ouc.edu.cn}

Yulin Fang Faculty of Business and Economics, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, SAR, CHINA {ylfang@hku.hk}

Jingmei Zhou Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Business, Renmin University of China, Beijing, CHINA {zhoujingmei@rmbs.ruc.edu.cn}

Kai H. Lim Faculty of Business, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong, SAR, CHINA {kai.lim@polyu.edu.hk}

Contemporary IT project teams engage in creative problem solving to address increasingly complex business problems, highlighting the need to promote IT project team creativity. Collaboration technologies are widely used in IT project teams, but little is known about what collaboration technology features can be used to improve IT project team creativity and what the underlying influencing mechanisms are. To address this important gap, the current study builds on the extended team knowledge framework to identify collaboration technology features and decodes their influencing mechanisms on IT project team creativity by drawing on the novel creative synthesis theory originating in the management literature to the IT project team context. We identify three sets of collaboration technology support features—awareness knowledge supports, long-term knowledge supports, and transitional knowledge supports—and posit that their use can improve IT project team creativity by facilitating the creative synthesis process, which includes the three subconstructs of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas. The research model is supported in general by empirical data collected through a multisourced survey of over 500 team members and their leaders from 62 IT project teams. We conclude with a discussion of theoretical and practical implications.

Keywords: Team creativity, creative synthesis process facilitation, collaboration technology, IT project teams

## Introduction

In the current digital age, organizations are increasingly utilizing information technology (IT) project development to create innovative business applications and explore new opportunities. As software applications are usually designed and developed for users, customers, and other stakeholders with unidentified or operationally ambiguous needs, there is more dependence on creative solutions in IT project development (He et al., 2022; Robillard, 1999; Tiwana & McLean, 2005). The ever-changing technological advancements in the IT landscape make IT project development even more challenging, and a variety of potential IT solutions are available for addressing poorly specified problems (Kudaravalli et al., 2017). Indeed, IT project works are widely deemed to be complex and nonroutine, requiring project teams to be creative in their problem-solving processes (Venkatesh et al., 2018). As a result, team creativity, which involves the production of novel and practical ideas, is becoming more valued in IT project development (Levina, 2005; Muller & Ulrich, 2013; Tiwana & McLean, 2005). Given the importance of team creativity, a vast amount of research has examined how to promote it (Aggarwal & Woolley, 2019; Hoever et al., 2012; Perry-Smith & Shalley, 2014), especially for IT project teams (Farh et al., 2010; Tiwana & McLean, 2005).

This study aims to examine IT project team creativity from the perspective of collaboration technology use. Collaboration technologies (CT) refer to IT that supports communication, coordination, and information processing among team members working on joint tasks (Bala et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2011). Contemporary teams, and IT project teams in particular, frequently use CT—including email, instant messaging, audio/video conferencing systems, knowledge management systems, screen sharing systems, content collaboration technologies, and project management systems—to facilitate efficient teamwork (Kang et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2011). Gartner Inc. forecasted that spending on collaboration software in 2023 would reach \$14.5 billion and that this figure would grow to \$24.7 billion by 2027 (Roth, 2023). Major companies such as Cisco, IBM, and HUAWEI have invested in various collaboration technologies to facilitate their IT project development (Bala et al., 2017; Liefner et al., 2019). Despite the widespread use of tools and technologies, however, inefficient collaboration is considered to be a major contributor to the high failure rate of IT project teams (Project Management Institute, 2021). The evaluation of how such widespread collaboration technologies can be utilized to enhance creative collaboration and team creativity is particularly helpful for IT project teams that have already invested in these technologies. Thus, research identifying relevant features from commonly used CT that benefit team creativity can provide insights into the added value that CT can bring in terms of improving creativity in IT project teams.

Despite the prevalent use of collaboration technologies in IT project teams, the literature is surprisingly limited in its understanding of whether and how collaboration technology can be used to enhance team creativity. Prior research has examined the effect of CT use on a variety of team processes and outcomes. For instance, Zhang et al. (2011) found that collaboration technology features can be used to facilitate team performance through enhanced collaboration, whereas Majchrzak et al. (2005) proposed the role of certain collaboration technology features in managing coordinative communication priorities and norms to avoid misunderstandings. While insightful, these studies have not addressed the pivotal effect of collaboration technology use on team creativity. Such creativity requires not only sharing knowledge and avoiding misunderstandings but also synthesizing diverse ideas to the extent that connections among these ideas can produce new understandings. Although some studies have implied that collaboration technology use has benefits for team creativity through improved interaction and boundary-spanning capability (Bala et al. 2017), especially for tasks with innovative objectives (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2009), no studies have formally theorized the effect of CT features on team creativity, let alone in the IT project context. We therefore need a more complete theoretical understanding of what collaboration technology features are important to IT project team creativity and the mechanisms through which they exert influence. Motivated by these research gaps, our research question is: What collaboration technology features can be used to improve IT project team creativity and how?

To address this question, we first build on and extend the team knowledge literature to identify three sets of collaboration technology features: the awareness knowledge supporting feature, the long-term knowledge supporting feature, and the transitional knowledge supporting feature. These supports in turn provide three types of team knowledge that can serve as critical cognitive resources for members collaborating to generate creative ideas in IT project teams. We adopt the theoretical lens of team knowledge because of the crucial role it plays as a cognitive mechanism in coordinating dynamic and complex teamwork that characterizes IT project teams as well as its recognized role as a key source of input to team creativity. We then theorize the mechanism through which the use of these features can improve IT project team creativity by drawing on the creative synthesis perspective (Harvey, 2014), an IS-novel theory on team creativity in the recent management literature. Harvey (2014) explains how a team’s knowledge resources can be synthesized to form a unique new understanding, which can guide members to approach problems with novel mindsets. This theoretical perspective proposes three key aspects of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas, which afford a nuanced comprehension of team creativity development process. In taking this approach, we formalize creative synthesis process facilitation as a second-order construct reflected by three first-order subconstructs based on Harvey’s (2014) original theory and offer a contextualized understanding of its impact on team creativity in the IT project team context. We tested the hypotheses through a multisourced survey of 62 IT project teams covering more than 500 team members and their project leaders.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, in integrating the team knowledge framework and the IS-novel creative synthesis perspective, we contribute to the IT project team literature by identifying CT features and examining the mechanisms through which these features influence IT project team creativity. The study also makes an empirical contribution by formalizing creative synthesis process facilitation as a second-order construct and developing its measures for empirical testing. Second, the study enhances the creative synthesis literature by explaining creativity synthesis from the perspective of collaboration technology use, a view that is highly relevant to IS research in general and IT project team research in particular. Third, the research contributes to the collaboration technology literature by recategorizing CT features and examining their impacts on IT project team creativity. ## Theoretical Background

## Collaboration Technology and IT Project Team Creativity

Team creativity has been defined as “the generation of novel and useful ideas on products, services, processes, and procedures” by a group of members working interdependently to achieve a shared objective (Shin & Zhou, 2007). Team creativity often occurs in interactions among team members, where individuals can build on and link each other’s unique knowledge, ideas, and skills, jointly building a collective of creative actions (Harvey & Kou, 2013).

The creation of IT end products for IT project teams is a new, or partially new, system of IT development that can address emerging business needs. From a lifecycle perspective, IT project problems generally start with issues related to the unidentified and/or latent needs of users, customers, and other stakeholders, where solutions require IT artifacts that can provide functionalities or affordances for users in a specific business context (Robillard, 1999; Tiwana & McLean, 2005). Users might not have a clear understanding of their business task requirements or know how to describe operationally ambiguous requirements (Eischen, 2002; Walz et al., 1993). Problems encountered during IT project development may be poorly specified or particularly challenging (Kudaravalli et al. 2017), and there is rarely “one right design” for an IT development problem. This is because there is often more than one solution to achieve the same end (Tiwana & McLean, 2005) and team members will likely have different interpretations of business requirements and how to combine technological components to address them. IT project development involves a continuous matching process between these latent business requirements and possible IT solutions (Nambisan et al., 2017). Robillard (1999) and Kudaravalli et al. (2017) described the process of IT development as the progressive disambiguation of open-ended problems. Creative IT project teams might therefore explore multiple approaches to a problem, where members can both expand the number of alternatives they evaluate and narrow their choice of solutions. IT project teams can facilitate creativity via the application and synthesis of individually held knowledge such as business analyses, user interface designs, quality tests, structural architecture, and database administration (Tiwana & McLean, 2005). In this sense, team creativity relies on the coordination of different members’ actions and knowledge directed towards common goals but goes beyond the more standard collaboration process by pursuing new and valuable ideas.

Considerable research has examined factors leading to team creativity in both general and IT project teams, where a major focus has been on the sources of team inputs. For instance, team composition factors such as demographics and personality attributes have been widely explored in the literature (Aggarwal & Woolley, 2019; Hoever et al., 2012; Homan et al., 2015; Kearney & Gebert, 2009; Miron-Spektor et al., 2011; Parke et al., 2021; Shin & Zhou, 2007; Taylor & Greve, 2006), where scholars found that creative ideas are more likely to occur after a variety of knowledge inputs and application of other resources (Harvey, 2014). Numerous studies have also shown that social networks (Jia et al., 2014; Perry-Smith & Shalley, 2014), leadership (Kearney & Gebert, 2009), and team climate— including goal orientation (Gong et al., 2013) and authentic affect climate (Parke et al., 2022)—promote the sharing and communication of diverse information. The generation of creative concepts can be further simulated by task characteristics, such as task complexity (Jia et al., 2014) and IT toolkits usage (Mannucci, 2017).

This study can be distinguished from the team creativity literature in its focus on collaboration technology, an understudied source of team input. Contemporary teams, and IT project teams in particular, frequently use collaboration technologies—including email, instant messaging, audio/video conferencing systems, knowledge management systems, screen sharing systems, content collaboration technologies, and project management systems—to support the communication, coordination, and completion of teamwork (Kang et al., 2012; Watson-Manheim & Bélanger, 2007; Zhang et al., 2011). These technologies can be viewed as a bundle of features that members select (Carte & Chidambaram, 2004) for use in various ways across teams. The ways in which teams employ specific technologies to perform tasks are expected to differ (Liang et al., 2010) even when these tools are the same across teams (Romanow et al., 2018). Leveraging a specific IT involves the selective deployment of features designed for use at certain points in time, which can influence and determine work outcomes (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Jasperson et al., 2005).

The extant literature on CT usage has examined its functional nature and effect on a variety of team outcomes, such as collaboration, communicative coordination, and performance, but continues to lack a focus on team creativity (see Table 2). For instance, research has shown that CT use can influence team performance based on the members’ capability for collaboration, which involves knowledge sharing, task planning, and communication factors (Bala et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2011). Romanow et al. (2018) demonstrated that the utilization of CT features by individual team members can enhance communicative coordination within the team, which is achieved by emphasizing factors such as communication timeliness, frequency, accuracy, and quality. Notably, the team processes that encompass coordination, collaboration, communication, and knowledge sharing highlight the importance of attaining a shared understanding among members. Such shared understanding has been recognized as a crucial intermediary process that leads to optimal team performance (Ellis, 2006; Gong et al., 2013; Homan et al., 2008; Hu et al., 2018; Parke et al., 2021; Sung & Choi, 2019; Zhang et al., 2011). Nevertheless, we argue that a dominant focus on shared understanding as an influencing mechanism falls short in explaining the role of creativity in the process. To achieve team creativity, the process demands that team members develop novel perspectives and knowledge forms, which requires not only a shared understanding but more importantly, the synthesis of diverse ideas to produce new insights and configurations. Accomplishing this objective is particularly challenging, however. IT project team members’ different perspectives on IT solution designs may detour or derail their efforts to converge diverse ideas into creative team output (Leonardi, 2011). It is therefore necessary to facilitate team efforts in synthesizing various concepts and considerations that can lead to the alignment of divergent views and the development of shared and novel understandings .

Some studies (Table 2) have suggested the beneficial role of CT use in team creativity. For example, Dennis and Valacich (1994) found that CT use enhances the generation of ideas within teams, Malhotra and Majchrzak (2009) proposed that CT usage yields greater benefits for team performance with nonroutine and innovative task objectives, and Bala et al. (2017) found that CT use improves new product development. Yet these studies did not address team creativity as an outcome, nor did they develop a formal theoretical account of which collaboration technology features matter to team creativity and how they operate. The literature has offered limited theoretical knowledge of what collaboration technology features are important to team creativity and their corresponding influencing mechanisms. Given this key gap in the literature, we aim to identify and examine existing collaboration technology features widely available to IT project teams that can be leveraged to improve IT project team creativity. We extend the team knowledge literature as the theoretical base from which to identify relevant collaboration technology features and draw on the creative synthesis perspective (Harvey, 2014) in the IS context to unveil their influencing mechanisms on team creativity.

## Identifying CT Features Based on Team Knowledge Framework

## Extending Team Knowledge Framework to Team Creativity Context

To identify collaboration technology features that foster team creativity, this study builds on the team knowledge framework (Espinosa et al., 2007) originating in the team coordination literature and extends it to the team creativity context. We chose the team knowledge framework as the key theoretical base to identify CT features for two primary reasons. First, team knowledge plays a crucial role in coordinating the highly complex, dynamic, and interdependent tasks that characterize IT project teams (Espinosa et al., 2007). Team coordination has been examined from three perspectives: mechanistic task programming, organic communication, and cognitive knowledge (Espinosa et al., 2007; Van de Ven et al., 1976). The coordination of repetitive and routine aspects of a task can be achieved mechanistically through task programming, including the use of schedules, plants, manuals, and specifications. For more uncertain aspects of a task (e.g., dynamic requirements), team members need to coordinate through organic communication (e.g., by feedback) (March & Simon, 1993). While the organic perspective is important for IT project teams, communication becomes far more efficient with better common ground in team knowledge for members with different expertise, roles, and foci (Cramton, 2001; Fussell & Krauss, 1992). Thus, the third perspective of cognitive mechanism, manifested through team knowledge, is essential to managing interdependent and highly complex tasks that require team cognition as the knowledge base for communication. Team members’ common ground in expertise, access to and familiarity with task domain knowledge, and awareness of who has done what within the team are examples of these factors (Espinosa et al., 2007). As such, among the three established perspectives, the cognitive mechanism captured by the team knowledge framework is best suited to address IT project teams featuring high task complexity and interdependence.

Second, the team knowledge framework focuses on knowledge that is crucial to team creativity. Team creativity development has been viewed as a specific kind of creative collaboration and coordination (Hargadon & Bechky, 2006; Lingo & O’Mahony, 2010) within which knowledge, as a cognitive resource, is a central source of inputs for team creativity development (Harvey, 2014). This is especially the case for IT project teams that handle complex user requirements and technological shifts in IT landscapes. IT project teams use knowledge of technical and business concepts, products, and processes as the common ground for members to build expectations about the future states of the task and to facilitate further communications (Carlo et al., 2012). Teams with access to a superb and relevant knowledge base can not only overcome barriers faster and with less difficulty but are also able to innovate more successfully and compete more effectively. For these two important reasons, we adopt the team knowledge framework as the theoretical lens while extending it to the team creativity context.

The team knowledge framework states that two elements of team knowledge—awareness knowledge and long-term knowledge (Cooke et al., 2000)—can efficiently facilitate team coordination (Espinosa et al., 2007; Rico et al., 2008; van der Haar et al., 2015), wherein IT features should be designed to support the provision of such team knowledge (Krancher et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2015). Awareness knowledge refers to up-tothe-minute information about activities conducted by team members, which serves as the context for their activities (Cooke et al., 2000). As awareness knowledge is not stable across time, sequence, or content, it needs continual updating, especially when the task situation changes. Among the different types of awareness knowledge identified in the literature (Bardram & Hansen, 2010; Dabbish & Kraut, 2008; Gutwin & Greenberg, 2004; Krancher et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2015), task awareness is the most important factor when designing collaboration tools to support team coordination (Espinosa et al., 2007). Task awareness in a team setting is defined as updated information about what is happening to shared tasks. Such awareness benefits team coordination by helping team members synchronize teamwork through a joint task focus (Espinosa et al., 2007), particularly for the dynamic management of work activities with limited direct communication (Rico et al., 2008). Task awareness is therefore especially important for IT project teams where tasks are highly complex and interdependent and team members have different information and foci.

Long-term knowledge refers to knowledge obtained over time via education, experience, or training (Cooke et al., 2000; Espinosa et al., 2007). This study emphasizes task-related knowledge such as expertise and skills (Cannon-Bowers et al., 1995) related to understanding key products, concepts, and processes in IT development. IT project development should build on knowledge from multiple technical and business domains (Tiwana & McLean, 2005). For instance, a developer’s knowledge of software engineering and a business analyst’s knowledge in examining and assessing business requirements are useful for a range of IT project tasks. Awareness and long-term knowledge are helpful in coordinating shared knowledge among team members about the task state, task domain, and the development of technical products. Shared knowledge helps team members coordinate processes that can be used to develop more accurate explanations and expectations about task events and team members’ behaviors and assists them in finding more common ground in their communication (Espinosa et al. 2007; van der Haar et al., 2015; Rico et al., 2008), thereby facilitating teamwork.

While team creativity requires the coordination of different knowledge sets possessed by team members to achieve shared knowledge (Tiwana & McLean, 2005), it goes beyond this in the development of novel and useful ideas. Apart from the two team knowledge elements crucial for team coordination, we posit that the creativity development process also requires processing transitional knowledge, defined in the developmental psychology literature on learning as an unstable state wherein “one who has not yet displayed consistent knowledge of a concept but whose underlying cognitive structures are sufficiently developed to allow him to accommodate new information in that concept” (Perry et al., 1988, p. 360). In studies on team processes and states, transition depicts the phase when a collective reflects on their previous performance and prepares for upcoming actions that directly contribute to task accomplishment (Marks et al., 2001). Transitional knowledge therefore involves an evolution or transformation of these understandings from one level or stage to the next and emerges when team members revisit past solutions or explore new alternatives. Analogically, in the current study, transitional knowledge refers to a dynamic form of team knowledge, which is based on the cognitive processing of prior knowledge and experiences, and prepares the team for the further enactment and implementation of creative ideas. As transitional knowledge is inherently incomplete, it is viewed as a work in progress that evolves out of team member reflection and exploration in changing circumstances (Kallinikos et al., 2013).

Transitional knowledge differs from long-term knowledge such as expertise: whereas the former maintains a changing state and needs ongoing verification and modification as a task progresses, the latter is more stable and less subject to change. Transitional knowledge is always equivocal and provisional in reflecting an evolving understanding, for example, about the extent to which the focal idea is feasible or (dis)advantageous to its alternatives. Transitional knowledge also differs in nature from awareness knowledge. That is, while team awareness knowledge is relevant to situational changes (Espinosa et al., 2007), transitional knowledge relates to changes in the understanding of task components such as task questions and solutions. We argue that transitional knowledge is uniquely critical for team creativity in that it provides members with opportunities to develop new understandings of the questions and challenges at hand, recognize the benefits and shortcomings of the generated ideas, and identify directions for further actions. It is less relevant for routinized tasks, however, where no additional new knowledge is required for and obtained from task completion.

## Identifying Collaboration Technology Features

Collaboration technologies can be viewed as tools that offer diverse forms of team knowledge as cognitive resources to promote team creativity (Boland et al., 1994). Based on the three sets of team knowledge, we categorize collaboration technology features herein and coin them accordingly as task awareness-supporting features, long-term knowledge supporting features, and transitional knowledge supporting features. First, we propose that collaboration technology features supporting task awareness knowledge include the visualization of work progress (Tripp et al., 2016; Zammuto et al., 2007) and notification (Zhang et al., 2011). The feature of work progress visualization provides graphic representations (e.g., viewer and roadmap) that create awareness of the task situation at both universal and localized levels by displaying work that has been completed and is yet to be completed (Tripp et al., 2016; Zammuto et al., 2007). Notification is the feature that reports immediate project status change (Zhang et al., 2011), which creates local awareness of the task situation. These two features jointly enable team members to form a shared awareness of the project’s status at various levels, thereby helping develop a focus among team members.

Locating and leveraging relevant task knowledge within the knowledge stock of the team is imperative for solving the given task problems (Faraj & Sproull, 2000). Information retrieval research has suggested that humans locate relevant information primarily through scanning (i.e., general browsing of information) and searching (i.e., seeking answers to specific questions) (Vandenbosch & Huff, 1997). Accordingly, we propose that collaboration technology features that support long-term knowledge include syndication and searching. Syndication is a feature that supports the long-term knowledge scanning process. Designed to improve content management quality (Tremblay et al., 2007), syndication provides mechanisms that can, for example, summarize and group topics and display them at a single location so that team members have better access to various pieces of information (Zhang et al., 2011). One example of this feature is the Really Simple Syndication (RSS), a type of web-feed format that enables users to update their work in a standardized format via subscription to content from multiple sources. When team members try to look up specific pieces of knowledge from past projects using this feature, all the related information and perspectives are presented in a single location. Syndication thus provides opportunities for identifying associations among different perspectives by enabling easy access to and assessment of these perspectives, facilitating the development of different solutions to problems (Zhang et al., 2011).

Searching is a feature that can facilitate locating and finding information that users need (Zhang et al., 2011). Users assess this feature using criteria such as the speed at which the information can be located, the relevance of the information, and its ease of use. Quick and accurate searching of relevant and high-quality content, supported by advanced database technologies and designed functions such as credibility rating (Poston & Speier, 2005), enables team members to easily traverse across different sources of knowledge, including internal and external team knowledge, as well as information or understandings from different members that contribute to their collective body of knowledge (Majchrzak et al., 2005). By easily and accurately locating the information members need among vast resources, collaboration technologies can help users form a more complete picture of targeted interests and concerns.

Transitional knowledge that revisits past understandings and explores new alternatives allows for the reconfiguration of information. Revisiting past solutions entails evaluating, updating, and modifying existing knowledge, while exploring novel alternatives involves making attempts to find new solutions to problems (Døjbak Håkonsson et al., 2016; He & Wong, 2004). We therefore propose two collaboration technology features supporting transitional knowledge, that of recombination and incomplete knowledge entry. Recombination refers to the process of building on others’ past contributions to form solutions (Faraj et al., 2011), which occurs when team members freely borrow from each other’s past work, such as the coding or management practices the IT project team has previously worked on. As a feature that supports members in reusing existing solutions in the IT project team context, recombination is evident when team members apply computeraided tools to draw on internal and external code sources (e.g., GitHub) and integrate them into their own work in progress, with or without revisions of the original knowledge.

Incomplete knowledge entry is a feature that supports team members in collectively exploring alternatives to address questions with unknown solutions. It allows employees to enter incomplete content (e.g., an unfinished template) and then invites inputs from different members at a later time (Majchrzak et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2011). These inputs can take various forms, such as updates on the main body of knowledge, annotation of certain contents, and threaded discussions (Markus et al., 2002). Even though such knowledge pieces are experimental and underdeveloped, one major benefit of this feature is that it enables members to play with their own ideas and those of others, and thus initiate more discussions on incomplete ideas (Zhang et al., 2011). For instance, the literature has suggested that emailing partial analyses to co-workers can catalyze discussion (Markus et al., 2002). When team members have more information exchanges, they will likely be exposed to different thoughts or perspectives that help them build alternative and/or better solutions to achieving task objectives and goals (Malhotra et al., 2001). In summary, we extend the team knowledge framework to the team creativity context to propose CT features that support task awareness knowledge, long-term knowledge, and transitional knowledge.

## The Creativity Synthesis Perspective

The next step in theorizing is to identify the mechanisms through which the CT features affect IT project team creativity. To do so, we build on the creative synthesis perspective, a novel theory that explains how team creativity is developed by synthesizing diverse knowledge resources within the team (Harvey, 2014). Through its specification of influencing mechanisms of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas, the creative synthesis perspective offers more nuanced insights into the creativity development process than other similar concepts in the literature, such as information elaboration, expertise integration, and shared understanding (see Table 3 above). Specifically, information elaboration and expertise integration, both of which capture the extent of discussion and integration of team members’ information and expertise, are found to enhance IT project team creativity (Tiwana & McLean, 2005; Hoever et al., 2018). These concepts have been criticized, however, for not being clear about how the process unfolds (Zahra et al., 2020), nor does related research reveal how the highly fluid and contextual process of integration and elaboration might manifest (Van den Bosch et al., 1999; Zahra et al., 2020). Being an identifiable process, integration or elaboration remains as a black box (Barley et al., 2018; Zahra et al., 2020). Likewise, team researchers have viewed shared understanding (e.g., collaboration know-how, knowledge sharing, and shared mental model) as an intermediate status of the team process that precedes team performance (Ellis, 2006; Sung & Choi, 2019; Zhang et al., 2011). Yet the research to date has only addressed the elimination of misunderstandings among team members, which is insufficient to achieve creativity that requires novel understanding. The creative synthesis perspective thus adds to the literature by offering insights into how synthesis and integration among different ideas can be processed in team creativity development endeavors.

Harvey (2014) defined the creative synthesis process as the combination of group members’ cognitive, social, and environmental resources, which can facilitate exceptional creative outcomes (Chen & Adamson, 2015). According to this theory, intricate thoughts can be deconstructed into elemental structures that involve the development of complex connections among previously unrelated concepts. The essence of creative synthesis lies in integrating diverse knowledge inputs from team members to form a shared and inventive understanding that is unique to the team. Creative synthesis and its three key process facilitators constitute the core of its theoretical lens. Creative synthesis is a state of understanding that observes novel connections among the diverse knowledge elements that constitute a problem.

Harvey (2014) described three key process facilitations of creative synthesis—collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas—which can enable the team to achieve the state of creative synthesis. Through these processes, team members can then comprehend and solve collective problems continuously in a novel way, increasing the chances of attaining a final creative solution (Harvey, 2014). Considering causal proximity, it is more sensible to connect the use of collaboration technology to the team processes of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas, rather than creative synthesis as an emergent state, as evidenced by the literature (Bala et al., 2017; Romanow et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2011). Hence, we theorize creative synthesis process facilitation using the three team process dimensions adapted from Harvey (2014) in the IS context.

Collective attention is a form of concentrating on one or more ideas from different perspectives that draws viewpoints from all team members. It encompasses the extent to which members maintain sustained and concentrated attention to exchanged information in their communications (Chen & Adamson, 2015). Having collective attention is a vital underlying assumption for members looking to effectively perform their teamwork (Metiu & Rothbard, 2013). During the collaborative process, team members typically do not merge divergent ideas unless they have been discussed and considered within the team context (Vera & Crossan, 2005). As members may become more deeply engaged with these collectively developed ideas during the process, it is more likely that their focal ideas will be creative and innovative. Prior studies have found that group creativity indeed increases when members attend to each other’s viewpoints, needs, and actions (Hargadon & Bechky, 2006).

Similarity building relies on the extent to which team members identify and emphasize similarities and associations among knowledge elements held by each other (Harvey, 2014). It is an essential component of fostering creativity, especially for teams whose members have diverse expertise, such as IT project teams in the current study. While synthesizing diverse inputs is vital for team creativity, the natural tendency of individuals to react negatively to differing perspectives presents a challenge (Muller & Ulrich, 2013). This issue can be addressed by similarity building, which stresses finding commonalities and connections among different perspectives. Building similarities does not imply consensus, however. Rather, it is a way to reconcile and leverage different ideas to facilitate task completion. Examples of similarity building include techniques that use analogies to identify commonalities among otherwise disconnected ideas and the association of new problems with previous tasks (Langer & Moldoveanu, 2000). In searching for similarities among different ideas, team members are stimulated to learn proactively (Sung & Choi, 2019), not only about their own ideas but also those of other members (Zhang et al., 2011). Research has implicitly confirmed that team members with diversified backgrounds who can relate their own ideas to different ideas from others can generate more creative thinking (Kohn et al., 2011).

Enacting ideas involves team members’ effective experimentation with ideas created by group members before final implementation (Austin, 2018; Harvey, 2014). By provisionally putting these ideas into action, their enactment can go beyond purely cognitive or verbal activities, such as elaborating or identifying the (dis)advantages of a solution (Harvey, 2014). Common ways to enact ideas include drawing or illustrating (Carlile, 2002; Carlile, 2004), building prototypes (Hargadon, 2002; Schrage, 1999), and following up with conversations about how to realize an idea (Ford & Ford, 1995; Harvey, 2014). By allowing such convergence on a tentative representation of an idea or concept, the process of enactment enhances the development of a collective group orientation that can be further elaborated; the enacted ideas can also serve as a valuable mechanism for integrating diversified knowledge held by team members (Majchrzak et al., 2012).

Collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas are the three indicators of creative synthesis process facilitation. For example, a team with superior creative synthesis process facilitation should be able to exhibit every aspect of such facilitation, which may not be the case for less-developed teams. The direction of causality in this process is from the construct of creative synthesis process facilitation to these three indicators. Moreover, these indicators are based on the same nomological framework, which focuses on the process facilitation of creative synthesis. Finally, there is also high covariation among the indicators, as each indicator can influence the others (Harvey, 2014). For example, team members collectively attending a discussion can build a sound basis for understanding the potential similarities among disparate perspectives. Accordingly, collective attraction to the same idea may fuel interactions among team members that can enact an idea. Enacting ideas may influence collective attention by illustrating what is and is not collectively known about an idea. Consequently, following precedent cases (LePine et al., 2008; Mathieu et al., 2006; Maruping & Agarwal, 2004) wherein a single team process construct is reflectively composed of multiple subprocesses, we specify creative synthesis process facilitation as a second-order construct reflected by the three first-order subconstructs of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas in order to more faithfully represent the interrelated nature of the three facilitations and make them operationally measurable, as discussed in the methods sections.

In addition to decoding the three facilitations directly related to team creativity development (Chen & Adamson, 2015), the creative synthesis perspective can serve as the theoretical lens for understanding the mechanisms through which the use of the identified CT features affects team creativity. This theory acknowledges the knowledge inputs of cognitive resources as a necessary precondition of creative synthesis (Harvey, 2014). That is, team knowledge inputs provide needed cognitive resources that can be transformed into creative solutions through creative synthesis process facilitation (Harvey, 2014). CT features can be leveraged to facilitate access to and processing of team knowledge inputs necessary for task completion (Zhang et al., 2011). As such, creative synthesis process facilitation provides a reasonable foundation for theorizing the mediating mechanism in our research. Notably, in doing so, we also contribute to the creative synthesis perspective by offering new understandings of which CT features in relation to team knowledge inputs can influence the creative synthesis process, a linkage currently missing in the literature.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Based on the aforementioned theoretical background, this study builds a research model (Figure 1) that explains the effect of collaboration technology feature use on IT project team creativity through creative synthesis process facilitation.

## Creative Synthesis Process Facilitation and IT Project Team Creativity

We propose that creative synthesis process facilitation is conducive to IT project team creativity. Based on the creative synthesis perspective (Harvey, 2014), the three team processes of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas facilitate the emergence of creative synthesis, where team members have a new and shared understanding that is unique to the team. Guided by this novel understanding, team members can more easily generate a series of creative ideas. In addition to this overall effect, we argue that every aspect of the creative synthesis process facilitation directly influences IT project team creativity.

First, the nature of IT project tasks signifies the role of enacting ideas for team creativity. Idea enactment involves the transformation of abstract concepts and ideas into more tangible and visible manifestations, such as prototypes, representations, and simulations in the early stage of IT development (Lu et al., 2019). In idea enactment, team members typically simulate certain aspects of the proposed concepts, where the prototypes may be completely different from the final product. Enactment not only facilitates feedback from multiple stakeholders in the nascent stage of the project, which is crucial for refining and iterating ideas to make them creative, but also promotes a culture of continuous improvement and learning. Moreover, through enactment, team members can compare and integrate diverse solutions, enhancing their understanding of how their ideas fit into the broader IT project landscape (Harrison & Rouse, 2015). This broader understanding provides opportunities for members to better know each other and take each other’s perspectives into consideration (Harvey, 2014; Lu et al., 2019). For example, at SpaceX, members of the software development team often experiment with alternative designs in the early stages of their work. When modifications are needed, members engage in discussions to deliberate and make decisions collaboratively. Researchers have recognized this approach to be highly creative and productive, as it facilitates extensive exploration or even substantial alterations to the original design (Dove et al., 2023). Hence, enacting ideas can improve IT project team creativity through the process of multiple explorations.

Second, building on similarities that exist among different team members’ perspectives can boost team creativity. A consistent theme emerging from the research examining IT development is the wide heterogeneity of individual expertise, skills, and roles (Kudaravalli et al. 2017). In IT project teams, it is the synthesis of these unique knowledge elements that leads to creative output. This process is challenged, however, by people’s natural inclination to react negatively to perspectives that differ from their own thinking (Muller & Ulrich, 2013). By stressing associations among different perspectives, similarity building can help overcome this challenge. For example, software architects may be attracted to or impressed by certain elements of a business analyst’s knowledge of user experiences and needs. Acceptance of disparate ideas helps create mutual cognitive engagement in others’ perspectives that can benefit the entire team. Moreover, similarity building enables members to extract a higher-level abstract understanding of task-related issues, promoting exploratory learning (Reyt & Wiesenfeld, 2015) and the reframing of problem-solving logic (Sassenberg et al., 2017). Similarity building in IT project teams where members have diversified backgrounds can thus enhance team creativity.

Finally, collective attention can benefit team creativity in IT project teams. In IT project teams, the interdependent nature of the task necessitates a collective focus on developing shared understandings for problem-solving (Tiwana & McLean, 2005). An IT project team usually consists of members playing various roles—including user interface (UI) designers, structural architects, database administrators, quality testers, business analysts, and security specialists—who collaborate to design, develop, and maintain IT solutions. Members with different roles and varied expertise may also have different foci that are more relevant to their own knowledge domain (Majchrzak et al., 2012). For instance, business analysts may gather and analyze requirements and collaborate with UI designers, and structural architects may explore the interaction among different components of the software, which together can help the team achieve a smooth, user-friendly, and engaging experience. Collective attention serves to bridge the distributed focus in IT project teams and further facilitates the synthesis of information among team members. Such increased cognitive engagement in each other’s perspectives can provide opportunities for team members to approach the task and problems from a more comprehensive and nuanced perspective (Milliken et al., 2003).

In the teamwork context, it is the interrelationships among Harvey’s (2014) three subconstructs (i.e., collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas) that can improve team creativity. According to Harvey (2014), team creativity can be achieved through a shared and new framework that can connect team members’ different perspectives. This process could be improved through the mutual influences exerted among the three process facilitations. Common attraction to the same idea can fuel similarity building among team members, which can further contribute to the enactment of an idea. Furthermore, enacting ideas as a collaborative process can help team members attend to an idea through their illustration of what is and is not collectively known about this idea. In this way, the three synthesis processes interact to provide a foundation for novel ideas to develop. We thus propose:

H1: Creativity synthesis process facilitation is positively related to IT project team creativity.

## Collaboration Technology Feature Use and Creative Synthesis Process Facilitation

We next posit that team members’ use of awareness knowledge supporting features—i.e., notification and the visualization of task progress, as described—enhances creative synthesis process facilitation. Notification is the automatic reporting of changes made by members, where the visualization of work progress shows the movement of the entire project and task toward achieving its goal. The use of these features helps team members become more aware of critical changes involving the task as as they happen (Zigurs & Buckland, 1998) as well as the big picture of how the task progresses (Tripp et al., 2016). IT project team members are often assigned modularized tasks that make up their own work focus, which may result in challenges to synthesizing ideas from different members. The use of task awareness features can ameliorate this issue by providing necessary information about the immediate changes of new knowledge and the overall trajectory of a task toward its goal. Such background information enables team members to gain an updated and comprehensive comprehension of action sequences, timing, and temporal boundaries that encompass the participants roles and activities (Espinosa et al., 2007). Consequently, team members are better equipped to articulate and communicate their own ideas and harmonize the contributions of others within a shared framework. Moreover, such updated contextual information serves to mitigate knowledge asymmetry and potential misunderstandings during group interactions, which could negatively affect the creative synthesis process facilitation. Majchrzak et al. (2005) and Zhang et al. (2011) highlighted the significance of knowledge contextualization in communication, particularly in identifying the evolving nature of changes over time. This is especially important for effective collaboration in nonroutine tasks where solutions require the synthesis and integration of different knowledge elements.

In IT project teams, members often possess diverse individualized foci due to the modular work style and the distinct roles played by business analysts, structure architects, and developers. The shared background information derived from the use of awareness knowledge supporting features helps facilitate a seamless focus transition from the individual to the team level, potentially enhancing the team’s capability to form collective attention. Team members in this case can also more easily situate their communications within a shared context, enabling them to better comprehend views and actions from other members. This approach helps foster similarity building using disparate perspectives and activities. Furthermore, awareness knowledge supporting features explicitly show the task progress as a whole and the presence of new knowledge. Consequently, team members would more likely be inspired to investigate new actions and enactments that can push their progress forward (Mason & Bar, 2012). We thus propose:

## H2: The use of awareness knowledge supporting features is positively related to creativity synthesis process facilitation.

We then posit that the use of long-term knowledge supporting features, including syndication and searching, can enhance creative synthesis process facilitation. Specifically, syndication summarizes related topics in one place in a succinct format (Zhang et al., 2011), and the use of syndication features supports relevant information gains and comparisons through scanning. The feature of searching allows team members to quickly and easily find required information upon request (Zhang et al., 2011), where the stronger the capability of the collaboration system to enable smooth and accurate information searching, the greater the amount of high-quality information that team members can access. The use of long-term knowledge supporting features would certainly be expected to improve the capacity of team members to access related information and viewpoints, allowing them to compare and synthesize the collected information to gain a more complete comprehension of tasks, concerns, and possible solutions (Majchrzak et al., 2005).

In IT project teams, the use of long-term knowledge supporting features, either by searching or scanning, can generate a great amount of valuable information or solutions derived from past projects and related topics. Team members can not only directly contrast the advantages and disadvantages of different solutions, but they can also gain more awareness of how disparate pieces of information can be connected and synthesized (Majchrzak et al., 2005). The process of collective attention and building similarity requires some level of understanding related to other team members’ ideas, even if this understanding is rough (Harvey, 2014). The connected informational nodes can serve as such a basis for collective attention and similarity building. In addition, when comparing the effectiveness of different views that can best address the focal aspects of their projects, team members may enact these ideas by making changes to the architecture or running the codes, as this is the straightforward and effective way for IT projects to move forward (Austin, 2018). We thus propose:

## H3: The use of long-term knowledge supporting features is positively related to creativity synthesis process facilitation.

Creative synthesis process facilitation also benefits from the use of transitional knowledge supporting features such as recombination and incomplete knowledge entry. The feature of recombination enables team members to try out different configurations of existing ideas from other members, thus encouraging them to explore alternative ideas and combinations while building on each other’s contributions (Faraj et al., 2011). The use of incomplete knowledge entry, where team members are able to enter underdeveloped knowledge and make corrections to them at a later time (Zhang et al., 2011), is a provisional and exploratory procedure that not only allows contributors to explore novel alternatives but also invites other team members to make ongoing contributions (Levina, 2005). In using transitional knowledge supporting features, team members can play with the combination of diverse ideas, either by drawing on past projects or inviting fresh inputs. By engaging in this dynamic process, team members can discern how their ideas intersect with or complement those contributed by others. Recognizing these connections can also help team members understand the broader context and enhances the potential for the synthesis of various viewpoints into a cohesive whole.

Specifically, in IT project teams, the use of the recombination feature can support the system architecture engineers and developers when experimenting with existing architectures, code snippets, and functions that can address certain aspects of their own projects. By using incomplete knowledge entry, team members can also create a new but unfinished template and invite the whole team to further explore final solutions. As a result, the use of the recombination and incomplete knowledge entry features can directly benefit idea enactment. Previous studies have characterized these yet-to-be completed digital artifacts of transitional knowledge as epistemic objects (Ewenstein & Whyte, 2009), which can serve as catalysts that motivate team members to delve into improving and finalizing artifacts. In the process of experimenting with different combinations of ideas, team members can also better identify how their inputs interconnect. Therefore, the enacted ideas or prototype from idea enactment can, in turn, help team members become more attentive to the focal task and facilitate the identification of similarities. Thus, we propose:

H4: The use of transitional knowledge supporting features is positively related to creativity synthesis process facilitation.

## The Mediating Role of the Creative Synthesis Process Facilitation

The literature has found that the use of collaboration technology features can significantly improve project team performance through proficient knowledge management and streamlined team collaboration (Bala et al., 2017; Majchrzak et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2011). Moreover, studies have suggested the benefits and contributions of collaboration technology to creativity development, as evidenced by findings that the positive effect of collaboration technology use on team performance is particularly salient for innovative tasks (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2009). However, the existing literature lacks a formally theorized mechanism to address how and why collaboration technologies can be effectively used to augment IT project team creativity. We propose the creative synthesis perspective as a plausible explanation and posit collaboration technology use as a multifaceted team input that not only provides various sorts of team knowledge but also functions as an essential coordination channel. Such inputs drive the intermediate team process of creative synthesis process facilitation, which ultimately influences team creativity as the output (Mathieu et al., 2008). Specifically, we contend that creative synthesis process facilitation is a key mediator that transmits influences of collaboration technology use on IT project team creativity in the following ways.

First, the use of awareness knowledge supporting features for notification and visualization improves creative synthesis process facilitation by drawing on and strengthening the shared attention of team members toward project status and task progress (Majchrzak et al., 2005; Tripp et al., 2016). This shared focus on task progress with more contextualized knowledge serves as a common base through which members can synthesize relevant information and diverse inputs, and jointly cultivate new and emerging ideas that lead to team creativity. Hence, we propose that creative synthesis process facilitation mediates the relationship between the use of awareness knowledge supporting features and IT project team creativity.

Second, the use of long-term knowledge supporting features for syndication and searching improves creative synthesis process facilitation by enhancing accessibility to more information of higher relevance and quality, which enables the comparison of diverse perspectives. This empowers team members to construct a more comprehensive understanding of the task, which helps synthesize differing viewpoints and stimulate the generation of innovative ideas (Harvey, 2014; Tiwana & McLean, 2005). Hence, we propose that creative synthesis process facilitation mediates the relationship between the use of long-term knowledge features and team creativity.

Third, the use of transitional knowledge supporting features for recombination and incomplete knowledge entry can improve creative synthesis process facilitation by accommodating preliminary knowledge inputs for further exploration, composition, and evaluation of possible solutions to problems (Levina, 2005; Shneiderman, 2007). By employing transitional knowledge supporting features, team members can collectively try out and assess different combinations, thereby enhancing the potential for idea refinement and directly contributing to heightened team creativity (Shneiderman, 2007). Moreover, the use of these features aids team members’ recognition of the interconnectedness of various elements and ideas held among themselves and in relation to the overall project. This understanding can foster a more comprehensive synthesis of diverse perspectives, consequently stimulating the generation of novel ideas. Hence, we posit that creative synthesis process facilitation also serves as a mediator in the relationship between the use of transitional knowledge supporting features and IT project team creativity. In summary, we hypothesize:

H5: The use of (a) awareness knowledge supporting features, (b) long-term knowledge features, and (c) transitional knowledge features is positively related to IT project team creativity through the mediation of creative synthesis process facilitation.

## Methods

## Data and Sample

We conducted an online survey to collect data from a leading information and communication technology (ICT) solutions provider in China. A total data sample of 62 IT project teams was obtained. The participating corporation offers a wide range of services, including consulting, design, and software development, as well as testing and maintaining services for its customers in various industries such as telecommunication, digital finance, intelligent manufacturing, and the internet. The company has a core value of technology innovation and places great emphasis on creativity in their work. Corporate managers adopt the working form of IT project teams to provide customer service, with most members located at headquarters and a few operating at customer sites. Before data collection, two of the authors of this study conducted interviews with eight employees from this company, including managers, team leaders, and team members, to ensure that they viewed creativity as important to their daily work and relied on collaboration technologies for teamwork. These interviews confirmed that their teams were equipped with collaboration technologies, such as email, instant message, video- and audio-conferencing systems (WEBEX), team Wiki (CONFLUENCE), project management tools (JIRA), and integrated collaborative tools (SLACK). The IT project teams from this corporation thus provided an appropriate empirical context to collect data for this study.

To minimize common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003), data were collected from two sources—team members and team leaders—with the help of the company’s human resource management (HRM) department. In practice, an employee may be a member of multiple teams. To address this issue, after communication with the HRM department, employees were placed in teams where they served as core members. The survey first presented the name of the project team in which they were placed and explicitly stated that the survey questions were about their experience in this specific team. These procedures eliminated redundant survey responses, as employees appeared in only one IT project team and answered the questionnaire only once. Finally, we distributed questionnaires to 62 IT project teams, consisting of 566 individual team members and 62 team leaders.

## Data Collection Procedures

As the survey was conducted in China using the Chinese language, while the measurements were initially in English, a back-translation process (Brislin, 1986) was used to ensure that the English and Chinese versions of the scale were comparable and compatible. After the completion of the final Chinese version of the measurement, the survey was created and published through an online survey platform that is widely used in China. Data were collected from team members and leaders, where team leaders provided information on team size and team length (in months) and rated the creativity of their teams. Team members provided their demographic information and reported their perceptions of the team regarding collaboration technology use, creative synthesis process facilitation, knowledge sharing, and information elaboration.

To match members with their leaders and protect the privacy of participants, the following procedures were adopted. First, staff from the HRM department gave a unique number to each participant and provided this number to researchers. Importantly, the numbers were categorized to fit the chosen teams to which the participants belonged. Researchers also used this number in the survey instead of names to ensure participant privacy. Second, an individualized questionnaire was created along with a unique link for each participant labeled by their number, resulting in a list with the number, email, and link to the questionnaire. The HRM department then sent each number (representing individual participants) an email using their internal email system. The email explained the objective of the survey, confirmed participant privacy, and provided the link each participant should click to start their online surveys. We sent four follow-up emails requesting that participants complete the survey. Upon finishing data collection, we provided an aggregated report to the company concerning the creative performance of the IT project teams, without giving detailed data of each participant; only the research team had access to the survey data of individuals. Data from 485 individual members and 62 leaders out of the 62 surveyed teams were finally collected. The overall useful response rate was 89.97%, with each team having a response rate of more than 80%. This high response rate is due to the strong support that corporation management provided for the research project.

## Measurements

Measurement items were adapted from previous studies with established scales in place whenever possible to ensure measurement validity and reliability. Minor adjustments were made to ensure that all measurement items were appropriate in our research context. Items were measured using a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. The measurement scale for team creativity consisting of four items was adapted from Gong et al. (2013) and Shin and Zhou (2007). The constructs for the use of awareness knowledge supporting features, the use of long-term knowledge features, and the use of transitional knowledge features are specified as second-order formative constructs with two first-order reflective subconstructs. We specify each of these CT features as a second-order construct because the paired subconstructs are conceptually distinguished from each other and collectively constitute their corresponding high-order construct. Awareness knowledge supporting features include two subconstructs: notification and visualization of work progress; long-term knowledge supporting features consist of two subconstructs: syndication and searching; and transitional knowledge supporting features also include two subconstructs: incomplete knowledge entry and recombination. The measurement items for features of notification, syndication, searching, and incomplete knowledge entry were adapted from Zhang et al. (2011). Each feature was originally assessed with a single measurement item. We expanded this to three items for the measurement of notification, syndication, and incomplete knowledge entry, and four items for searching, by developing new items based on their respective definition. The scale for the visualization of the work progress feature had three items, adapted from Tripp et al. (2016).

Creative synthesis process facilitation is specified as a second-order reflective construct, with three first-order subconstructs: collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas. As this study is the first to operationalize the three key process facilitations out of the creative synthesis perspective, we followed the procedures suggested by Hoehle and Venkatesh (2015), MacKenzie et al. (2011), and Moore and Benbasat (1991) in developing their measurement items. As there is no existing measure for the feature of recombination, we also followed the same procedure to generate its measurement. A detailed illustration of the measurement development is presented in Appendix D.

Several control variables known to affect team creativity were also incorporated. The control variables include team size, the length of time since the team was established (labeled as team length), gender diversity, expertise diversity, average age level, average education level, knowledge sharing, and information elaboration <sup>2</sup> (Gong et al., 2013; Hoever et al., 2012; Zhou & Hoever, 2014). Both expertise diversity (using the proxy of educational background diversity) and gender diversity were measured with Blau’s (1977) index (1 − ∑ p<sup>2</sup>). The measurement for knowledge sharing comes from Choi et al. (2010) and the scale for information elaboration is from Kearney and Gebert (2009). As some of the teams surveyed occasionally had one or two members working at different locations, we also controlled for whether the team was distributed to different locations (1 = distributed, 0 = not distributed).

## Data Analysis and Results

We adopted a three-step approach to analyze the data. First, the measurement model was tested to examine the validity and reliability of the scale. Second, as the data to measure the team level constructs were reported by individuals, an aggregation test was conducted. Finally, the results of an ordinary least squares (OLS) analysis were reported.

## Measurement Model: Validity and Reliability

As assessments of the measurement model of formative constructs and reflective constructs are different, we assessed and reported the results, respectively. The convergent validity of the reflective constructs was assessed using two indicators (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). All values of the square root of average variance extracted (AVE) were greater than 0.50, and the loadings of the items on their own constructs were greater than 0.70, both suggesting satisfactory convergent validity. The discriminant validity of reflective constructs was also examined in two ways. First, the construct correlations were found to be lower than the square root of the AVEs of their constructs. The largest correlation was 0.60, less than the recommended threshold of 0.71 (MacKenzie et al., 2011). Second, all the items loaded well on their own construct and less well on other constructs. Thus, the results confirm good discriminant validity.

The construct validity of the formative constructs, including the use of awareness knowledge supporting features, long-term knowledge supporting features, and transitional knowledge supporting features, was assessed using latent indicator weights (Petter et al., 2007), which demonstrate the relative importance of the indicators for the formative construct (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). As shown in Table 4, all indicator weights were found to be significant. This indicates that all items made important absolute contributions to the formative construct. Hence, all indicators should be retained (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009) to maintain the completeness of the content coverage of the construct. All indicators were therefore retained in the following data analysis.

The reliability of the reflective constructs was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (Nunnally, 1975) and composite reliability (CR) (MacKenzie et al., 2011), where both had a threshold of 0.70. Results in Table 5 show that all the values of Cronbach’s alpha and CR were greater than 0.70, confirming good reliability for the reflective constructs. Reliability of the formative constructs was assessed by examining their multicollinearity (Sun et al., 2012). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check potential multicollinearity problems. Results in Table 4 show that the VIF ranged from 2.509 to 4.923, lower than the suggested threshold of 10.0 (Hair et al., 1998; Mathieson et al. 2001), thus indicating an acceptable reliability of the formative constructs.

## Aggregation Test

As the data of team variables were collected from individual respondents, we first needed to test whether it would be appropriate to average individuals’ responses to represent team-level variables using indicators of $r _ { w g }$ and intraclass correlation (ICC). The indicator of $r _ { w g }$ measures the degree of consensus among members in each team (Chan, 1998; Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). ICC has two indicators, ICC1 and ICC2, where the former measures the between-group variance in individual responses and the latter assesses the reliability of group-level means. The values of $r _ { w g }$ and ICC were assessed on each reflective construct.

Table 6 shows that the values of $r _ { w g }$ for all the constructs were larger than 0.70, which is the threshold for $r _ { w g }$ (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). The minimum requirement for the value of ICC1 is 0.05 (James, 1982). All values of ICC1 in the present study were much greater than this requirement. A widely adopted threshold for ICC2 is greater than 0.70. However, Klein and Kozlowski (2000, p. 225) pointed out that ICC(2) is essentially a function of ICC(1) that is adjusted for group size and gave an example to illustrate how ICC2 is susceptible to average team size (K). Specifically, if ICC1 equals 0.2, ICC2 will be 0.56 when K is 5, and 0.71 when K is 20. Hence, although the values of ICC2 in the current study slightly fall below the threshold of 0.70, we expect this index is suppressed by the small group size in our sample (K = 7.82), which is also the case for many previous studies (Hoever et al., 2018; Homan et al., 2015; Parke et al., 2022). Addressing this limitation, Crossley et al. (2013) suggested that if both the values of $r _ { w g }$ and ICC1 meet the requirements, the aggregation is still appropriate. We therefore conclude that aggregating the individual responses to the team level in the current study is appropriate.

## Preliminary Analysis

Our study design incorporates recommendations to alleviate common method bias following Podsakoff et al. (2003). Three remedies were procedurally adopted. First, data of predictor and criterion variables were collected from two different sources—team members and the team leader. Second, to improve measurement items, we followed rigorous procedures of pretest and adaptation to the context. Third, the order of the questions was randomized in the final questionnaire. Statistically, we performed a Harmon’s single factor test and marker variable analysis to assess common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The results show that common method bias is not a threat to the present study (Appendix B).

Hierarchical regression analysis with the technique of bootstrapping in SPSS 20.0 was used to test the hypotheses. Following the practice in the literature (Lu & Ramamurthy 2011; Zhou et al., 2022), we first calculated a single latent factor score for each reflective construct by averaging the scores of their multiple indicators. Then, the second-order formative constructs (use of the awareness supporting feature, use of the long-term knowledge supporting feature, and use of the transitional knowledge feature) were represented by the weighted means of their first-order subconstructs. The summated scale is a good representative of the original set of items when reliability and validity of the constructs have been established (Hair et al., 1998; Yuan et al., 1997).

## Hypotheses Tests

Table 7 presents the descriptive statistics and Table 8 shows the results of the hierarchical regression analysis. Model 5 in Table 8 confirms that creative synthesis is positively related to team creativity (β = 0.47, p < 0.001), supporting H1. Model 2 indicates that the use of awareness knowledge supporting features is positively related to creative synthesis at a nonsignificant level (β = 0.14, n.s.); thus, H2 is not supported. The use of long-term knowledge supporting features and the use of transitional knowledge supporting features are both significantly related to creative synthesis process facilitation (β = 0.18, p < 0.05 and β = 0.26, p < 0.001, respectively), supporting H3 and H4.

To test the indirect effect of creative synthesis process facilitation on the relationship between collaboration technology feature use and team creativity, we leveraged PROCESS in SPSS version 20 (Edwards & Lambert, 2007; Muller et al., 2005). Results are shown in Table 9. The indirect effect size of creative synthesis in the relationship between the use of awareness knowledge supporting features and team creativity is 0.08, with a 95% confidence interval of (-0.19, 0.74) containing 0, indicating that H5a is not supported. The indirect effect size of creative synthesis in the relationship between the use of longterm knowledge supporting features and team creativity is 0.13, with a 95% confidence interval of (0.02, 0.61), supporting H5b. The indirect effect of creative synthesis in the relationship between the use of transitional knowledge supporting features and team creativity is 0.28, with a 95% confidence interval (0.13, 0.97). H5c is therefore supported.

## Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate what collaboration technology features can be used to improve IT project team creativity and to identify the mechanisms through which they exert influence. We built a research model that examines the mediating mechanism of creative synthesis in the relationship between the use of three sets of collaboration technology features and IT project team creativity. We tested seven hypotheses, finding support for five of them. This study verifies that creative synthesis process facilitation— consisting of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas—positively affects IT project team creativity and that the use of the three set features of collaboration technologies enhances team creative synthesis process facilitation. Furthermore, our findings show that creative synthesis process facilitation mediates the relationship between the use of collaboration technology features and IT project team creativity, suggesting that the use of collaboration technologies contributes to IT project team creativity through the mechanism of creative synthesis process facilitation. These findings are generally consistent with our expectations in the proposed research model.

Unexpectedly, the positive effect of the use of the awareness knowledge supporting feature on creative synthesis process facilitation (H2) and the mediating effect of creative synthesis process facilitation in this proposed positive relationship (H5a) are not supported. This indicates that the role of collaboration technology is less salient when supporting task awareness for fueling creative synthesis process facilitation in IT project teams. One reasonable conjecture for this insignificant outcome is that in IT project teams, members may also concurrently rely on face-to-face interaction in combination with collaboration technologies to acquire team knowledge. The existence of multiple avenues for accessing awareness knowledge would therefore diminish the importance of a specific method—the use of awareness knowledge supporting features in collaboration technology—for creative synthesis process facilitation. That is, face-to-face interaction serves as a substitution for collaboration technology use in acquiring awareness knowledge. This may not be the case for the access of long-term knowledge and transitional knowledge, as these two types of knowledge are more complicated and it is thus less likely that the two forms of knowledge acquisition will substitute for or even complement each other. Following this logic, future research could investigate whether the extent of reliance on face-to-face interaction moderates the relationship between the use of collaboration technology features and creative synthesis process facilitation.

For the control variables, the distribution of the team, team diversity, and information elaboration were found to be positively related to team creativity, which is consistent with findings from the literature (Hoever et al., 2012; Zhou & Hoever, 2014). Somewhat unexpectedly, knowledge sharing was not found to be significantly related to team creativity, which resonates with the meta-analysis showing that information sharing is not always beneficial for team outcomes (Mesmer-Magnus et al., 2011). This finding generally makes sense, in that knowledge sharing is necessary but not sufficient for team creativity that requires new understandings to emerge from the process. The outcome corroborates our key thesis that creative synthesis process facilitation is a much more relevant mechanism in terms of its impact on team creativity.

## Theoretical Implications

This study makes several important theoretical contributions to the literature. First, it contributes to the IT project work literature by identifying collaboration technology features and theorizing the mechanism through which their use affects IT project team creativity. Although team creativity has been established as a research topic of concern in the management (Zhou & Hoever, 2014) and IS (Tiwana & McLean, 2005) fields, there is limited understanding of how collaboration technologies can be used to promote team creativity. To address this research gap, we provide nuanced insights into which collaboration technology features can be used to improve IT project team creativity and how they can achieve it—i.e., by extending team knowledge framework to the team creativity context and integrating it with IS-novel creativity synthesis theory. In doing so, we develop and operationalize the novel construct of creative synthesis process facilitation as a secondorder reflective construct with the three subconstructs of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas, and contextualize the impact of creativity synthesis process facilitation in the IT project team context. Therefore, we provide subtle insights into team creativity development process at the microlevel (Chen & Adamson, 2015). Further, by subjecting the creative synthesis perspective to empirical investigation, this study also makes an empirical contribution by developing and validating measures for creative synthesis process facilitation, thereby providing useful instruments for future empirical research (Harvey, 2014). These results largely confirm the importance of creative synthesis process facilitation which continues to hold when teams focus on IT projects and members rely on CTs for teamwork.

Second, this study contributes to Harvey’s (2014) creative synthesis theory by developing a set of theoretically derived collaboration technology features as antecedents of creative synthesis process facilitation in IT project teams. Since the inception and development of the theory, no study has examined the drivers of creative synthesis, which is likely due to the topic’s complexity and novelty. Our findings confirm that the use of collaboration technology generates various kinds of knowledge—one of the most important resources needed for creative synthesis process facilitation to function (Harvey,

2014). Drawing on the team knowledge literature and our contextualized analysis of team knowledge related to team creativity, this study identifies three sets of CT features, theorizes their relationships with creative synthesis process facilitation, and provides empirical verification. As such, this study is among the first to systematically identify the antecedents of creative synthesis process facilitation, specifically from the angle of collaboration technologies. The outcome of insignificant findings concerning the relationship between the use of awareness knowledge supporting features and creative synthesis process facilitation also indicates that there may be boundaries for the explanatory power of the collaboration technology perspective’s influence on creative synthesis.

Third, this study contributes to the literature on collaboration technologies. Previous studies have examined how the use of collaboration technology at the feature level affects team processes instrumental to team performance, such as absorptive capacity, team coordination, knowledge management activities, and communication (Majchrzak et al., 2005; Tiwana & McLean, 2005; Zhang et al., 2011). Limited attention has been paid to understanding how the use of collaboration technology enhances team creativity. We therefore take a further step by identifying collaboration technology feature sets that are theoretically relevant to team creativity, thereby extending the literature on the role of collaboration technologies to the team creativity research, in general, and to IT project team creativity, in particular. Moreover, by focusing on the use of collaboration technology at the feature level, the black box of how technologies can exert effects is opened, providing further evidence in support of the feature-centric view of technologies and their applications (Jasperson et al., 2005).

## Practical Implications

This study has significance for practitioners, especially in the post-COVID-19 era, in a wide range of industry and service fields where employees work in teams that rely on collaboration technologies for communication and task completion. First, it reveals team processes that can directly predict and enhance team creativity and suggests that managers and team leaders should provide encouragement and directional suggestions when team members are performing tasks involving creativity. For example, team leaders could direct members toward certain topics or tasks in their communications to develop and maintain collective attention. Team leaders could help guide team members to actively search for associations and similarities among each other’s experiences, perspectives, and expertise. Managers could also encourage team members to explore, enact, and experiment with their ideas, when talking to members about how to realize these ideas would also help them to better conceptualize and realize the ideas and their potential applications.

The study demonstrates that the use of collaboration technologies can provide IT project teams and their organizations with additional value pertaining to creativity, in addition to supporting general collaboration, which is the designed purpose of such technologies, as verified by existing studies (Bala et al., 2017; Zhang, 2011). Hence, to increase the benefits gained through collaboration technology investment, organizations should pay attention to the role of collaboration technologies in fueling team creativity for IT project teams that rely heavily on collaboration technologies for teamwork. To facilitate team creativity development, organizations could leverage or design collaboration technologies that have certain advantageous features. For instance, the feature of information syndication could be incorporated and established by organizations to provide team members with a clear understanding of what knowledge has been accumulated. The feature of incomplete knowledge entry could also be used to solicit continuous inputs from team members, providing a bridge to identify similarities among different ideas and perspectives. Moreover, training based on sound theoretical foundations and human resource practices could be provided to employees to help them become familiar with features that can improve team creativity development processes.

## Limitations and Future Directions

This research has certain limitations that can inform future research. First, we conceptualized creative synthesis process facilitation as a singular construct, as reflected by the three subconstructs of collective attention, similarity building, and enacting ideas. There are underlying mutual influences among the three-process facilitation, which is an interesting point that deserves more investigation. Future research could more formally examine the underlying iterative nature among the three subconstructs. Second, while this study demonstrates the general applicability of the framework, future research could examine task characteristics as a boundary condition of the theory. IT project teams may encounter tasks with different characteristics. For example, research has long acknowledged that some software development tasks have clear “right” and “wrong” solutions, whereas others do not (Kudaravalli et al. 2017). This captures the task characteristic of intellectiveness versus judgment (Leung & Wang, 2015). Future research might consider how the three aspects of creative synthesis process facilitation unfold differently in intellective versus judgmental projects.

Third, the current study primarily focuses on collaboration technology features as delineated in the extant literature and prevailing in IT project teams. Future research could consider emerging features novel to collaboration technologies that are more unique to team creativity development. In addition, while our study takes a cognitive perspective to identify collaboration technology features as antecedents to creative synthesis process facilitation, future research might examine other collaboration technology-related antecedents from the other two perspectives of social and environmental considerations proposed by Harvey (2014). Fourth, we used data on team creativity reported by team leaders. Although it is common to use team leaders’ assessment on team creativity (Gong et al., 2013), there will inevitably be some level of bias due to personal subjectivity. For example, team leaders might exaggerate or underestimate the creative level of teams. Yet considering that IT project teams—unlike brainstorming teams in lab experiments (Kavadias & Sommer, 2009)—do not generally have objective standards for measuring team creativity, limited additional data could be accessed to measure the level of team creativity in an unbiased manner. Finally, the survey data used to test our hypotheses are cross-sectional, meaning that the causal effects could not be tested. Further studies could consider the longitudinal design of data collection.

## Conclusion

Organizations are increasingly leveraging IT project teams to manage and perform innovation development work. Yet the literature on team creativity has offered few insights into what collaboration technology features can be used to promote IT project team creativity and their corresponding influencing mechanisms. This study develops and tests a novel research model that identifies three sets of collaboration technology features and explains the mechanism through which they improve IT project team creativity based on the creative synthesis perspective. Our study thereby provides both theoretical and practical implications for collaboration technology use in developing IT project team creativity.

## Common Method Bias

While we collected data from different sources, including team leaders and team members, to procedurally alleviate the concern for common method bias (CMB), the key variables including the independent variables and mediating variables were all self-reported by member employees. The data collected from the same source of team members may thus cause CMB to be suspect. Therefore, we also conducted statistical analysis to assess CMB. First, we performed a Harmon’s single factor test following Podsakoff et al. (2003). The results show that the first component in the model explained 28.06% of variance in the principal component analysis, much lower than the threshold of 50%. Second, per the recommended procedures, we performed the correlational marker variable analysis (Lindell & Whitney, 2001; Liang et al., 2019; Malhotra et al., 2006; Romanow et al., 2018). Following Lindell and Whitney (2001), the second-lowest positive correlation was identified as a conservative estimate of CMB (r = 0.02). The results of adjusted estimates with marker variables show that the adjusted correlations changed slightly in magnitude, with an average of 0.013, and remained unchanged in significance. Therefore, we conclude that CMB is not a major concern in the current study.

## Measurement Development Process

For the constructs that did not have existing measures, we followed the procedures suggested by Moore and Benbasat (1991), Mackenzie et al (2011) and Hoehle and Venkatesh (2015) to develop items. The constructs with self-developed measures include collective attention, similarity building, enacting ideas, and the feature use of recombination. We first collected items from the literature and then conducted interviews with five developers from two distributed software development teams for additional behaviors related to the constructs. We then conducted card sorting and a pilot study to establish measurement validity and reliability. Figure D1 shows the procedures used for developing new constructs of this study.

In operationalizing the constructs, we also referred to related literature. The primary work is Harvey (2014). In addition, we referred to literature that includes narratives on topics related to the respective constructs we aim to operationalize. For instance, to operationalize collective attention, we referred to narratives on collective focus of attention in communication (Metiu & Rothbard, 2013), and shared focus (Dennis et al., 2008). To operationalize similarity building, we referred to narratives about building on the ideas of others (Kohn et al., 2011). Considering that enacting ideas is enabled by ITs, we referred to the literature on the use of (IT) artifacts for collaborative practices (Majchrzak et al., 2012; Carlile, 2002, 2004) and also one paper concerning idea enactment at the individual level (Lu et al., 2019). To create the items for the feature of recombination, we referred to the collaboration technology literature (Majchrzak et al., 2005; Faraj et al., 2011). Table D1 shows the summary of the reviewed references. Items were created from the literature review and then discussed with five member from two distributed teams. After screening and modifying, 15 items of synthesis processes and three items of recombination were retained for card sorting.

Two rounds of card sorting were conducted to test construct validity and eliminate any potential ambiguity of items that are newly developed in the present research (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). After measurement item collection for both the established scale and the newly developed scale, eight participants were recruited to conduct two rounds of carding sorting. Four judges were recruited for each round of card sorting involving IT use constructs. In each round, the judges were different and independent. In the first round, all items were listed in an Excel file and sent to four judges, who were asked to categorize them according to the content of each item and then name each category. Based on th results of the first round of card sorting, the items were adjusted. In the second round, an Excel file with each construct definition and all randomly organized items was sent to a different group of four judges, who were asked to categorize each item to the listed constructs based on the given definition.

Two agreement measures of Cohen’s kappa and the placement ratio were adopted to evaluate the reliability of sorting results. Cohen’s kappa was used to assess the extent of agreement between two judges by taking the chance of agreement into consideration and the acceptable threshold for Cohen’s kappa was 0.65 (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The placement ratio assesses the reliability of the classification scheme and the validity of the items newly developed in the present thesis by counting how many items are correctly placed into the target category or construct. A higher percentage means that judges are more likely to place items within the intended theoretical constructs.

We first conducted the card sorting procedures for creative synthesis process facilitation and team creativity (Table D2). In the first round, three judges created seven categories and one judge created six categories. The average value of the interjudge raw agreement was 0.88; the average value of Cohen’s kappa was 0.86. The overall placement ratio of items within the target constructs was 91%. Then judges independently labeled and defined each category. Small changes were made to the items to remove ambiguity. In the second round, a different group of four judges was recruited for the card sorting task. A “too ambiguous/doesn’t fit” category was provided to ensure that the judges did not force any item into a particular category they found to be improper. The average value of the interjudge raw agreement showed an increase to 0.90. The average value of Cohen’s kappa was 0.89. The overall placement ratio of items within the target constructs was 94%.
