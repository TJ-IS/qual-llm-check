# Information as a diference: toward a subjective theory of information

Earl H. McKinney Jr<sup>a</sup> and Charles J. Yoos II<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Accounting and MIS, Bowling Green State University, Bowling Green, OH, USA; <sup>b</sup>Technische Hochschule Deggendorf, Deggendorf, Germany

## ABSTRACT

We propose a di<sup>f</sup>erence theory of information that extends Gregory Bateson’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition that information is any di<sup>f</sup>erence that makes a di<sup>f</sup>erence. Information arises from conception of di<sup>f</sup>erence, criteria, and meta criteria. Individuals perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences and conceive information from those di<sup>f</sup>erences. A <sup>fi</sup>rst di<sup>f</sup>erence is a perceived di<sup>f</sup>erence that leads to a second di<sup>f</sup>erence – a changing mind. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory provides a theoretical foundation for distinguishing the terms data and information. Additionally, it describes how individuals inform in uncertainty by applying and sometimes recursively applying criteria levels. We apply the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory to IS practice and research contexts.

## 1. Introduction

Information is a semantic chameleon (Thom, 1975), an umbrella term covering almost any content, a label thoughtlessly applied (Travica, 2011). It is frequently used as an unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed, re<sup>fl</sup>exive, all-purpose term applied to a wide variety of problems (McKinney & Yoos, 2010). Nobody knows what information means despite its widespread use and relevance to business (Martin, 1995). And, as Francis Bacon observed, accepting terms from conversational use limits the progress of science (Artz, 2016). In spite of these problems, the meaning of the term information is largely ignored in IS research (Boell, 2017; Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011; Kettinger, Zhang, & Chang, 2013; Mingers & Standing, 2017).

No term is more fundamental and vital to IS than information. Many have called information more important than data (Lee, 2010), more important than the artefact (Glazer, 1991), and foundational to IS (Beynon-Davies, 2009; Kettinger et al., 2013). Information forms most of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant compound labels in IS – information technology, information processing, information management, information sharing, information acquisition, and IS. Finally, Artz (2016) suggests that a well-formed theory of information is necessary to bound IS. Our reliance on this term is growing in the age of Big Data and analytics. A comprehensive theory of information would enhance the legitimacy of IS as a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c domain (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011), improve IS development and use and guide IS research (Lee, 2010).

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory presented here is a Type II theory in Gregor’s (2006) theory taxonomy: its goal is to explain and understand. As a Type II theory, it neither predicts nor o<sup>f</sup>ers testable predictions or propositions; rather it addresses how phenomena occur. Type II theories should be new, interesting, and explain something that was imperfectly understood beforehand. Such theories are judged by whether they provide new or intriguing insights. A Type II theory should:

show others how the world may be viewed in a certain way, to bring about an altered understanding of how things are or why they are, they are a device of sudden enlightenment, they startle the reader into satori. . .not to generalize, instead, as a surprise machine aimed at clearing away conventional notions to make room for artful and exciting insights. (Gregor, 2006, p. 624)

Examples include Orlikowski (1992), Porra (1999), Lamb and Kling (2003), and Levina and Ross (2003). Type II theories are particularly valuable in a design science such as IS (Rowe, 2012).

Before we present the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, we assign the word data to the tokens processed by machines. This leaves only the semantic aspect of information, that of meaning, to which our theory applies. We present the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory of information then apply the theory to IS practice and research. We conclude with limitations and future work and an explanation of why this theory of information is preferable. The Appendix is included that summarises the concept of information, from its early origins to its current widespread use.

## 2.1. The token and syntax views

Tokens, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Mckinney and Yoos (2010), are the undi<sup>f</sup>erentiated commodity of bits that are processed by machines. Data from Greek, diaphora means di<sup>f</sup>erence; while the Latin root, dare means to give (Checkland & Holwell, 1998). From these foundations, data is often de<sup>fi</sup>ned as facts (Galland, 1982), numbers that stand for an object or event (Knight & Silk, 1990), measures, or signs that relate to events (Martin & Powell, 1992). The problem with the token view is that the process by which tokens become information is unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed. A common example is placing data in a relevant context, such as an IS report, and that somehow causes information to be created. The creator of that information is unspeci-<sup>fi</sup>ed. This hidden process is unsatisfying, at best. Using data to reference tokens and reserving the term information for semantics will reduce confusion in the IS community. This di<sup>f</sup>erentiation is also suggested by Beynon-Davies (2009) and Travica (2011), and is consistent with most explanations that understand information as more than data (Checkland & Holwell, 2006; Kettinger & Li, 2010; Kettinger et al., 2013).

The syntax view de<sup>fi</sup>nes information as the measured relationship among tokens (Mckinney & Yoos,

2010). On this view, the absence of syntax is randomness or entropy. The classic work of Shannon and Weaver (1949) on the mathematical theory of communication de<sup>fi</sup>nes information as the di<sup>f</sup>erence in entropy between two states. Information reduces uncertainty. In the social world of IS, quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of uncertainty can be di<sup>fi</sup>cult, and at times misleading; as a result, the syntax view of information is less appropriate for IS than a semantic de<sup>fi</sup>nition.

## 2.2.1. Representation

A sign represents information about an object for a particular observer. A transaction record is information about the purchase for the analyst (Mckinney & Yoos, 2010). An emoticon is information about something else to an interpretant (Beynon-Davies, 2018). Representations always simplify objective reality, literally “re-presenting” it. The best, most useful, information is the most accurate and speci<sup>fi</sup>c representation of that object (Boland, 1987). Dretske (1981) speci<sup>fi</sup>es further – that information is the regularity, the causal dependence, of the sign on the object as in smoke (a sign) depends on <sup>fi</sup>re.

The representation view has several advantages. Its widely used theories and de<sup>fi</sup>nitions are succinct and related to common intuitions about information; it helps observers to provide a useful account of important aspects of their environment. The representation view is the most widely used view of information in business, computer science, psychology, and IS. In IS, common representations are typologies, entity–relationship diagrams, and database records. To Weber (2003), representation is the raison d’etre of information systems. One limitation of the representation view is that the object or observer is assumed, and when they are, the sign becomes the information by some unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed process as in the token view. Another limitation is the di<sup>f</sup>erence between accurate representations and misrepresentations and how misrepresentations, particularly of concepts, can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and corrected. For example, if one analyst evaluates recent security lapses (signs) and concludes risk is low, and another considers the same signs but considers the risk high, is one a misrepresentation?

## 2.2.2. DIKW

The most common theory about information in IS textbooks is the DIKW theory based on the work of Langefors (1973) and Acko<sup>f</sup> (1989). This theory o<sup>f</sup>ers speci<sup>fi</sup>c de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of data, information, and knowledge depicting them as a pyramid where information is based on data, knowledge is based on information, and wisdom is based on knowledge.

Information is the meaning that results from data processing, commonly, information is processed data. Information rests on data, is inferred from data. Information and data are objectively true, as they correspond to real or abstract objects. Data are true and all information and knowledge are built on true elements. Information answers who, what, where, and when type questions (Acko<sup>f</sup>, 1989). Knowledge for the informant is an organisation or structure of information and these knowledge structures have a longevity that data and information do not have.

In DIKW, information is neither formally de<sup>fi</sup>ned nor made mutually exclusive from data and knowledge (Kettinger & Li, 2010; Travica, 2011). Further, DIKW theory o<sup>f</sup>ers few explanations about the key terms used to describe information – meaning and context. This failure contributes to ambiguity about what information is, how meaning is judged (Travica, 2011), and if information can have di<sup>f</sup>erent meaning to di<sup>f</sup>erent individuals (Kettinger & Li, 2010).

## 2.2.3. Other objective theories

Other more recent objective theories of information have abandoned the knowledge construct. Mingers and Standing (2017) propose an objective and veridical theory where information is carried in signs and messages; information is what could be inferred about states of a<sup>f</sup>airs that could not be inferred without the sign. Data is a collection of signs put together for a purpose. Information exists without observation, and is always true.

Separately, Floridi (2011) attempts a broad general de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information (GDI) where information comes from data that are truthful, meaningful and well-formed. Well-formed data are clustered together correctly, according to the rules (syntax) that govern the chosen system, code or language being analysed. Adriaans (2010) critique is that this theory adds little to the understanding of actors within the system.

A timetable listing the arrival times of trains can be used to contrast these semantic de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of information. On the representation view, the timetable is a sign, is information, about trains to an observer. In DIKW, arrival times are data, arrival time information results from processing that data as well as from answers to “who”, “what”, “how many”, and “when” types of questions. In Mingers veridical theory, information is carried in the timetable about the arrival of trains. To Floridi, the timetable is information because it is about something (rendering it meaningful), it is true, and it is wellformed (it appears in an appropriate format).

## 2.3. The adaptation view

The adaptation view is a subjective framework for information brie<sup>fl</sup>y introduced in Mckinney and Yoos (2010). The adaptation view posits that systems conceive information based on perceptions of di<sup>f</sup>erence. A system may be a mechanism, organism, or organisation, such as a machine, a mind, or a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Being informed is creative, unique, and dynamic; all information in the adaptation view is continually “made up” by each system. Thus, information in the adaptation view depends completely on the system’s perception of a di<sup>f</sup>erence and if the system changes due to that perception. If a change occurs, information was conceived. The environment does not contain nor does it externally impose information on a system.

To return to the timetable example, in the adaptation view, the timetable is data. Observers of the timetable conceive their own information, change their minds, from di<sup>f</sup>erences they perceive in the timetable. One observer creates information that the train he is waiting on is late, another person that no trains arrive from Lancaster after 10:00 pm, and another that there are more arrivals than he previously thought.

Mckinney and Yoos (2010) did not present or list theories of information that relied on the adaptation view as they did with other three views, because there were no adaptation view theories of information. Unlike their descriptions of the other three views which included speci<sup>fi</sup>c information theories, for the adaptation view they claimed that autopoiesis, second order cybernetics, and cybersemiotic reflect the adaption view. These are theories of system adaptation with a subjective foundation, but are not themselves theories of information. Here we present a speci<sup>fi</sup>c theory of information founded on the adaptation view.

The adaptation view of information, without any particular formal theory, has already been applied in a wide variety of MIS and computer science research <sup>fi</sup>elds suggesting that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c theory of information as subjective change may have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant utility in a variety of research domains. For example, Lee et al. (2010) employ the adaptation view, as well as the other three views of information to contrast an IT artefact and an IS artefact. Spagnoletti, Za, and Winter (2013) examine the relationship between the four information views and type of simulation system used in research. Torres and Sidorova (2017) link the value of BI to organisational outcomes and use the adaptation view to examine the adaptation of the organisations.

The adaptation view and the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory rely on Bateson’s cybernetic epistemology, his ecological philosophy and its accounts of self organisation and self-regulation (Bateson, 1973). In his epistemology, the cycle between a system and its environment produces an ongoing feedback loop that changes the system over time. The system notices di<sup>f</sup>erences, perhaps the di<sup>f</sup>erence between its goal and its current state, and takes action. While Bateson applied this epistemology across a wide spectrum of domains, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory only addresses a speci<sup>fi</sup>c act – how an individual’s mind is changed in an environment of uncertainty.

## 3. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory of information

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, which utilises the assumptions of the adaptation view, extends Bateson’s (1973) de<sup>fi</sup>nition of information and speci<sup>fi</sup>es a new term, criteria, to clarify how people conceive di<sup>f</sup>erences that make a di<sup>f</sup>erence in uncertain environments. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory arranges criteria into a hierarchy of meta and recursive levels, explained further below in six propositions.

Before the propositions of the theory are presented, it is important to discuss two assumptions required for the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory that are not necessary for the adaptation view. First, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory only addresses how individuals, not systems are informed. By addressing only how individual people are informed the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can focus on the uncertain environment of individuals and how perception in that environment a<sup>f</sup>ects informing. Second, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is philosophically nominalist/anti-positivist. While the adaptation view adopts a subjective philosophy; the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is more speci<sup>fi</sup>c a nominalist ontology, and anti-positivist epistemology.

In Table 1 earlier, column three showed the philosophy of each of the information views. With the exception of the adaptation view, all information views are objective – they assume a realist ontology in which the world external to the individual is comprised of immutable structures independent of individuals’ appreciation of it (Burrell & Morgan, 1979), and they employ a positivist epistemology. The goal of positivism is to create knowledge that predicts the regularities and causal relationships in the social world using a systematic protocol in accord with the canons of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c rigour.

The nominalist ontology assumed by the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory holds that the individual’s world is nothing more than names and labels that can be used to describe and communicate perceptions of the external world. Names and labels are abstract objects such as numbers, propositions and constructs, but these abstract objects are always particular to the individual, not universal.

As anti-positivist, the encountered world is relativistic and can only be understood from the point of view of the individual, not the observer. The technique of study is to “get inside” individuals and situations and become involved with the <sup>fl</sup>ow of life using surveys, hermeneutics, case studies, and journals. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is a Type II theory and hence makes no attempt at predictions but rather aims to facilitate understanding of how phenomena occur and to bring about an altered understanding of how things are.

## 3.1. Propositions of the diference theory

The speci<sup>fi</sup>c propositions of the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory are listed in Table 3. Each is discussed in turn.

## Proposition 1: Individuals seek to adapt in uncertainty

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory assumes the individual’s environment is persistently uncertain, and that business contexts are characterised by complexity, change, and ambiguity. By uncertainty, we mean a very large, complex set of constantly changing elements that generate an uncountable number of possible permutations and interpretations (Premkumar, Ramamurthy, & Saunders, 2005). This view is derived from Weick’s notion that the environment is a changing stream that supports many interpretations, its lack of clarity makes multiple explanations possible, and it is unique to each individual; the context is always fuzzy (March, 1994; Martin, 1995; Weick, 1995).

This uncertainty is pernicious and not a temporary condition to be overcome with better algorithms, technology, or procedures, nor is it the result of inadequate understanding. Even after thorough analysis and deliberation, every business setting can still be characterised by an uncountable number of relevant elements that enable many interpretations. This uncertainty about the correspondence between what is known and what exists is a fundamental characteristic of the business/organisational setting.

Theories that stipulate that information must be true ignore the central role of uncertainty and they underestimate the need to understand how individuals inform themselves in it. In the syntax view and in objective semantic theories of information, states of nature or of the world are typically quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed, information helps an individual decide among these states. In our view, there are no limits to the “states” that an individual can imagine or perceive. Uncertainty is the sine qua non of the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory. Thus, our <sup>fi</sup>rst proposition is that individuals seek to adapt, to make themselves more <sup>fi</sup>t, in an environment that is persistently uncertain.

## Proposition 2: Individuals perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences in uncertainty

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is consistent with Gregory (1987) that perception is the process of becoming aware. Perception is not the passive receipt of signs, but is shaped by experience, education, and expectation (Berger & Luckmann, 1966; Checkland & Holwell, 1998; Lako<sup>f</sup>, 1987). In addition, context also shapes perception – perception is strongly a<sup>f</sup>ected by the qualities of other objects in the setting such as the contrast e<sup>f</sup>ect (Wundt, 1904). Perception is active (Gibson, 1979; Heft, 2003; Merleau-Ponty, 1962); we perceive the world, but not through a passive objective process. Rather, we are embodied subjects involved in existence. For example, if we cannot read small font, we move the print closer to our eyes. Perception augmented by experience is a central tenet of channel expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999) and embodied realism theory (Lako<sup>f</sup> & Johnson, 1999).

A di<sup>f</sup>erence is a relationship between things, attributes, properties, ideas, or another relationship (a recursive relationship); there are an in<sup>fi</sup>nity of di<sup>f</sup>erences (Mingers, 2017). Di<sup>f</sup>erence can also reside in data, in symbols, in parts or wholes, in sights or sounds, or in any attribute of any phenomenon. However, di<sup>f</sup>erences are perceptions; and as such are subject to limitations of perception just described.

## Proposition 3: Information is a changing mind

When someone understands a di<sup>f</sup>erence that makes a di<sup>f</sup>erence, that individual’s mind has been changed. Thus, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne information as a changing mind. (We make no claim, however, that using criteria to make di<sup>f</sup>erences that make di<sup>f</sup>erences is the only way minds change, it is only one way. For example, minds change without a perception as in a dream or by a strong passion or emotion.)

As mentioned earlier, the adaptation view describes system adaptation while the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory addresses how individuals perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences and conceive information. The mind is a speci<sup>fi</sup>c and limited type of system to which the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory applies. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory’s change of mind is an instance of a change in the system.

This idea of information-as-change is consistent with other work. Mackay (1969), echoed recently by Bergman, Beyth-Marom, and Nachmias (2003) and Kettinger and Li (2010) explained that information results in a change in the conditional readiness of an individual. In his classic example, MacKay explained that the switches in rail yard that control where trains go are moved by information. He applied that to the mind where information changes our “circuits” so that later when trains or opportunities arise we act di<sup>f</sup>erently than before we had the information. Boland (1987) also linked information to change de<sup>fi</sup>ning information as a change in the knowledge, beliefs, values or behaviour of a person.

Autopoiesis also relates information to change as it seeks to explain systems adaptation in uncertain environments. Autopoiesis literally means “auto (self)-creation” (Maturana & Verela, 1980). The term describes the nature of living systems, from individuals to legal systems to societies (Luhmann, 1995). Information for an autopoietic system is not externally imposed by the environment, nor how the system perceives the world, nor what the system knows about itself, but rather how the system recreates and adapts itself based on what it perceives. “. . .the nervous system does not ‘pick up information from the environment, as we often hear the popular metaphor of calling the brain an ‘informationprocessing device’ is not only ambiguous but patently wrong” (Maturana, 1987, p. 169). To be autopoietic, a system changes, it adapts.

We refer to the changing mind by saying that information is conceived from di<sup>f</sup>erence. Thus, di<sup>f</sup>erences are perceived; information is conceived. For example, if a passenger examining the timetable mentioned earlier perceives a di<sup>f</sup>erence between the scheduled arrival time and the actual arrival time (i.e., her transportation is late), she might change her mind about subsequent dinner plans. According to the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, information is a process not an object. Information is adaptation.

## Proposition 4: Individuals conceive information from perceived di<sup>f</sup>erences based on criteria

According to the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, information is conceived from perceived di<sup>f</sup>erences. As stated, individuals can perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences in phenomena (e.g., texture, size, melody) as well as concepts (e.g., simplistic, useful, costly, reliability). In a setting of uncertainty, an individual can perceive more di<sup>f</sup>erences than in a setting of certainty – a stable, countable, unchanging context. Thus, the more uncertainty, the more possible information.

Criteria are dimensions of di<sup>f</sup>erence that determine which di<sup>f</sup>erences make a di<sup>f</sup>erence. Individuals employ criteria to conceive perceived di<sup>f</sup>erences in-toformation. These dimensions may be simple, such as colour, velocity, or cost; or criteria may be a composite such as variable cost, inelastic demand, or deceptive velocity. Criteria are learned from education, experience, and communication. New criteria allow an individual to conceive new information and are an important component of learning. Criteria also help an individual ignore irrelevant di<sup>f</sup>erences.

A criterion is an abstract, particular object. Abstract, meaning neither temporal nor spatial, like numbers and propositions and particular like tastes in that each are instantiated by only a speci<sup>fi</sup>c individual at a speci<sup>fi</sup>c time. While criteria labels, for example marginal cost, can be shared, each individual application of that label will create a unique and particular cost di<sup>f</sup>erence. A marginal cost of 7 cents will mean many di<sup>f</sup>erent things to di<sup>f</sup>erent individuals and di<sup>f</sup>erent things to the same individual at di<sup>f</sup>erent instances. The abstract objects of marginal cost and 7 cents are not information, the di<sup>f</sup>erences conceived with the 7 cents that change a mind are information. The more exact term for criteria is criteria label, but we most often use criteria for simplicity (and because all terms are labels to nominalists).

Clearly, for an individual, not all perceived di<sup>f</sup>erences are conceived into information. However, some insignificant di<sup>f</sup>erences may be remembered and re-conceived later. The train passenger may recall the train was also late on the previous two Saturdays. The individual may recall these earlier perceptions and with the new criterion, change their mind, about these earlier incidents. Criteria create a loop between perception and conception. For example, the passenger has now learned the criterion day-of-the-week. He or she can use this criterion in future planning to allow additional time on Saturdays for a late arrival.

## Proposition 5: Criteria are hierarchical

According to the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, criteria are organised in hierarchical levels. The concept of a hierarchy of levels is derived from type theory, based on logic and set theory work (Russell & Whitehead, 1913), a set at a higher level contains objects (and sets) at a lower level, each level synthesises and organises the level below. The idea of levels is also used in database practice where meta data are at a higher level than the data they describe. In the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, higher level criteria, called meta criteria, are criteria that apply to criteria a level below; they are used to inform the individual about perceived di<sup>f</sup>erences in criteria.

Criteria are concepts and as such individuals can perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences in them by applying criteria to them. In this case, we say the individual has applied meta criteria to perceive di<sup>f</sup>erences in the <sup>fi</sup>rst-level criteria. Consider a passenger evaluating transportation options. The passenger might employ three criteria to conceive transportation information – convenience, speed, and cost. The passenger might also employ a meta criterion of accuracy to conceive di<sup>f</sup>erences in accuracy among these three criteria. The passenger may use that meta criterion and comprehend that speed is the least accurate criterion and cost the most accurate criterion.

Meta criteria help an individual inform at the higher, meta-level. In theory, criteria exist in an endless level of progression of meta criteria, meta meta criteria, meta meta meta criteria, etc. To continue the example above, the passenger may use the meta criteria of communicable and reliable in addition to accuracy. Meta meta criteria might then be consistency – which meta criterion can I consistently employ? While the number of levels of meta criteria has no theoretical limit, in practice, more than three levels is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to sustain. A <sup>fi</sup>nal clari<sup>fi</sup>cation, meta criteria are not necessarily broader, more important, or more abstract than criteria at lower levels.

## Proposition 6: Recursive meta criteria are criteria applied to themselves

Recursion is the application of an idea to itself, and is common in programming, music, art, mathematics, logic, and set theory (Hofstader, 1970). A common recursion involves learning. At one level, learning can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as matching a stimulus to a response. At the next higher level, a recursion of learning is learning to learn (Bateson, 1973).

A special type of meta criteria are recursed criteria. A recursed criterion applies to itself at the next higher level. For example, the passenger may apply convenience to itself – is the convenience criterion convenient to use; or the cost criterion – is it costly to calculate cost? Like meta criteria, the hierarchy or levels of recursion are theoretically endless.

## 3.2. Further discussion of propositions

While individuals may explicitly employ some criteria, they often use implicit criteria as well. In these cases, the individual is unaware of the criteria they are using to conceive information. Information is not countable or static; an individual constantly perceives and conceives while perhaps being only dimly aware about their informing process. Information is also not countable or static because it often creates a ripple e<sup>f</sup>ect as the original di<sup>f</sup>erence changes an individual’s mind about a variety of things. For example, the forecast for rain changes my mind about clothing, which leads me to perceive I don’t have appropriate clothing choices, that I want to live elsewhere, and that my tolerance for rain or other inconveniences is low. While perceiving and conceiving can be perceived as sequential as when an individual re<sup>fl</sup>ects on a setting, they are often not even discrete. Conception and perception frequently occur simultaneously as when an individual is engaged in activity.

To see these ideas in one more example, consider a business analyst examining a worksheet of sales numbers. The sales numbers are data. The analyst is uncertain about many things – a recent change in sales, the accuracy of the numbers, whether trends will continue, the ambiguity of the e<sup>f</sup>ect of prior actions. When the analyst perceives di<sup>f</sup>erences in the data and those differences change the analyst’s mind, information has been conceived. The analyst may have used criteria such as salesperson experience, date, and location. The analyst may be employing meta criteria of reliability or e<sup>f</sup>ort on these criteria. These explicit criteria are mixed with implicit criteria. A recursed criterion might be experience – how much experience does the analyst have with the criterion of experience? The analyst is adapting by informing in uncertainty.

Note how this example is di<sup>f</sup>erent from the common examples of information used in objective theories. Examples in those theories are a knock on the door, a storm cloud, or a warning light on a dashboard. Each of these identi<sup>fi</sup>es a particular message or sign, the theory explains how information is conveyed or carried by a particular message or sign, or if it is well-formed. While the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can explain how an individual conceives information from di<sup>f</sup>erences between this message or sign and another, the more important insight is that the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory recognises that many signs or messages are always available and that all signs or messages are subject to the perception of the individual.

## 3.3. Diference theory compared to objective theories

Table 4 summarises major di<sup>f</sup>erences between the objective theories and the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory. These di<sup>f</sup>erences are (1) information does not arise from data nor is it carried by signs; information arises from conception, criteria, and meta criteria; (2) information does not relate to reality or correspond to true states of the environment; it is simply a changing mind; (3) information does not reduce uncertainty; it is proportional to uncertainty; (4) information is not external or object based; it is completely internal to an individual; (5) information is not a single version of truth; information varies across individuals, (6) information is not carried or conveyed by a particular message or sign, it is conceived from the multitude of available messages, signs, or data in an uncertain environment. These di<sup>f</sup>erences can be attributed in part to the two types of theory being compared. Objective theories are typically Type I, Analysis and Description theories that describe the attributes of information; the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, Type II attempts to explain how informing occurs.

One criterion common to objective theories is that information is veri<sup>fi</sup>able, and this veri<sup>fi</sup>cation, a correspondence to a reality, can be done external to the individual. If the truth of information can be determined, then a database (information base) can be said to supply one source of truth, and the quality of the information can be determined. In addition, objective theories of information generate a more consistent set of information across individuals, not everyone has their own information. With objective theories, repeatable experiments are more straightforward, the key variables are manipulatable, and measures in the reduction in uncertainty are available.

Truth is the key element in choosing between an objective and subjective theory of information. Objective theories ground information in truth so that applications of the theory result in a reduction in uncertainty. However, the adaptation view and the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory begins with recognition that the environment most commonly encountered in business does not permit truth. Even if yesterday’s transaction for \$5000 is true in that it actually corresponds to the event, measurement concerns (Mckinney, Yoos, & Snead, 2017; Stonebreaker & Hong, 2012) make sceptical analysts wary. Even if we grant truth to that “information” it raises more uncertainty than it resolves, questions that are more important – will this continue today, will the customer pay it later, will we have the inventory, will our suppliers have it, will their suppliers have it, and so on. All these more essential questions require meaning, they require information as the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory de<sup>fi</sup>nes information. It is the ability to ask and answer these type of questions, the process of being informed, that is more essential to how individuals use “information” artefacts than how the data is communicated or stored, or if it is “true”.

We believe that this pursuit of truth by objective theories is futile. To seek to reduce uncertainty, to reduce untruth, puts us on the wrong side of uncertainty and makes individuals less <sup>fi</sup>t to adapt in their environment. It <sup>fi</sup>ghts uncertainty, rather than leveraging it as the source of, and opportunity for, informing. Further, it makes us look for information in the wrong place and with the wrong characteristics, and we miss out on using the label to describe the most important aspect – how individuals create meaning, how individuals change their minds, a step too often overlooked. It leads to the use of misguided and sti<sup>fl</sup>ing statements like, “We have all the information”, “send me the information I need”, “the numbers speak for themselves”, “the data tells us. . .”, and “one source of truth”.

## 4. Application of the theory

To structure this application section, we o<sup>f</sup>er a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed three-step technique as shown in Table 5. A di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller, a practitioner or researcher applying the difference theory, can consider each of these steps to generate insight.

## 4.1. Implications for practice of team collaboration

To demonstrate the applicability of the theory, consider its application to a common IS task – supporting the collaboration of a team. The challenges of collaboration and understanding others have often been overlooked but have recently become the focus of new research (Jung, 2009; Sandis, 2019). In this particular scenario, IT managers and business managers are debating the merits of various IT infrastructure improvements to decide how best to allocate resources; the three topics in Table 5 are applied to this scenario in the following three paragraphs.

Uncertainty includes what each individual knows about each attribute of each of the possible improvements, the implications that will result from choosing among the improvements, the knowledge and motivations of the individuals, and how each individual evaluates each alternative and attribute. These elements are changing as new data, alternatives, and attributes are discovered and communicated. Each of these elements and their changes are ambiguous – the alternative chosen will not work out as planned, the costs are always estimates, supporters may be in favour of a solution for any combination of reasons, the motivations of all the individuals are never clear and shift often, data is incomplete, individuals can’t know what others have ignored, learned and dismissed, and known but never communicated, and each communicative act could imply several meanings.

An individual using the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory would recognise the opportunity to inform in uncertainty – the greater the recognised uncertainty, the greater the opportunity to develop criteria and become informed. The di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller does not assume that information can be sent or received. He (or she) would ask for data, what the data means to others and how others are forming insights and conclusions. The di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller begins with an initial set of criteria such as cost, feasibility, e<sup>f</sup>ect on sales, training required, good for my career, reliability, and e<sup>fi</sup>- ciency and seeks to infer what criteria others seem to be using and apply his own version of those criteria labels. The value of team diversity, individuals with a variety of di<sup>f</sup>erent backgrounds, is that each team member uses much di<sup>f</sup>erent criteria. The difference modeller will consider the aspects of uncertainty in order to grow their set of criteria. What is unknown about an attribute of an alternative, what might be some of the implications of choosing an alternative, and what is the knowledge and motivation of my fellow team members? He uses verbal and print data available to all participants to conceive information using his own unique criteria. Nevertheless, he realises that teammates use di<sup>f</sup>erent criteria, and as a result, the meaning he intends by his words are always to some extent understood di<sup>f</sup>erently, they are “misunderstood” to some degree.

Individuals without the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory do not explicitly consider criteria hierarchy – meta criteria or recursion. By making meta criteria explicit, the di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller can help team members become informed about the value of their own criteria. For example, he notices cost is a common criterion label and that other team members seem to rely on cost because they appreciate that it is speci<sup>fi</sup>c and reliable; they also seem to use cost because it is measurable. The di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller would infer speci<sup>fi</sup>c, reliable and measurable are an initial set of meta criteria labels and might then ask teammates if they use speci<sup>fi</sup>c, reliable and measureable to evaluate and re<sup>fi</sup>ne their criteria. If not, they might then discuss these meta criteria. One simple recursion is based on the criterion of cost – what does it cost to use cost as a criterion. If it is costly, this di<sup>f</sup>erence changes the di<sup>f</sup>erence modeller’s mind about the criterion of cost and he would re<sup>fi</sup>ne it or delete it. Similarly he might ask if reliability is reliable, and if measurement is measureable.

## 4.2. Implications for research on team collaboration

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory provides new opportunities to address existing team collaboration research topics. First, work on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of personality on team communication might be improved by including assessments of the awareness of uncertainty by team members. According to collaboration research of Gong, Cheung, Wang, and Huang (2012), employees with proactive personalities engage in more information exchange and display more creativity. The difference theory suggests that awareness of uncertainty may mediate the e<sup>f</sup>ect of proactive personality on exchange and creativity. That is, proactive employees may perceive opportunity in uncertainty as teammates are more receptive and creativity is more useful than when perceived uncertainty is low.

Criteria may play an important role in a variety of team research domains. According to Mesmer-Magnus and Dechurch (2009) research is needed to understand better how information is shared within and across teams. According to the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, information is never directly shared, but data as well as criteria labels used by each individual can be communicated, discussed, and adopted. Another avenue, Media Richness Theory (MRT) posits that the more ambiguous and uncertain a task the richer the appropriate media needs to be (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Media is a key factor because of the variety of criteria individuals are applying is high when perceived uncertainty is high. Criteria may also be important to improving our understanding of creativity on a team. Potentially creative ideas are always considered helpful by their creator, but more helpful to the team is the creator’s ability to describe the criteria he or she is using that illuminate the creative idea.

Team research can also apply the idea of criteria hierarchy. One ongoing topic in team performance is explaining how the team’s use of ICT changes over time (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2012; Thomas &

Bostrom, 2010). The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that one factor that changes over time is criteria. Criteria are not static and change with experience, education and communication. One way to model this change is to understand how meta criteria change over time. Changes in the use of ICT may be linked to adoption of new criteria and meta criteria over time.

Norms greatly in<sup>fl</sup>uence team collaboration and ICT use (Mckinney, Barker, Smith, & Davis, 2004; Poole, Keyton, & Frey, 1999; Weick & Roberts, 1993). In the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory norms are criteria, the criteria used by individuals to inform themselves about the appropriateness of their behaviour and the behaviour of others. Research can examine the role of norm meta criteria, the criteria for assessing these norms. One meta criterion might be acceptance of new norms, another, how well an ICT promotes norms.

One particular aspect of collaboration, trust, is particularly well modelled by meta criteria. Trust reduces uncertainty on a team, and improves collaboration (Belanger, Watson-Manheim, & Jordan, 2002; Gefen, Benbasat, & Pavlou, 2008). The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that a trustworthy teammate might be one whose criteria can be inferred by others (a meta criterion of transparency) as teammates may be able to anticipate when a trustworthy teammate will change their mind.

## 5. Discussion

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory addresses the semantic use of information as a changing mind, an internal construct that varies from individual to individual. Based on a nominalist, anti-positivist foundation, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory explains how an individual creates information in uncertainty using criteria, criteria meta levels and recursion. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory makes a sharp distinction between data and information by reserving the term data to mean tokens, the bits manipulated by machines, and information to mean a changing mind.

To be consistent with the di<sup>f</sup>erent theory, we suggest criteria for individuals to use to assess the theory. We suggest that a theory of information should be comprehensive, simple to employ and appropriate for the business environment. A comprehensive theory is broadly applicable and wide ranging. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests information is subjectively semantic, and occurs everywhere individuals adapt in uncertain environments. Di<sup>f</sup>erences can be perceived in virtually every IS domain, from every type of message, screen, data, or symbol. Most all IS topics are primarily concerned with how IS inform end users.

In addition to being comprehensive, we believe a theory of information should be su<sup>fi</sup>ciently simple to be widely employable by end users. It should be simple enough to be used and employed by end users to assess their own informing and to provide feedback to IS professionals about the contributions of an

IS to their informing. At its core our theory – information is a changing mind based on perceptions of di<sup>f</sup>erence – is su<sup>fi</sup>ciently simple to be broadly employed.

A theory of information should be appropriate, suitable, <sup>fi</sup>t for the uncertainty of the business environment. While Big Data proponents wisely call for decision makers to be data driven, more data leads to more uncertainty. The data on revenue last quarter creates uncertainty – will this continue, what are the measurement assumptions, how do others view this data and so on. To avoid drowning in the volume and velocity of data and uncertainty that arises from it, practitioners and researchers need a theory that helps them see this data deluge and its uncertainty as the source of potential di<sup>f</sup>erences that can be channelled, converted, into information. One of the most important contributions of the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is to relate uncertainty to information, not as a reduction in uncertainty like objective semantic theories, or mathematically like Shannon and Weaver, but as a subjective and individual opportunity – the greater the perceived uncertainty, the greater the number of potential di<sup>f</sup>erences to be perceived and the greater the opportunity for an individual to be informed. We posit that all contemporary business environments can be viewed as uncertain. In all these environments, a theory of information built on levels of criteria allows individuals to inform themselves more broadly in any domain or business context.

We use three criteria for an information theory – comprehensive, simple and appropriate. We informed ourselves of these criteria by using meta criteria – criteria should be challenging, appropriate for a Type II theory, and be appropriate for IS. As a result, we ruled out other potential criteria for our theory such as rigorous, complete, or systematic.

In addition to information theory criteria, we have also nominated Type II theory criteria of surprise, enlighten, and unconventional. Surprise and enlightenment are di<sup>fi</sup>cult for authors to assess. However, the theory is unconventional as it is contrary to conventional descriptions and theories of information. According to conventional theories, books, papers, Web sites and lectures contain or carry information, but in the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, they do not. They allow an individual to conceive information only if di<sup>f</sup>erences are perceived and criteria are used.

## 5.1. Implications of the theory

While still in the development stage, some of the implications of the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can be sketched. Individuals armed with the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can realise that others have created legitimately peculiar information for themselves and that encourages them to listen more closely to others and stay curious, as there is no end to the information they can create for themselves. Learning might also improve. Awareness that all information is self-created narrows responsibility for learning to the individual. Provoked by this responsibility, end users might take greater advantage of self-service analytical tools and the availability of data to conceive their own information.

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can also help end users and analysts understand reactions to a new IS. Prior to a new IS, individuals had grown accustomed to informing themselves from the data in the previous system. Without being consciously aware of it, they had learned where to look for the data to perceive the di<sup>f</sup>erences they needed to conceive their information. Informing had become automatic and implicit. Because it required little e<sup>f</sup>ort on their part, individuals believed their information was in the system – which is not surprising because it is called an information system. With a new IS, data is presented in a new way, requiring unanticipated explicit e<sup>f</sup>ort by the individual to <sup>fi</sup>nd it and create di<sup>f</sup>erences with it. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory provides a potential explanation for identifying why informing breaks down with the new system and provides a new theoretical opportunity to evaluate and explain reaction of individuals to new systems.

The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can also make the requirements determination step of systems development more e<sup>fi</sup>cient and e<sup>f</sup>ective. With an objective view of information, developers typically condense the requirements generated by a group of users to a single set of information requirements for the system to deliver, a set of requirements that are a compromise of each end user’s informing needs. With the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, developers expect that each end user will create information from the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the data supplied by the proposed IS. Reducing this variety to a single set of requirements consumes time, restricts informing of the end users, limits the organisation’s requisite variety, and inhibits the opportunity for end user innovation. Certainly not all projects can use this approach; the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that one important criterion is supporting a variety of end user informing needs. This criterion should be evaluated with other criteria such as ease of use, performance, durability, cost and time, using meta criteria such as measurability and importance.

With this theory, decision makers will better understand that the goals of being “data driven” and analysis driven, while laudable, emphasise data rather than information. Often, data driven proponents argue that management should listen to the data, to allow the data “to tell us what to do” (Ohemeng & Kofosu-Adarkwa, 2015; Schmarzo, 2013). Others argue that businesses should be analysis driven, not just data driven; also clearly wise, but the results of analysing data, of processing data, are still data. With the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory, more data and analysis are still good, but not because they answer questions, but because they lead to more questions and more uncertainty, giving analysts the opportunity to create more di<sup>f</sup>erences and information. Data and the results of analysis do not themselves speak, rather individuals create information from the data and analysis, and this vital and empowering step at times can be overlooked.

The theory also provides new perspectives and opportunities for current IS research topics. In AI, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that unsupervised machine learning will be more e<sup>f</sup>ective than traditional expert systems. With expert systems the criteria for each branch, each rule, is made explicit; with unsupervised techniques, the software is given meta criteria – <sup>fi</sup>nd criteria (i.e., patterns) that achieve a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goal. As available data and the potential di<sup>f</sup>erences in those data increase, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of unsupervised techniques, like unsupervised human exploration of data, will prove to be superior to techniques that use <sup>fi</sup>xed criteria or <sup>fi</sup>xed rules. Research using the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can examine meta criteria and meta meta criteria to use in machine learning applications.

Another research issue the theory might also support is the distinction between data security and information security and related distinctions in privacy. The di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that data security evaluates unwarranted access or change to data while information security accesses how unauthorised individuals can use data to inform themselves in ways the owners of the data seek to avoid. In privacy research, the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that data privacy is concerned about the loss of control of personal data while information privacy is preventing unauthorised individuals from using personal data to change their mind. Research can examine the criteria used by unauthorised individuals, if awareness of those criteria can improve data security and privacy, and the characteristics of personal data that most often lead to changing minds.

For user experience research the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory suggests that the variety of criteria and meta criteria employed by end users are keys to determining their expectations and satisfaction. Research can evaluate how end users adopt their criteria, the robustness of those criteria to changes in settings, tasks, and applications, and if training or organisational culture in<sup>fl</sup>uences criteria adoption.

While these positive implications of the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory can be proposed, its disadvantages are also evident. It is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to test empirically with conventiona methods as it does not provide testable hypotheses. It also requires a willingness to move away from anchoring information in truth. This is clearly an o<sup>f</sup>-putting step, common to all subjective approaches. When information is decoupled from truth, everything, or too many things, are information or lead to information.

If information is subjective, common artefacts that “contain” information no longer do – libraries, books and websites for example. Finally, information in the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory is unlimited, and as a result, more is always available and determining when enough of it is su<sup>fi</sup>cient is not clear.

Further, the terms misinformation and disinformation must also change from their conventional use. In our theory, misinformation is a term that describes a situation where one individual believes another individual has informed themselves inappropriately. Disinformation implies that data is communicated to intentionally mislead or to deliberately create the opportunity for an inappropriate meaning by another individual.

## 5.2. Further research

The theory itself is just a start; and its limitations suggest further work. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, next steps should better relate the theory to ongoing developments in the philosophy of information, and with concepts in emerging information domains such as situation semantics (Barwise & Perry, 1983), semiotics (Brier, 2005; Sebeok, 2001), and information science (Bawden, 2007; Capurro, 2003). The appropriateness of the theory can be evaluated by comparing it to other decision theories such as multi attribute decision theory (Belton & Stewart, 2001; Keeney & Rai<sup>f</sup>a, 1976) and the design theory nexus (Pries-Heje & Baskerville, 2008). The theory will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from further research on how individuals can improve their informing process, how criteria are learned, communicated, and adopted. Research might also identify the conditions under which the theory applies to systems other than individuals, such as AI software, groups or organisations. Finally, future research might also evaluate the usefulness of the theory to other IS research domains such as design, user trust, and governance.

We do not intend to say that information phenomena of other theories such as signs, messages, or representations are unimportant or what they carry or convey is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. These phenomena are clearly important on their own and also play a role in the perception of di<sup>f</sup>erences. However, we suggest that anchoring information to them creates theories that are less simple, comprehensive and applicable than the di<sup>f</sup>erence theory. Finally, an emphasis on these phenomena can lead to under appreciation of the role of the informant in the conception of information.

## 6. Conclusion

As echoed by objective theory proponents, it is essential for the IS community to establish an understanding of information. Information plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in most all IS concepts and with the growth of analytics, information security, and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence, the need for a theory of information has never been greater. We have presented an initial theory of information based on the adaptation view, a shift away from traditional objective views. The chief motivation for this shift is the inadequacy of objective views to address the uncertainty common in the business and organisational context.

We hope to promote a debate in the IS community about information. We believe the community should choose to a<sup>fi</sup>x the label information deliberately for the most utility. Most researchers who re<sup>fl</sup>ect on this issue already agree that a subjective approach to the phenomena of informing or creating meaning is reasonable (Morgan & Smircich, 1980). We seek to anchor the label information to this ubiquitous subjective phenomenon, not to the objective truth or certainty of the data or messages said to carry the information.

## Appendix Historical background and current uses of Information

The concept of information was <sup>fi</sup>rst labelled in Greek as forma and in Latin as informatio and informare meaning reinforcement or detection, to make or produce both technically and biologically, to describe or de<sup>fi</sup>ne, to unfold or illuminate, or to put into form (Applegate, McFarlan, & McKenney, 1999; Marcus, 2000). Virgil wrote of Vulcan and the Cyclops hammering out (informatum) lightning bolts for Zeus. Tertullian called Moses populi informatory, the people’s educator or molder, and Augustine de<sup>fi</sup>ned information as a molding process such as the impression of a ring on wax, calling the process of visual perception informatio sensus (Capurro, 2003). In medieval times information gave a certain form or character to matter or to the mind (Campbell, 1982), the Old Oxford English Dictionary de<sup>fi</sup>nes information as forming or molding the mind (Artz, 2016). Historically, information began as a semantic principle used to explain meaning.

With the advent of the industrial age and new communication networks, information, in addition to a semantic concept, also began to refer to objective measurements of communication. Shannon’s revolutionary mathematical theory <sup>fi</sup>t the bill; it objectively de<sup>fi</sup>ned information for communication networks as the measurable reduction in uncertainty or entropy (Shannon, 1948; Shannon & Weaver, 1949). It is widely accepted in the engineering and applied mathematics domains. In this view, labelled the syntax view in McKinney and Yoos (2010), information is related to novelty: a less probable event is more informative as it reduces more uncertainty than a more probable event (Bar-Hillel & Carnap, 1953; Dretske, 1981). The classic example is the di<sup>f</sup>erence between a fair coin and a coin with the same image on both sides. Flipping a fair coin has a less predictable outcome, and reduces more uncertainty; <sup>fl</sup>ipping a coin with the same image on both sides informs us of nothing we do not already know – there is no uncertainty and thus, no information. At about the same time, popular use of the term information also referred to the tokens produced and processed by computing devices, information and data began to be used interchangeably.

## Common uses of information in science: Semantic, entropy reduction, data

As information came to mean many things, its use became pervasive in the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community. To this day, information plays an increasingly fundamental role in a wide variety of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c domains including physics, astronomy, biology, psychology, medicine, political science, physiology, economics, behavioural science, and philosophy (Burgin, 2010; Robinson & Bawden, 2014; van Rijsbergen & Laimas, 1996). It is hard to envision another concept that is fundamental to as many domains. In these domains information is used in three general ways – semantically, as a measure of entropy reduction (syntax), and as data or tokens processed by computers. Economists, using the term semantically and as token consider information a primary ingredient for development equal in importance with capital, labour, and raw material (Capurro, 2003). Biologists use information as entropy reduction and see it as fundamental to traits, causal processes, the transmission of genes and as the central de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristic of all life forms (Kaye, 1995; Reading, 2006; Smith & Szathmary, 1999). Physicists, using exclusively an entropy de<sup>fi</sup>nition, view information as a fundamental objective property of the universe alongside matter and energy (Kornwachs & Jacoby, 1996; Wiener, 1961). Indeed, several prominent physicists have suggested that the physical world is comprised of information with energy and matter as incidentals, that all “its” come from “bits” (Berkenstein, 2003; Weizsacker, 1985; Wheeler, 1990).

These semantic, entropy reduction and data views on information are the basis of the information taxonomy presented in McKinney and Yoos (2010). As shown in Table 1, we label data as the token view, entropy reduction as the syntax view and semantic as either the representation view or the adaptation view. In the remainder of this section we narrow our focus to information use in IS only.

## Common uses of information in information systems: Semantic, data

The syntax view of information, as a reduction in entropy, is not common in IS (McKinney & Yoos, 2010). The two uses of information in IS – semantic and data – may be attributed to the distinctive blend of people and technology unique to the

IS domain. A concept of information occurs in both the ambiguous and uncertain domain of people seeking to create meaning by interacting with IT (semantic information), and also the mathematical, <sup>fi</sup>nite and objective domain of technology (data). With technology issues, objective assumptions are very appropriate; however, wherever individuals create meaning, these objective assumptions are a poor <sup>fi</sup>t.

In IS research, as in practice, information is both semantic and data. In recent IS research information as meaning is used in privacy studies that examine customer willingness to participate in personalisation and the assurances of transparency (e.g., Awad & Krishnan, 2006). Information is meaningful because it represents something to an individual. Information as meaning is also evident in IS research on decision making as it is the meaning of information that a<sup>f</sup>ects how individuals make decisions (e.g., Clark, Jones, & Armstrong, 2007). In other research, information as data or token is used in Kumar and Benbasat’s (2004) study of the encoding, task type and complexity of line graphs where the information from a graph is received by the viewer, coded into memory and later retrieved. Information as data is also evident in IS research that examines information-processing activities (e.g., Kwon, Oh, & Jeon, 2007).

Just as we can attribute common IS use and research to two broad categories of semantic and data, we can also map de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and theories of information to the same general categories. Examples of semantic de<sup>fi</sup>nitions include meaning derived from data, data plus meaning (Checkland & Scholes, 1990), and a stimulus that expands a world view (Madden, 2004).

When information implies data or tokens, de<sup>fi</sup>nitions include statements or facts received by individuals with some form of worth (Losee, 1990), and the assembly of data in comprehensive form (Rouse, 2002). These de<sup>fi</sup>nitions are typically o<sup>f</sup>ered as byproducts of theories of other phenomena such as knowledge (Checkland & Holwell, 2006; Kettinger & Li, 2010), communication (Mokros, 1993; Schement & Ruben, 1993) or semiotics (Beynon-Davies, 2009; Brier, 2005). While these various de<sup>fi</sup>nitions have been suggested, there are no established comprehensive theories of information within the IS domain or the business domain (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011; Kettinger et al., 2013; Lee, 2010; McKinney & Yoos, 2010).
