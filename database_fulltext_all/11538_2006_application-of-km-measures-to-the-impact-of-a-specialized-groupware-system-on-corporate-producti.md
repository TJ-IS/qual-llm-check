# Application of KM measures to the impact of a specialized groupware system on corporate productivity and operations

Hassan A. Artail <sup>\*</sup>

Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, American University of Beirut, P.O. Box 11-0236 Riad El-Solh, Beirut 1107-2020, Lebanon

Accepted 12 February 2006

## Abstract

Here we describe an experiment to show the improvement in productivity resulting from use of a specialized groupware system, using quantitative data and observations of the knowledge management (KM) processes, styles, and critical success factors. Data describing the work process before and after the deployment of the system was applied to measure the impact on performance, operations, and knowledge sharing behavior. The attitude of the organization toward knowledge sharing and the deployed groupware system was then studied along several dimensions that represent KM styles, by assessing the KM orientation and motivation of the organization. Lessons learned were presented and used for directing the attention of management to the importance of supporting collaborative and KM technologies for corporate strategic competitiveness. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Knowledge management; Knowledge sharing; Collaboration; Groupware applications; Organizational performance

## 1. Introduction

Increasing competitive pressures are driving organizations to use technology for improving corporate efficiency and effectiveness. Groupware applications are being adopted by organizations to improve collaboration and knowledge sharing; these two factors have been seen as essential in providing strategic competencies. The firm’s ability to capture, organize, and disseminate knowledge allows it to improve decision making, process efficiency, quality, and customer satisfaction, and to reduce costs [43]. Some authors contended that winning firms must constantly innovate and transform by harnessing their intellectual capital [35]. Others have asserted that the basic economic resource is no longer capital or natural resources but knowledge [17].

Groupware systems are typically off-the-shelf products deployed across organizations and include E-mail, electronic bulletin boards, and group support systems. Many require significant effort on the part of users to collaborate, causing them to be underutilized and preventing their potential benefits from being realized [30]. Faced with this challenge, many are looking for ways to increase the rate of diffusion and adoption of these technologies within organizations [29] by studying behavioral elements (e.g., motivation and attitude) as the primary causes of users’ resistance toward new systems and technologies [8]. Furthermore, IT research is not making full use of behavioral science [7]. Some authors have suggested that expectancy theory should be used in examining users’ acceptance and intent to use an IT system [27,15]. Others have employed an equityimplementation (E-I) model to study the impact of IT on the work environment [25], while another group focused on the effect of IT on core job dimensions [38].

Little work has been attempted to analyze user acceptance of custom-made groupware systems that have been developed to enhance certain processes and improve the performance of particular groups through communication and collaboration. These systems are different from their COTS counterparts: they are more focused, tailor-made for specific applications, and target user preferences and organizational culture. Our work centered on a locally developed groupware system, data analysis and presentation system (DAPS). This system introduced efficiencies to the operations of an engineering group within a large auto manufacturer working on the development and calibration of the car’s emissions controls and depending on test results (managed by a separate organization) to determine its progress. The groupware system was specifically designed to provide a medium for managing knowledge and to speed up repetitive operations. Basically, we examined and formalized the effects of DAPS on organizational productivity and facility planning, and assessed behavior toward this system, which is considered a knowledge management (KM) initiative.

## 2. Literature review

KM is the systematic and organizationally specified process of acquiring, organizing, and communicating knowledge so that employees can use it to become more effective and productive in their work [1]. Many researchers use the notion of a life cycle of activities, or KM sub-processes [28]. Most of these are articulated in four phases: knowledge creation, knowledge organization, knowledge formalization, and knowledge sharing and usage.

## 2.1. Categories of knowledge management systems

Many tools and information systems have been designed to implement KM processes in order to empower the acquisition, integration, sharing, and dissemination of organizational knowledge [3,40]. These were termed Knowledge Management Systems (KMSs) and may be split into four categories [37,42]:

(1) content management tools that can integrate, classify, and codify knowledge from various sources,

(2) knowledge sharing tools that support sharing knowledge between people or other agents,

(3) knowledge search and retrieval systems that have some knowledge discovery abilities,

(4) general KMSs that represent an overall solution to the company’s knowledge management needs.

In a study by Gutierrez-Segura et al. [21], four categories of tools were also identified but they are slightly different; they are tools that:

(1) manage explicit knowledge,

(2) formalize capturing and analysis of knowledge,

(3) exchange knowledge (unstructured knowledge and collaborative work),

(4) help create new knowledge.

Generally, KM technologies and solutions have been designed to perform functions relating to one or a combination of these KM processes. Most have been designed to meet one particular function, while others have been developed for one enterprise.

## 2.2. Critical success factors

Businesses have experienced difficulties in effectively using KM technologies. Pan and Scarbrough [33] identified three components that play a role in successful implementation of KMS: infrastructure, infostructure, and infoculture. The first referred to the hardware and software systems connecting users and allowing them to interact. The second component represented the set of rules that govern the exchange of data and messages. The last involved the background knowledge and social relationships in the work group plus core values and beliefs influencing employees willingness to exchange knowledge and help solve problems. Actually, there is a large body of work on the utilization and success conditions of knowledge management solutions that addresses a wide range of issues. Those conditions are summarized into a list of critical success factors, which are then related to the three components mentioned above, and shown in Table 1.

We believe that KM studies should prove the value of links between KM and performance using empirical data or intermediate measures. For example, in a study of a digital collaboration software system, Orlikowski [32] showed that there was an increase in productivity using an intermediate measure, which was the creation of a knowledge repository to share processes and methods.

## 3. Objectives and methodology

Using KM measures, we sought to produce a descriptive and explanatory theory of the improvements and changes triggered by the deployment and use of the groupware system, DAPS. A similar approach had been used by Orlikowski [31] and others; they integrated specific concepts and findings into an analytical framework for conceptualizing adopted IS tools.

We used DAPS in a case study to demonstrate that focused (tailored to the specific needs of the organization) and collaborative IS tools and knowledge sharing systems play an important role in improving organizational productivity and market competitiveness. To accomplish this we followed a three-step process where we:

 identified the KM features of DAPS.

 established direct links between the integration of DAPS into the work environment and the observed improvements in productivity and operations.

 learned from the users how they perceived the system as a KMS and also about the degree of knowledge sharing in the organization.

Our research methodology involved grounded theory methodology [41]. Inductive analysis allows a researcher to develop a theoretical account of the features on the basis of empirical observations and data [26]. We used test case data to infer KM characteristics about the groupware system and the work environment. Therefore our work aimed at identifying the basis or explanation of the measured and observed improvements due to the KM methods. The focus was on an objective and static description expressed strictly in terms of causality [5]. Much of the data collected for the study came from interviews and observations of work processes and test operations. Moreover, the author was a member of the organization in which the study took place and this allowed him to hold numerous informal one-on-one and group interviews to augment the study.

## 4. Case study

## 4.1. Organization and process overview

The DAPS system was deployed in the emissions test lab (ETL) of a car company that produces both cars and trucks. The ETL department provides other engineering divisions with testing support services, including data acquisition and processing. It is a facility that includes test chambers (cells), where cars are driven on a dynamometer that simulates driving conditions. When tested, the car is connected to measuring devices, instruments, and data acquisition systems.

There are more than 30 different tests that engineers may run during the car’s development phase. These can be run in two modes: cold and hot testing. The cold test requires that the car be stabilized by parking it in a room with constant temperature and humidity for 12 h before it is driven and tested. The hot test on the other hand does not require stabilization. Existing data indicate that all cars run through the same mix of tests. Before being tested, the car must be instrumented and resources (manpower and test cell) scheduled. After testing, engineers analyze the measurement data and make corresponding changes to the car systems; then a new test may be requested and run. This testing cycle continues until the engineer is satisfied with the car’s performance. As depicted in Fig. 1, data analyses and presentations play an important role in this cycle and represent a key enabler in developing and optimizing the performance of the car’s emission controls.

As emission test data files are large (in the order of 5 MB on average), the tasks of performing analyses and designing presentations can take considerable time. Typically, engineers search for specific data ranges and subsets that are thought to correlate with observed external behaviors, establish relationships, identify appropriate statistics, and select effective visualization objects to present the results. Corresponding judgments are then made and used to make alterations to the car system parameters in an attempt to improve performance. As engineers become more experienced, they are able to design more effective analyses and presentations in less time. However, job rotation is encouraged by management and many experienced engineers leave the emissions test group after several years and are replaced by less-experienced people. It thus became evident that a way of storing and transferring developed knowledge and experience was necessary.

## 4.2. Application overview

DAPS offers data analysis and presentation capabilities and was designed to work with emission test data archives. It has both a stand-alone and server version. Fig. 2 shows how an engineer uses the standalone version to perform data analyses on test data (from the FDB server) and generates custom presentations, saving the setup in configuration files, which can be stored locally and become available to the whole user community, improved through feedback, and used as templates for new configurations. Moreover, the engineer can use existing configurations in the KBP server and apply them against test data files to perform analyses and generate presentations. The standalone application and the KBP server enable engineers not only to benefit from others’ work but to build on existing configurations to create new or replace old ones.

For the server version the engineer can specify one or more configurations to be applied on the test data. As shown in Fig. 2, solid lines indicate physical connections while dashed lines represent logical connections and the circled numbers indicate order of operations:

(1) the engineer submits a test request,

(2) test is scheduled to one of the test cells,

(3) test is run,

(4) data is transferred to the FDB server,

(5) KBP server generates the analyses and presentations for a configuration,

(6) the presentations are e-mailed or sent to the engineer’s printer.

The step –i indicates that this engineer or someone else had previously created the specified configuration(s) and saved it/them in the KBP server.

## 4.3. Data collection

Data was collected by: querying the database, structured or informal interviews, and observations. The informal interviews were held with personnel of the ETL department and observations were made by the author and people interviewed. This allowed for capturing more information [18] and provided a means of validating the data.

Data from the database focused on concrete data, such as productivity and its implication on product time to market. Furthermore, we were able to make direct inferences about the degree of attained knowledge sharing among the users of DAPS. The purpose of structured interviews was to understand system characteristics and intangible factors that included management support and commitment, organizational culture, user involvement, peoples roles and human interfaces, user experience with DAPS and other IS systems, and knowledge sharing practices. Finally, observations were used to confirm our intuition, as it related to operations and coordination.

It helps to note that there was an overlap between data collection and analysis, particularly during the structured interview process. This helped considerably in determining the data relevance and completeness. In particular, some survey data was collected through oneon-one interviews but most were returned by users at differing times after they had been received. It was important to determine when we had a representative number of completed surveys. To do this, we kept soliciting survey feedback until the statistics of the answers (means and variances) stabilized. Thus we were performing data analysis while collecting data, while the analysis of database data was strictly performed after data collection.

## 4.4. Data description

The data that characterized each test consisted of emission data (produced by emission analyzers), car performance data (collected from the car’s onboard computer), and environmental data (e.g., room temperature, humidity, etc.). Additionally, the test cell computer kept track of timing information (e.g., test start and stop) and data about the personnel involved in running the test (e.g., test requestor, cell operator, and car driver) as well as data that describe the car. This whole data was identified using a unique test ID that was also associated with a variety of index values, such as car ID, cell ID, test runtime, etc.

After creation, the test data moves from one system to another. Once a test is completed, the data stays on the local computer database for about 24 h but a copy gets transferred to the lab’s central database, where it resides for about a month. Then (influenced by storage availability) the data is moved to a long term storage system where it stays for at least 7 years to allow an engineer to review the car’s development history. The central database was a Sybase<sup>1</sup> relational database that interfaced to the long term storage system, allowing for running SQL queries to retrieve particular data items collected in a 7-year time frame.

The test time data obtained from the central database belonged to 16 randomly picked cars tested before DAPS was put online and to another 16 cars tested 1 year after the system had been introduced. The purpose of the 1-year delay was to use data that reflected system usage after engineers had sufficient time to learn the system and use it regularly. The two sets of 16 cars were chosen to have the same model mix (number of small cars, mid-size cars, trucks, minivans, etc.). It was noted that test times depended on the type of test rather than the model of the car, although the model played a role in the values of the emissions data (e.g., higher or lower CO values). Cold tests took much longer than hot tests, because of the conditioning that was needed. The number of cold tests per car ranged between 7 and 11 and for hot tests between 22 and 31.

To simplify our analysis, we enumerated the tests according to their occurrence in the testing process and averaged the test times for each across all cars (see Table 2). For example, the first value in the third column represented the average time that the first test consumed across all 16 cars. The values under the label M were the numbers of cars that ran the corresponding number of tests, e.g., the last value in the second column shows that only two cars ran through the eleventh cold test. It should be noted that although Table 2 only shows 11 hot tests per car (to conserve space), there were actually between 22 and 31 hot tests per car, a fact that is accounted for in the statistics in the bottom of the table. Correspondingly, under the ‘‘Hot’’ category, the reported statistics in the last two rows correspond to 31 averages, while those under the ‘‘Cold’’ category correspond to 11.

## 4.5. Impact of the deployment of DAPS on productivity

A Student’s t-test was used to determine whether significant variations existed between the ‘‘Before’’ and ‘‘After’’ sets of samples. The statistics were computed for the ‘‘Cold’’ and ‘‘Hot’’ categories (the symbol n denotes the degrees of freedom for the test) and the corresponding results were reported at the bottom of Table 2 and used to determine the corresponding confidence level. Using this data, we know that the average time to run a cold test was reduced by 2.23 h with a confidence level of 99.5%. Similarly, the average time to run a hot test was reduced by 0.44 h with a confidence level of 98.5%. Fig. 3 plots the cumulative gains over all the cold and hot tests, where it can be noticed that only the hot tests contribute to the rise of the top curve after the 11th test.

The database test data for the period that preceded the deployment of DAPS indicated that the average yearly capacity of the test facility was 36 cars. The time savings per car was 32 working hours, which brings the yearly savings for this load to 29 weeks. After the deployment of DAPS, an average car consumed about 300 h of test cell time and thus, the time saved allowed for testing about four extra cars per year. Also, time to market per car was reduced by almost a week.

## 4.6. Benefits versus cost analysis

The entire DAPS implementation was carried out by the software development group (the ETL department) in the test facility. The group’s main functions included providing technical support to the end user community, fixing software bugs, and maintaining and upgrading the many servers in the system, including those computer systems that controlled the test chambers. Major software systems were outsourced and the group supplied the specifications and acted as the interface to set up schedules and ensure smooth integration. Small and medium-sized systems were usually developed by the group itself. DAPS started out as a stand-alone desktop application developed by two members of the group, but features and enhancements were added over time. DAPS is now a key server application that supports close to a 100 product development engineers. In estimating the amount of total effort to develop DAPS, we interviewed the developers. In the process, we learned that many had invested personal time writing code and designing the user interface. Many were uncertain of the exact time (in weeks) they spent but were able to provide a range. In all, the estimates indicated that the organization spent about 60 man-weeks on DAPS development.

The useful life of DAPS was estimated as about 5 years, with an average of 10 weeks per year of development effort expended on adding features and enhancements. Given the average time saving per car (from Table 2) and the yearly capacity of the test cells, the cumulative savings sum up to 34 weeks per year. Thus, it could be shown that the ROI, given a 5 yearamortization schedule, was close to 80% based on test runtime savings and not including any other benefits.

## 4.7. Impact on operational effectiveness

Another major benefit was in improving predictability of the test time. The management had to periodically estimate the capacity of the facility in order to meet forecast demand. Variability in test duration greatly affected the accuracy of such estimates. The standard deviations of the tests after implementing DAPS were reduced substantially (by a factor of about 2) when compared to the corresponding tests before DAPS. Indeed, the average variability was reduced by 1.38 h with a 100% confidence level for cold tests and by 0.91 h, also with a 100% confidence level, for hot tests. These results indicated that facility resource planning could be improved.

## 5.2. Effect on group performance

In principle, the car engineer has to identify factors that would explain behavior and performance of a car’s emission system. But the different systems in the car are interdependent and affected by external factors, such as ambient temperature and road conditions. Finding the root cause of a specific phenomenon may require running more than one test and performing rigorous data analyses. DAPS provided tools that were designed to work with emissions data. These could be grouped into five categories: (1) unveiling hidden trends and events, (2) speeding up the plotting, (3) keeping engineers informed about the status of their tests, (4) providing a data store for preserving knowledge and expertise, and (5) offering tools for facilitating collaboration and knowledge sharing. ## 5.3. Success factors

Many factors may be responsible for the success of a knowledge sharing system, including cultural elements (e.g., user involvement and organisational support) as well as technological elements (e.g., system capabilities and user friendliness). The factors included:

 Motivating employees to support knowledge sharing; the employee performance rating system had elements to encourage this through both results and behavior. The first focused on accomplishments (jobspecific tasks and completed assigned objectives) assessed by the employee’s direct manager. The second consisted of eight components, two of which directly related to knowledge sharing (helping and collaboration and communication and leadership), and were assessed by six colleagues based on the employee’s performance.

 Tolerance for risk and making mistakes: the behaviors part of the rating system also includes two components (taking calculated risks and openness to new ideas). The first dealt with engaging in risks that are linked with promising results while the second encouraged considering new approaches to issues and listening to others.

 Formalizing part of the interactions among work groups: there was no direct evidence of increased interactions as a result of introducing DAPS but data and observations showed that configurations formalized and possibly empowered interaction through designing, modifying, and posting configuration files using the system utilities.

## 6.1. Constructs and measures

The questions were designed to addresses the key intangible and technology factors. In particular, they were intended to show their impact in encouraging knowledge sharing, the compatibility of DAPS with the work norms of employees and the processes of the organization, the degree to which employees felt they have control over their work, and the amount of effort it took to use the system [11].

Out of 84 engineers, 64 returned the surveys (a 76% response rate). All respondents answered all questions, and results are summarized for most of the questions as percentages, as shown in Table 5. For each of questions 1 through 5 and 7 through 9, the table shows the fractions of users who selected each answer. In the last column, the values were scores to represent the weighted answers in the range of 1–5, computed by simply multiplying the ratio (fractional values) by the corresponding answer indices and adding the products.

Questions 6 and 10 were treated differently, since respondents could select more than one choice. For Question 6, results were analyzed to calculate the level of additional features that users demanded (computed as the number of additional features reported by each respondent and their distributions, as shown in Table 6). The level of additional features was computed by summing their weights (offset by one), through multiplying the distributions by their corresponding number of options. The users requested on average 2.64 additional features, implying that they see potential in the capabilities and would like to extend them. As for Question 10, the results were categorized into formal and informal communication mechanisms (see Table 7) where the corresponding scores were used in assessing the KM styles in the organization.

The next step was to link the 10 survey questions to the six groups of KM learning styles. We followed a strategy that resembles the Honey and Mumford Learning Styles Questionnaire [23]. This method uses 80 tick-box questions that identify the personality type of the respondents and relate that to four learning styles: activists, reflectors, theorists, and pragmatists. A scoring system is used in which the 80 questions are divided into four groups corresponding to their styles, where the number of ticks in each group gives a score that signifies the ‘‘degree of belonging’’ to a learning style. The method then results in four weights that map the questions to the learning styles. In our study, we associated each of the 10 questions with the six KM learning styles of Choi and Lee (Section 2.3) based on its intended and possible interpretations.

## 6.2. Analysis of the results

The answers to the first two questions confirmed the conclusion found in the analysis of the time data. The score of answers to the first question (4.08) clearly shows that the user community perceives DAPS as a tool that adds value to the process. The second question related to management’s involvement and whether it had played a role in the adoption of DAPS as a tool for supporting core job functions. The individual answers suggested that management could participate more actively in encouraging personnel to use the tool on a more regular basis. As to Question 3, which asked users about their views for deploying DAPS to other areas in the company, the majority was not enthusiastic about extending the use of the system to other areas and did not believe this will result in significant additional knowledge sharing. A possible interpretation of this outcome is that users felt the tool would become less focused and more complex.

It was obvious that the work environment exhibited some dynamism and system-orientation. Although there were some loners in the organization who did not contribute significantly to the knowledge sharing process, the majority was integrative and aggressive, employing networks of communication and depending on knowledge to produce better results.

## 7. Lessons for management

Since the effects were quantifiable and measurable, it was not difficult to demonstrate that there is a causeeffect relationship between the deployment of DAPS and observed gains. The next step was to build a case for upper management.

## 7.1. Local IT support

Upper management was considering eliminating the IT group or at least reducing it to one or two developers, arguing that software development was not a core job function within a car manufacturing company. Local management (department managers and first-line supervisors) felt that departmental software development could be consolidated into one corporate-wide IT organization, restricted to providing support and consultation.

Our study identified the contributions of DAPS in reducing the time to market in product development and increasing organizational effectiveness. Having a local IT group has unique advantages. Among them is the group’s involvement in day-to-day operations that allow it to develop detailed knowledge of the operations, processes, and equipment. Furthermore, daily interaction with car engineers has allowed the group personnel to develop intricate knowledge of car tests and an understanding of the challenges and problems of engineers. This argument, with our findings, triggered middle management to change the IT group name to the Emission System Integration Group to protect the group and inform upper management that it was an integral part of the organization.

## 7.2. Spending on knowledge capturing and documentation

This study showed that the collaborative KMS introduced quantifiable improvement in efficiency and effectiveness of core operations by saving process time and increasing operational predictability. Technically, DAPS reduced the loss of knowledge and expertise caused by job rotation. Furthermore, DAPS helped the organization build an infrastructure for documenting and storing knowledge of organizational functions. As this effort involved a long-term investment, it required a sustained commitment from management with dedicated resources.

## 8. Concluding remarks

Many researchers have proposed different frameworks and models for assessing the benefits of IS and empowering the implementation of KM processes in organizations. Here, we discussed a case study that employed proposed frameworks and models to examine the impact of deploying a specialized and locally developed groupware system on the productivity of a product development group and on the effectiveness of its operations. We analyzed the extent to which this system addressed the KM critical success factors for knowledge sharing and presented evidence in support of the hypothesis that knowledge capturing and sharing were taking place. A survey was conducted to understand user characteristics and management support. The feedback supported the conclusions drawn from the analysis of the test data and allowed us to rate the organization along six dimensions that represented KM styles or methods. Recommendations to management involving organizational structure and spending on IT were formulated.
