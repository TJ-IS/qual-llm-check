# COMMITMENT AND REPLACEMENT OF EXISTING SAAS-DELIVERED APPLICATIONS: A MIXED-METHODS INVESTIGATION<sup>1</sup>

Xiao Xiao Department of Digitalization, Copenhagen Business School, 2000 Frederiksberg DENMARK {xx.digi@cbs.dk}

Saonee Sarker McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22904 U.S.A. {saonee.sarker@comm.virginia.edu}

Ryan T. Wright McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22904 U.S.A. {rtwright@virginia.edu}

Suprateek Sarker McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA 22904 U.S.A. {suprateek.sarker@comm.virginia.edu}

Babu John Mariadoss Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409 U.S.A. {bjohnmar@ttu.edu}

As the highest level of cloud computing delivery model, Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) has gained considerable popularity in the industry as a new way of deploying IT solutions, due to its low cost and high elasticity. However, the new business model associated with SaaS highlights the importance for SaaS vendors to understand how to retain customers in a hyper-competitive market. In particular, increasing customer retention and preventing customers from replacing the adopted SaaS applications has become a crucial task for all SaaS vendors. In this study, using a mixed-methods approach, and drawing on the cognitive–affective–conative– action (CACA) framework, we investigate the IS replacement phenomenon in the context of SaaS-delivered applications. Our qualitative study allows us to develop an IS-centric view of customer commitment by differentiating between organizations’ commitment to the SaaS application and to the cloud computing technology in general, while the subsequent quantitative study validates the difference between the two types of commitment and helps understand how they together influence organizations’ intentions to replace a SaaS application. Our results generate important theoretical implications for research on IS replacement and clarifies the concept of customer commitment. We also offer practical guidelines to SaaS vendors on how to retain customers so as to survive/thrive in this competitive market.

Keywords: Cloud computing, software-as-a-service, commitment, IS replacement, mixed-methods

## Introduction

Often regarded as the highest level of the cloud computing delivery model (Iyer and Henderson 2010), SaaS (Softwareas-a-Service) refers to software or applications delivered as a service to customers via the Internet (Owens 2010). Apart from enabling a subscription payment model that allows the customers to “pay-as-you-go” (Choudhary 2007), and requiring minimal demands for IT operational tasks such as infrastructure application management (Lee et al. 2003; Winkler et al. 2011), SaaS offers huge elasticity that allows organizations to dynamically scale their IT resources both up or down (Marston et al. 2011). However, this new delivery model also comes with its drawbacks, such as security and privacy concerns (Ryan 2011), problems with customization that are only available at higher layers (Xin and Levina 2008), and issues around service quality and availability (Marston et al. 2011). Despite the limitations, cloud applications continue to be an important strategic lever for organizations. Gartner predicts that by 2021 more than half of global organizations will adopt an all-cloud strategy (Panetta 2017). Specifically, SaaS adoption continues to grow at about 20% per year (Darrow 2017).

Given the promising market of SaaS, it is expected that the industry will become increasingly competitive with the entry of traditional software vendors who have started to provide a web-based, hosted version of their signature software (Cusumano 2010; Theuma 2015). This poses huge challenges for vendors that only offer SaaS products. Specifically, vendors with the SaaS business model are no longer able to rely on high up-front payments by customers but instead must rely on on smaller periodic subscription payments, implying that the key to survival for SaaS vendors is to decrease customer attrition so as to keep cash-flow going (Kasznik 2014; Senovo 2018). In other words, “churn is king” in the SaaS market (Kasznik 2014). A recent survey with over 300 SaaS providers revealed that, economically speaking, it is much cheaper to retain existing customers than to acquire new ones, with the cost of the latter notably increasing each year while the cost of the former staying stable (Skok 2015). Hence, to survive in such a competitive market, it has become extremely important for SaaS vendors to retain their customers (Benlian et al. 2011; Opara-Martins et al. 2016). This problem can be further worsened due to the minimal vendor interaction implied in the definition of SaaS (Mell and Grance 2011) which leaves most of the responsibilities related to configuration, implementation, and intra-organizational training to the customers themselves, potentially causing customer frustration, and ultimately resulting in replacement. This is especially true for customer groups who have a relatively lower IT sophistication level and/or with fewer IT resources (e.g., small- and medium-sized enterprises, and nonprofit organizations). Indeed, as industry experts indicate, in the SaaS context, vendor switching happens often, and such switching can even entail going back to on-premise solutions (Barney 2015). For instance, according to a recent study, 40% of surveyed customers reported moving applications back from cloud to a traditional setting (Wong 2017).

Given the above, in this study, we focus on the new challenge of customer “churn” within the SaaS context. Churn has been widely used in the marketing literature and is defined as “customer movement in and out of the marketplace” (Keaveney and Parthasarathy 2001, p. 374). In our context, we conceptualize churn as IS replacement: premature termination of the use of the current SaaS system and replacement of it with an alternative. We emphasize that such termination does not include retirement of a dated system; rather, it signifies a replacement of a system that is clearly still within its promised life-cycle. To ground our investigation, we draw on the theoretical concept of commitment, which is defined as “a frame of mind or psychological state that compels an individual toward a course of action” (Meyer and Herscovitch 2001, p. 303). Commitment has been widely used in the marketing discipline to study customer retention and customer switching behavior, both at the individual level and the organizational level (Bansal et al. 2004; Ganesan et al. 2010; Gustafsson et al. 2005; Morgan and Hunt 1994). Given that the IS replacement phenomenon embodies both the concepts of customer retention and switching behavior, the theoretical lens of com mitment is appropriate and leads to the following question: What is the role of commitment on the replacement of SaaSdelivered applications?

We believe that the answer is of theoretical importance from two perspectives. First, it holds potential to contribute to the stream of research on cloud computing broadly and on SaaS specifically. While there is abundant academic research on SaaS-related topics within the IS discipline, the majority of studies have focused on the issue of initial adoption (Benlian et al. 2009; Cho and Chan 2015; Oliveira et al. 2014; van de Weerd et al. 2016), followed by several econometric studies on the pricing models (Choudhary 2007; Li et al. 2017; Ma and Seidmann 2015). Very few studies focus on postadoption issues, with two studies aiming to understand the impact of SaaS adoption (Jede and Teuteberg 2016; Winkler and Brown 2013), and another study examining factors that are essential to successful SaaS assimilation (Wright et al. 2017).<sup>2</sup> Our observation is echoed by a recent literature review by Wang et al. (2016) who conclude that existing research on cloud computing is still in its nascent stage with foundation-building conceptual studies dominating the field, and with the majority of empirical research focusing on adoption. Thus, it is of theoretical interest to look beyond initial adoption of SaaS, and our study endeavors to address such an objective by investigating the issue of SaaS replacement.

Second, the focus on the SaaS context will allow us to develop contextualized understanding (Hong et al. 2013) of the IS replacement phenomenon, especially in light of the theoretical lens of commitment. We would like to argue that the increasing servitization of IT (Bardhan et al. 2010) has contributed much to the commoditization of computing resources, as demonstrated by the rise of cloud computing. Such a trend in practice has rendered certain theoretical phenomena such as churn relevant in the business-to-business (B2B) IT sector, which has historically not been particularly important in the on-premises context. We acknowledge that existing IS research has addressed replacement related issues in the business-to-consumer (B2C) context, often in the form of switching of online services (e.g., Carter et al. 2014; Kim et al. 2005; Kim and Son 2009; Xu et al. 2014). The different characteristics of the B2B sector and the SaaS context offered opportunities to discover additional dynamics of customer switching behavior.

To answer our research question, we adopt a mixed methods design (Venkatesh et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2016). As Venkatesh et al. (2016) argue, such a design is appropriate when existing research on a topic (such as SaaS replacement and the role of commitment) is either fragmented or missing. In developing our contextualized understanding of commitment within SaaS applications, we drew inspiration from Hong et al. (2013). We specifically followed their guidelines for single-context theory contextualization by first identifying general theories that may help in understanding the types of commitment or role of commitment within SaaS, then contextualizing by drawing on qualitative data and Oliver’s (1997, 1999) cognitive–affective–conative–action framework, and, finally, developing the SaaS-specific research model including contextualized factors, and testing it using a quantitative study.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: first, we review pertinent literature on IS replacement and then describe the theoretical foundation of our investigation; next, we discuss the qualitative study; thereafter, we develop the research model based on the results of the qualitative study and relevant literature; the quantitative study is then described, followed by a discussion of the findings; finally, we highlight the theoretical and practical implications and limitations of the study.

## Literature on IS Replacement

IS replacement may be seen as being a part of the family of concepts dealing with IS discontinuance.<sup>3</sup> Furneaux and Wade (2011) defined IS discontinuance as “the cessation of the use of an organizational information system” (p. 574). Such a definition not only includes replacement of the current system but also emphasizes the natural ending of an information system’s life-cycle. However, with SaaS solutions, the concept of a life-cycle is outdated, since by definition such systems do not require significant updates by customers and are always the current and only version available. O’Reilly (2007) famously stated that cloud services have ended the need for software release cycles. Such nuances suggest that the replacement of a SaaS solution should be investigated using a different set of theoretical lenses than those used to study an on-premise system. We define IS replacement as IS customers’ decision to terminate the existing exchange relationship with the current vendor regardless of product life-cycle considerations. This concept is more similar to customer switching in the marketing discipline, or the phenomenon of churn (Keaveney and Parthasarathy 2001) than to the term IS discontinuance used prominently in the IS literature.

Keaveney and Parthasarathy (2001) argue that churn or customer movement in and out of the marketplace (in the context of online services) involves two possibilities: (1) switching to another provider with similar services, or (2) discontinuance of the usage of the current services altogether. Along similar lines, replacement of a SaaS product could also be accomplished through the adoption of a new solution similar to the existing one from another vendor, or by the adoption of a totally different solution provided by another vendor (thereby giving up the use of the current type of system entirely). However, from the vendors’ point of view, these two scenarios bear similar consequences: customers moving out of their marketplace. Therefore, in this study, we view IS replacement from the vendors’ perspective and do not distinguish between the two scenarios mentioned above on how customers switch, but rather theoretically posit the common denominators that can prevent IS replacement from happening. Doing so will help generate guidelines for SaaS vendors on how to increase customer retention and survive in a competitive market.

Our literature review on IS replacement and related concepts such as discontinuance, migration, upgrading, and switching (see Appendix A Table A2 for a full review) reveals a paucity of IS studies on replacement in general, and none in the specific context of SaaS. Among the exceptions are works by Kim et al. (2005) and Kim and Son (2009) who conducted multiple individual-level studies on post-adoption issues (e.g., switching, continued use) related to the use of online services. A recent trend has seen studies extending service switching to the context of online social networks (e.g., Xu et al. 2014). At the organizational level, a few studies have been done on enterprise systems (Furneaux and Wade 2011), packaged software (Khoo and Robey 2007), open systems (Chau and Tam 2000), and off-the-shelf software (Castner and Ferguson 2000).

We argue that the SaaS context presents a novel phenomena that not only deserves separate examination, but also calls for different theoretical considerations. For instance, not only are there no software release cycles of SaaS, but cloud-based IT solutions are characterized by their quick deployment (Armbrust et al. 2010). Hence, it is much easier for organizational cloud adopters to experience new competing solutions at any point of time including the adoption and deployment phases of another solution. Therefore, the focus on SaaS replacement will allow us to revisit a traditional research topic “with a more contextual[ized] lens,” as called for by Johns (2017, p. 586). Similarly, in the IS area, scholars have suggested that the study of technology especially deserves better contextualization (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001), and that the contextualization should be centered around the characteristics of the technology (Hong et al. 2013). Given such assertions, it can be expected that the context of SaaS results in changes to the factors affecting how and why replacement occurs. Thus, a new study on the replacement phenomenon in the SaaS context is warranted.

## Theoretical Lens

Given the low technical barrier to replacing SaaS, there is a need to explore how to prevent SaaS solutions from being replaced in organizations. Commitment is an important consideration for SaaS vendors; nevertheless, there is a dearth of research about commitment in the extant IS literature. That said, commitment has attracted a great deal of attention from researchers across many other disciplines. Most of the current commitment-related studies were built upon the seminal work of Meyer and Allen (1991) who proposed different forms of commitment: (1) affective commitment, related to positive feelings and affections, (2) continuance commitment, associated with potential costs of the switching/termination behavior,<sup>4</sup> and (3) normative commitment, related to the obligation and responsibility to the action resulting from social pressures and personal norms. It should be noted that the commitment concept here refers to attitudinal commitment, a psychological state, as opposed to behavioral commitment, the action of persistence (Allen and Meyer 1990; Meyer and Allen 1991), with the former capturing the attitudes signaling attachment, which often leads to the latter behavior, representing the action itself. Past researchers have adapted Meyer and Allen’s (1991) tripartite conceptualization of commitment to study the relationship between customer commitment and customer retention/switching behavior, at the individual and at the organizational level. At the individual level, customer commitment has been found to impact many constructs including brand loyalty (Pritchard et al. 1999), future intentions regarding the product/service (Garbarino and Johnson 1999), and word-of-mouth (Palmatier et al. 2006). At the organizational level (within the B2B context of relationship marketing), Morgan and Hunt (1994) identified relationship commitment, together with trust, as the key mediators in the relational exchange, and recognized the effect of relationship commitment on the switching behavior for any party that is involved in such a relational exchange (i.e., buyers, sellers, etc.).

In this research, we adopt the concept of customer commitment to study IS replacement in the context of SaaS. We believe that the application of commitment theory, which takes into consideration emotion-related factors, to the study of IS replacement in the context of SaaS is altogether appropriate. The literature on commitment suggests that commitment is negatively associated with constructs related to switching behavior.<sup>5</sup> Next, we focus on understanding the nature of commitment within the SaaS context through Phase I of our mixed-methods approach.

## A Mixed-Methods Investigation

In this study, we draw on Teddlie and Tashakkori (1998, p. 5) who argue that mixed-methods studies “contain elements of both the quantitative and qualitative approaches.” We follow

Venkatesh et al.’s (2016) guidelines for conducting mixedmethods designs, which range from defining the purpose of the design, the epistemological assumptions, and data collection and analysis strategies, to ensuring the quality of inferences drawn from the different phases of the design. As per their suggestions, we start off by articulating three research questions (one qualitative, one quantitative, and one mixed-methods). In terms of the purpose of our study, we see it as falling under the category of “developmental” (Creswell et al. 2008; Sarker et al. 2018; Tashakkori and Teddlie 2008; Teddlie and Tashakkori 1998; Venkatesh et al. 2013). In such contexts, “questions for one strand emerge from the inferences of a previous one … or one strand provides hypotheses to be tested in the next one” (Venkatesh et al. 2013, p. 26). Specifically, the purpose of the qualitative study is to develop the concept of “commitment” within the context of SaaS while the purpose of quantitative study is to test the constructs and the research model generated through the qualitative investigation. We argue that such a “developmental” approach is necessary in our study because of the limited use of organizational-level commitment in the IS literature. The concept of commitment is well-established in the literature on consumer behavior where the emphasis has been more directed toward customers’ relationship with the vendor/ seller/supplier (Bansal et al. 2004; Gustafsson et al. 2005; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Pritchard et al. 1999; Verhoef 2003). In an IS context, we are interested in the relationship between the organization and the service itself. Further, past application of the commitment concept by IS scholars has mostly been at the individual level with the focus on products/ services such as website and virtual world (Kim and Son 2009; Li et al. 2006; Zhou et al. 2012).

We adopt multiple paradigms as our epistemological strand, where, during the qualitative phase, we adopt a more inductive approach, and then use deductive testing in the quantitative phase (thereby subscribing to the dialectic stance). Our methodology can be classified as “mixed-methods multistrand” (Venkatesh et al. 2016, p. 443), following a “sequential exploratory design” (Creswell et al. 2008, p. 68). Our study also falls within the realm of “dominant–less dominant design” where “one paradigm is dominant, while a small component of the overall study is drawn from alternative paradigm” (Teddlie and Tashakkori 1998, p. 44). As for sampling, we use a sequential sampling strategy with parallel samples, and data analysis is also done sequentially to help build the research model for the quantitative study from the results of the qualitative study. In Appendix E, we elaborate upon our decision choices surrounding the mixedmethods design (adapted from Venkatesh et al. 2016), and in Appendix F, we show how we follow established criteria for mixed-method designs (Venkatesh et al. 2013).

## Qualitative Study

## Data Collection and Data Analysis

As part of a larger project on cloud computing, data for this study was collected from in-depth interviews conducted with SaaS customers of Cloud Inc. Foundation (pseudonym). Cloud Inc. is most reputed for its cloud-based customer relationship management solutions and its innovativeness in delivering IT service to customer firms from various backgrounds. Cloud Inc. Foundation is the branch that serves nonprofit organizations and higher education institutes with Cloud Inc. products at discounted prices. The sample of the qualitative study was drawn from Cloud Inc. Foundation’s nonprofit customers, and was constructed to examine a broad spectrum of organizational adoption of the Cloud Inc. application. First, we gathered data from organizations that were currently using Cloud Inc.’s SaaS solution. Second, to provide an understanding of IS replacement, we gathered data from organizations that used the Cloud Inc. application for at least 6 months, and then replaced (or were in the process of replacing) this application with another solution. Our interviewees included 21 individuals who were/are in charge of the SaaS solution in their respective organizations. Semistructured interviews were conducted over the phone (due to the fact that these organizations were located throughout the United States). All interviews were recorded and transcribed. We present the profiles of the interviewees as well as their organizations in Table C1, Appendix C.

The theoretical framework of customer commitment provided guidance for our data analysis (Walsham 1995). Due to the exploratory nature of the qualitative study (Sarker et al. 2018), we did not constrain ourselves to existing theoretical ideas but also allowed new insights to emerge (Ravishankar et al. 2011). To be specific, during the analysis process, relevant concepts were identified by the authors through systematic reading and rereading of the interviews, and labeling and relabeling of the concepts (Walsham 2006). The process was iterative, and roughly resembled a constant comparative analysis, ending when theoretical saturation occurred (Glaser and Strauss 1967). Table C2 in Appendix C presents the analysis process by mapping out the main constructs with supporting empirical data.

## Ensuring Quality of the Qualitative Inquiry

Throughout the qualitative study, we have taken various measures to ensure the design quality and the explanation quality of our inquiry, as per Venkatesh et al.’s (2013) guidelines. To start with, in terms of design adequacy, the case company we chose, Cloud Inc., is a major vendor in the SaaS market, who has been facing issues of customer attrition (which prompted them to collaborate with us for this research project). Therefore, it can be argued that the selection of the organization is suitable for the purpose of the study. Second, we have made sure that we were able to enter the field with credibility, by establishing contact points through executive level managers at Cloud Inc. Third, when selecting interviewees, we ensured that we had access to customer organizations that have had experience with the Cloud Inc. application, and recruited organizations with different types of experiences and different levels of success with the Cloud Inc. application. Finally, when conducting interviews, we followed a high-level guideline to ensure consistency, while at the same time being sensitive to the principles of flexibility, non-direction, specificity, and range (Flick 2014).

To ensure analytic adequacy, we fully transcribed our interviews. Also, the data analysis process took place in an iterative manner, where the authors identified and coded relevant concepts through systematic reading of the interviews (Walsham 2006). By engaging in such constant comparative analysis, we ensured that theoretical saturation was reached in the end (Glaser and Strauss 1967). Furthermore, we maintained a transparent analysis process by including quotations where possible and also by presenting the main concepts with supporting quotes in Table C2 (Sarker et al. 2013).

Finally, we ensured inferential validity by establishing that the constructs identified through the qualitative study were plausible and further validated in the quantitative survey.

## Findings

In this section, we present the findings of the qualitative study. To start with, our interviews revealed that IS replacement in the SaaS context is definitely a concern. This was highlighted by a number of interviewees expressing the intention and/or action of their organizations to replace the current Cloud Inc. application with new ones. Some of the organizations intended to make the switch to an equivalent cloud solution, while others decided to roll back to their existing on-premise systems.

Such intention and/or action to replace the existing Cloud Inc. SaaS application are often associated with the negative attitudes developed toward the application, throughout its implementation and use process. For instance, the director of an organization that was in the process of replacing Cloud Inc.’s application with another cloud-based solution said:

I am not at this stage convinced that Cloud Inc. [SaaS application] could do what we needed it to do or what we wanted it to do .... [Only] some of our users were using it … many of the people using it didn’t feel that the interface was intuitive as they had hoped for. Once they got used to it their attitude transitioned from dislike to sort of apathy. I don’t know that they were ever enthusiastic about using it but they continued to use it and saw it as a tool which maybe was as good as we can hope for.

As this quote indicates, the negative attitudes toward the Cloud Inc. SaaS application are formed through the direct experience with the application, and usually caused by certain characteristics (or perception of the characteristics) of the application itself. The common issues echoed by many interviewees include mismatch of labels, the need for extensive customization (not a turn-key solution), and high levels of complexity. In other words, as they adopt the Cloud Inc. application, these nonprofit organizations tend to find the solution too complicated to configure and use over time; some organizations also find the system not very suitable for the nonprofit environment, as lamented by one of the interviewees:

Yeah. I mean I guess it is a pretty typical .... Like every organization that would use the nonprofit starter pack you need to essentially kind of make a decision about how you want to deal with that situation. There are really just a couple of routes that you can choose. None of them are perfect but that tends to be just because the [Cloud Inc. application] was built as a business to business sales tool rather than a business to consumer tool.

For certain organizations, these problems are simply too significant to overcome, and the negative attitudes regarding the application eventually lead to replacement.

On the other hand, positive attitudes toward the Cloud Inc.’s application or even the company itself were also observed in our interviews, and such attitudes were generally associated with a successful experience with the product, showcasing a state of bonding to the use of the application (Gilliland and Bello 2002). The system administrator of an organization that has been using Cloud Inc.’s application for a while and is planning to continue to do so highlights such a situation:

I like Cloud Inc. I find it generally to be a pretty intuitive tool and one that from a maintenance perspective is really easy to add and remove fields or build new reports or do things that in other tools can often take more technical expertise to be able to do.

The commonly mentioned features of the Cloud Inc. solution include ease of use and its customizability. For example, some organizations are able to utilize the application’s horse power (although mentioned by other interviewees as being more than what was needed) to realize its potential in meeting various work demands.

Such positive attitudes are often associated with general satisfaction with the application, as many of these organizations have achieved wide-spread adoption of the tool:

So it was actually a pretty good adoption here. I mean I am not saying that [the Cloud Inc. application] is the perfect solution. There are issues with it, but I will say that is was well adopted. I think that we did a really good job at adapting ourselves to it.

Further, organizations that are generally satisfied with the adoption and use experience tend to hold affection toward the Cloud Inc. application, and some even express their commitment to the application despite external changes. The director of one organization mentioned that they continued with the Cloud Inc. application even when their funding provider asked them to switch to another system, in part because of the tremendous success they have had with the deployment:

Oh yeah, people like [Cloud Inc. application]. They like [Cloud Inc. application] a lot. I mean it has made our lives much easier and you can do it anywhere. We issue laptops. It is amazing what we have done. I mean we turned this elephant in a bath tub definitely .... For example, the county adopted another system that is not compatible with [Cloud Inc. application] and we stuck with our data and have taken them on like almost a battle with the city, which is our funding source. They wanted us to throw our system out but we have clung to it.

Our interviews also revealed that such commitment to the Cloud Inc. application could be derived from the investment (especially monetary) organizations have made to adopt and implement the application, echoing the concept of calculative commitment (Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). As one of the interviewees mentioned, considering the financial investment they have made, they are willing to or even happy with sticking to the current Cloud Inc. solution:

So yeah those ten licenses are free and the paid licenses are heavily discounted but once you are hitting 200–300 users you are putting a decent amount of money towards this system every year … as we have grown … each year we say: wow, this is a fair amount of money that we are putting towards licensing every year .... That is generally what brings us back to the place where we are happy with the system and do understand that it is not like there is a lot of other choices out there that are of no cost or anything like that.

In summary, the results above align well with the theoretical lens of commitment, by showcasing that commitment to the existing application, affective and calculative, could prevent organizations from replacing it. Furthermore, such commitment is often developed through organizations’ positive experiences with the application in the deployment process, and is associated with a general level of satisfaction.

The interview data also demonstrated that customers’ commitment can be directed toward the cloud computing technology in general. For instance, many interviewees revealed that the initial decision to adopt the Cloud Inc. solution was to a great extent based on the fact that the solution is cloud based:

So for that reason and just for the fact that we didn’t have IT staff that could be going out to all of these cities and uploading software onto hundreds of computers around the country. Those reasons really made us think about a cloud solution, something that we didn’t have to load in software, something that had transparency.

Further, many organizations, while using the Cloud Inc. application, may recognize the benefits of the cloud-based solutions, such as elimination of installment, elimination of infrastructure support, reduced IT responsibilities, ubiquitous accessibility, or even multi-tenancy (one installation, many instances). In other words, the possibilities offered by the cloud technology can lead to these positive experiences and hence a certain level of satisfaction. Such experiences and satisfaction could result in positive attitudes toward the cloud computing technology or cloud-based IT solutions in general, as the system administrator said when he advertised Cloud Inc.’s application to the users during the training:

Generally I sort of advertise … all of the benefits of … a cloud-based system.

Additionally, we would like to highlight that such commitment to the cloud computing technology in general should not be seen as being similar to commitment toward Cloud Inc.’s product, as our results indicated that the attachment towards cloud computing can be developed independent of the cloudbased product. For instance, the director of another organization who chose to replace Cloud Inc. application with an on-premise solution lamented on the fact the replacement is not cloud-based:

It is a hosted platform. It is certainly different than cloud meaning that we are contracting directly with [an on-premise system] to maintain the system and to host it. There are a significant number of pain points meaning that as [this on-premise system] comes out with new versions we still have considerable pain to in effect upgrade to the next version. It is very different than cloud based. I would prefer to be on something that was truly cloud based or SaaS. This is not it.

Finally, not only have organizations shown different opinions toward the Cloud Inc. SaaS application and toward the cloud computing technology, commitment to the technology itself can also prevent replacement from happening, even when commitment to the product is absent. For instance, one of the interviewees, the director of an organization, told us that he thinks highly of cloud computing and is very committed to the technology, but on the other hand, his commitment toward the Cloud Inc. application is not as high, due to the fact that it does not fit his organizational needs:

First of all I am a big cloud computing fanatic … and I have been for a while. So that is my gestalt … I am completely dedicated to it … but I do think Cloud Inc. [SaaS application] is more complicated than it needs to be for my needs.

In fact, this director did not choose to replace Cloud Inc.’s application, despite his not-so-positive experience of the application itself. He told us that he is still learning to figure everything out with Cloud Inc.’s CRM application as he believed that cloud computing is a “great thing.”

## Summary of Results for the Qualitative Study

The results of our qualitative analysis highlight two important aspects of commitment in the SaaS context. First, commitment toward the cloud computing technology can be different from commitment toward a certain cloud computing product (in this case, Cloud Inc.’s SaaS-delivered application). To be specific, commitment to the Cloud Inc. application often resulted from successful (or partially successful) deployment of the application. On the other hand, commitment to the cloud computing technology in general can be developed regardless of the commitment to the application itself (this is demonstrated in Table C2 by the large number of interviewees who praise cloud-based IT solutions in general). Thus, organizations can be attached to the features/characteristics associated with cloud, regardless of whether they bond with the specific application or not. Such commitment to cloud computing may be triggered by past successful experiences with cloud-based solutions in general, positive experiences with certain cloud-specific features (e.g., ubiquitous access, elimination of installation, etc.), or general discourse regarding the “revolutionary” nature of the technology within the community (e.g., Ramiller and Swanson 2003).

Following such findings, we argue that it is important to contextualize the construct of commitment within IS by differentiating between commitment to the technologyenabled product<sup>6</sup> and commitment to the technology itself. It is to be noted that different SaaS applications provided by different vendors each have their own attributes, which are not shared by the others. For instance, Iyer and Henderson (2010) categorized major cloud computing vendors using two dimensions: level of community involvement in API (application program interface) maintenance, which determines the extent to which customers are able to configure their SaaS application, and the extension of API usage by third parties, which also has implications for the features of the cloud service offered. Furthermore, a SaaS application is always associated with the vendor company itself, suggesting that factors such as brand reputation can also have an impact on customer’s feelings toward the product (e.g., Bansal et al. 2004). In this sense, we believe it is reasonable to treat commitment to the cloud computing technology as a separate concept from commitment to the SaaS application.

The qualitative study not only highlight differences within the commitment concept in the SaaS context, it also revealed that commitment is composed of both affective and calculative components. Our findings show that affective commitment can be developed from positive experiences with the application. For example, many organizations expressed their excitement about the application since it allowed them to digitize and streamline the organizational process with total in-house efforts, which was unthinkable earlier. As for calculative commitment, our findings are in line with existing literature, in the sense that it reflects the “need to” dimension of the commitment concept (Bansal et al. 2004; Meyer and Herscovitch 2001). For instance, calculative commitment to the Cloud Inc. application is often associated with switching costs and/or sunk costs as well as the evaluation of alternative options whereas calculative commitment to the cloud computing technology is more related to the fear of losing out on the cloud-specific features and characteristics. It should be noted that our study did not reveal an instantiation of normative component—as per Meyer and Allen’s (1991) original conceptualization of employee commitment. We believe that such a commitment, which refers to social pressures, is less relevant in the business context. Such a finding is also consistent with the existing marketing literature where most studies focus only on affective commitment and calculative commitment (e.g., Davis-Sramek et al. 2009; Ganesan et al. 2010; Gilliland and Bello 2002; Gustafsson et al. 2005; Verhoef 2003).

To summarize, the qualitative study findings related to affective and calculative dimensions are consistent with existing literature on commitment. Further, the qualitative study highlights the multidimensionality of the commitment concept in the SaaS context, and the differences between them. By differentiating between commitment to the application and commitment to the underlying technology, we hope to develop an IS-centric view of commitment and add richness to our understanding of the IS replacement phenomenon. What was not clear from our data or in the extant literature is the manner in which the different types of commitment influence replacement intention. For this, we turn to the existing literature.

## Research Model

Informed by our findings from the qualitative study, we develop our research model, drawing on the cognitive– affective–conative–action (CACA) framework of Oliver (1997, 1999). The CACA framework delineates how customer retention (conceptualized as behavioral loyalty, the action component) is achieved, and establishes its relationship with customer commitment (conceptualized as attitudinal loyalty, the conative component) (Chiou and Droge 2006). Barring a few studies (e.g., Kim et al. 2005; Kim and Son 2009), extant literature on IS replacement emphasize the role of cognitive and affective variables (Bhattacherjee 2001), but neglect conative variables such as commitment, which is an important construct as per our qualitative findings. Finally, past research suggests that Oliver’s (1999) framework is suitable for high-involvement decision making, which is consistent with the organizational decision-making situation in the SaaS context (Chio and Droge 2006; Davis-Sramek et al. 2009).

The CACA framework starts with the cognition of the brand/product, which, according to Oliver (1999, p. 35), can be based on “recent experienced-based information.” This element is usually captured by customers’ perception of product/service quality (Oliver 1997, 1999). The affective element of the framework captures the development of the attitude toward the brand/product “on the basis of cumulatively satisfying usage occasions.” In other words, customer satisfaction can lead to conative loyalty that represents “deeply held commitment to buy” (Oliver 1999, p. 35). Finally, we have the action element, which captures customers (or intention to engage in) repurchasing behavior (Chiou and Droge 2006). We show how we apply the CACA framework to our context in Table 1, and then present our research model in Figure 1.

Service quality perceived by customers represents one type of cognitive response to the brand, based on the immediate experience with the service provided by the vendor (Chiou and Droge 2006; Oliver 1999), whereas satisfaction is often viewed as the affective element that represent an overall judgment of the exchange experience (Anderson and Weitz 1992; Oliver 1999). Results of our qualitative study echoes this and demonstrates that customers who are satisfied with the Cloud Inc. solution often praise the positive aspects of the product, such as its flexibility and ease of use. Various studies, both in marketing and IS disciplines, have established the relationship between perceived service quality and user satisfaction (Benlian et al. 2011; Davis-Sramek et al. 2009; Garbarino and Johnson 1999; Grover et al. 1996; Taylor and Baker 1994). Based on the above, we state as follows:

## H1: Perceived service quality of SaaS-delivered applications is positively associated with the overall satisfaction of SaaS customers.

We capture conative loyalty through customers’ commitment to the SaaS-delivered application, which has been conceptualized as existing in two forms: calculative commitment and affective commitment (Chiou and Droge 2006; Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). We define affective commitment to the application as the emotional attachment to the particular SaaSdelivered application provided by a specific SaaS vendor, stimulated by the interaction with the application; and we define calculative commitment to the application as the attachment to the application caused by the feeling of loss (Gustafsson et al. 2005; Zhou et al. 2012). According to the CACA framework, when satisfied, customers are likely to engage in reciprocal exchange, and develop an attachment to the exchange relationship (Davis-Sramek et al. 2009; Garbarino and Johnson 1999; Johnson et al. 2001). In the context of SaaS, customers may develop positive feelings toward a certain SaaS application along the process of adoption, implementation, infusion, and daily usage, which resembles certain level of affective attachment. This is often triggered by the high level of satisfaction enabled by the constructive experi ence with the application, as demonstrated by our qualitative study.

Similarly, high level of satisfaction with the application will also lead to calculative commitment, due to enhanced feelings of losing the existing benefits enjoyed by the organization through the use of the application (Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). As one of the interviewees mentioned, for them to even consider other options, “there would have to be another really, really attractive system out there that offered benefits, whether that be functionality or cost benefits that [Cloud Inc. application] didn’t offer.” Thus:

H2a: Overall satisfaction of SaaS customers is positively associated with customers’ commitment to the SaaSdelivered application.

We believe that customers’ commitment to the cloud computing technology is also composed of both the affective and calculative dimensions. We argue that overall satisfaction of SaaS customers can reinforce their bond to the technology itself as they realize and benefit from cloud specific features, such as elimination of infrastructure support, reduced IT responsibilities, ubiquitous accessibility, or even multitenancy through the use of specific SaaS-based applications. In this sense, customers’ satisfaction from using a certain SaaS product could lead to attachment toward cloud-based solutions in general (Chiou and Droge 2006; Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). Furthermore, consistent with past research on switching costs, we argue that the more satisfied the users, the more switching costs may increase. In other words, given the increase in their knowledge stock about the technology (i.e., the sunk cost of learning), there is increase in their calculative commitment (Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). Similarly, dissatisfied users may decrease their knowledge stock, resulting in the reduction of the sunk costs of learning, and thereby their calculative commitment. Dissatisfied customers may deliberately choose to reduce their switching costs, because in such situations of lower (calculative) commitment, switching becomes “less punishing than it might have been” (Davis-Sramek et al. 2009; p. 444). Thus:

## H2b: Overall satisfaction of SaaS customers is positively associated with customers’ commitment to the cloud computing technology.

Finally, as the last link of the CACA framework, we capture the behavioral loyalty (customer retention in the marketing context) through the concept of IS replacement, defined as customers’ intention to terminate the exchange relationship with the specific SaaS-delivered application (Oliver 1999). In the SaaS context, customers’ commitment to the SaaSdelivered application signals positive reaction toward the business relationship with the SaaS vendor, which cannot be separated from the use of the application itself (e.g., Li et al. 2006). It is well documented in the literature that customer commitment is positively associated with customer retention (Bansal et al. 2004; Gustafsson et al. 2005; Kim and Son

2009; Li et al. 2006; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Pritchard et al. 1999; Verhoef 2003; Zhou et al. 2012), or negatively associated with customer switching intentions (Fullerton 2003). Thus:

H3: Organization customers’ commitment to the SaaSdelivered application is negatively associated with their replacement intention of the application itself.

Furthermore, we argue that the generic characteristics associated with the cloud computing technology (i.e., unrelated to the vendor or the application) have a bearing on the benefits organizations could receive by using cloud-based IT solutions, in the way that organizational processes or capabilities will be significantly impacted by the technology. For example, organizations will lose abilities, such as ubiquitous access, dynamic elasticity, and access to unlimited computing resources, if they stop using IT solutions supported by and enabled by the cloud computing technology (Carter et al. 2014) irrespective of the specific application offered by vendors. Therefore, it should be expected that organizations with strong attachment to such cloud-enabled features will be less likely to initate the replacement, especially if the alternative is traditional on-premise solutions. Additionally, the results of the qualitative study have suggested that commitment to the cloud technology itself is enough to prevent replacement. Specifically, some organizations continued with the Cloud Inc. application because of their faith in cloud computing in general, even though their experiences and perceptions of the application itself were not so positive. Thus, we argue:

H4: Organization customers’ commitment to the cloud computing technology is negatively associated with their replacement intention of the SaaS-delivered application.

## Quantitative Study

As phase II of our mixed-methods approach, we conducted a cross-sectional survey to empirically test our research model. Our survey sample consisted of customers from the Cloud Inc. Foundation and was independent from the sample used in our qualitative study. A pilot study was conducted first to test the survey and the procedure (see Appendix D for more details).

## Measures

We used established instruments to measure our constructs (see Table D1, Appendix D, for a complete list of our instruments). Replacement intention was measured using a threeitem instrument refined from Furneaux and Wade (2011). We conceptualized commitment as a multidimensional construct composed of two sub-constructs: affective commitment and calculative commitment, consistent with the conceptualization in previous literature (Allen and Meyer 1990; Fullerton 2003; Pritchard et al. 1999). Further, we followed Polites et al. (2012) and Wright et al. (2012) to model commitment as a multidimensional construct: first-order reflective and secondorder formative. Affective commitment and calculative commitment each represents a unique aspect of the commitment concept and form the “overarching construct” based on the common variance (Polites et al. 2012). Instruments for the first-order commitment constructs were adapted from Li et al. (2006). SaaS service quality was measured using the adapted SaaS-Qual instrument developed by Benlian et al. (2011), which is a first-order reflective, and second-order formative construct composed of six factors: rapport, flexibility, features, security, reliability, and responsiveness. Finally, satisfaction was measured by a single question on the overall experience with the Cloud Inc. system adapted from Bhattacherjee (2001).

Additionally, we included a set of control variables: system investment, system coverage, organizational size, license number, time length since adoption, and IT sophistication level.<sup>7</sup> System investment was measured by an instrument adapted from Furneaux and Wade (2011). System coverage was measured by an eight-point scale of the percentage of organizational process covered by the application (Castner and Ferguson 2000). Organizational size was captured by a seven-point scale reflecting the number of employees in the organization. Information regarding the number of licenses was gathered from Cloud Inc. Foundation. The general IT sophistication level was measured by a seven-point scale. To test and control for possible common method bias, we also included techno-overload (adapted from Tarafdar er al. (2007)) as a marker variable in the survey (Lindell and Whitney 2001).

## Survey

The sample for the main study consisted of 800 organizations, which are or used to be customers of Cloud Inc Foundation. The online survey was sent out through emails to a key informant, which assumes that a single respondent’s opinion on the subject is able to represent the experience of a group or an organization (Segars and Grover 1993). This data collection method has been widely adopted in organization-level studies (e.g., Benlian et al. 2011; Furneaux and Wade 2011). To further ensure that the respondents answer the questions on behalf of the organization, we specified in the introduction of the survey that respondents should only answer the survey if they have been highly involved in the whole process of the organization’s deployment of the Cloud Inc. application. Also, we phrased all questions toward the experience of the organization by using words such as “our organization” or “we” in all the relevant statements. One notification email was sent one week before the distribution and one reminder email was sent one week after the distribution.

Our final sample included responses from 190 organizations with a 23.75% response rate, which is consistent with prior studies involving a similar population (e.g., Furneax and Wade 2011). To rule out possible non-response bias, we first conducted an archival analysis (Rogelberg and Stanton 2007) comparing our sample with secondary data obtained through Cloud Inc., namely the number of Cloud Inc. application licenses acquired by each organization. A t-test showed that there is no significant differences between these two groups (t = 0.089, p = 0.929). As a second step, we analyzed the respondent organizations by comparing early respondents and late respondents (median-split). Again, a t-test between these two groups showed no significant difference across all variables measured. Table 2 presents the details of our sample.

## Discussion

We started our study by posing the question: What is the role of commitment on the replacement of SaaS-delivered applications? The results of the quantitative study highlight customers’ commitment in the context of SaaS as a multilayered concept that can be understood as commitment to the application at one layer and commitment to the technology at another layer. Our initial qualitative study suggested this possibility, given that interviewees differentiated between a specific IT solution (e.g., the Cloud Inc. application) and the underlying technology (e.g., cloud computing), implying that their attitude toward the technology was not identical to their attitude toward the technology-enabled application. For instance, some organizational representatives (interviewees) praised the capabilities of the cloud computing technology (such as ubiquitous access and dynamic elasticity) but, at the same time, criticized the attributes of Cloud Inc.’s application (such as user interface and level of customization allowed). Our quantitative study further proved this point by validating the discriminant validity between these two types of commitment and demonstrating their independent influences on organizations’ replacement intention of the SaaS-delivered application. Further, through the post hoc analysis of mediation effects, we found that though commitment to the cloud technology is not a mediator in our model, it still continues to directly influence replacement intention.

Such findings suggest that commitment to the SaaS product and commitment to the underlying cloud computing technology are two conceptually different constructs. We recommend that researchers and practitioners consider this multilayered conceptualization which echoes the recent view within the IS discipline on the multilayered nature of IS products (Xu et al. 2010). On the other hand, our results support the commitment–retention relationship in the extant literature and emphasize the (more) important role played by commitment to the product. Therefore, we are able to extend such relationship to the B2B cloud setting, which highlights the commoditized nature of organizational IS resources (Bardhan et al. 2010).

Additionally, although SaaS is often touted as a delivery model with lower lock-in effects than on-premise solutions (Cusumano 2010), our post hoc analysis suggests that calculative commitment to the SaaS-based application is relatively more important than the other three types of commitment when it comes to influencing SaaS replacement. Such a result can be explained from two perspectives. First, it resonates with the SaaS context, in which minimal vendor interaction is needed (Owens 2010). This consequently leaves less room for affective commitment, a construct rooted in emotions and social exchanges, to develop (Gustafsson et al. 2005). Indeed, many interviewees mentioned a lack of vendor support as one of the negative aspects related to their experience of the SaaS deployment. Second, the sample of our study came from the nonprofit sector, which is associated limited resources and low levels of IT knowledge and skills (Hackler and Saxton 2007; Zhang et al. 2010). Therefore, it is expected that these organizations will focus on balancing their economic concerns with the fear of losing the existing benefits associated with the SaaS application. This is consistent with our qualitative study where many interviewees emphasized the switching costs (both in terms of economic investment and loss of existing benefits) as one major issue when it comes to considering alterative solutions. For instance, one of the interviewees admitted that they will consider switching to an alternative solution if only the alternative provides “cost benefits” that Cloud Inc. does not offer, especially given that “the main issue that has come up as we have grown in our usage is the cost.” Such a finding, to a certain degree, contradicts the common assertion of cloud solutions bearing low switching cost and lock-in effect (e.g., Cusumano 2010). On the other hand, it also resonates with some of the recent discussions amongst the cloud industry experts regarding the cost structure of cloud solutions, especially in relation to possible “hidden costs” and accumulated costs in the long run (Bourne 2015; Palmer 2016).

Finally, our mixed-method design sought to answer the following question: How do different aspects of commitment influence SaaS replacement? The results in general support our research model and confirm the relationships between all the elements in the CACA framework. We would also like to note that we were able to develop a holistic understanding of the research question posed based on the inferences made in the qualitative study and in the quantitative study. In other words, we have integrated the results from both studies into a “theoretically consistent meta-inference” (Venkatesh et al. 2013, p. 24). Table 3 summarizes the meta-inferences.

## Contributions

In this study, we have strived to provide a contextualized understanding of IS replacement or the churn phenomenon. We argue that cloud-based IT solutions in general, and SaaS in particular, due to its distinctive delivery model and associated characteristics, deserve separate investigation. Our effort in developing a contextualized theory on IS replacement can be categorized as single-context theory contextualization, as per Hong et al.’s (2013) definition (see Table 4 for the steps involved in contextualization). We grounded our study in the theoretical concept of commitment (or, specifically, the CACA framework) that helped establish the relationship between customer commitment and IS replacement. The qualitative study helped contextualize the general theory, which resulted in the decomposition of the core construct, customer commitment, into a multilayer concept: commitment to the SaaS application and commitment to the cloud computing technology. The survey results confirmed these relationships.

Our contextualized theorization of IS replacement has a number of contributions. First, we would like to emphasize that our study shows the important (and independent) role of “commitment to the technology” in preventing SaaS replacement. In this research, we forward and develop the construct of commitment to technology, an IS-centric construct of customer commitment, thereby responding to Truex et al.’s (2006) call for substantively adapting theory from other reference disciplines to the IS context. Our conceptualization of commitment to technology is also a response to recent calls by scholars in organizational research (Johns 2017) and IS research (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001) who have suggested that the study of technology especially deserves better contextualization, and that the contextualization should be centered around the characteristics of the technology itself (Hong et al. 2013). Thus, consistent with the discussion in recent IS literature on developing constructs focusing on the specific technology itself—that is trust in technology (Lankton et al. 2015; McKnight et al. 2011; Vance et al. 2008); technology self-efficacy (Compeau et al. 2017)—we posit that commitment to the technology is different from commitment to the product, both conceptually and empirically, as demonstrated by our findings. On one hand, it is influenced by product quality and hence customer satisfaction, as the characteristics of the technology itself are operationalized and reflected in the product. On the other hand, it can be speculated that commitment to the technology is also stimulated or reinforced by other, non-product related factors. For instance, our qualitative data has revealed that the organizational customers may sometimes not be convinced by the Cloud Inc. application but are firm believers of cloud computing. Existing research on organizing vision has indeed highlighted how discourse of a certain technological innovation often influence practitioners’ view and use of the said technology (Ramiller and Swanson 2003; Swanson and Ramiller 2004). Therefore, while commitment to product is always dependent on the specific product, commitment to technology can capture the “fads and fashion” and the influence of the broader environment, especially when the discourses around the technology are still being shaped by the community (often at the early stages of a technological innovation). In this sense, we suggest that future studies focus on uncovering other, external antecedents that affect commitment to technology as well as how the hype around a specific technology plays a role in shaping such commitment.

Furthermore, the findings of our study—the multilayered nature of commitment—echo that of existing studies, such as Xu et al. (2010) and Lansing and Sunyaev (2016). However, our study goes beyond this existing work by providing the empirical evidence, both quantitative and qualitative, to support such a multilayered conceptualization. A broader implication of such findings also contribute toward how we conceptualize the IT artifact. Although numerous arguments have been made surrounding the issue of IT artifacts, most of them focus on the role of IT artifacts and their interactions with human agency (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). Taking a step back, we wonder whether it is useful to rethink the operationalization of the IT artifact and to consider the difference between the technology-enabled product/service/system/application/solution and the technology itself—and under what circumstances or contexts would such a differentiation generate additional insights about the phenomenon. In our case, a SaaS-delivered application offered by a certain cloud vendor is a bundle of the underlying technology (e.g., virtualization, multi-tenancy, redundancy), the software platform, and the services provided. While the underlying technology is the same across all the cloud vendors, the software platform and the features offered often differ.

Burton-Jones and Volkoff (2017) argue that the IT artifact should be treated as a multilevel system, both from an organizational point of view (individual, group, organization level), but more importantly (in their case of IT use) from a data point of view (parts and whole, types, and subtypes). We would like to add that the multilevel nature of IT system also includes its architecture. That is, an IT artefact can consist of the infrastructure, the platform, and the application/system (Hanseth and Lyytinen 2010). In our case of a SaaS-delivered application, much of the touted characteristics such as ubiquitous access or security matters, that drive or impede adoption (e.g., Bhattacherjee and Park 2014; Oliveira et al. 2014) are due to the different delivery model. Hence, the differentiation is discerned between the application and the underlying technology, and how the infrastructure and the platform are provided is where the innovation lies (Venters and Whitley 2012). This is the context of SaaS, which is shared by many other technologies, given the proliferation of digital platforms and platform-based products (e.g., Parker et al. 2016; de Reuver et al. 2017).

However, such context can be different for other emerging technological innovation. For instance, blockchain has been touted as the next technological revolution (e.g., Beck et al. 2017; Iansiti and Lakhani 2017). In the context of blockchain-based solutions, the innovation lies in the architecture, which provides the benefits of decentralization, transparency, security, etc. (Iansiti and Lakhani 2017). Therefore, differentiating the blockchain technology and the application residing on top (e.g., Bitcoin or artwork authenticity app Varisart) will help us develop a more stratified understanding of the phenomenon. For instance, if we study trust in the context of blockchain, questions can be asked about the different roles played by trust toward the technology itself versus trust toward the application or the technology provider. Or, simply, whether the trust will be the same if the same features or principles (e.g., decentralization, transparency) are realized without the specific technology in question. Going back to the previous discussion on organizing vision (Ramiller and Swanson 2003; Swanson and Ramiller 2004), having a multilayered conceptualization of IT artefact and related phenomenon will enable us to discuss such technological innovation in a far more nuanced way.

Finally, our findings resonate with the broader literature on the commitment–retention relationship (Bansal et al. 2004; Fullerton 2003; Gustafsson et al. 2005; Kim and Son 2009; Li et al. 2006; Morgan and Hunt 1994; Pritchard et al. 1999; Verhoef 2003; Zhou et al. 2012), as we have confirmed that commitment positively influences retention intention in the B2B setting. Doing so, this study contributes to the literature on cloud computing by moving beyond adoption and investigating the drivers of churn (Kasznik 2014), and at the same time highlighting the commoditized nature of IT resources as seen in the SaaS context (Bardhan et al. 2010). Moreover, we argue that such similarities in findings have offered us an opportunity to integrate different streams of literature, which, as advocated by Johns (2017), is equally important in contextspecific studies. The findings of our study imply that there is potential to integrate theories on IS replacement (and related phenomena such as IS continuance) (e.g., Kim and Son 2009)

from the IS discipline and studies on churn (e.g., Ascarza et al. 2016; Reinartz et al. 2004), customer retention (e.g., Nitzan and Libai 2011) or customer switching (e.g., Wieringa and Verhoef 2007) within the marketing discipline. Following our arguments above on multilayered conceptualization, multilevel theories (Johns 2018) can be developed as a result of reconciling and integrating these two streams of research. We believe that such an integration will provide new avenues of theory development, as we witness the trend of technology becoming ubiquitously embedded in our daily lives (Yoo 2010), and increasing proportion of products and services being delivered through digital means.

## Practical Implications

First, the fact that organizations’ commitment to the cloud computing technology showed a significant effect on organizations’ replacement intention suggests to SaaS vendors that, to retain customers, they should also consider endeavors aimed at promoting the cloud computing technology in general, rather than just focusing on the specific service/ application. As highlighted in the popular press (AVG PR 2012), many small organizations may not understand the specifics of cloud computing and therefore have a hard time understanding the differences between cloud computing platforms, applications, services, and so on. Further, we would like emphasize that when marketing SaaS-delivered applications, it might be enough or optimal for SaaS vendors to only keeping their clients satisfied. Since the effect of satisfaction on replacement does not go through commitment to the technology (as demonstrated in the post hoc analysis), SaaS vendors need to additionally invest in programs that can improve (affective and calculative) commitment to the technology itself. Additionally, we would like to highlight that such implication,—the need to emphasize benefits related to the underlying technology—goes beyond the context of cloud computing, and can be applied to new products based on technological innovations. For vendors offering such products, especially at the early adoption stage, making the underlying technology and its advancement a selling point could be a fruitful route to pursue.

Second, even though it is argued that the lock-in effects are not as strong in the context of SaaS-delivered IT solutions due to some of its characteristics (Cusumano 2010), our findings indicate the opposite, reflecting the fact that technical characteristics are not the only source of lock-in effects. We believe that it is crucial for SaaS vendors to increase customers’ commitment to the application, especially the calculative commitment, due to its central role in retaining customers. Although switching costs might not be as important in the context of SaaS as in other context, when compared with other factors, it is still the single most important variable in influencing customer retention. We reason that this is especially the case when customers use the SaaS solution for a certain amount of time with growing number of licenses. As indicated by some of the interviewees, the growth of SaaS use within the organization further highlights the issue of cost. From cloud users point of view, the implication here is the need to be cautious about long-term costs associated with cloud-based solutions. While up-front capital investment is no longer the issue, accumulated operational costs in the long run, especially in light of expanded usage, should be carefully evaluated. Further, such switching costs often also involve benefits related to functionalities of the particular solution. Therefore, cloud vendors should find ways to increase switching costs, such as by improving service quality (Benlian et al. 2011) or by establishing certain loyalty program (as in the B2C sector) where accumulation of benefits could be further awarded (i.e., each additional year of usage can be rewarded by free consulting hours).

## Limitations and Future Research

One of the primary limitations of our study is that in capturing replacement intention, we did not make a distinction between customers’ intention to replace the SaaS product with another product with the same technology (e.g., another SaaS-based application delivered by another vendor) and their intention to replace with another product with a different technology (e.g., back to an on-premise solution). The reasoning behind such an approach was two-fold. First, we believe that given our focus on vendor’s perspective, these two scenarios of replacement have the same impact on the vendor (that is, they lose their customer), therefore the distinction was not necessary. Also, this type of thinking on replacement reflects the churn concept, which was used as the foundation for conceptualizing our dependent variable. Further, our interaction with the vendor Cloud Inc. also suggests that they view the competition in terms of application substitution and do not focus on the underlying technologies. For instance, one of the employees said:

I would say most of the customers I interact with are going to buy something .... That doesn’t mean they are going to buy Cloud Inc. We are often competing with some other software. They may buy something else (emphasis added).

Second, our qualitative investigation also suggests that sometimes a replacement decision can be made by a customer without a clear alternative in mind. Organizations have often removed Cloud Inc. from their infrastructure first and then moved on to search for a better alternative. In such a situation, the customer would only know what their replacement intention is, and not what they are replacing with. Further, our qualitative investigation also seems to suggest that when making a replacement choice, the organizations tend to focus on overall functionalities and what the replacing solution is able to provide compared to the existing solution, and not on whether the existing solution is cloud or not. This is, not surprisingly, similar to Cloud Inc’s perspective on competition mentioned earlier. Hence, it is not clear whether the decision to switch can be clearly categorized into vendor switching versus technology switching. For instance, some interviewees talked about switching to another solution as “kind of the opposite experience as Cloud Inc.” Even though both solutions were cloud-based, the interviewee tried to emphasize the fact that this other solution is much more intuitive to use, and hence the opposite of Cloud Inc.

On the other hand, we are fully aware that the lack of differentiation between these two replacement scenarios has important implications for our results. While we have empirically established the independent influences of commitment to SaaS application and commitment to cloud technology on replacement intention in general, we cannot provide an answer to whether these two types of commitment exert different effects under different replacement scenarios. For instance, commitment to the technology might not be as important when the replacement is also cloud-based, compared to when the replacement is not cloud-based. Therefore, we call for future research to examine whether commitment to the technology and commitment to the product exert different influences on these two types of replacement behaviors. The existing literature on back-sourcing can shed light on the potential of this direction (Whitten et al. 2010; Whitten and Leidner 2006). Further, as mentioned in practical implications, underlying technology can play an importance role for customer retention when it comes to new products based on technological innovation. Thus, it will be interesting to conduct a longitudinal study to compare the role of commitment to the technology over time.

## Conclusion

In this study, drawing on the theoretical concept of customer commitment, we investigated the IS replacement phenomenon in the context of a SaaS-delivered IT solution. By differentiating between organizations’ commitment specifically to a given SaaS application of a vendor and to the cloud computing technology in general, we were able to develop an IScentric view of customer commitment and also understand how these two types of commitment together influence organizations’ intention to replace an IS. Replacement of existing SaaS amongst nonprofit organizations is a serious issue for SaaS vendors, and we hope that our study provides some theoretical and practical clarity on core antecedents of this phenomenon.

## Details of the Pilot Study

A link to the online survey was sent out via email to the main contacts (normally CIOs or project managers in charge of the SaaS deployment process) from a randomly selected 100 customers of Cloud Inc. Foundation. This “key informant” approach to data collection assumes that a single respondent’s opinion on the subject is able to represent the experience of a group or an organization (Segars and Grover 1993). This data collection method has been widely adopted in organization-level studies (e.g., Benlian et al. 2011; Furneaux and Wade 2011). To ensure that the respondent is indeed able to reflect on the organization’s experience with the SaaS application, we specified in the introduction of the survey that respondents should only answer the survey if they have been highly involved in the whole process of the organizational deploymen of the Cloud Inc. application. Also, we phrased all the questions toward the experience of the organization by using words such as “our organization” or “we” in all the relevant statements. We initiated the data collection process by sending out a notification to all the potentia participants one week before the actual survey was sent, and then we sent a reminder one week after the link to the online survey was distributed. In the end, 47 completed responses were collected. Based on the results of the pilot study, we were able to validate the measurements and also finalize the procedure of the actual study.

## Modeling of the Commitment Construct

Commitment has been measured predominately as a mono-dimensional construct with reflective indicators (e.g., Morgan and Hunt 1994) or as discrete multiple mono-dimensional constructs with reflective indicators (e.g., Gustafsson et al. 2005). Petter et al. (2007) argue that most constructs in IS are a combination of two or more dimensions, and recommend that when this is the case it is better to treat the construct as multidimensional in order to provide parsimony and minimize problems with measurement. Taking these ideas together, we have developed a multidimensional conceptualization of commitment that includes two first order dimensions: affective and calculative commitment.

We further explored additional ways of modeling commitment. In sum, there are five possible ways of modeling a multidimensional construct: (1) superordinate (relationships flow from the construct to its dimensions), (2) aggregate (relationships flow from the dimensions to the construct), (3) profile (dimensions are combined to form categories), (4) fit (alignment between dimensions), and (5) mixed models (mixed relationships between dimensions and constructs) (Polites et al. 2012). In the end, we decided to model commitment as aggregate construc with affective commitment and calculative commitment as its formative subdimension. Modeling commitment as a high-order construct allows researchers to develop and model complex concepts within a broader nomological network (Wong et al. 2008; Wright et al. 2012). Following

Jarvis et al.’s (2003) criteria, we would expect the two dimensions of commitment to to be composed of its dimensions (e.g., Davis-Sramek et al. 2009). We would also expect that dropping a dimension in the higher-order commitment constructs (e.g., calculative commitment or affective commitment) would change the overall meaning of the higher-order construct and therefore the aggregate high-order construct is theoretically superior.

## Test of Common Method Bias

To examine whether common method bias was a problem in our data, we have conducted the following tests. First, Harman’s single factor test was used (Podsakoff and Organ 1986). We conducted an exploratory factor analysis in SPSS, and the results showed that 10 factors were extracted with Eigen values of one or greater. Further, only about 16.7% of the variance was accounted for by the first factor, suggesting that common method bias was not an issue (Podsakoff and Organ 1986).

Next, as a more robust test for common method bias, we used a marker variable—techno-overload—to further rule out the possibility of common method bias, as we expect that at least one of our main constructs (e.g., commitment to the application, reliability) is not theoretically related to techno-overload (Lindell and Whitney 2001; Podsakoff et al. 2003). The highest correlation between the marker variable and any of the core constructs was 0.083, which demonstrated that there is no common variance between theoretically unrelated constructs. Further, Table D4 presents the adjusted correlations of the related variables, and all the adjusted variables remain significant at p < 0.01. Considering all of the above, it is reasonable to determine that common method bias is not an issue and the nomological relationships presented are strongly supported by the data.

## Details of the Post Hoc Analysis

LeBreton et al. (2007) suggests that one needs to establish both incremental and relative importance. Incremental importance can be established by a hierarchical regression. We then ran a hierarchical regression test using SPSS. The hierarchical regression was run in a step-wise fashion with two models. In the first model, we only include commitment to the application as the independent variable; in the second model, we add commitment to the technology as the second independent variable. Table D6 shows that the R square changes from the baseline model to Model 1, and from Model 1 to Model 2 are significant at p < 0.00 level and p < 0.05 level respectively, and hence establishes the incrementa importance of the two independent variables.

Once the incremental importance was established for both commitment variables, we moved to test for their relative importance. LeBreton et al. argue that relative importance can be established through a dominance analysis. Dominance analysis allows researchers to discern the relative importance of predictors by examining the resulting change in R<sup>2</sup> to all possible subset of regression models. It helps researchers “to identify variables that tended to “outperform” other variables across various models” (LeBreton et al. 2007, p. 480). General dominance is the calculated average semi-partial correlations on the predictors. This provides an understanding of the predictors’ total contribution to the dependent variables. Rescaled dominance is computed by dividing the general dominance estimate by the total R<sup>2</sup>. In our analysis, we were interested in understanding the contribution of two predictors: the second level constructs commitment to the application (APP), and commitment to the technology (TECH). We were also interested in the contributions of the first level constructs: affective commitment to the application (APPAC), calculative commitment to the application (APCC), affective commitment to the technology (TECHAC), and calculative commitment to the technology (TECHCC). Our ultimate DV in this dominance analysis is intention to replace the application.

Specifically, in order to calculate the comparative analysis for our model, 15 models were run in SPSS. Each model represented all the different permutations of the predictors necessary for a dominance analysis (see Table D7). The R² were estimated for each model and then the average R² values were calculated for the predictor with each variable. The general dominance was the actual amount of variance attributed for by the predictor. For example, we see that APP CC accounted for 0.18 of the total R² 0.370, or 47% of the total variance (i.e., rescaled dominance). Table D7 provides the complete findings. Our dominance analysis provides evidence that APP CC contributes the most variance to intention to replace the technology, while APP AC, Tech CC, and Tech AC all contribute about equal amounts of variance.

Next, an analysis was conducted to understand the contribution to the APP and the TECH. We found the general dominance of the APP counted for 0.25 of the total 0.37 R² for the model. Rescaling this is 67% for the APP and 33% for the TECH (see Table D8).

Further, comparative relative weights (Azen and Budescu 2003) are calculated to (1) test the statistical significance of each of the variables, and (2) test difference in general dominance between APPCC and the other three less significant predictors (APPAC, TECH AC, and TECH CC). We estimated the significance of the relative weights using a boot strapped process with 10,000 resamples. As illustrated in the Table D9, each of the relative weights are significantly different from zero. Further, we see that APPCC is significantly different from APPAC, TECHCC, and TECHAC. Finally, we also calculated the comparative relative weights for APP and TECH (see Table D10). We found that the APP Commitment relative weights were statistically different from the TECH commitment relative weights at p < 0.05.

## Results of the Mediation Test

The first step is to see if there are direct effects from the independent variable (IV) to the dependent variable (DV) when executing the research model. We found that satisfaction has significant direct effect on replacement intention and that service quality has significant direct effects on commitment to the technology and commitment to the application (see Table D11). This indicates mediations. Next, using Smart PLS 3.2.7 (Ringle et al. 2015) we calculate three path weights for the mediations models: (1) from the IV to the mediator, (1) from the mediator to the DV, and (3) from the IV to the DV. Next, we estimated the significance levels for all three paths (see Table D12). Hair et al. (2016) state tha when all three paths are significant there is partial mediation. Table D12 reveals that commitment to the application partially mediates the relationship between satisfaction and replacement, and satisfaction partially mediates the relationship between service quality and commitment to the application. The partial mediation is complementary, as the a × b × c value is positive (Zhao et al. 2010), which means that the mediators are needed to best explain the variance in the DV. Finally, Table D12 reveals no mediating effects of commitment to the technology (between satisfaction and replacement) and satisfaction (between service quality and commitment to the technology).
