# Prospering in a transition economy through information technology-supported organizational learning

Marius Janson,\* Dubravka Cecez-Kecmanovic<sup>†</sup> & Jo e Zupan i <sup>‡</sup>zˇ cˇ cˇ

\*University of Missouri-St. Louis, One University Boulevard, St. Louis, MO 63121, USA, email: janson@umsl.edu, <sup>†</sup>University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia, email: dubravka@unsw.edu.au, and <sup>‡</sup>University of Maribor, Presernova 11, 4000 Kranj, Slovenia, email: joze.zupancic@fov.uni-mb.si

Abstract. This paper presents the findings of a longitudinal study of the Slovenian company Sava during its 1995–2004 transition period when it adapted to and prospered in a free market economy. The company is particularly interesting because of its successful transition from a socialist company operating in a protected market to a privatized company operating in a capitalist global market, as well as the pivotal role of information technology (IT)-supported organizational learning that brought about radical change and successful transition. Our investigation of Sava’s experiences demonstrates how the company’s increasing attention to organizational learning, integration of working and learning, and its constant innovation of products and processes created new needs for IT support that motivated the adoption of new IT systems (such as Lotus Notes, document management systems, SAP), which in turn increased Sava’s capacity to learn. Furthermore, our study reveals how the role of IT systems in organizational learning depends on the nature of learning (single-loop, double-loop or triple-loop learning) and the organizational level at which learning takes place (individual, group/department or organization). By providing insight into the emergence of distinct types of ITsupported learning and their vital role in Sava’s successful transition, the paper contributes to a deeper understanding of the relationship between IT and organizational learning that is relevant and inspiring to other companies, especially those operating in transition economies.

Keywords: organizational learning, information technology, computer information systems, organizational strategies, economic transformation, globalization

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Organizational learning is of intense interest to academics and practitioners (Fiol & Lyles, 1985; DeGeus, 1988; Stata, 1989; Senge, 1990; Huber, 1991; Argyris, 1992; MacDonald, 1995; Nevis et al., 1995; Lipshitz et al., 1996; Wishart et al., 1996; Holmqvist, 2003). Organizational learning occurs when members of an organization change their shared assumptions and beliefs and in turn change the range of their behaviour and enhance their capacity to act. Huber (1991) states that organizational learning comprises four processes: knowledge acquisition, information distribution, information interpretation and organizational memory. Goodman & Darr (1996; 1998) contend that computer-based information systems and information technologies (IT) are essential for these four processes to be successful. We emphasize that the authors cited above focused on organizational learning as practised by companies located in Western industrialized countries. The need for organizational learning, however, has not been lost on managers of companies located in former socialist countries. Many have recognized that organizational learning would be essential to their company’s survival in a business environment transitioning from a centrally planned economy to a knowledge-based economy ruled by free market principles (Czeglédy, 1996; Kuhlenbruck et al., 2003).

Stata (1989) mentioned that organizational learning in Western industrialized countries is typically motivated by a firm’s loss of competitive ability and the imperative to retain and sustain competitive advantage under familiar market conditions and international competition. Companies in former socialist countries, often only recently privatized, suddenly entered economic environments characterized by free market conditions that were unfamiliar to their management and employees. Survival under the new socio-economic circumstances typically required simultaneous coping with a change of ownership and management structure, as well as a new political and economic system and market conditions. Survival in these circumstances essentially depended on companies’ ability to acquire, process and interpret information both internally and from their environment in order to support organizational learning and organizational change. The collapse of many formerly state-owned firms in Central and Eastern Europe’s transition economies may be attributed to failed organizational learning and the lack of effective IT support (Kuhlenbruck et al., 2003). With this pattern in mind, we embarked on a longitudinal interpretive study of Sava, a Slovenian company that successfully operates in a transition economy. By investigating Sava’s learning practices we sought to provide insights into the relationship between organizational learning and IT support that might explain Sava’s successful privatization experience and prosperity in free market conditions.

As Slovenia was the most developed republic among the six republics comprising the former Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia the transition to market economy was less traumatic compared with other Eastern and Southern European countries. The relatively good economic position at the beginning of the transition made it easier for Slovenia to face the challenges of an open market-driven economy (Pra nikar & Sveinar, 1998; Pra nikar et al., 2001). Thesˇ sˇ reforms included stabilization of the economy, liberalization of trade, abolitions of most duties, which resulted in increased product market competition. Furthermore, Sava lost its traditiona market for its major product, rubber automobile tires after Slovenia’s secession from and subsequent political dissolution of the former Yugoslavia during the early 1990s.

Heretofore, high demand for Sava’s automobile tires in the former Yugoslavia, coupled with stringent import barriers, had ensured the company a profitable existence and steady growth. The loss of its home market led to a crisis that company management tried to resolve by attempting to penetrate markets in Western Europe and Central and Eastern Europe. However, Sava’s top management quickly realized that the free market economy and global competition necessitated an entirely new corporate strategy and management style, and much more attention to efficiency, productivity and product quality (Sava 1995 Annual Report).

Our 1997 interviews with the company’s President and other members of top management uncovered corporate leaders who struggled to come to grips with free market conditions and fierce competition. Further discussions revealed that managers were concerned about the nexus between IT application and organizational and personal learning. That is to say, top management recognized, although rather vaguely, a connection between the organization’s ability to learn and IT support. For example, apart from recognizing a need to gain expertise in free market style management, top executives were concerned about their ability to capture, distribute and deploy knowledge emerging from Sava’s experiences and work practices – deemed to be vital to company survival.

In the longitudinal study of Sava during its transition to a market economy described in this paper, we examined processes and practices of organizational learning and the ways organizational actors used IT systems in enacting these processes and practices. By analysing and interpreting interview data, company annual reports and other internal documents, and newspaper articles, we identified a complex relationship between organizational learning and IT. On the one hand, management recognized a need for organizational learning, and this recognition pointed towards a need for IT. On the other hand, new IT capabilities caused management to realize new modes of organizational learning. By providing insights into Sava’s organizational learning practices and its use of IT to enable and advance learning, we aim to derive lessons that are relevant and inspiring to other companies and especially those in transition economies.

The remainder of our paper comprises five sections. In Section 2 we discuss different approaches to organizational learning and its relationship with IT. We then describe the study’s research method (Section 3) and the Sava Company (Section 4). Based on empirical evidence from Sava, in Section 5 we provide an in-depth analysis of different organizational learning processes and the way they were supported by IT systems. In Section 6 we discuss these empirical findings and interpret the relationships between organizational learning and IT support as they emerged during Sava’s trajectory into the free market economy. Section 7 offers concluding remarks.

## 2. ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING

The literature on organizational learning offers many different perspectives on the nature of learning that is considered organizational. Broadly speaking the differences – rooted in the basic assumptions regarding the entity that actually learns – can be seen along a broad spectrum. At one end of the spectrum, organizations are conceived as ‘mental entities capable of thought’ (Sanderlands & Stablein, 1987) and learning by sensing their environments and ‘acting in response to these sensations’ (Lee et al., 1992; Jones, 1995). In other words, an organization is an entity in its own right that learns. This perspective is exemplified by Huber (1991, p. 89) who states that an organization learns ‘if through processing of information the range of its potential behaviors is changed’. At the other end of the spectrum, learning is perceived as an attribute of the individual, and organizational learning is understood as an aggregate of individual learning (Schneider & Angelmar, 1993). Between these two ends of the spectrum lies a vast number of approaches to organizational learning that attempt to resolve the contradiction between the individual and the collective nature of organizational leaning (e.g. Argyris & Schein, 1978; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Kim, 1993; Jones, 1995; Pentland, 1995; Williamson & Iliopoulos, 2001; Holmqvist, 2003). Furthermore, Argyris & Schein (1978) identify an inherent paradox in the nature of organizational learning:

Organizations are not merely collections of individuals, yet there is no organization withou such collections. Similarly, organizational learning is not merely individual learning, yet organizations learn only through the experiences and actions of individuals.

In an attempt to resolve this paradox, Crossan et al. (1999) argue that organizational learning is a process comprising intuiting and interpreting at the individual level, integrating at the group level, and institutionalizing at the organizational level. The critical issue here is to ‘move beyond the anthropomorphic metaphor of organizations as individual cognizers’ and comprehend the social and situated character of learning (Pentland, 1995, p. 2). While it is individuals who make sense of events and learn, they do it intersubjectively in a social context, through social interaction. In other words, organizational learning is seen as a social affair – ‘an integral and inseparable aspect of social practice’ (Lave & Wenger, 1991, p. 31). Organizational learning can thus be defined as the social production of intersubjective experiences and organizational rules, structures and relationships that lead to changed organizational behaviour (Weick, 1979; Holmqvist, 2003).

Furthermore, Bateson (1972), Argyris & Schein (1978), Hawkins (1991; 1994), Torbert (1994) and Snell & Chak (1998) have defined different types of organizational learning that are particularly relevant to our longitudinal study. These types relate to the nature and degree of change of organizational behaviour. The model of organizational learning consisting of singleloop, double-loop and triple-loop learning as defined by Snell & Chak (1998) is presented in Figure 1. A brief description of each type follows.

Single-loop learning involves adaptive responses: measuring an organization’s performance, comparing it with its stated goals and taking corrective action to close the gap. Through singleloop learning organizational actors detect deviations from their theory-in-use in response to a changing environment and take corrective action. Single-loop learning contributes to an organization’s knowledge and competency base without, however, altering its goals, strategies or mental maps. For example, single-loop learning comprises adjusting a production process to ensure that the product meets quality standards.

Figure 1. Single-, double- and triple-loop learning. (Adapted from Snell & Chak, 1998.)

Double-loop learning involves evaluating and changing organizational goals, organizational strategies and mental maps. Double-loop learning is typically initiated when established mental maps and ways of understanding the business become inadequate. For example, when a product’s market-share drops significantly, a company is forced to rethink its understanding of market needs and competitors’ advantages and develop new mental maps that enable the company to change its marketing strategy.

Triple-loop or deutero learning (Figure 1) occurs in response to a realization that existing mental models and ways of organizational learning no longer suffice. Snell & Chak (1998) state that the essence of triple-loop learning consists of inventing new processes, methods or strategies for reframing and generating new mental maps. Such reframing takes the form of rigorous questioning, self-critique and critical reflection on double- and single-loop learning processes themselves.

As explained above, mental maps play a vital role in organizational learning (Senge, 1990; 1992). By becoming an organizational member, an individual not only learns about operating procedures and organizational structures, norms and rules, but also gradually acquires shared beliefs, perceptions and mental maps or models of understanding the world. These shared beliefs and mental maps enable learned responses and consistent actions by actors across an organization (Senge, 1992; Spicer, 1998; Chermack, 2003). However, as Argyris & Schein (1978) caution, there is a danger of their institutionalization that may lead to protecting the status quo and in effect preventing the search for new understanding and new mental maps or, in other words, preventing double-loop learning (Kim, 1993). Our interest in the Sava Company arose in part because of its potential to shed light on how radical changes in social and economic environments can trigger changes in organizational and managerial mental models.

Organizational learning as described above involves complex interactions among individuals, organizational structures, culture and socialization, as well as between an organization and its environment (Barlett, 1932; Argyris & Schein, 1978; Fiol & Lyles, 1985; Senge, 1990; Swan, 1995; Huber, 1996; Wijnhoven, 2001). Because such interactions depend on information flow and the ways information supports and stimulates learning processes, it has been argued that organizational learning can be enabled and facilitated by computer-based information systems (Stata, 1989; Pentland, 1995; Robey et al., 2000). The role IT and information systems play in learning processes is seen differently from the point of view of alternative perspectives of organizational learning. On the one hand, information systems, which store, analyse and communicate theoretical and experiential information (Huber, 1996) are seen as ‘sites of cognition within organizations’ and are proposed to constitute the ‘organizational mind (Jones, 1995, p. 72). On the other hand, information systems (and other IT applications) are viewed as supporting formal and informal learning by individuals (especially managers) and thereby supporting organizational learning (Stata, 1989). A third role and conceptualization of IT is proposed by considering organizational learning as a social practice. IT applications are seen as mediators of organizational ‘theory-in-use’. Because they support structured and unstructured dialogue and negotiation it is proposed that IT can help reveal assumptions and beliefs, support reflection and creative synthesis and ultimately affect shared mental maps. Even though it is argued that specific information systems have to be designed to fulfil such roles (Stata, 1989; Williamson & Iliopoulos, 2001), we have still to see empirical evidence confirming that this actually happens in practice.

Our research study is motivated by the need to develop a better understanding of the relationship between organizational learning and IT in real-life organizational contexts. While much of the literature draws from experiences of companies in western, market-driven economies, this relationship between organizational learning and IT is believed to be even more critical to companies in transition economies. Our longitudinal study of the Sava Company, which endured a radical change both internally (privatization and change of management system) and externally (loss of market and the transition to an open, market-driven economy), provided a particularly pertinent setting to investigate this relationship. First, we examined how and to what extent Sava managers and employees used various IT systems to support organizational learning. We were interested in uncovering explicit attempts to develop or acquire particular information system that enabled gathering, storing, analysing and communicating information for the purpose of organizational learning. Second, we searched for evidence of information systems that specifically supported single-loop, double-loop or triple-loop learning, as well as the nature of ‘support’ for each type of learning. Third, we investigated the extent to which past managerial and organizational experiences influence organizational learning, and managerial and organizational mental models. Another research question was whether change in the learning process itself affect the use of information systems or alter information requirements? Finally, we asked, what can we learn from Sava’s organizational learning practices and its use of IT to enable and advance knowledge that might be relevant and inspiring to other companies in transition economies?

## 3. RESEARCH METHOD

Organizations seeking to make the transition from a protected socialist economy to an open market economy embark on a radical and typically multiyear organizational change process (Roland, 2000). During this transitional period, opinions, ideas and actions of members of top, middle and operational management, as well as those of workers undergo profound changes. Therefore, a longitudinal case study is required to understand how a company’s management team and its employees perceive and respond to the challenges of an economy in transition.

Our decision to study the Sava Company was motivated by several considerations. In 1997 we conducted preliminary interviews with six Slovenian companies selected as exemplars of companies that had started the transition process (following purposeful sampling in Patton’s sense, 2002, pp. 230–242). These companies were the Port of Koper (http://www.luka-kp.si), Lek Pharmaceuticals (http://www.lek.si), Merkur (a chain of hardware stores) (http://www. merkur.si), Sava Rubber (http://www.sava.si) and Zito (a flour milling and bakery company) (http://www.zito.si). We decided to make Sava the object of our study because the company’s top and middle management teams were very proactive and committed to organizational learning, informatizing the organization, extensively innovating and restructuring the organization, embarking on a joint venture with a foreign partner, and significantly diversifying product lines. Moreover, the company was willing to grant the authors access to important information such as annual reports, company documents and manuals, and newspaper articles. Finally, Sava’s top management agreed to grant the authors access to large numbers of employees at all managerial levels over a protracted period of time.

After the aforementioned preliminary investigations and agreements, we commenced our empirical study of Sava’s economic transition in 1997. To understand the spirit of the time and the political and economic circumstances under which the transition started we explored Sava’s history and past experiences. That is to say, we tried to get insight into the changing contexts – economic, political, social and legal – that impacted Sava’s transition. In so doing we conducted a historical study of Sava during the 1995–97 period based on company annual reports and other documents. In 1997 we also conducted interviews with middle and top managers and engaged in informal discussions with employees in order to learn about their authentic experiences and views of organizational learning and the application of IT. We then continued by regularly collecting Sava’s annual reports and numerous internal documents and relevant press articles. As we analysed the data that we collected following our initial 1997 interviews and developed tentative interpretations of Sava’s transition processes, we realized the need for a second round of interviews with the main actors to gather their new views, mental maps and vision for the company’s future. This second round of interviews took place in 2003. Empirical material collected thus far includes two sets of interviews in 1997 and 2003, company annual reports for the years 1995–2004 and the 2005 half-yearly report, internal company documents and documents on Sava affairs published by newspapers and trade press, Slovenian Governmental Agency reports, industry reports and other public documents (Tables 1 and 2).

Our case study was interpretivist in nature focusing on the organizing processes rather than on the organization (Weick, 1979). This view of organizational learning opened up an understanding of ways IT systems were adopted and used in support of organizational learning (Darke et al., 1998; Silverman, 1998; Stake, 2005). Our data gathering methods can be located at different points within the ‘distance – engagement’ spectrum as defined by Nandhakumar & Jones (1997, p. 113). On the one hand we analysed published data (annual reports, press articles) and internal documents that are ‘distant’ from the subjects. On the other hand we designed, conducted and analysed semi-structured interviews with managers and observed processes in the workplace environment, which involved more intensive ‘engagement’ with subjects (located in the middle of the Nandhakumar and Jones’ spectrum). Even though we would have liked more opportunities for interviews and observations (which demonstrates our preference for more engaging methods), collecting different kinds of empirical data from a variety of sources benefited our research study. While exploring our research questions we drew on employee reports concerning their experiences and accounts of events and processes, and we checked these events against alternative sources. We drew on published sources to learn more about the same events and processes that, in many instances, enabled triangulation of the findings. Based on our experience with respect to collecting and analysing annual reports and internal documents, and independently published resources, we conclude that the research process was not as ‘distant’ a data gathering method as presented by Nandhakumar & Jones (1997). Our method involved informant clarification and explanation to improve the accuracy of our own interpretations.

The interviews with Sava’s top and middle managers were conducted on-site in October 1997 and June 2003, and were audio-taped and then transcribed at a later date (Table 1). Interviews typically started with a discussion about Sava’s history, the interviewee’s background, his/her career with the company, and changes in business practices arising from transitioning to a market economy. The interviews were semi-structured, with the interviewers directing the interview to varying degrees of success. An interview guideline ensured that all topics were discussed without preventing interviewees from telling their story in their own way.

We also audio-taped our impressions of the interview and the conditions under which we conducted the interview. We took notes and often conducted preliminary analyses immediately after completing each interview. Then, at a later time, the interviews and our notes were transcribed to text in their entirety and subjected to in-depth analysis. Finally, in an additional effort to ensure the factual accuracy, that is to say, descriptive validity (Maxwell, 2002), we returned the transcribed interviews to our informants for their inspection. Several of our informants provided additional information or made corrections.

As a further concern for the descriptive validity of our research, the analysis and interpretation of documents and interview-based narratives occurred within the social context, cultural values and historical experiences that are characteristic of Slovenia. Knowledge concerning such matters was readily at hand because two of the three authors are intimately familiar with the economic, cultural and political conditions in former Yugoslavia. One author is a Slovenian by birth and life-long resident of Ljubljana, Slovenia. A second author is by origin a Yugoslav, a former resident of Sarajevo, Bosnia-Herzegovina, now an Australian citizen, residing in Sydney. Both authors have personal experiences of the Yugoslav self-management system and have also conducted research into the use and effects of information systems in Yugoslav companies.

Analysis of our data started early on as we collected historical documents and conducted the first set of interviews. After studying annual reports and various internal documents, and later on interview transcripts, we tried to grasp how actors perceived and affected changes in their environment (i.e. market, political, social and legal) as well as changes because of Sava’s privatization. As we collected more empirical material and established a meaningful dialogue with company informants we were able to connect the dots and construct our own descriptions of changing economic and other environmental conditions as well as Sava’s emergence as a successful player under free market conditions. Next we focused on deliberate managerial actions to develop a learning organization, as well as the emerging practices of organizational learning and informants’ accounts of the use of various IT systems. Thus, our understanding of the subjects’ interpretations was based on and validated against the historical background of the broader social, economic and political changes. Because of the iterative nature of these processes, we went through several hermeneutic circles (Klein & Myers, 1999) before arriving at a satisfactory understanding.

Understanding subjects’ interpretations was, as often happens, intertwined with our theoretical interpretations. That is, we viewed the findings within the theoretical lens of the single-, double- and triple-loop learning model (Argyris & Schein, 1978; Snell & Chak, 1998). This effort made organizational learning phenomena appear in sharp focus, enabling their bracketing (Denzin, 2002) – inspecting, dissecting, uncovering, defining and analysing their characteristics and structures and relationships with IT. Building on bracketing processes, we then extended the organizational learning model so as to be able to differentiate between learning processes at the individual, group (or department) and organizational levels and to investigate the specifics of IT support. The theoretical interpretation of organizational learning and the role of IT thus constructed were contextualized in Sava’s social milieu and the informants’ experiences of transition. This contextualization ‘brings the phenomenon alive in the worlds of interacting individuals’ (Denzin, 2002, p. 359). Through contextualization we aimed to demonstrate how lived experiences shape the specific nature of particular organizational learning processes (within a single-, double- and triple-loop learning model and at the individual, group and organizational level), which in turn determine the type of IT support, and the meaning of IT use in these learning processes. Theoretical interpretation emerged while going through yet another series of hermeneutic circles – back and forth between empirical findings (i.e. informants’ interpretations) and our own provisional theoretical interpretations.

Finally, we addressed the inevitable question of generalizability, that is to say, can the lessons learned from Sava’s case study be relevant and inspiring to other companies in transition economies? Our primary motivation to study the Sava Company was to develop in-depth understanding of organizational learning and its relationship with IT in a real-life context. We conclude that our interpretations of the Sava case, including a theoretical understanding of organizational learning and IT, can make sense in other companies and situations (Yin, 1984; made similar arguments). For instance, a different company in transition might be interested to compare its development with Sava’s development as a learning organization including the emergence of specific learning processes and the use and role of IT. We suggest that the study of Sava’s organizational learning has potential to make an important contribution to knowledge precisely because of Sava’s particular experiences with radical economic, social and political changes. Furthermore, we suggest that its strategy of IT-supported organizational learning contributed greatly to its successful transition to a free market economy.

## 4. THE SAVA COMPANY

Sava, a diversified manufacturer of automobile and other tires and specialty rubber products, was founded in 1920 in the City of Kranj, Slovenia. The Company weathered several difficult critical periods: German occupation during WWII, post-war economic rebuilding, secession from the former Yugoslavia and privatization. Sava privatized in 1995 by transferring 15.58% of stock to the workers, 11.06% to the Restitution Fund, 13.93% to the Pension Fund and 21.40% to the Development Fund. The remaining 38.03% was placed on the market in accordance to the Law on Privatization of 1992. This law required that enterprises contemplating privatization had to transfer 20% of the stock to the workers, 10% to the Restitution Fund, 10% to the Pension Fund and 20% to the Development Fund of the Republic of Slovenia. The remaining 40% could be sold to insiders (i.e. current and former employees and management) or placed on the stock market through private bidding, public offering, auction or the voucher method (Kne evi & Pahor, 2004).zˇ c<sup>¢</sup>

Sava designed and produced innovative and high-quality rubber tires for automobiles, motorcycles, scooters and bicycles, as well as other rubber products. After Slovenia’s seceding from the former Yugoslavia, Sava no longer enjoyed a protected position and access to its traditional domestic market. The company was forced to penetrate foreign markets, first in Germany and Eastern Europe. Moreover, because automobile tires are a global product, the company found it difficult to compete against major automobile tire producers such as BF Goodrich, Bridgestone, Goodyear, Michelin and Pirelli. The recognition that Sava could not compete effectively in the global environment motivated members of top management to seek a joint venture with one of its competitors, as Sava’s President explained:

Today there [are] fewer than 10 players [in the world’s car tire] market. [They] have the market [on account] of the very large development costs [for tires]. We concluded that it would be [unwise] to enter this market on our own . . . We decided to shop around [for a partner]. We just completed a joint venture agreement with Goodyear, in Akron, [Ohio, USA]. (Company President, interview, October 1997)

Negotiations between Goodyear and Sava resulted in the partnership named Sava Tires, in which Goodyear invested \$120 million USD and acquired a 60% interest in Sava’s automotive tire production facilities (interview with Company President, October 1997; Robinson, 2000). After concluding the joint venture two companies existed: Sava Tires, which produced rubber tires, and Sava, which produced specialty rubber and chemical products.

The joint venture with Goodyear provided Sava with marketing expertise, increased sales volume and exposure to managerial knowledge that Sava desperately needed in order to survive in a free market-oriented economy. A single IT department that was managed and operated by Sava’s IT staff supported the combined operations of Sava Tires and Sava. This meant that Sava benefited from new IT systems, with both Goodyear and Sava financially supporting their design and implementation. This situation changed in 2003 when Sava ended this relationship with Goodyear and implemented its own IT systems (Sava 2003 Annual Report).

The joint venture was beneficial to Goodyear as well because it provided access to Eastern European markets and a skilled work force. In fact Goodyear’s Chief Financial Officer stated:

We were looking for a business environment where production would be at high quality level and where there are local suppliers and raw materials. It was not only a business partner we have found in Slovenia, but also a well-educated and highly skilled work force. (Robinson, 2000)

Sava’s top management used the financial proceeds arising from the joint venture to create Sava Holding, which comprises six divisions – Rubber Manufacturing, Chemicals, Trade, Tourism, Real Estate and Investment Finance (Sava 2002 Annual Report). The Rubber division manufactures rubber offset printing blankets and rubber rolling blankets for the paper industry. The Chemicals division manufactures industrial powder coatings and colour paints. The Trade division comprises a retail store chain that offers customers over 1000 products. The Tourism division manages hotels, golf courses and other tourist facilities in Slovenia. The Real Estate division manages and develops properties in Slovenia. Finally, the Investment division supports the financial activities of the other divisions and invests in other profitable opportunities in Slovenia (Sava 2002 Annual Report).

Sava’s top management was careful to keep its options open concerning the company’s relationship with Goodyear. This approach showed great foresight because it prevented the serious complications that can occur when large foreign companies conclude joint ventures with smaller companies operating in transition economies (Turk, 2003). After 5 years of joint venture experience Sava decided to sell its share in Sava Tires to Goodyear: Sava sold 20% of its shares in 2002 and the remaining 20% of its shares in 2004 (Kaplan, 2002; Sava 2002 and 2004 Annual Reports). Thus, in 2004 Goodyear became the sole owner of Sava Tires (producing automobile tires), while Sava remained the owner of the rest of the company (producing tires for scooters, motorcycles, go-karts, solid tires and tires for fork-lift vehicles as well as specialty rubber and chemical products). This in effect spelled the end of the joint venture between Goodyear and Sava (Sava 2004 Annual Report).

## 5. ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING AND IT

Members of Sava’s management team were acutely aware that privatization and free market conditions necessitated a fundamental reorientation of the company’s business strategy. Early on, during the first years of transition, the company’s top management promoted a ‘goal to become [a] permanently learning company’ as essential to achieving success (especially for the part of Sava not included in the joint venture with Goodyear) (interview with Company President, October 1997). They put emphasis on their employees, their knowledge, creativity and innovation:

At Sava, we are well aware that our employees, their knowledge and creativity, represent the company’s competitive advantage and its strength. As we change processes, programs and products, we also change the nature and culture of work. (Sava 1997 Annual Report)

How to stimulate innovation is one of our most important responsibilities. I think that delegation of responsibilities helps to develop creativity and stimulate people to be innovative. Because if they have to help themselves [and] to think how to manage some problem [that] is the best way to achieve [innovation]. (Company President, interview, October 1997)

Such a view of innovation and management structure (‘delegation of responsibility’) that stimulates innovation ties in with the company’s traditional focus on and experience with participative management during the socialist period before privatization. The former Yugoslavia was the first socialist country to reform its economic system and introduce some forms of a market economy, workers’ ownership of companies and the so-called self-management system in 1965 (Roland, 2000; Cecez, 2004). Workers rights in participating in decision-making were later guaranteed by the Yugoslav Constitution, and the self-management system was regulated by law. Workers participated in various forms of decision-making, such as electing members of the Workers Council. While a company executive played typical management roles – being responsible for operations and management of functional areas (e.g. production, finance, marketing or personnel) as well as for the company as a whole – it was the Workers Council that made strategic, investment and other key decisions. Such forms of decisionmaking were criticized for being inefficient and also formalistic (with management and political structures de facto holding power). However, there were also observable benefits: workers typically identified with and were committed to the company, and demonstrated motivation for improvements of work practices and innovation of products and processes. These views and experiences informed top management’s approach to developing a learning culture and a learning organization.

Each annual report from 1996 to 2004 reveals progressive growth of a learning culture and provides many instances of organizational learning. For example, in 2000 employees participated in an average of 33 hours of instruction and training at a cost to the company of \$230 000 USD. Furthermore, Sava conducted annual campaigns that invited employees to submit innovative ideas to advance Company processes and products. The Company’s innovation campaign of 2000 resulted in 249 submissions with an economic value of approximately \$650 000 USD (Sava 2000 Annual Report).

The commitment to and appreciation of the organizational learning concept are most succinctly summarized in Sava’s 2001 Annual Report:

The culture of excellence and the culture of a learning company in Sava are being combined. In 2001 as well, the range of acquiring new knowledge increased and forms of education in various domains . . . aimed at instigating and directing change. The most extensive [courses] were devoted to comprehending the ‘20 Keys’ method, introducing new software and marketing. (Sava 2001 Annual Report)

The 20 keys method, which was developed by Kobayashi (1995), is an extensive benchmarking system for improving an organization’s competitiveness and profitability. Each of the 20 keys, i.e. cleaning and organizing, rationalization by applying management by objectives, improving team activities, etc., receives a key ranging from 1 (amateurish) to 5 (best) according to a well-defined scoring method. The 20 keys method centres on achieving the company’s strategic goals, improving the speed with which the company learns, eliminating waste, motivating employees and ensuring long-term competitive advantage.

Particularly revealing is the twofold attention on excellence and learning. An educational programme emphasizing theoretical as well as practical aspects of ‘instigating and directing change’ and learning the ‘20 Keys’ method as well as new software supported the culture of learning. For example, in 2001 of all courses taught, 33% were devoted to business studies, 25% to communication, information flows and personal growth and 5% to social sciences.

Furthermore, continued commitment to knowledge, knowledge sharing and learning resulted in the creation of ‘competence centers of knowledge’:

Competence centers of knowledge are an organizational form of managing key strategic fields and successors of present support functions. Through a management network the development and transfer of the most advanced management know-how . . . has gained significance. The . . . key task of the competence centers, formed in 2002, is . . . to encourage the flow of knowledge . . . which the companies in the [Sava] Group use to establish competitive advantages . . . Developing a system of a ‘learning company’ and training ‘learning groups’ . . . increase the level of knowledge transfer and [knowledge] conversion. (Sava 2002 Annual Report)

The most recent recognition of Sava’s success in building a learning organization came from the Slovene Chamber of Commerce. Based on the analysis and evaluation of the 500 larges Slovene companies:

Sava achieves the best result and receives a joint recognition by the USP Institute and the Slovene Chamber of Commerce for the achievements in developing the learning company concept in 2004. (2005 Half-yearly Report)

Sava’s successful development as a learning organization was supported and enabled by various applications of IT. Our interviews and company annual reports include multiple references to information systems and IT, and their role in supporting organizational learning, coordinating company activities and decision-making. The following quotes typify management’s attitude and action concerning IT and learning:

To the greatest possible extent we are seeking to exploit the advantages provided by advanced information technology in producing information, rationalizing business and introducing commercial activities. Considerable attention is therefore being devoted to the process of employing information technology in all spheres. Its development is being supported by extensive investments, the most prominent being the SAP business information system. (Sava 2001 Annual Report)

We use SAP . . . for gathering strategically important information about . . . customers . . . and about competitors [from whom] the customer also buys [such as the] competitor’s market share. SAP [is used for gathering and storing] data on the customer’s core competencies, competitive position, [and] investments [which enable] predicting the customer’s future and [relate that] view with [Sava’s] future. (Interview, Marketing Research Manager, 2003)

Information on competitors is very hard to obtain . . . you have to use different channels to obtain [it] . . . which means different people in different locations. With Lotus Notes . . . [different people in different locations] there is no problem. (Interview, Marketing Research Manager, 2003)

[Ten years ago there was much] resistance to [introducing] electronic administration . . . people were used to communicating with their secretary who wrote things down. IT changed our work and our daily lives. [We use] Lotus Notes to [effect] organizational change and [create] operational change. (Interview, Research Director, 2003)

[Manufacturing] a high tech product [entails] many raw materials [and] it is difficult to [keep track] of everything . . . to manage all this information. It was difficult . . . everything was on paper. So we [demanded] information systems, we tried [alternative] systems but [finally] Sava settled on SAP [which proved to be a] good decision. (Interview, Factory Manager, 2003)

The emphasis is primarily on business and secondarily on IT. Management at all levels conceives of IT as a tool that supports the company’s goals, business strategy and organizational learning culture. This was already the case just after the company’s privatization, and it in turn motivated the researchers to undertake a longitudinal study of the company’s progress towards instituting organizational learning supported by IT (Mustonen-Ollila & Lyytinen, 2003).

Furthermore, we examined whether IT systems specifically support single-loop, double-loop or triple-loop learning and the nature of ‘support’ in each of these types of learning, which we discuss in the following sections.

## 5.1. Single-loop learning and IT

Product quality was among the key concerns in Sava’s expansion to global markets. Quality control was enforced by well defined operational standards as explained by the President:

Quality management systems are described in the quality control handbook as operational standards. [Factory workers should] perform their assignments accordingly and in doing so they will perform their work correctly. (Company President, interview, October 1997)

Furthermore, Sava sponsored a variety of training courses focused on achieving desired product quality:

[The training goal is to instruct] managers and factory workers concerning the use of technology in work processes. [Courses include] identifying product quality problems visually and by X-ray, and categorizing product quality problems as arising from manufacturing processes, from working procedures, or from production tools and equipment. [The teaching methods] comprise lectures and demonstrations. (Sava Course Catalog, 1997)

Quality control processes and standards as well as training courses exemplify single-loop learning as they assist employees in their individual learning aimed at achieving product quality goals. These processes, standards and courses improve managers’ and workers’ understanding of the company goals and quality assurance strategies (without questioning or altering them) and also increase their competency in achieving them.

Sava managers also emphasize that IT applications played an important role in such quality assurance processes. The most essential was the implementation of SAP:

[Before SAP] the production worker had to alternate between Excel tables. [The Excel tables] were like different SAP sections. At the moment we still follow the quality [assurance] approach, [but we do this] using SAP. (Factory Manager, interview, June 2003)

When [the] sales department needs information on a product produced for a customer we [pull] it from our information system [SAP] – when the product was produced and [its] quality parameters. [However, quality parameters] are still checked manually. (Factory Manager, interview, June 2003)

As part of Sava’s strategy to achieve globally competitive quality products, SAP adoption specifically targeted single-loop learning in production processes. Besides providing more accurate, timely and comprehensive information, SAP advanced quality assurance processes, making them more effective and efficient. For example, before each production run a production worker at Sava receives product specifications from SAP. The worker then checks whether the parameters of the item produced are within range and, in case they are not, the worker checks whether corrections can be made. If corrections prove to be impossible, the item is set aside for a supervisory inspection (Factory Manager, interview, June 2003).

An interview in 2003 with the Director of Research and Development provides one additional example of the connection between single-loop learning and information systems in the area of new product development:

[Today] life is easier – we are faster. Thirty years ago we had technical information in the form of documents that were copied and [stored] in the traditional way. [We] had about twenty typists who collected, revised, and stored [documents]. Presently documents are retrieved [by accessing] the computer. (Director of Research and Development, interview, June 2003)

When a computerized document base enables easy access to and sharing of technical information and product research documents in a department, single-loop learning is exemplified. In this instance, the computerized document base changed information processing practices in the Research and Development Department and increased engineers’ capacity to design, innovate and improve products. What is interesting here is that the nature of IT support for the single-loop learning differs from that provided by SAP in product quality insurance. While SAP in production quality control is primarily embedded in individual single-loop learning, the use of the computerized document base enables knowledge sharing and supports intersubjective learning processes in a collective (that is, in the Department). However, both are examples of IT-supported organizational learning as they assist employees in advancing their actions (changing behaviour) and achieving company goals.

Part of Sava’s strategy to develop a learning organization was the implementation of Lotus Notes to advance internal communications and communications with its customers. The application of Lotus Notes in managing customer relationships illustrates single-loop learning at the organization level:

Customer information, customer complaints, customer product quality problem announcements, and customer requests for information are all stored in Lotus Notes. This is a great help because in the past such information was scattered in files maintained by each salesperson separately – a customer’s phone call might be handled by salesperson A and stored in his file and the next time the same customer calls it might be handled by salesperson B and stored in his file system. Using Lotus Notes, however, ensures that all the information on a particular customer is contained in one file and accessible by any salesperson that gets a complete picture of the customer in question. (IT manager, interview, 2003)

A Manager of Market Research expresses a similar view:

Lotus Notes, without any complicated programming, is used to coordinate the work in the Sales Department . . . in working with concrete customers. . . . Everybody can see directly from a monitor of a PC which steps or activities concerning a certain customer are done. You don’t need to call somebody . . . You just make some clicks and you see directly. (Interview, 2003)

Lotus Notes enables a unified attitude and policy in managing customers across the whole company by providing a unique source of customer information, including the history of dealing with individual customers, and effective support for addressing customer requests. By using Lotus Notes, which operates in concert with SAP, Sava is improving its response to customer requests and is building better relationships with customers. In other words the company’s knowledge and competency base is improving, enabling consistent implementation of policy and achievement of goals without altering either of them. This by definition is single-loop learning at the organization level (Snell & Chak, 1998).

## 5.2. Double-loop learning and IT

After experiencing initial challenges of privatization and exposure to global markets, Sava’s top management decided to search for new ideas and innovations in-house, among its employees. Drawing from past experiences of worker participation, they believed that the employees, and especially production workers, are an essential source of innovative ideas. Thus, in 1996 the Sava Company started an extensive 3-year programme aptly named ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ (Company President, interview, October 1997). One year into the programme individuals throughout the company contributed some 350 usable proposals (Figure 2). It is noteworthy that the majority of the proposals came from production floor workers.

We shall discuss one proposal that we observed first hand in 2003 that illustrates doubleloop learning. Figure 3 shows a step in the manufacture of rubber blankets for offset printing. The production step requires that a metal strip (Figure 3, right hand side) be affixed to the side of the rubber blanket. Originally this production step required two workers – one worker held down the rubber blanket on a worktable while the second worker covered its edge with glue and then affixed the metal strip onto the rubber blanket.

The factory director explained the operation as follows:

[We] glue a [metal] strip to a rubber blanket. One [worker] holds the [rubber blanket] and the [second] worker applies the glue and affixes the strip. However, on one occasion a worker borrowed a heavy weight from a foreman with which to hold the blanket down on the worktable. He then could perform the entire operation by himself. (Factory Director, interview with authors, June 2003)

The workers proposed that a metal clamp be built and mounted onto the worktable to hold the rubber sheet in place. One worker could then apply the glue to the edge of the rubber sheet and affix the metal strip. Because the metal clamp holds down the rubber sheet, one worker can perform the entire operation, which heretofore required two workers.

The proposal illustrates double-loop learning because the workers addressed inefficiencies in the production process and reflected on the production steps that they had performed over a long period of time. Next, such reflection led the workers to experiment and generate new knowledge to deal with their work situation, thus revising their mental maps. This example also illustrates how working and learning are intrinsically linked (Lave & Wenger, 1991) and that ‘learning-in-working’ can be seen as an occupational necessity (Brown & Duguid, 1991)

When the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign commenced in 1996, it relied on IT in a very limited way. Individuals entrusted their innovative ideas to company-provided forms that were then forwarded to a central location, where they were entered into a computerized database. A committee consisting of the factory manager, a technical expert, and additional individuals when necessary, evaluated the proposals on their ability to contribute to the quality of the work place, simplify work, increase product quality or reduce production cost. After 4 years, this successful campaign transformed into a systematic practice of stimulating and progressing innovative ideas and proposals for company improvements:

Two years ago we started with 61 work groups [that] meet weekly for sixty minutes to discuss proposals for improving [work]. Proposals are [still] in paper form. It is [desirable] to enter proposals [directly] into a computer database and we [are] looking for software packages to process [proposals] electronically. (Director of Research and Development, interview, June 2003)

Company management collaborated with the working groups on a regular basis (Figure 4). Through regular meetings they systematically examined performance and achievement of goals, and discussed and defined future developments and goals. The emphasis was on sharing knowledge both horizontally among the working groups and vertically between the working groups and management. Such knowledge sharing was supported by different IT applications – ‘information boards’ for information dissemination, an IT system that maintained employees proposals, as well as records of discussions, evaluation and implementation of proposals. These IT applications are expected to be replaced by a ‘knowledge management database’, which in 2003 was at the very early stage of development (IT Manager, 2003 interview).

Another process named Sava Dialogue involved annual ‘systematically run interviews between executives and each employee’ (Sava 2002 Annual Report). Sava Dialogue was seen as a management tool complementing work group processes (see Figure 4):

The principal aim of Sava Dialogue is to provide coordination of corporate goals (business plans) and daily activities of individuals (goals, activities and personal development). This helps individuals to more easily understand the long-term goals of the Company . . . The first part of the Annual interview, in which the manager and employee specify key goals and the tasks to achieve them, is directed to the principle of ‘management by objectives’. The second part is ‘development’ and is intended for personal growth. Together they form a tool on the basis of which quality development measures are prepared for the individual to enable him or her to achieve the set goals more easily, increase his or her responsibility and fulfil his or her ambitions according to personal improvement in the direction of the Company’s set strategic goals. (Sava 2002 Annual Report)

The ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign involved different forms of doubleloop learning when it extended with work groups and Sava Dialogue. Through Sava Dialogue employees assessed (by self- and peer evaluation) their competencies and skills vis-à-vis the goals and tasks to achieve them, within the broader context of company strategic goals. This in turn was the basis for preparing personal development plans. In such a way employees are engaged in a learning process that helps them change their views of the business and of the business environment and also helps them reframe their roles and tasks in line with their deeper insight into the company’s changing vision and strategic goals. The work groups extended the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign when they shared in the process of analysing and reframing problems, leading to changing mental maps and setting new corporate goals. These are double-loop learning processes simultaneously operating at the group and the organization level. In this example we saw Sava management actively searching for and building IT applications to support these specific learning processes.

Another example of IT-supported double-loop organizational learning comes from the Marketing Department. As Sava’s marketing director explained:

We used Excel [to analyse] systematically collected data about competitors in different countries. The purpose was to set goals for the future, to make plans for future sales. We [now] use SAP not only to run production but also to gather strategically important information about our customers . . . We use SAP to collect data not only when a customer buys from us but also from other competitors . . . we collect key information [about] the customer . . . it may be only the price or it may be other reasons which [motivate the customer to buy] from the competitor. (Marketing Director, interview, June 2003)

The marketing director further explained her views concerning SAP by emphasizing that its usefulness arises from the manner in which it was adopted. In her view, expanding the SAP implementation to customer management and market analysis has the potential to assist the Marketing Department in ‘better understanding their customers and their existing markets’ and ‘predicting prospective markets’. This in turn means potentially changing perceptions (or mental maps) about these markets, ultimately resulting in changing strategies. In this case we see an expectation that SAP implementation in the Marketing Department will in fact support double-loop learning at both the department and the company level.

## 5.3. Triple-loop learning and IT

In its transition to an open market with global competition Sava paid considerable attention to improving production and producing globally competitive, quality products. As we have seen in previous sections, single-loop learning was sufficient in achieving the desired product quality; in addition, double-loop learning was required to improve the understanding of new markets, rethink products and processes, and set new goals. While these learning processes are shown to be essential for Sava’s transition to a market economy, they initially operated within a mental model reflecting Yugoslavia’s brand of socialism. However, Sava’s privatization changed ownership from employees to shareholders. As a result the Board of Management made major business decisions instead of the Workers Council. Privatizing required abandoning the socialist self-management mental model in favour of an altogether different capitalist mental model (Bogdan & Andreja, 1992). A transition of this kind required triple-loop learning (Figure 1).

The President’s position in the 1996 Annual Report, while indicating clear market orientation, reflects some ambiguity in terms of the underlying mental model:

It is because of [our] customers that Sava is oriented towards the development, manufacture and marketing of high quality products, which in turn, calls upon us to instill, encourage and motivate knowledge, creativity and productivity in all our employees. In turn, because of the customers, our shareholders can expect good business results. Customer satisfaction is the only guarantee of a yield on the company’s investment and job security for its employees. (Sava 1996 Annual Report)

The President is addressing profit expectations of the shareholders but he is also concerned with employee job security. The new company structure and roles of top management, especially in terms of their responsibilities to the shareholders, gradually led to a new mindset more attuned to the contemporary socio-economic conditions in Slovenia and the company’s new identity.

Evidence of a new mindset appeared in 1996, when the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign was introduced. As top managers were concerned with Sava’s ability to radically transform its products and processes, they became aware of the limitation of organizational innovation and learning processes. They felt the need to change the approach to company learning. So they introduced a new structure for generating and sharing innovative ideas and for arriving at new collective mental maps. This new structure was supported by a particular IT system: as ideas and proposals for innovation were uploaded into the system they could be accessed and assessed by all other employees; they were also processed and evaluated by work groups. This organizational structure relied on the participation of all employees and therefore had a potential to change collective mindfulness.

Throughout the period of transition while triple-loop learning was under way, members of top management became increasingly aware of IT’s contribution to developing and supporting the market-oriented mental model. In fact an annual report specifically mentioned IT in connection with the new mental model:

Special attention was devoted to public relations and marketing communications, informatics, logistics and purchasing. Many significant development projects drew to a close: reorganization of the Sava Group [comprising six divisions: Rubber Manufacturing, Chemicals, Trade, Tourism, Real Estate, and Investment/Finance.], renovation of basic information support (SAP R/3), and the creation of a business strategy for trade partners. (Sava 2000 Annual Report)

Furthermore, a subsequent annual report cited important reasons for upgrading IT hardware and software:

We tackled the complete innovation of the information system based on SAP software. [Thus] new solutions from January 2002 are ensuring quality supervision of a considerable number of business operations. (Sava 2001 Annual Report)

Elsewhere the 2001 annual report mentioned the major purposes of IT modernization, namely, increasing efficiency and effectiveness by creating uniform information systems with common databases.

Effective and efficient communication among organizational members is a major requirement for forming and maintaining mental models. Thus, it is noteworthy that the 2002 annual report addressed IT-supported communication systems:

Internal communication and independent applications for e-accounts, project management, a set of organizational regulations and investment are all supported by Lotus Notes software. (Sava 2002 Annual Report)

Further analysis of interview data demonstrates that IT applications were successfully implemented throughout Sava during the period from 1995 to 2002. These IT applications were implemented in response to information processing needs that arose from ever increasing demands for organizational learning. In short, the increasing complexity of organizational learning processes was the driver for implementing IT applications.

## 6. DISCUSSION

In its transition to a private company operating in global market conditions Sava demonstrated considerable capacity for reconceiving not only its environment but also its own identity. Our study reveals an exemplary case of radical organizational change enacted through organizational learning variously supported by IT. Importantly, specific processes of organizational learning – underpinning radical change – involved innovations of communicative practices and informating processes through IT applications. Most of the top managers we interviewed believe that organizational learning enabled by IT was the key to the company’s successful transition. Sava’s 1995–2004 annual reports and our interviews made clear that shortly after privatization top management decided to create a culture of learning and deemed IT the tool for this purpose as well as for information acquisition, distribution and communication.

The importance accorded to IT increased significantly during the 1997–2004 period. To illustrate, in 1997 Sava used IT to enhance corporate communication using email. In 2002, however, the company’s IT centre had implemented Lotus Notes that enabled interpersonal communication, online discussion and conducting meetings. Lotus Notes enabled information sharing up and down the corporate hierarchy as well as horizontally within and among individuals and working groups. Next, in 2000 Sava purchased and implemented the SAP software package: first modules for managing production operations, quality control and production machinery maintenance, and then modules for managing purchasing, sales, marketing and logistics operations. A key research question in our study is, how did IT enable and support specific types of organizational learning processes, and what is the nature of support for each type?

Learning at each level can take the form of single-loop, double-loop and triple-loop learning, as presented in Table 3.

From our analysis in the previous section, summarized in Table 3, we can see a wide range of learning processes, each with distinctive management problems, information needs, IT support and determinants of success.

For instance, Sava’s implementation of SAP for production management helped production workers to check the quality of parts, machine operations and products and take corrective actions. Here, first-loop learning leads to individuals’ adaptive actions for quality standards. The goal is predefined and mediated by SAP. Individual learning involves mastering the use of SAP. The success of such learning depends on the IT system (SAP) – its comprehensiveness, friendliness, adaptability, timeliness, accuracy, etc.

An example of double-loop learning at group and organizational levels occurred when Sava implemented the ‘A thousand ideas for a better future’ campaign. The focus of learning was on work groups across the organization, including management (Table 3). Group learning and intergroup learning required an open dialogue, non-defensive reasoning, free and open inquiry, and effective communication tools. Learning involved questioning assumptions and beliefs, reframing problems and developing a new shared understanding and new mental maps within groups, among groups and between groups and management (see Figure 4). Sava’s approach reflects Brown & Duguid’s (1991) view that knowledge and innovative capacity are distributed throughout all communities that make up an organization:

For it is the organization’s communities, at all levels, who are in contact with the environment and involved in interpretive sense making, congruence finding, and adapting. It is from any site of such interactions that new insights can be coproduced. (p. 52)

Sava’s management realized that the success of double-loop learning depends on opportunities for social interaction (61 work groups met weekly), capacity for intersubjective sensemaking and shared understanding, and negotiation skills as well as organizational culture. While SAP provided useful reports, double-loop learning also required highly specific IT support that was emergent and situated in local conditions. This is why Sava’s management actively searched for an adequate IT system. The nature of double-loop learning is such that IT support (‘Information Boards’ and IT systems that supported collaboration and processing of proposals) increases effectiveness of these processes, but does not alter them substantially.

After Sava’s privatization top management critically assessed the company’s position in the global market, its financial and intellectual resources, and the need for creating a corporate culture that promoted organizational learning. Sava’s joint venture with Goodyear arose from this assessment and it provided the company with financial resources and access to managerial know-how, marketing expertise and computer-based information systems. Sava’s management benefited from interorganizational learning (Holmqvist, 2003), but soon realized that Sava needed to search for its own pathway to transition. Top management did not assume that there is a ‘right’ answer or a universal approach to be discovered. Rather, they drew from the company’s experiences and relied on their employees as key resources in exploring innovative ways for learning, asking new questions, developing new ideas and becoming a new organization. Through triple-loop learning top management opened new channels for stakeholders’ participation so that different questions can be asked, different perspectives explored and different sorts of explanations offered (Snell & Chak, 1998).

The introduction of the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign and its subsequent extension with work groups established a new structure for organizational learning. The new structure facilitated changing the organization’s values, beliefs and rewards, and arriving at new collective mental maps and goals. Interestingly, the success of this campaign (hundreds of ideas generated each year and a high employee participation rate) has been largely attributed to Sava’s past experiences with self-management. In hindsight it may sound paradoxical that with triple-loop learning Sava’s top management relied on socialist experiences when they initiated the change of the collective mindset from a socialist to a capitalist one. However, a more subtle analysis reveals that it may not have been all that paradoxical.

Sava’s top management approach to a learning company was consistent with Pedler’s et al. (1991) definition of a learning company as one ‘which facilitates learning of all its members and continuously transforms itself’ (p. 2). Most characteristics of a learning company – the learning approach to strategy, participative policy-making, informating, enabling organization structures, self-development opportunities for all, etc. – were present at Sava to a large degree. Company experience with workplace democracy and self-management from the socialist period provided a fruitful soil for implementing organizational learning. Before the dissolution of the former Yugoslavia, workers in Slovenian factories engaged in participative decision-making concerning business strategy, organizational structure, production plans and other issues. Corporate programmes were undertaken to educate employees and enable them to respond effectively. While transforming itself Sava drew from its past and, in Truex et al.’s (1999) words, ‘used its own identity as the primary point of reference when it reconstructs itself’ (p. 118). In such a process

the socially constructed realities of an organization form the basis for the next version of the organization. As an organization adjusts and changes it does so with reference to its former self in a more or less constant mode of self-reproduction. That is, in continuously reproducing itself the organization must do so with constant reference to itself, its past practices, its values, decisions, contracts, and commitments. (Truex et al., 1999, p. 118)

But self-reproduction and reconstruction as part of organizational learning leave nothing quite the same. For instance, the introduction and implementation of ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ processes clearly established the power of top management. They made a decision to involve employees. The practice of triple-loop organizational learning in this case – while drawing from employees’ past experiences – enforced the new power structure and the new capitalist mindset. Top management enacted organizational learning based on their prerogatives as representatives of investors’ and shareholders’ interests rather than employees interests.

When participating in the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ initiative and in doubleloop learning, employees risked being conned ‘into believing that they have more rather than less power’ (Coopey, 1995; Snell & Chak, 1998, p. 338). While we were not able to establish this, we certainly witnessed top management obtaining control over resources, information, company policies and strategies, as well as learning structures and processes. Statements by Sava’s President confirmed that by 2003 top management operated according to this new mental model. In an interview with a newspaper reporter Sava’s President emphasized the necessity for further radical changes with profitability increase as a major driver:

Many changes must occur in Sava so that in five years profitability will increase three times (i.e. from 3% to 9%). We will not accept a ‘convoy mentality’ which is waiting for the slowest performer. We will either replace the ship’s captain or sink the ship. (Sava President, interview June 2003)

The President made it clear that there would be no place for non-performers and that any under-performing unit (‘ship’) would get a new manager or would be closed (‘sunk’).

On another occasion, while addressing the Board of Management, the President stated:

How should we proceed? As economists we adhere to a clearly designed plan and goal. In the Sava Group, simultaneously with standardizing other business procedures and strategic functions, we are concluding the establishment of business policies to achieve sustainable development in each of our strategic divisions. and – what we believe is most important – we shall incorporate the principles of sustainability in all our products and services and meet them with all our employees. (Sava 2003 Annual Report)

The President referred to his colleagues on the Board as ‘economists’ committed to economic stability and growth. He further asked, ‘How should we proceed?’ That is, he asked, how should we, as the Board of Management in charge of the company’s future, proceed? At the end he identified employees as agents in the service of achieving the Board’s goals. In conclusion, the changes we could observe in previous quotes signify that the mental model from the period before Sava’s privatization, which was informed by socialist ideals, was indeed replaced by an entirely new free market-oriented mental model informed by capitalist ideals.

Such a radical change in mental models and company identity was possible a result of top management’s commitment to a learning organization and their initiative in enacting and persistence in practising triple-loop and double-loop learning. The change in mindset and mental maps happened at the top management level. But thanks to new learning structures, processes and IT systems, ‘grass-root’ members engaged in double-loop learning: they participated in intersubjective sense-making concerning the new environment and the new company vision (e.g. through workgroups’ interaction with management) as opposed to a passive response to the initiatives of top management. It is important to emphasize that top management demonstrated sensitivity to issues of workplace democracy and workers’ participation: they respected former (socialist) values while promoting new values and developing a new company identity and directions attuned to a radically changed environment and private ownership.

## 7. CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

Sava’s privatization and its successful transition from a socialist self-management system and protected market conditions to a capitalist system and global markets followed a unique transformation path. However, the experiences gained and lessons learned may be valuable to other companies, especially those in transition economies. In particular, by drawing on insights from Sava’s transformation to a learning organization, this paper contributes to understanding the nature of organizational learning and the role of various IT systems that enable and support learning.

Broadly speaking Sava’s case can be interpreted as a confirmation of Robey’s et al. (2000) optimistic scenario that ‘information technology may increase the capacity of organizations to learn and [that], simultaneously, learning capacity may affect the degree to which new technologies are adopted and used effectively’ (p. 147). As we have shown, the adoption and use of various IT (such as Excel, document management systems, SAP, Lotus Notes) did indeed increase Sava’s capacity to learn; conversely, Sava’s orientation to learning, integration of working and learning, and ongoing innovation of products and work processes created new needs for IT support and motivated the search for and adoption of new IT systems. However, the major contribution of this study is a deeper understanding of the intricacies of the relationship between organizational learning and IT support.

By investigating organizational learning in the context of changing work processes and ongoing transformation of organizational practices, we suggest that the role of IT varies depending on the nature of the learning. The range of relationships between organizational learning and IT investigated in our case company are systematized in Table 3. In this table we applied an extended Snell & Chak’s (1998) framework that combines, on one dimension, types of learning (single-, double- and triple-loop learning) and, on another dimension, the level or focus of learning (individual, group/department and organization learning). The use of this framework to analyse and systematize organizational learning and its relationship with IT at Sava illustrates how it may be useful in other cases and contexts.

In single-loop learning, where learning processes are typically well structured, the role and tasks of IT systems can be defined in advance and often realized with off-the-shelf software products (e.g. Enterprise Resource Planning system, Lotus Notes, document management system). The use of such IT systems, however, becomes so embedded in work processes that they become an integral and indispensable part of the learning loop, so much so that the breakdown of the IT system interrupts both working and learning. Sava’s experience also shows that the success of single-loop learning depends on workers’ skills and their training in the IT systems’ use.

Double-loop learning is, as we have seen, less structured and a much more complex learning process. While we saw that double-loop learning is also embedded in the working processes, the cycle of learning is much longer and more diffused than in single-loop learning. Consequently, we saw more variety in the roles and tasks that IT systems played. We saw the generic need for organizational members to be well informed, to communicate and share their assumptions, views and mental maps. Such needs can be fulfilled by traditional management and executive information system (such as SAP) and electronic communication systems (emails, electronic discussion, electronic bulletin boards). In double-loop learning the needs of actors may also be idiosyncratic, dependent on the nature of the business, organizational culture, the specific nature of the learning, and the expectations and skills of participants. This was the case with the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign, which required an IT system to support submitting, managing, evaluating and implementing proposals for innovation and change. With both types of IT support for double-loop learning – generic and specific – the role of the IT system is a supporting one. IT systems may improve learning and make it more efficient and effective, as was the case at Sava. But the essence of learning remains dependent on human creativity, cooperative relationships, socially shared knowledge and collective meaning-making. Based on our interpretation of empirical evidence from Sava we would agree with Jones (1995) that IT systems’ contribution to learning ‘comes not from their retention of specific organizational descriptions’ as the viewpoint of Argyris & Schein (1978) suggests, but from their effect on the general ongoing process of interpretation through which individuals construct and reconstruct their interpersonal milieu (Walsham, 1993, p. 73).

The role of IT systems in triple-loop learning is even more complex and subtle. Visionary, risk-taking, responsible and reflective leaders were required for successfully identifying obstacles to learning in the past, promoting the culture of learning and inventing new structures, modes and strategies of learning. Evidence from Sava’s successful transformation confirms what many authors emphasized: that company leaders play the key role in both double- and triple-loop learning (Torbert, 1994; Coopey, 1995; Snell & Chak, 1998). Sava leaders critically appraised old values, business assumptions and the company’s (in)ability to deal with global markets and develop and promote new values, assumptions and a corporate vision (which in fact is double-loop learning). But more importantly, Sava’s leaders opened new channels for collective knowledge sharing, established new structures (e.g. workgroups) and implemented new IT systems for organizational learning (e.g. SAP, Lotus Notes). The link between triple-loop leaning and IT applications in Sava can be seen from two perspectives:

1 IT applications broadly support triple-loop learning: Sava’s top management is generally informed by various IT systems that contribute to their awareness and understanding of organizational problems and increase their sensitivity to specific learning issues; and

2 IT applications are envisaged as part of new channels and structures for organizational learning. For instance, when the ‘A thousand ideas for a better tomorrow’ campaign was introduced it opened a new channel for double-loop learning that required a new IT system to support it. When implemented the system also assisted top managers in comprehending the emergence of learning processes, in appreciating the nature of engagement and the extent of employees’ participation, and it helped them understand the emergence and proliferation of new mental maps throughout the company.

Finally, an unexpected implication of our study concerns the controversial issue of workplace democracy, participative policy-making and egalitarian power structure that is intrinsic to the notion of a learning organization (Pedler et al., 1991; Coopey, 1995; Snell & Chak, 1998). Such a Utopian view is contrasted with ‘Foucaultian gloom’ where only the powerful ‘learn and win (Snell & Chak, 1998, pp. 338–339) while others are no more than instruments in their hands. First, we found that some form of workplace democracy, participative policy-making and egalitarian power structure that remained from Sava’s socialist past was conducive to and provided a fertile soil for the development of a learning organization. Second, we found that the development of organizational learning, and especially the practice of double- and triple-loop learning, established and strengthened top management’s power position while relying on employees’ participation. Therefore, Sava’s case seems to support neither view – Utopian or Foucaultian. Rather, one might argue, Sava found its own way in balancing the contradicting demands of a learning organization and profit maximization.

He is Editor in Chief of Organizacija, a professional journal published in the Slovenian language.
