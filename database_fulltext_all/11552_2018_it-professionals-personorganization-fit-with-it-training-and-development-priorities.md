# IT professionalsˈ person–organization fit with IT training and development priorities Stephen C. Wingreen\* & J. Ellis Blanton<sup>†</sup>

\*Information Systems and Decision Sciences, University of Canterbury, New Zealand, email: stephen.wingreen@canterbury.ac.nz, and <sup>†</sup>Management Information Systems, University of South Florida, USA, email: eblanton@coba.usf.edu

Abstract. Person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t (P–O <sup>fi</sup>t) research and practice have been hindered on account of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of operationalizing the richness, complexity and subjectivity of the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t phenomenon. P–O <sup>fi</sup>t for technology professionals is further complicated by the rapidly changing demands the IT profession places on its constituents to continually engage in training and development. A human capital perspective is adopted as a lens through which to view the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, and Social Cognitive Theory is proposed as a framework within which to incorporate the principles of Concourse Theory, which is the guiding philosophy of Q-Methodology and Q-sorts. The Q-methodology was used as a means to operationalize the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with respect to IT training and development. Analysis revealed <sup>fi</sup>ve distinct P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types of perspectives that explained 35% of the population variance. Post-hoc analysis of the <sup>fi</sup>ve types revealed that they are interpretable through the lens of the human capital perspective. The results show promise for continued research on the subject, as well as implications for both researchers and practitioners.

Keywords: Q-methodology, Q-sort, Concourse Theory, IT professionals, training assessment, professional development, Social Cognitive Theory, human capita perspective, person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t, <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital

## INTRODUCTION

This study represents the authorsˈ latest in a stream about how IT professionals maintain up-todate technical competencies, which began with the adoption of the human capital perspective, and a fresh re-conceptualization of person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t (P–O <sup>fi</sup>t) from the framework of Socia Cognitive Theory (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). The reconceptualization led to the development of new methods and measurements using Concourse Theory and the Q-methodology (Wingreen et al., 2009; Wingreen & Blanton, 2006). In this paper, we articulate a new theory that integrates the underlying foundations of both the social-cognitive re-conceptualization of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t theory, and Concourse Theory, our theory of measurement, into a cohesive whole by which to investigate the IT professionalˈs subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t beliefs and behaviors.

The diverse, and broad range of competencies, career paths, work environments and related factors that are manifest among IT professionals, along with the numerous types of IT jobs, career paths, people and the bewildering variety of tasks and responsibilities accompanying them make it extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to form generalizable models of the IT profession from which theories, predictions, hypotheses and tests may be undertaken. In their research, Prasad et al. (2007) admit the inadequacy of research with regard to the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of IT profes sionals. Prasad et al. (2007) found that the IT profession is highly segmented, differentiated, specialized and compartmentalized, thus making generalization from <sup>fi</sup>eld research dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, and hence, conclude that ‘one size does not <sup>fi</sup>t all’, with respect to employment arrangements. In other words, research in this area must account for the broad variation within and between persons, and groups of persons in a systematic way.

The human capital perspective proposes the intrinsic value of the human resource, and thus provides a useful foundation on which to begin an inquiry that is centered on persons, and groups of persons, rather than generalized to the profession as a whole. For example, in ou own research (Wingreen et al., 2009), we develop a typology of IT training and development preferences, and demonstrate how the different types are correlated with IT career paths. Along the same theme, Bullen et al. (2007), in their investigation of the changing skill pro<sup>fi</sup>les of the IT workforce, make a distinction between an ‘industrial strategy’, where employees advance through internal career ladders by developing <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c skills, and the ‘craft strategy’ where employees maintain more loyalty to the profession than to the <sup>fi</sup>rm while maintaining a more generalized skill set. Jobs higher up the ladder are typically governed by the industrial strategy, while jobs farther down are governed by the craft strategy.

<sub>–</sub> fi fi <sub>ˈ</sub> P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is ideally the manifestation of a desire to cultivate <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital, which in the case of IT professionals is uniquely prioritized on the maintenance and development of technical competencies (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). In the context of this paper, P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as IT professionalsˈ beliefs about the correspondence between their personal priorities for IT training and development factors, and the priorities of their organization. The IT professionalˈs self-evaluation of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is a special case of a concept-matching process that is proposed by Social Cognitive Theory, and is inherently subjective in nature (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). Furthermore, in theory, P–O <sup>fi</sup>t should be able to capture the richness and complexity of professional development across the full range of the IT profession. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, P–O <sup>fi</sup>t for an IT professional involves the individualˈs self-assessment of which training and development activities must be undertaken so as to endow the IT professional with the greatest amount of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital possible.

Theoretical development of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, however, although widespread in the general literature, has been beset by systematic shortcomings with respect to its measurement and operationalization (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005; Kristof, 1996; OˈReilly et al., 1991; Edwards, 1991). Research on P–O <sup>fi</sup>t also suffers from complications related to the personˈs subjective assessment of <sup>fi</sup>t (Cable & DeRue, 2002; Kristof, 1996; Prasad et al., 2007; Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). Hence, there are very few studies that either operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with measurements that capture the richness and complexity of the phenomenon, or capture the subjectivity of the person (Kristof, 1996; Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). However, Concourse

Theory, the guiding philosophy behind the Q-methodology, is a theory of communicability that supports the analysis of a subject domain based on the naturally occurring discourse of the domain. Q-methodology involves correlating people by their subjective measurement of a representative population of tests (Stephenson, 1953; Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980). Hence, Q-method is valuable both for its ability to capture the personˈs subjectivity across a wide range of organizational conditions, and to operationalize peoplesˈ subjective beliefs about their work, which has been a consistent shortcoming of previous research on P–O <sup>fi</sup>t

It is therefore the goal of this research to propose and develop an integrated, coherent system of theory, methods and measurement to support the investigation of the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development that is capable of capturing the richness and subjectivity inherent in the phenomenon, and thus support a means to differentiate between groups of IT professionals based on their subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t perspectives. In achieving this goal, this research will also identify a typology of IT professionalsˈ P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is sensitive to the context in which the P–O <sup>fi</sup>tting occurs, such as type of organization, type of job, situational dynamics or individual characteristics. Although some forms of <sup>fi</sup>t have been previously investigated in the IS literature, the subject of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t of IT professionals in general, and especially their P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development, requires some de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the larger context. This review will therefore address the current state of research on IT pro fessionals, and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t in other <sup>fi</sup>elds that may be transferable to the context of IT professionals organizational <sup>fi</sup>t in the context of developing and maintaining technical competencies. A review of Concourse Theory and the Q-methodology literature is in the ‘Methodology’ section.

## Human capital and IT professionals

The authors began this stream of research with strong convictions that the most valuable asset of the IT organization is the IT professionals themselves. Our beliefs were rooted in both practice, from our previous experiences in management, and in theory, most especially the ‘human capital perspective’, which proposes that the human resource is a primary strategic asset of the firm, and therefore, as a primary source of value. it must be managed accordingly. For example Buller and McEvoy (2012) define human capital to include the knowledge, skills and abilities (KSAs) of the <sup>fi</sup>rmˈs workforce, and theorize that P–O <sup>fi</sup>t and its associated positive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are a potentially sustainable source of human capital, and therefore should be managed as a strategic asset. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT professionals, as a source of human capital, provide the greatest value to the <sup>fi</sup>rm by their KSAs that pertain to the <sup>fi</sup>rmˈs information systems, and therefore the maintenance and development of IT professionalsˈ KSAs becomes a strategic activity of the <sup>fi</sup>rmˈs management. With these beliefs as guideposts, we began an investigation on the drivers of value in human capital among IT professionals.

## Knowledge, skills and abilities (KSAs) of IT professionals

Of all the many characteristics and attributes of the IT profession, perhaps the most distinctive pertain to the set of KSAs that are mastered by IT professionals. This line of research is characterized by a high degree of pragmatic relevance because it is driven by current trends in the IT workforce. Lomerson and Pollacia (2006) and Pollacia and Russell (2007) exemplify this type of research, in their studies about how college studentsˈ knowledge of IS careers affect enrollments in IS programs. Similarly, Aasheim et al. (2009) and Boyle and Strong (2006) both address the labor market demands for IT skills of college graduates.

The dynamics of IT KSAs and their association with career paths and career stages has expanded another line of inquiry on the topic of IT competencies. Competency, like the KSAs themselves, is a multi-faceted construct that is sensitive to the context of organization, job and local dynamics (Basselier et al., 2003: Basselier & Benbasat. 2004: Schambach & Blanton 2002). Motivation to update plays a critical role in the IT professionalˈs development and maintenance of competency (Couger & Zawacki, 1978; Kollman et al., 2009; Schambach & Blanton, 2002; Wingreen & Blanton, 2007), which then leads to engagement in training and development activities (Schambach & Blanton, 2002).

## Assessment of IT training and development needs

The management of IT training and development is the subject of a growing stream of IS research that is being fed both by growing interests among researchers about how IT profes sionals acquire and maintain professional skills and competencies (Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002; Lohman, 2009; Major et al., 2007), and by practitioners interested in the management and development of the <sup>fi</sup>rmˈs internal IT skill portfolio (Pfeffer, 1994). This is relevant because IT training and development is the process associated with the individualˈs maintenance and development of technical competency (Schambach & Blanton, 2002).

These concerns have led to the production of some useful tools for the assessment of IT training and development. For example, researchers and practitioners have developed instru mentation for the organizational Technical Updating Climate (Farr et al., 1980; Kozlowski & Hults, 1987), frameworks for assessment (Rowe, 1995; Mirabile, 1991) and inventories of skills necessary for regional economic development (AlignMark, 2000). The Technical Updating Climate assessment is of particular interest for its identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, de<sup>fi</sup>nition and validation of speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of the organizational climate that directly affect the development and updating of technical personnel: supervisor support, information exchange between colleagues, support for professional updating and development, organizational innovation policy and challenging job assignments (Kozlowski & Hults, 1987). The literature on IT professionals, their KSAs, training and development assessment is also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because it represents the body of discourse on the subject, and as such forms a sort of ‘ground state’ for the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t.

## Management strategies for IT professionals and the IT workforce

Innovative and creative management strategies have become necessary on account of the unique, dynamic nature of the IT profession. Successful management strategies tend to focus on <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital, and are also known to affect innovative work behavior, reduce turnover, increase organizational commitment (OC) and retention, and lengthen the average tenure of IT professionals (Newton et al., 2008; Agarwal & Ferratt, 2002; Ferratt et al., 2005; Joseph et al., 2007). Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital is also related to certain types of KSAs more strongly than others (Lee & Wingreen, 2010).

Because by de<sup>fi</sup>nition <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital may only be developed over time once an employee is within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, this line of research is a natural companion to the research on retention-turnover and commitment, IT career paths and in particular the <sup>fi</sup>rmˈs establishmen of internal career paths for IT professionals. Management strategies that favor the development of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital prioritize the ‘industrial strategy’ of establishing internal career paths over the ‘craft strategy’, because the industrial strategy cultivates greater loyalty and commitment to the <sup>fi</sup>rm whereas the craft strategy cultivates more commitment to the profession (Ang & Slaughter, 2001; Bullen et al., 2007). Management strategy also directly affects job search behaviors of IT professionals, and therefore has a direct effect on <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital (Ferratt et al., 2012). Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital strategies that emphasize internal career paths result in lower turnover (Ferratt et al., 2005), which by logical extension satis<sup>fi</sup>es the need for lengthier organizational tenure that is required to develop <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital. Accordingly, various ‘human capital’ variables such as gender, age and length of tenure are correlated with the development of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital (Levina & Xin, 2007).

Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital is proprietary in that a <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot duplicate the <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c capital of its competitors, but must develop its own. As a proprietary asset, therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital is a source of competitive advantage in much the same way as proprietary technol ogy is a source of competitive advantage (e.g. Carr, 2003). To the extent that the development of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital is predicated on OC and lengthy organizational tenure, and is a source of competitive advantage when successful, so human capital theory occupies an important place in the body of research on the topic of management strategies for IT professionals.

## Person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t and the IT professional

Realizing the need to account for processes that operate over long periods of time, we then adopted and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the existing theory of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, renaming the process, ‘P–O <sup>fi</sup>tting’, to emphasize the temporal, dynamic qualities of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). Our theory in cludesthetraditionalconceptualizationof‘P–O<sup>fi</sup>t’asastateof<sup>fi</sup>tbetweenpersonandorganization at the time the measurement is taken, but we also include dynamic cognitive processes that unfold iteratively over time (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). We theorized that the value of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital should improve over time, as the personˈs organizational <sup>fi</sup>t improves through the dynamic process of ‘P–O <sup>fi</sup>tting’, over the length of their organizational tenure, and that P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is, in fact, a direct manifestation of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007).

P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is the extent to which the priorities of the person are aligned with the priorities of the organization. IT professionals are unique in their burden to continually develop and maintain a variety of technological KSAs (Schambach & Blanton, 2002; Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). For IT professionals, therefore, their efforts to maintain good organizational <sup>fi</sup>t with regard to IT KSAs are a manifestation of their desire to cultivate <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital.

## Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital and person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t

By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital may only be developed within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, and may not be hired, borrowed, or imported from the labor market, or from competitors. Therefore, only the <sup>fi</sup>rm ˈs internal processes are directly responsible for the cultivation of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital. P–O <sup>fi</sup>t involves the personˈs direct, active engagement with their organization, and is therefore a primary source for the development of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital, because it is dependent on the extent to which persons must actively align their own values with those of the organization in order to achieve ‘good’ P–O <sup>fi</sup>t (e.g. Buller & McEvoy, 2012). Therefore, a measure of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t must also be a measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital. This concept is suggested by recent research (Wei, 2015), which has demonstrated that employees who are high in general (as opposed to <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c) human capital factors are more likely to have turnover intentions – which is a negative <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital factor – but this relationship is moderated by good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, which weakens the effect. Or, to put it another way, employees who have good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t are less likely to be motivated by general human capital factors with regard to their turnover intentions. Because turnover intentions are negatively related to OC, which is a positive <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital factor, then we may also theorize that the same employees are richer in <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital factors on account of their good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t.

The existing P–O <sup>fi</sup>t literature has demonstrated that employees, through the experience of ‘socialization’ become increasingly more well-adapted to their organizations over time (Cable & Parsons, 2001; Riordan et al., 2001). The concept of socialization is relevant because it acknowledges that P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is a dynamic process that unfolds over time (Van Maanen, 1978), a fact that makes socialization a key tool for managers who wish to cultivate <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital (Klein et al., 2015; Coff & Kryscynski, 2011). The dynamic aspect of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is importan for this paper because some research on socialization suggests that employees in high-skil positions may be especially susceptible to poor <sup>fi</sup>t in the area of training and development (Riordan et al., 2001; Delobbe & Vandenberghe, 2001). The concept of socialization, however, is weak at best in its ability to explain IT professionalsˈ pursuit of training and development. In our previous theoretical research, we propose that the pursuit of technical training and develop ment opportunities is the means by which IT professionals maintain P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with regard to their technical competency (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007).

## Social cognitive foundation of person–organization Fit

The need for our theory to account both for social adaptation and the acquisition and ongoing maintenance of technical competency led us to adopt Social Cognitive Theory as a foundation, because it is often used in the context of social learning, and ‘mastery modeling’ for the acquisition of technical skills. For a full review of the previous literature, we refer the reader to our theoretical research (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007), the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant aspects of which we will also summarize in this section. Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1997) proposes dynamic behavioral processes that unfold and develop over time through a process known as ‘mastery modeling’. Mastery modeling refers to how individuals master behavior by means of a concept-matching process that resembles the core principles of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t in the wider literature (Wood & Bandura, 1989). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a personˈs subjective <sup>fi</sup>t assessment involves a dynamic comparison between his or her own needs and values and the perceived needs and values of the organization (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). SCT is relevant to the context of this study both because it de<sup>fi</sup>nes competencies broadly enough to include the types of skills and behaviors that are of interest to IT professionals and its proposition that P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is a dynamic and subjec tive phenomenon. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, SCT de<sup>fi</sup>nes competency to include ‘patterns of competencies and behavior related to the ‘upgrading of skills [to keep pace] with rapid social and technologica advances’ (Bandura, 1997, p. 16 & pp. 36 ff.).

P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is affected by employer and employee selection factors at the pre-entry and early stages of employment (Kristof, 1996; Riordan et al., 2001), and affected by individual socialization and other related factors that take time to develop in the continuing and ongoing stages of employment (Cable & Parsons, 2001; Riordan et al., 2001; OˈReilly et al., 1991). Because <sup>fi</sup>rmspeci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital may only develop over extended periods of time while the employee remains in the same <sup>fi</sup>rm, it is the factors that operate in the latter stages of employment tha are most relevant in the context of this research. SCT has been used as a foundation by which to predict that OC is a positive outcome of good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, because of its associated social components and cognitive evaluations, and the time periods required to develop beliefs about OC (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007).

Different ‘types’ of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t are also manifested, which are dependent on both the context of the situation and the individualˈs subjective interpretation of the context (Kristof, 1996). Supplementary–complementary <sup>fi</sup>t is the degree to which <sup>fi</sup>t is either based on the similarities between the person and organization, or whether the person <sup>fi</sup>lls a gap in the organization, or ‘complements’ it. Needs–supplies <sup>fi</sup>t is the extent to which the needs of either person or orga nization are supplied by the other. Demands–abilities <sup>fi</sup>t pertains to the degree to which the personˈs abilities satisfy the organizationˈs demands (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). All of these types of <sup>fi</sup>t arise from the substrate of the personˈs subjective assessments of the context in which they occur.

## Subjective vs. objective paradigm for person–organization <sup>fi</sup>t

The ‘objective’ and ‘subjective’ paradigms are typically conceived as being at opposite ends of a philosophical continuum (Gioia & Pitre, 1990; Kuhn, 1962). Both objective and subjective paradigms accept the existence of a ‘reality’ that may be observed. However, at one end of the continuum, the objective paradigm assumes that the reality is external, consistent between observers, observable and measurable with reproducible methods. This ‘objective’ external reality is assumed to be the same for all observers, and therefore, all observers using the same methods should be able to reproduce or replicate the observations of others. At the other end of the continuum, the subjective paradigm assumes that observations and measurements are subject to the observation itself, and the observerˈs own ‘internal’ reality, and are therefore unique to the observerˈs perspective. The subjective observations and measurements of multiple observers may agree, although that does not alter their fundamentally subjective quality. In other words, there is no ‘threshold’ beyond which a set of subjective observations becomes ‘objective’ by the inclusion of additional observations. In the context of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, the subjective paradigm accounts for why two IT professionals, with all other factors being equal – with the same KSAs, quali<sup>fi</sup>cations, training, work environment, salary, nature of work and so on – may yet report radically different P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, because each IT professional operates according to their own, unique, internal ‘theory’ of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t.

Although subjective evaluations, known in SCT as self-evaluations, undergird the core processes of SCTand P–O <sup>fi</sup>t (Wood & Bandura, 1989; Wingreen & Blanton, 2007), the subjectivity of a personˈs <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions has been problematic, because research tends to operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with objective measurements and analytical techniques (OˈReilly et al., 1991; Kristof, 1996; Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). On account of the variety of IT jobs, careers and roles in the IT profession, and following previous research (Prasad et al., 2007), we do not theorize a singular P–O <sup>fi</sup>t construct for IT professionals. The concept of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is rich enough to accommodate multiple ‘types’ of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t for IT professionals, but methodologies and measurements have not yet been developed and deployed to properly operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t theory in the subjective paradigm. In the context of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t research, this implies that a theory of measurement should be integrated with a theory of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, creating a cohesive whole that is capable of accommodating subjective <sup>fi</sup>t perceptions on their own terms, rather than forcing them into an ‘objective’ research paradigm.

## METHODS

At this stage, we realized the need for a theory of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with an integrated theory of measurement, which was capable of operationalizing the personˈs subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t beliefs across a rich and deep <sup>fi</sup>eld of personal and organizational issues. Yet, the methods of assessment used in the literature tend to be relatively simplistic in their approach and do not account well for either the subjectivity of the individualˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t assessment or the multitude of factors involved in IT training and development. Specifically. the dominant methodological paradigm assumes: (1) a-prior assumptions about what to expect when measurements are taken, (2) the independence of measurement error, (3) between-subject independence (measurement of one subject does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the others) and (4) measurements are objective rather than subjective. The dominant ‘objective’ paradigm is also characterized by sacri<sup>fi</sup>ces in phenomenological richness in order to favor theoretical and methodological precision. This poses some serious drawbacks because previous research indicates that this kind of assessment, when applied to IT training and devel opment factors should violate the assumptions of the dominant methodological paradigm.

In some previous research, we adopted Q-methodology in order to test its adequacy to operationalize the subjective beliefs of IT professionals as they pertain to training and develop ment (Wingreen & Blanton, 2006; Wingreen et al., 2009). Our initial research was successful; therefore, the Q-methodology was adopted and used in accordance with the goals of this research to operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t for IT professionalsˈ training and development priorities.

## Q-methodology and IS research

Q methodology has been rarely used in previous IS research. A tutorial on the use of q-sorting (Thomas & Watson, 2002) illustrated the procedure by obtaining Q-sorts on MIS faculty beliefs about how to train MIS Ph.D. students. Q methodology has also been used to examine competencies of software engineers (Turley & Bieman, 1995), organizational subcultures and their relationship to DSS user satisfaction (Kendall et al., 1987), process improvement (Ponsignon et al., 2014), search engine interface development (Meloche & Cheng, 2005), wik development (Hasan et al., 2007) and the effective use of metaphors in the language of systems development (Kendall & Kendall, 1993).

## Concourse Theory

Stephenson (1986b) relies on the philosophy of William James (1891) to develop Concourse Theory. James (1891) proposes that the cognitive process consists of transitory and substantive components, and that these components are complements to one another. James compares a personˈs cognitive process to a bird <sup>fl</sup>ying between perches – the bird is either <sup>fl</sup>ying or perched, but both are complementary elements of the same process; in the same manner, the personˈs transitory thought process periodically perches on substantive thoughts. This also happens to be a very good way to describe the dynamic cognitive evaluation process of Social Cognitive Theory, which proposes that the personˈs dynamic P–O <sup>fi</sup>t evaluation process is punctuated by cognitive behaviors, such as the concept matching, cognitive representation and vicarious rehearsal processes, all of which bear a strong resemblance to the substantive thoughts proposed by Concourse Theory (Wingreen & Blanton, 2007). In the context of this paper, the transitory component of the cognitive process is functionally equivalent to the cognitive evaluation process of Social Cognitive Theory that the individual engages to form P–O <sup>fi</sup>t evaluations, and the substantive component is equivalent to operant subjective <sup>fi</sup>t assessment. This makes Concourse Theory and Q-Methodology an ideal choice in situations governed by Social Cognitive Theory in general, and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t evaluation in particular.

A concourse is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the universe of statements that comprise a domain of interest (Stephenson, 1986a). A representative set of Q-statements is then derived directly from the concourse on the subject of interest. It is not necessary that Q-statements represent every aspect and nuance of a domain, only that the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of statements is a representative cross section of the discourse on the domain (Stephenson, 1953; Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980). Statements are selected by various means intended to assure the representativeness of the set of Q-statements – literature review, expert feedback, small group discussion on the domain of the concourse and others (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980). Hence, the set of Q-statements represent the concourse in the same way that a representative sample of people represents an entire population of people.

Previous research has tended to implement the q-sort and q-factor analysis, while downplaying or ignoring the central signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of Concourse Theory in the analysis and interpretation. Because Concourse Theory provides a philosophical framework for the interpretation of q-sorts and q-factor analysis, it is necessary to apply its principles in order to avoid potentia threats to the interpretation of the results (Klaus et al., 2010). In the context of this study, it wil be implemented by observing patterns of complementarity that are manifested through the q-factor analysis. Concourse Theory also leads us to anticipate the richness of the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t phenomenon to be revealed through the richness of the IT training and development environment, and revealed by means of the q-sort and q-factor analysis.

## Q-sort

Philosophically, the Q-sort is the substantive artifact of a transitory and dynamic cognitive process, and therefore supports the individualˈs subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t evaluation. For these reasons, Concourse Theory and its associated methods and measurements are appropriate both to operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development and to identify a typology of IT professionals’ P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development.

The individual prioritizes the Q-statements by sorting them into categories that approximate a normal distribution, with fewer statements sorted into the extreme categories and more in the central categories. Because all statements are sorted relative to one another, any given Q-sor represents the personˈs coherent point-of-view on the concourse. By making value judgments about which statements are more important than others, the personˈs subjective viewpoint on the concourse is operationalized. Statements are sorted <sup>fi</sup>rst into the extreme categories (e.g. most preferable and least preferable) and then into the more moderate and neutral categories (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980).

## Q-factor analysis, statement rankings and factor arrays

Q-factor analysis, a form of centroid factor analysis, is then used to form groups or types of peo ple based on their naturally existing subjectivity (point-of-view, perspective, etc.) by correlating different peoplesˈ Q-sorts with each other (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980; Stephenson, 1986c). Q-factor analysis provides the same information as other forms of factor analysis, except that the subject of analysis is the people rather than the variables. Factor loadings refer to a personˈs association with the factors, and factors represent ‘types’ of perspectives held by the people in the study. A q-factor analysis also reports a ‘factor array’, which consists of the statement rankings that de<sup>fi</sup>ne any given factor. Where the research is concerned with the association of a person with a factor, the individual factor loadings are used. When the research is concerned with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and interpretation of a factor, the factor arrays and associated statement rankings are used.

## Pilot study and instrument development

The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a concourse, development of the set of Q-statements for IT training and development, instrument development, and measurement procedures followed the recommendations of previous research on the subject (Stephenson, 1953; Stephenson, 1986a; Stephenson, 1986b; Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980; and Thomas & Watson, 2002). Whereas in r-methodological techniques, the goal is to obtain a representative sample of a population in order to make generalizations about the population from a set of variables, in Q-methodology, the goal is to obtain a representative sample of the concourse in order to make generalizations about the concourse from a set of people. The highest probability of achieving a representative sample of the concourse is achieved by a review of the literature, conversations and interviews with people who participate in the concourse, and input from domain experts about the content of the sample of q-statements (Brown, 1980; Dennis, 1988). These guidelines were ful<sup>fi</sup>lled in this study by generating q-statements through the course of a three-phase pilot procedure that included (1) literature review, expert panel discussion, (2) focus group discussion about potential items to include in the set of q-statements and (3) data collection in a small (n < 30) group. This procedure yielded a set of statements that pertain to a variety of training and development concerns, such as resources available for training and development, content areas and venues for the delivery of training and development.

For this study, q-sorts were obtained from study participants for both personal and perceived organizational preferences for IT training and development. To operationalize P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with regard to IT training and development, the Q-sorts for personal preferences and perceived organizational preferences were then combined into a single distribution for each person which was then subject to a Q-factor analysis. The combining of two separate q-sorts into a single distribution forces the inclusion of equal numbers of person and organization Q-sort items in each category of the distribution, and is a type of structured procedure performed in previous research with Q-sorts (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, combining both person and organization Q-sorts is operationally equivalent to asking the person to sort equal numbers of person and organization statements into each category o the Q-sort distribution. Because the participants both observed and measured their own person and organization priorities, the demands of the subjective paradigm are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, and it is in this manner that we operationalize the subjective measurement of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with an integrated theory of measurement.

## A note on the status of reliability and validity in Q-methodology

Reliability and validity do not have the same status in Q-methodology as they do in r-methodological research. In r-methodological research, where objectivity is highly valued, objective measurements are critical to accomplish the goals of the research, and therefore the measurements must be error-free (reliable), and must really measure the thing that they purport to measure (valid). In Q-methodology, because analysis of the personˈs subjective viewpoint is the goal; reliability and validity apply to the person rather than the measurements. The same questions about reliability and validity simply have no meaning when applied to the personˈs subjective viewpoint (Dennis, 1988). By what standard shall we validate a personˈs subjective viewpoint, and what does it mean to ask whether a personˈs subjective viewpoin is ‘error-free’? It has been suggested that if a personˈs subjective viewpoint is by de<sup>fi</sup>nition valid, and because there can be no validity without reliability, a demonstration of the former is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to establish the latter (Lincoln and Guba, 1985).

Notwithstanding, there are some things Q-methodologists observe in the interest of ‘due diligence’. There is a kind of response bias that is peculiar to Q-methodology in which the respondent sorts the q-statements in the order they were presented. This criterion identi<sup>fi</sup>ed n = 1 Q-sort that was correlated $\mathsf { r } > 0 . 9 5$ with the order in which the statements were given, which was subseguently removed from the data set. Another kind of bias peculiar to P-O fi research occurs when the respondent rates the organizationˈs priorities as identical to their own, which in the context of this study is an indication that the respondent was not thoughtfully engaged with the q-statements while performing their q-sort. This kind of response, although theoretically possible, would be exceedingly rare if the respondents were thoughtfully observing the q-sort procedure, and the probability of there being more than one response like this in a data set of $n = 2 9 8$ is exceedingly small. This criterion identi<sup>fi</sup>ed $\mathsf { n } = 1 9$ responses that were cor related $\mathsf { r } > 0 . 9 5$ , which were subsequently removed from further consideration. Removing these q-sorts from the analysis poses a minimal threat to the validity of the results because by de<sup>fi</sup>nition they cannot belong to the q-factors (types) that already exist in the data. In r-correlational factor analysis, this would be analogous to removing a variable from the study – as long as there are other variables representing the factor, or if the removed variable does not represent any existing factor, the validity of the result is unaffected.

## ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION

Q-factor analysis, unlike other, more familiar forms of factor analysis, creates factors consisting of people rather than measured variables, by operating on correlations between persons Q-sorts. The analysis, as such, involves an interpretation of the results, in this case the identi <sup>fi</sup>cation of a typology of IT professionalsˈ P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with IT training and development.

Q-factor analysis solutions. however, are analyzed and interpreted in similar fashion to other forms of factor analysis. Like other forms of factor analysis, Q-factor analysis is indeterminate, which means there are an in<sup>fi</sup>nite number of solutions that may produce the same statistics. This requires the analyst also to consider the interpretability of the solution in terms of common sense. In other words, the factors must be both interpretable and statistically sensible. In practice, this means that the analyst seeks a solution that maximizes both statistical parsimony and explanatory power, and passes the test of common sense.

Using these guidelines, a <sup>fi</sup>ve-factor solution was found that explains 35% of the variation in the data. The correlations between factors were all low $( - 0 . 3 5 < \mathsf { r } < 0 . 3 5 )$ , which indicates that the factors are statistically discriminant. As we will see in the ensuing discussion, the factors are also interpretable, which is a desirable characteristic in Q-methodological studies (Brown, 1980).

The discussion and accompanying analysis will focus on the types of IT professionals with regard to their subjective <sup>fi</sup>t, or lack thereof, in the domain of IT training and development. Good <sup>fi</sup>t will be judged by an assessment of the extent to which personal and organizational preferences for technical training and development received similar high rankings (e.g., if both were ranked in the top third of the concourse). Consequently, poor <sup>fi</sup>t will be judged by an assessment of the extent to which personal and organizational preferences received opposite rankings (e.g., one in the top third and one in the bottom third of the concourse). The subject of the discussion will be the reports of individual factor scores (Table 1) and statement rankings with z-scores for each P–O <sup>fi</sup>t type (Table 2). These reports are standard output of the Q-factor analysis procedure used by the PQMethod software.

## Interpretation of factors as types of IT training and development P–O <sup>fi</sup>t

Table 2 reports the statement rankings for each factor with associated z-scores for each state ment. The rankings and z-scores are indicators of the priority of each statement for each P–O <sup>fi</sup>t type. In the following discussion, values in parentheses represent the rankings for both personal and organizational preferences for each respective training and development issue (person ranking/organization ranking). Because the most salient information is contained in the extremes of the Q-sort distribution (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980), interpretation of the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types will focus <sup>fi</sup>rst on the training and development factors given the highest priorities, with particular focus on good <sup>fi</sup>t (high person and high organization priorities) and poor <sup>fi</sup>t (high person/low organization, or low person/high organization priorities). Priorities near the middle of the distribution are not as salient (Nunnally, 1978; Brown, 1980), and will only enter into the discussion to the extent that the person places a high priority on a statement while the organization does not (e.g. neutral priority), and vice-versa. This study will adopt the rule-ofthumb that the 18 statements ranked in the top and bottom 1/3 of the distribution will contain enough information to interpret each respective type. Supplementary–complementary <sup>fi</sup>t wil be evaluated by whether or not the <sup>fi</sup>t supplements the needs of the organization, as evidenced by good <sup>fi</sup>t with a positive <sup>fi</sup>t trend, or complementary if the <sup>fi</sup>t trend is reported as good despite there being a lack of <sup>fi</sup>t in evidence. Demands–abilities <sup>fi</sup>t will be assessed by evaluating whether the <sup>fi</sup>t type is represented primarily by good <sup>fi</sup>t in the area of KSA development. Needs–supplies <sup>fi</sup>t will be evaluated by determining whether or not the <sup>fi</sup>t type is represented primarily by good <sup>fi</sup>t in the area of training and development resource needs and provisions.

## Type 1: software development with poor organizational support

Type 1 P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is characterized primarily by good demands-abilities <sup>fi</sup>t in the areas of: programming languages (1/2), Development tools/applications (3/8) and Databases (4/5). Type 1 <sup>fi</sup>t is also characterized by poor <sup>fi</sup>t in the areas of: time off from work for training (7/50), adequate funding for training (9/44). The lack of <sup>fi</sup>t appears to be primarily associated with a needssupplies <sup>fi</sup>t type. Type 1 IT professionals have the training priorities of software development with poor organizational support for training and development. It is interesting to note that members of this type place low priority on the development of IT management skills, while their employers prioritize management skills highly, which may be a manifestation of complementary <sup>fi</sup>t, and therefore desirable.

## Type 2: system engineering and architecture with fair organizational Fit

Type 2 IT professionals have a perspective that may be generally characterized as system architecture and engineering (on account of the deeper technical priorities than Type 1) with generally good organizational <sup>fi</sup>t with regard to training and development. Like Type 1, Type 2 P–O <sup>fi</sup>t is also characterized by good <sup>fi</sup>t in the areas of: programming languages (5/1), Development tools/applications (3/2), operating systems (6/7) and data storage (10/8), all of which justify the higher career classi<sup>fi</sup>cation as engineers and architects by comparison to Type 1 IT professionals. Type 4 IT professionals have training and development priorities for project management with good overall <sup>fi</sup>t. Although they experience poor <sup>fi</sup>t only with regard to self-training/tutorials/ manuals (41/16), they generally experience only a moderate lack-of <sup>fi</sup>t in other areas. Type 4 <sup>fi</sup>t is characterized by good demands-abilities <sup>fi</sup>t for IT training and development in IT management skills (1/3) and systems analysis and design (2/4), and needs–supplies <sup>fi</sup>t with develop ment tools/applications (6/11). Type 5 IT professionals experience good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t only with regard to training and development for: telecommunications (3/14) and IT management skills (18/15), both of which represent demands–abilities <sup>fi</sup>t. Type 5 <sup>fi</sup>t conveys the general impression of IT profes sionals whose employers are making con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands on their career development, which would also imply a poor overall needs–supplies <sup>fi</sup>t.

## Post-hoc analysis of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types

A post-hoc analysis was undertaken to examine the relationship of the <sup>fi</sup>ve P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types to various human capital variables that have been of interest to previous research, and a measurement of the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend. P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend consisted of a survey item that asked the respondents to evaluate whether their overall organizational <sup>fi</sup>t was getting better or worse. P–O correlation is the correlation between the personˈs reported person and organization q-sorts, meaning that a 1.0 is a perfect correlation between person and organization q-sorts (items for person and organization are sorted in the same order), and 1.0 is a perfect negative correlation (items for organization are sorted in reverse order of the person sorts). OC was measured using the 15OCQ scale, and in theory, to the extent that OC measures the commitment of employees to their organization, also is an indicator of human capital. Table 3 reports the corre lations $( \mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ between age, gender $( \mathsf { f } = 1 , \mathsf { m } = 0 )$ , years with employer, years in position, years in <sup>fi</sup>eld, and ‘goodness’ of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, and factor loadings for Types 1 – 5. Because Q-methodology assumes a heterogeneous, rather than a homogeneous population, the post-hoc analysis is expected to reveal unique relationships between each P–O <sup>fi</sup>t type and human capital variables.

As expected, Types 1 – 5 demonstrate that each P–O <sup>fi</sup>t type possesses a unique perspective that is related to human capital characteristics, and is useful for differentiating it from other P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types. Gender (r = 0.206, f = 1, m = 0), P–O correlation $( \mathsf { r } = - 0 . 1 7 9 )$ and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend $( \ r _ { \ r } = \ r _ { - } 0 . 1 5 4 )$ are correlated with Type 1 <sup>fi</sup>t, software development with poor organizational support. Gender (r = 0.149, f = 1, m = 0), years with employer $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { r } } = - 0 . 1 8 7 )$ , and years in <sup>fi</sup>eld $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { r } } = - 0 . 1 6 1 )$ are all negatively correlated with Type 2 <sup>fi</sup>t, system engineering and architecture with good organizational <sup>fi</sup>t. Age and years in position are negatively correlated (r = 0.163 and 0.222, respectively), OC is positively correlated (r = 0.240), P–O correlation is positively correlated (r = 0.283) and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend is positively correlated $( \mathsf { r } = 0 . 1 8 5 )$ with Type 4 <sup>fi</sup>t, project management with fair P–O <sup>fi</sup>t. Age and years with employer are both positively correlated (r = 0.189 and 0.220, respectively), and P–O correlation is negatively correlated (r = 0.152) with Type 5 <sup>fi</sup>t, telecommunications management with poor <sup>fi</sup>t. The negative trend of P–O correlation associated with Types 1 and 5 appears to support the notion that their generally poor <sup>fi</sup>t is attributable to a lack of supplementary <sup>fi</sup>t.

In summary, the <sup>fi</sup>ve IT training and development P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types were both interpretable and were distinguishable with regard to their unique correlations with human capital variables and self-reported P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend. These results support an expanded concept of P–O <sup>fi</sup>t in the context of IT training and development, one which acknowledges a variety of unique IT training and development types and their separate relationships with various human capital factors.

## P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types and human capital

Previous research on P–O <sup>fi</sup>t has approached the phenomenon from an objective perspective that does not account for either the subjective nature of the individualˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t evaluation or whether groups of individuals share the same subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t perspective. The <sup>fi</sup>ve subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types revealed by this study demonstrate that IT professionalsˈ P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with training and development is not an objective, one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all phenomenon, but rather a subjective and dynamic phenomenon that is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by many personal, organizational and career factors.

## Gender and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t

Type 1, ‘software development with poor organizational support’, and Type 2 <sup>fi</sup>t, ‘system engineering and architecture with fair organizational <sup>fi</sup>t’, are the only types in this study tha are correlated with gender – females exhibit a higher af<sup>fi</sup>nity for Type 1 than males, and males exhibit a higher af<sup>fi</sup>nity for Type 2. However, Type 1 is also negatively correlated with P–O correlation, and P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend, indicating that the greater the correspondence between person and organizational q-sorts, and the more positive the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend, the less likely a person is to be Type 1. Whereas, Type 2 is not correlated with either P–O correlation or P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding may give a clue as to the meaning of previous research, which found that women had fewer opportunities to interact with their peers in the organization than men, and were, on average, younger and less experienced than their male peers (Igbaria & Chidambaram, 1997). Good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t requires the personˈs continual engagement with the work environment, including oneˈs coworkers, and if it is true that women have fewer opportunities to interact with their coworkers, then it should not be a surprise that it is also related to poor <sup>fi</sup>t, as we observe in Type 1. Type 2 <sup>fi</sup>t is similar to Type 1, for its emphasis on software development, but unlike Type 1, there is no evidence of poor <sup>fi</sup>t. Once again, it follows to reason that, if it is true that male employees have more opportunities to interact with their co-workers, then we would expect thei <sup>fi</sup>t to be better, by comparison, as we observe in Type 2.

It is also possible that for Type 1, a poor <sup>fi</sup>t trend enhances the perceptions of poor organizational support, or vice-versa. In other words, there may be an interaction effect between <sup>fi</sup>t trend and beliefs about organizational support. Either way, it is sensible that the two should be correlated.

## Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital indicators: organizational commitment, P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend and P–O correlation

Type 4 (project management with good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t) is the only type that is positively correlated with OC (r = 0.240), P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend (r = 0.283) and P–O correlation (r = 0.185). This makes Type 4 the most well-<sup>fi</sup>t and most-committed employees in this study, and are therefore deserving of some attention. However, these relationships weaken over time, as evidenced by Type 4ˈs negative correlations with age (r = 0.163) and years in position (r = 0.222). This may be an indication that, as Type 4 IT professionals advance in their training and development, they transition to another type. The negative correlation with age may be indicative that Type 4 is populated by aspiring, younger IT professionals, who wish to acquire a richer skill set that will allow them to advance into a project management position.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding may also provide some insight into previous research, which suggests that employees in high-skill positions are more susceptible to poor <sup>fi</sup>t, whereas employees in lower-skill positions are not as vulnerable to poor <sup>fi</sup>t (Riordan et al., 2001; Delobbe & Vandenberghe, 2001). In other words, if Type 4 are early-career, and low-skill employees who are in the beginning stages of KSA development and acquisition, then they are more likely to exhibit good <sup>fi</sup>t than their more-skilled peers, as we do, in fact observe for Type 4. More data is necessary to demonstrate the dynamics involved for Type 4, and this is an opportunity for future research.

Type 4 seems to be the kind of employee who behaves as the theories in the empirical, objective paradigm would predict. It is not surprising to <sup>fi</sup>nd such ‘theoretical exemplars’ in a study, as we have, but we are also reminded that Type 4 is only one of <sup>fi</sup>ve types we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Firm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital indicators: age, years with employer, years in position, years in <sup>fi</sup>eld

If Type 4 is deserving of attention because they are the only type in this study that exhibits good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t, as measured by P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend, and P–O correlation, and most-committed, then the op posite is true for all the other types. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, why is there only one type of IT professiona that exhibits good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with training and development priorities, and that it seems to occur more often among younger, inexperienced IT professionals? Types 2 and 3 do not exhibit good <sup>fi</sup>t, neither do they exhibit poor <sup>fi</sup>t. However, Type 1 exhibits poor <sup>fi</sup>t, as measured by P–O <sup>fi</sup>t trend and P–O correlation, and Type 5 exhibits poor <sup>fi</sup>t as measured by P–O correlation, but their <sup>fi</sup>t improves with age, and years with employer, as evidenced by the positive correlations with Type 5 <sup>fi</sup>t.

High turnover and short-lived job tenure are grim realities of the IT profession that are wellknown and well-documented in the research. In this study, the ‘average’ IT professional has changed jobs 2.5 times since <sup>fi</sup>nishing college, or once every 2.7 years (n = 216). Furthermore, IT professionals are not only prone to change jobs more frequently, but also to change careers entirely, because of the effects of workload and work exhaustion on OC, and turnover intentions (Armstrong et al., 2015). Burnout, job dissatisfaction and the need to maintain general technical competencies, a form of general human capital, to preserve the IT professionalˈs value on the job market are the primary drivers of career transition (Joia & Mangia, 2015). This suggests that one explanation for the scarcity of good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t in this study might be attributable to the fastpaced, high demands that IT jobs place on IT professionals to maintain their KSAs. Furthermore, IT professionals who are continually engaged in general skill development are cultivating general human capital, rather than <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital, and are therefore more prone to turnover, as demonstrated by previous research (Wei, 2015). This may also explain why Types 1, 2, 3 and 5 exhibit fair and poor <sup>fi</sup>t – they simply do not remain with their employers long enough to generate the <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital necessary to maintain good P–O <sup>fi</sup>t. Type 5, however, may require a caveat, because, although their <sup>fi</sup>t is generally poor, as evidenced by a negative P–O correlation, this effect may be moderated by age and years with employer, which are positively correlated with Type 5. More data will be required to understand the dynamics of Type 5 <sup>fi</sup>t.

Because IT careers unfold and change over time, and this study has only taken a snap: shot of the IT workforce at one point in time, we are not able to account for the movement of IT professionals between Types 1 and 5 over time. Age, years in position, years in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and years with employer only give us the likelihood that at an IT professional might be found in any given type at this point in time. Previous research has found that the careers of IT professionals unfold in predictable sequences over time. For example, research has demonstrated the patterns of how IT professionals enter, and leave the profession, and the duration of their tenure in each stage of their career (Joseph et al., 2012), so it stands to reason that we should predict movement between Types 1 and 5 over the course of time also. Indeed, because Types 1 – 5 explain only 35% of the variance in the data, most of the IT professionals in this study do not load signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly on any one of the <sup>fi</sup>ve identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed types, which is an indicator that they may be in transition between two types. More data is necessary to understand the mechanisms behind such movement, and this could become the topic of future research.

## The enigma of Type 3, network management with weak organizational support

Type 3 IT professionals, those who prioritize training for network management, with weak orga nizational support, distinguish themselves by not being associated with any other variables in this study that are theorized to affect <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c human capital. Their <sup>fi</sup>t is neither good nor bad, and temporal variables seem to have no effect on IT professionalsˈ association with Type 3 training and development. In other words, their behavior and priorities seem to be governed by an entirely different theory than the rest. This is neither unusual nor surprising, because the subjective paradigm explicitly assumes that people may behave according to their own in ternal ‘theory’.

One explanation for Type 3 may be that, because network management is associated with infrastructural technologies, from which very little competitive value may be derived (Carr, 2003), perhaps on account of this a resource-based perspective might make more sense of Type 3, than a human capital perspective? In other words, those who prioritize training and development for networks and telecommunications may see themselves, or their parent <sup>fi</sup>rms may see them, more as a resource for their parent <sup>fi</sup>rm, than human capital. In theory, competitive value is derived from proprietary applications of IT (Carr, 2003), which may also include propri etary human capital, but in areas where proprietary value is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, or impossible, as is the case for employees associated with infrastructural technologies, it is more likely that IT profes sionals might successfully govern themselves and their behavior more consistently with a resource-based model of value, than a human capital model.

Type 3 IT professionals serve as an example that demonstrates the value of investigating P– O <sup>fi</sup>t with an integrated system of theory and measurement suited for the subjective paradigm. The existence of Type 3 would be problematic for empirical positivist research within the objec tive paradigm, but adds richness and depth to research within the subjective paradigm, and opens the door to develop a new theory to explain an enigmatic sub-group of the IT workforce.

All of these correlations between subjective training and development <sup>fi</sup>t types and objective human capital factors suggest that there are dynamics underlying an IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t that deserve consideration. When it comes to IT training and development, it seems that human capital factors play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the development of IT professionalsˈ subjective perceptions about their organizational <sup>fi</sup>t. Furthermore, their subjective perceptions form naturally occurring training and development types that are both distinguishable from one another and bear unique relationships with human capital factors.

## Future research

The Q-methodology is capable of moving P–O <sup>fi</sup>t research into a people-centered, subjective realm that cannot be supported by other research methods – a realm that is more appropriate and consistent with the subjective nature of the personˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t assessments. Subsequent research should account for individual subjectivity and build typologies based on the shared subjective viewpoints between people. Future research could address whether the <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>t perspectives are independently correlated with the various outcomes that have been the object of previous research (e.g. career path, nature of work, performance, commitment, turnover, satisfaction, competency, etc.).

The results of such research could serve as an aid for IT managers looking to improve the strategic alignment of their ITworkforce with the goals of the organization. IT managers who are tasked with developing training programs for the organizationˈs ITworkforce may use information from one or more of the <sup>fi</sup>ve P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types as a sort of blueprint for the organizationˈs training programs. IT managers may also use the instrument developed in this study to make their own assessment, which may then be compared with the results of this study, and used for the purposes of decision support and planning. Likewise, individual IT professionals are able to obtain informa tion that may support the planning of their own personal training agenda and career goals.

The Q-methodology is ideally positioned to capture the natural ebb and <sup>fl</sup>ow of the IT profession. The Q-methodologyˈs focus on correlating persons rather than variables makes it appropriate for tracking the dynamics related to how both individual IT professionals and groups of IT professionals navigate the course of their career. Indeed, Concourse Theory predicts that the <sup>fi</sup>ve <sup>fi</sup>t types revealed in this study are in constant <sup>fl</sup>ux as IT professionals independently converge upon the types from their diverse backgrounds, and then diverge to various unexplored outcomes along their chosen career paths. Indeed, because only 35% of the variance is explained in this study, most of the IT professionals in this study are in various states of transition between the <sup>fi</sup>ve types, or are converging to form new types not revealed by this study. Furthermore, we may identify more than <sup>fi</sup>ve types of IT training and development <sup>fi</sup>t, but the subsequent types are expected to be moderately-to-highly correlated with the <sup>fi</sup>ve primary types and be represented by smaller and smaller segments of the population, on account of the heuristic criteria used in this study to select the <sup>fi</sup>ve types. If a more generous heuristic is adopted, we would discover more types, as smaller and smaller sub-types begin to separate themselves from, and IT professionals in transition between types begin to associate themselves with the <sup>fi</sup>ve primary types identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by this research. Given the complexity and richness inherent in the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t phenomenon, the wide range of factors involved in IT training and development, and the number of possible combinations and permutations of rankings that are possible with the Q-statements represented in this study, it is amazing that there are P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types at all – why would we expect to <sup>fi</sup>nd anything given the subjective nature of the topic?

## CONCLUSION

Our research indicates that the P–O <sup>fi</sup>t construct is a complex, multidimensional construct with its underpinnings in the subjectivity of the person. Furthermore, this research demonstrates that subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t beliefs are shared among members of the IT profession, as evidenced by the <sup>fi</sup>ve types of IT training and development <sup>fi</sup>t discovered by this research. With regard to IT training and development <sup>fi</sup>t, there is a clearly distinguished typology of subjective P–O <sup>fi</sup>t that is related to objective human capital variables, and there is reason to believe that other objective measures of the population of IT professionals (e.g. individual differences, attitudes and so on) may also help shed light on the dynamics of the training and development <sup>fi</sup>t.

Concourse Theory and its associated Q-methodology have demonstrated an adequate foundation from which to investigate the subjective and dynamic domain of the IT professionalˈs P–O <sup>fi</sup>t with respect to training and development. Future empirical investigation and theoretical development may be aimed at understanding and developing the unique dynamics that underlie each type. The <sup>fi</sup>ve P–O <sup>fi</sup>t types contain information that should also be of interest to practitioners for the wealth of practical information encompassed by each perspective.
