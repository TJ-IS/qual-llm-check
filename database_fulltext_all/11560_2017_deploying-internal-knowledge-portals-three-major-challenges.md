## A B S T R A C T

Knowledge portals (KPs) are highly integrative knowledge management systems (KMSs) that promise to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of the use of organizational knowledge. KPs have the potential to stimulate the processes of knowledge management and knowledge integration; improve communication and collaboration; and to increase the competitiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, KPs face major challenges in practice, and many KP implementation efforts fail. This paper provides an extensive review of current information system (IS) research on KPs. It focuses on internal KPs and suggests that there are three major deployment challenges: (1) suf<sup>fi</sup>cient participation, (2) a favorable organizational culture, and (3) knowledge integration.

## 1. Introduction

The knowledge-based view of the <sup>fi</sup>rm [81] describes knowledge as a key resource in organizations, suggesting that organizations can be viewed as knowledge systems [2,37,102]. However, as knowledge per se resides solely in the minds of individuals, the knowledge within an organization is highly distributed, often suboptimally allocated, and not readily available where it is needed. The problem of dispersed knowledge suggests the value of knowledge integration (KI), which denotes the combination and systematization of individuals’ knowledge to make it available to others as valuable, context-sensitive knowledge [2]. Grant [42] says that the integration of individuals’ specialized knowledge is the essence of organizational capability.

One way of achieving KI is to deploy a knowledge portal (KP), a type of knowledge management system (KMS) that strives to provide a “one-stop knowledge shop.” KPs are in effect a relatively new class of system, integrating repository and network functionalities. They provide a single point of access to the available knowledge in an organization (or even beyond), reprocessed in such a way that it is useful and applicable for a knowledge- seeking user. Furthermore, they have the potential to stimulate the processes of knowledge management and KI; improve communication and collaboration; and increase the competitiveness of the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

However, many organizations experience dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in obtaining full value from their KP implementations, despite (in some cases) spending millions of dollars on them [27]. A key problem is that KPs pose challenges beyond those in other information systems (ISs) and KMSs in particular, as our literature review below demonstrates. These challenges are related to the different perspectives of stakeholders and the effort of integrating or translating between these perspectives. It seems that knowledge is quite an intractable resource, and implementers of KPs struggle both to get individuals to contribute their knowledge and to provide knowledge seekers with useful reprocessed knowledge.

Given that research on KPs has now been taking place for just over one decade, we believe it is an appropriate time to summarize and capture the current state of the art in IS research as per KPs. Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution of this paper is to provide a review of previous empirical studies of actually implemented KPs (see the Appendices for a complete list). The purpose of this review is to take stock of the current state of the art regarding KPs and identify the most important challenges that organizations face in deploying them. The three major deployment challenges that we identify are research gaps that now need to be <sup>fi</sup>lled. The second contribution is to provide some direction for future research and, in particular, research that addresses the challenges that we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. However, instead of simply suggesting future directions, we actually propose a set of hypotheses that IS researchers might use in their studies. The overall aim is to help organizations in their efforts to deploy internal KPs.

This paper is organized as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne and conceptualize KPs as systems integrating knowledge management and portal functionality. We then identify three main challenges in

We discuss these challenges and provide a set of testable hypotheses about the challenges related to the performance of KPs. The <sup>fi</sup>nal section is the conclusions and implications.

## 2. De<sup>fi</sup>nition and components of KPs

## 2.1. A definition of KPs

Drawing on the KMS literature, knowledge can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a justi<sup>fi</sup>ed belief that potentially increases an entity’s ability to take effective action [1]. In this view, knowledge is possessed and exercised by persons [34] and is derived from <sup>fl</sup>ows of information mentally processed relative to existing beliefs and commitments [75]. It is subjective [33,77]; dynamic [27,38]; not self-contained [102]; socially constructed [1,44,109]; and affective [49,64,66]. Knowledge as de<sup>fi</sup>ned above only exists in an individual’s mind [1], [34]. Knowledge can be decoupled from an individual’s mind in the form of knowledge artifacts that can be physically stored as documents, records, videos, etc. [25,24].

Knowledge management stands for the processes of “identifying and leveraging the collective knowledge in an organization to help the organization compete” [1][1]: 113, addressing knowledge creation, knowledge storage and retrieval, knowledge transfer, and knowledge application [1,51,82]. Knowledge management is complicated by the nature of knowledge and the need to address it indirectly through knowledge artifacts.

KMSs are systems that manage or provide access to knowledge artifacts. Several types of KMSs can be distinguished by the source of the knowledge provided, i.e., whether it is internal or external. Internal KMS process knowledge originating only from members of the organization, regardless of the knowledge seekers. External KMSs involve knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows from external sources toward the internal sphere (note that most of the time external KMSs will also address internal knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows). Issues including questions about appropriate standards [54,55]; knowledge leakage from internal to external [13]; multilingual environments [111]; and knowledge politics [23] arise.

A portal is a system designed to provide secure, customizable, personalizable, integrated access to dynamic information from a variety of sources, in a variety of source formats, wherever it is needed [30,95]. An important aspect of a portal is the repository of information to which it provides (ubiquitous) access. Its gateway character is commonly mentioned in the form of a web site [95]. In organizational contexts, portals are embedded in an organization’s intranet. The integration of Internet access and related network functionalities are supplementary features.

A KP is one type of KMS [7,90,92,95,116], which integrates knowledge management and portal functionalities as de<sup>fi</sup>ned above. A KP purposely supports and stimulates interlinked processes of knowledge management such as knowledge transfer, knowledge storage and retrieval, knowledge creation, and knowledge application [51], thus addressing inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and ineffectiveness in the use of organizational knowledge processes. KPs potentially foster mutual knowledge and understanding, and hence foster connections and social ties among users [96]. One of their most important features is their focus on KI [57,90], the so called “one-stop knowledge shop.” Integrating knowledge is important as it can lead to higher competitiveness [2,42,80] by transforming speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge into collectively valuable knowledge [77]. KPs speci<sup>fi</sup>cally address organizational capabilities derived from organizational learning [90].

In this paper, we focus on internal KPs only – processing knowledge originating from members of the organization (see Fig. 1) – and disregard systems implemented on interorganizational levels, although some of our insights might be applicable to the latter as well.

## 2.2. KP components and embedded functionalities

We will discuss the typical components of a KP: (1) knowledge repositories; (2) knowledge organization systems; (3) search; (4) applications and services; (5) collaboration and communication tools; (6) personalization and role management; and (7) the uni<sup>fi</sup>ed interface (see Fig. 2). We thereby are able to distinguish between the repository and the network focus of a KP. A KP with a repository focus means that it exists primarily to provide access to an organization’s knowledge repository artifacts in a codi<sup>fi</sup>ed form and hence can be accessed by one person at a time. A KP with a network focus, however, exists primarily to foster interaction among KP users.

## 2.3. Knowledge repositories

In the context of KI, providing and integrating access to an organization’s knowledge repositories of knowledge artifacts in a codi<sup>fi</sup>ed form is a key task for KPs [20,78,100]. We refer to this functionality of KPs as the repository focus. Repositories can be as simple as plain databases [14] or more sophisticated by, for example, including synthesized knowledge artifacts such as codi<sup>fi</sup>ed best practices [43,68].

## 2.4. Search

For all KMSs, search represents an essential part of the knowledge retrieval process. Basic categories are standard, concept-based, and metadata search [20]. The integration of varying sources and evolving insights into search is a particularly important issue for KPs [100]. The search engine is intertwined with the KP interface; it is usually implemented as a static feature [20], but requires adapted and contextualized display [30].

## 2.5. Knowledge organization systems

Knowledge organization systems constitute an essential component of a KP, as they address KI by structuring metainformation for underlying repositories and networks [20]. Under this broad concept, we subsume the more speci<sup>fi</sup>c content management systems, which offer the possibility of classifying and (re-)codifying knowledge artifacts from various sources in an integrative manner [7]. Other important subcategories are document and project management systems, as well as knowledge maps [57]. Simple components of knowledge organization systems can be registers and categorizations [20]. Registers are lists or indexes of information, for instance, comprising glossaries, dictionaries, or authority <sup>fi</sup>les [20] that facilitate a common understanding and language. Categorizations are relevant, in particular, for facilitation of the knowledge retrieval process, and are important no matter how much powerful the search engine is [37]. They comprise, for example, subject headings or content separation schemes [20].

On a more complex structural level, knowledge organization systems include taxonomies. For instance, a knowledge organization system usually contains an organizational information taxonomy [30] or “business information directory” [31], representing a metadata catalog prompted by the different publishing units and ideally comprising all codi<sup>fi</sup>ed information existing in the organization [31]. In addition, a knowledge organization system can integrate a variety of other taxonomies, such as simple thesauri or more complex ontologies. The latter, by giving information a semantic underlay [20,61], can enhance the search function [48] and other functions of the system [7]. The purpose of taxonomic metainformation in the knowledge organization systems is to provide context and to indicate where knowledge or knowing individuals can be found [1,61]. Thus, knowledge organization systems contribute to navigation and coordination, thereby enhancing knowledge retrieval, storage, and transfer processes.

## 2.6. Applications and services

A KP delivers integrated access to different software tools and a variety of services to facilitate knowledge work [41], such as multirepository support, process, and web service applications [20]. Multirepository support refers to applications that overlay a variety of other repositories, thus providing an integrated point of access for separate systems. One can consider the integration of applications and the integration of repositories as complementary parts of a holistic integration of existing IT [14]. The main issue is the visual integration of different interface structures without losing the applications’ functionality or giving up on the KP’s established logic of use.

## 2.7. Unified interface

A KP’s uni<sup>fi</sup>ed interface is the point of visual contact with the user. It must offer direct access to relevant features. Explicit personalization, for example, not only be provided for the content shown but also for the visual representation itself [95]. The interface’s key function is visual integration, having to be geared to the user experience in order to present all functionalities; for example, enabled by knowledge organization systems, it complements content pages by content relevant pages, thereby helping the user to interpret the main body of knowledge [20].

## 2.8. Personalization and role management

By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, portals offer customization and personalization to reach a higher degree of structure and usefulness of retrieved information and distinguish them from common web services [7]. To do so, personalized user and role management, which recognizes and administrates user access [14], is required [20]. Roles can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned according to tasks [80], with the purpose to predetermine knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows toward user groups [14] as speci<sup>fi</sup>cally as possible. Role management can be considered to be a prerequisite for “tailored” personalization.

There are two possibilities for supplementary personalization. On the one hand, the KP will allow the users to organize knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows [20], providing them with means to avert an over<sup>fl</sup>ow of information and save browsing time [100]. This is referred to as explicit personalization or (user) customization. On the other hand, the KP can personalize the web page itself based on rules or user behavior [7,36], that is, implicit personalization.

## 2.9. Collaboration and communication tools

In addition to the repository-oriented functions of KPs discussed so far, some types of knowledge are most readily transferred through direct interaction [73]. To that end, KPs can also offer collaboration and communication tools to connect people [7]. We refer to this focus of a KP as the network focus, as it relates to the system’s capability to enhance the communication network of participants. Work-group productivity tools and specialized transactional functions make an effort to foster and facilitate collaboration and communication by providing a convenient platform [30]. Available tools are extremely diverse, among them e-mail, shared document writing spaces, net meetings, and video conferences [57]. One notable example is semantic blogging, considered especially pertinent for more decentralized knowledge management [15].

In summary, a KP integrates knowledge management and portal functionality, and purposely supports and stimulates knowledge in order to improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of the organization. By combining repository-oriented functionalities, such as repository access, knowledge organization system, search, and applications and services, as well as network-oriented functionalities such as collaboration and communication tools, a KP provides secure, customizable, personalizable, integrated access to information from a variety of sources.

## 3. Research approach

To identify the main challenges of deploying internal KPs, we conducted a literature review covering >25 years (1988–2015) of empirical studies of KPs that actually had been or were in the process of being implemented.

The literature review was conducted as follows. By using ABI INFORMS and EBSCO, the <sup>fi</sup>rst step was to perform keyword-based searches on the terms “knowledge portal,” “knowledge management system,” “knowledge network,” “knowledge and intranet,” “knowledge integration,” “knowledge repository,” and “knowledge platform” for the years 1988–2015. We then selected those papers that presented empirical studies, i.e., cover the actual implementation of a KMS/KP in an organization. In addition, we took advantage of our reading of the theoretical knowledge management literature to identify additional journal papers that presented empirical studies of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementation. We excluded papers that did not discuss KPs from our analysis, but analyzed papers that discuss a KP (given our de<sup>fi</sup>nition above) while using different words (e.g., a KMS). Following this approach, we ended up with 61 studies on KP deployment. The 61 papers are listed in the Appendices.

In a second step, we closely analyzed the 61 studies. We <sup>fi</sup>rst grouped the papers into four categories by the nature of the system described. Table A1 in Appendix A lists empirical studies of KPs that discuss KPs in general. Table A2 in Appendix A lists those studies that focus on the repository aspects of KPs, while Table A3 in Appendix A lists those studies that discuss the networking aspects of KPs. Table A4 in Appendix E lists those studies that discuss KPs, but were more focused on KMS in general. In this last group, KPs are mentioned but there is no in-depth discussion of the KP per se.

In the third step, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the most important deployment challenges mentioned in the papers. We found that, despite all the previous research on KPs, three main challenges remain. These challenges are (1) SP; (2) OC, and (3) KI. The next section discusses these challenges in detail.

Pursuing a three-round, iterative process, we then coded each paper as to whether it discussed approaches related to addressing these KP deployment challenges. For the coding, the challenge SP refers to getting individuals to contribute their knowledge and encouraging them to work on validation and maintenance in order to maintain knowledge quality at a high level. The challenge OC describes a holistic arrangement of structures [10], to which organization members refer when they act or seek to generate action from others [5], including rules, practices, behaviors, values, preferences, and attitudes [56], marked by varying degrees of visibility [3,58]. Finally, the challenge KI refers to providing and integrating access to an organization’s knowledge repositories of information and knowledge artifacts. All the three rounds of coding for deployment challenges were performed by two postgraduate students, supervised and double checked by the academic mentor (one of the authors). There were few disagreements about the coding, but if any arose, they were discussed and resolved. A summary of the papers and the coding is given in Table 1.

## 4. Three challenges for successfully deploying KPs: discussion and research hypotheses

In this section, we discuss the three major KP deployment challenges found in our literature review. These three challenges represent research gaps in our current knowledge. In order to provide some direction for future research, we also suggest a set of 14 testable hypotheses that subsequent IS research studies might use. Our claim is that these hypotheses are reasonable conjectures based on our analysis of the literature, and might be helpful to IS researchers.

## 4.1. Sufficient participation

KPs, like all KMS, need to induce SP to be successful. They are useful only as far as knowledge or information is contributed and absorbed by participants [9,33,47,52,56,65,103,117]. Hence, KPs need to get individuals to share their knowledge and encourage work on validation and maintenance in order to maintain knowledge quality at a high level. Therefore, we propose:

## H1. The higher the level of participation, the greater the success of a KP.

As ISs, KPs are subject to the usual range of IS adoption factors, such as perceived usefulness or ease of use. However, in common with other kinds of KMSs, KPs face several additional issues in encouraging participation. We will discuss two issues in particular: motivation to contribute and knowledge quality.

First, KPs must address the users’ motivation to contribute knowledge to the system. Contributing to the system is bound to impose costs, which consists of time, effort, and expected followup requests [52]. A further factor is the possible loss of power when a contributor’s personal knowledge base becomes less unique, leaving them less irreplaceable, and perhaps less valuable after their contribution [24,52]. This concern is critical especially in the codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of tacit knowledge, as, in this case, individuals disclose more personal knowledge and partly give up their status [72]. As they partially avoid codi<sup>fi</sup>cation, network-related KPs should be less susceptible to this issue [101]. Thus, we further propose:

H2. The more users consider knowledge to be personally valuable, the less likely they are to contribute it to a repositoryrelated KP.

H3. Users will be more likely to contribute knowledge they consider personally valuable to a network-related KP than to a repository-related KP.

For the organization to bene<sup>fi</sup>t, participants may need to be motivated to add their knowledge to a repository, even if it does not appear economically rational from their individual point of view, i.e., the individual costs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed above seem to outweigh the actual or potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [52,63]. The calculation is comparable to a public-good dilemma, as sharing of knowledge will make it available to others, irrespective of a direct compensatory reciprocal contribution [10,67].

Additional motivations might arise out of reciprocity, denoting the expectation of being able to seek knowledge later as compensation for one’s own contribution [47]. Moreover, based on the conviction that people share knowledge for altruistic prosocial reasons [109], the joy of helping others while expecting nothing or very little concrete in return could be a solid motivator for users to contribute [52]. Finally, while the mere seeking of a social relationship is unlikely to be a participant’s prior concern, the wish to belong to a community might matter [3,109,117]. Therefore, we propose:

H4. The more a user uses a KP personally, the more likely the user is to contribute to the KP.

H5. The more a user feels part of the group using the KP, the more likely the user is to contribute to the KP.

As per the hierarchical level of goal attainment to which remuneration should be bound, the literature tends to argue in favor of incentives relating to team, unit, or organization goals as opposed to individual bonuses [45,76,84,104]. Generally, competition within a group appears to hamper knowledge sharing, whereas the combination of individual and group incentives might make knowledge sharing seem more rational [94]. Hence, we propose:

H6. Users will be more likely to contribute to a KP if there are group-level performance rewards than if there are individuallevel performance rewards.

A second problem in encouraging KP use is encouraging work on validation and maintenance in order to maintain knowledge quality at a high level. Although it is the perceived information quality that counts, that is, “the extent to which an individual believes that a repository provides precise and accurate content that meets his or her knowledge needs” [33] [33]: 84, validation processes are frequently implemented without reference to participants’ beliefs, perceptions, and behaviors [33]. On the one hand, participants might be less motivated if their contributions are reedited, rejected, or delayed [3]. On the other hand, KPs must try to guarantee high degrees of objectivity and reliability for other participants through stringent validation [33]. Assuring quality, usability, relevance, and usefulness of the knowledge provided by KPs needs continuous effort. However, knowledge-managementrelated approaches to continuance are yet to be used [47]. Hence, we propose:

H7. Knowledge validation efforts will increase perceived knowledge quality (i.e., increase perceived usefulness) and thus encourage use.

H8. Knowledge validation efforts will increase the perceived dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of contributing (i.e., reduce ease of use) and thus decrease contributions.

## 4.2. Favorable Organizational Culture

KPs need to be accompanied by a favorable sociocultural environment. One de<sup>fi</sup>nition of OC is that it is a holistic arrangement of structures [10], to which organization members refer when they act or seek to generate action from others [5], including rules, practices, behaviors, values, preferences, and attitudes [56], marked by varying degrees of visibility [3,58]. One potential problem with trying to in<sup>fl</sup>uence OC is that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to alter directly [10].

OC may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the success of any KMS, a KP in particular, by in<sup>fl</sup>uencing individuals’ willingness to share data, a key factor as noted above. For example, a culture may prompt knowledge hoarding: a competitive culture purportedly leads to individuals keeping their knowledge for themselves [56,104]. In contrast, a supportive culture may lead to a state of less self-interest, in which the individual no longer considers the organization’s knowledge as distinct from their own and even feels the moral obligation to share [106,109], based on the internalization of shared values [40,66]. We therefore propose:

H9. The more competitive the OC, the less likely a user is to contribute to a KP.

Of course, organizations succeed in making their culture part of the individual’s mindset to quite varying extents [45,106] and these efforts are subjected to external and overall economic factors as well [40,106]. Beside internalization and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, OC can create strong social norms, which might signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly limit perceived costs of compliance to the system and reduce knowledge hoarding [66]. Finally, as with other ISs, senior management support plays a pivotal role for successfully deploying KPs [7,24]. Therefore, we propose:

H10. The stronger the social norms for use created by the OC, the more likely a user is to use a KP.

## 4.3. Knowledge integration

To be a gateway to various underlying sources of knowledge, KPs need to integrate multiple repositories and applications. Several authors (e.g., [16,26,98] use the term “one-stop knowledge shop” to describe this purpose. However, to be useful as a comprehensive knowledge supply not only requires pooling of knowledge but also provides it in a coordinated and meaningful form [57]. KPs should provide mechanisms to integrate extensive and dispersed knowledge in various facets and from diverse sources. However, it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve the right balance between centralization (onestop shop) and decentralization (dispersed knowledge) in knowledge management initiatives [37]. Therefore, we propose:

## H11. KPs that provide KI will be more successful.

A variety of factors complicate KI. The diversity of the knowledge itself poses challenges. It is in the nature of systems that they are able to deal best with codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge [29,42], which derives from the contribution of explicit knowledge or of tacit knowledge that has been explicated. However, explication and codi<sup>fi</sup>cation of knowledge are complex processes [24] that have costs and may lead to knowledge losses [1,42,107,114] by “divorc[ing] the codi<sup>fi</sup>ed knowledge from its context” [37][37]: 29. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of codi<sup>fi</sup>cation rises with the degree of tacitness of the knowledge [2]; it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for individuals to communicate tacit knowledge [1,72,116] – particularly in a generalizable way [105] This dif<sup>fi</sup>culty is a key problem in all KMSs, and poses a particular problem for integrating knowledge for a KP. Therefore, we propose:

H12. The more diverse and tacit the knowledge, the less successful the KP will be in providing KI.

Although repository-focused KPs address asynchronous and explicit knowledge transfer (e.g., via a knowledge repository), network-related KPs address tacit knowledge. They provide individuals with means for direct electronic communication or contact [114]. Such an approach can support a diversity of transfer mechanisms, such as storytelling [24,71] or other best-practice sharing [37,106]. Hence, we propose:

H13. KI of tacit knowledge will be more successful with network-related KPs than with repository-related KPs.

A second problem arises from the diversity of potential participants in a KP. For example, different mechanisms and incentives must be applied to integrate customers’ knowledge [80], as customers’ knowledge will be even more dispersed than organizational knowledge [23]; although as noted earlier, we are focusing this work on internal KPs in part for this reason). Issues of missing structure, relevance, reliability, and quality might become even more common [69]. Furthermore, regulatory boundaries may constrain full exploitation of all collected information [22]. Differing needs for timeliness aggravate the challenge of integrating diverse knowledge. Considering the issue of leakage, KPs need to balance the fact that knowledge that is easily available for customers is also available for competitors [22]. Diversity also hampers knowledge transfer in network-related KPs. Practical experience will be sequentially recorded and recommended [72], but as such experience is subjective and rooted in action, deriving speci<sup>fi</sup>c insights can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. This problem will be especially signi<sup>fi</sup>cant if participants are only loosely tied together and have

H14. The more diverse the users of a KP, the less successful the KP will be in providing KI.

## 5. Conclusions and implications

KPs are a relatively new type of KMS that strive to provide a “one-stop knowledge shop” in an organization integrating both repository and network functionalities and can be powerful tools. Although research on KMS in general is now well established, our literature review has revealed that it is only in the past decade that IS research has focused on KPs.

We have therefore attempted to provide a reasonably comprehensive snapshot of the current state of the art of research on KPs. As a new class of system, we found that KPs pose additional deployment challenges beyond those in other ISs and KMSs in particular. Based on our analysis of the research literature, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three main challenges to deploying internal KPs, namely (1) SP, (2) OC, and (3) KI. In order to provide some direction for IS research on KPs, we have suggested a set of hypotheses that could be tested in future studies.

One implication of our work is that the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in deploying internal KPs should not be underestimated. None of the three challenges we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are easily addressed. For example, the management and organizational development literature shows that management attempts to change OC is a fraught enterprise, requiring managers to negotiate meaning and power–resistance relationships. The IS research literature also shows that obtaining SP is not easy either.

Another implication of our work is that KPs are not for everyone. Our literature review suggests that if an organization only requires straightforward top-down information dissemination or if it is involved in purely creative “brain-storming” work, it might be best to use a repository KMS or a networking KMS, but not an integrative KP. Hence, not all organizations need to deploy a KP.

Overall, our work re<sup>fl</sup>ects a common conundrum for organizations: either they address tacit knowledge in a long-term focused, interaction-related, and laborious manner within collocated organizational settings, or they content themselves with more frugal explicit KI, which may still yield timely results and be adequate for extremely dispersed settings [29]. We hope that the three challenges we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed along with our suggested hypotheses are helpful for IS researchers who want to study this topic further. For some organizations, the answers to these challenges could potentially help them to save millions of dollars or at least improve their chances of deploying internal KPs effectively.
