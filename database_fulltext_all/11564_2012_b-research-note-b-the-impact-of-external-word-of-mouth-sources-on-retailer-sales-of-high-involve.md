## 1. Introduction

Online word-of-mouth (WOM)—such as consumer opinions, user experiences, and product reviews—is becoming a major source of information for consumer purchase decisions (e.g., Dellarocas 2003, Godes and Mayzlin 2004). As a result, retailer-hosted (internal) and independent (external) WOM sources have proliferated. Extant literature shows that internal WOM has a significant and positive impact on retailer sales (e.g., Chen et al. 2007, Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). However, research to explicate the relative impact of internal and external WOMs is still evolving. Furthermore, most current studies focus on WOM impact on low-involvement products like books and CDs, but not high-involvement products such as digital cameras or other durable goods. This research investigates the differential impact of internal and external WOM on retailer sales in the context of high-involvement products.

Numerous studies and surveys point toward the growing use of internal and external WOM sources in consumer purchase decisions. Over 60% of consumers utilize online WOM when making purchase decisions (Razorfish.com 2008), and WOM is widely viewed as more credible than advertisements and other sellergenerated product information (Nielson 2007). There are two main types of WOM information: external WOM hosted by third-party infomediaries and internal WOM hosted by retailers. Anecdotal evidence suggests that both types play a role in the decisionmaking process. For example, according to a comscore.com survey, around 40% of respondents used external WOM websites in their purchase decisions (ComScore 2007). An E-Tailing Group survey showed that 98% of shoppers read reviews on retailers’ websites (Freedman 2008).

Given the availability of both internal and external WOM, an interesting research question is estimating the relative impact of different WOM sources on product sales. Extant studies have focused generally on the impact of internal WOM on retailer sales, finding that internal WOM has a significant influence (e.g., Chen et al. 2007, Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). These studies, however, center on low-involvement products with relatively simple functionality and low prices. In these cases, a customer may not necessarily engage in an extensive information search.

Marketing literature establishes that consumer information-search behaviors differ markedly with product involvement (Clarke and Belk 1978, Engel and Blackwell 1982). Product involvement refers to a consumer’s perceived importance of, and interest in, a product (Traylor 1981, Hansen 1985, Richins and Bloch 1986). For low-involvement products, a consumer rarely engages in an “extensive search for information or a comprehensive evaluation of the choice alternatives” (Zaichkowsky 1985, p. 341). But for high-involvement products, a consumer often spends considerable time searching for product information to make the right decision (Clarke and Belk 1978). In general, product involvement varies across individual consumers, but product characteristics play a significant role. Prior studies have classified high-involvement and low-involvement products based on the extent of risk perceived by consumers (Hoyer and MacInnis 2008). Durable products such as consumer electronics, appliances, and automobiles are typical examples of high-involvement products, since wrong purchase decisions have financial implications and force consumers to deal with poor products for long periods of time (Laurent and Kapferer 1985). Thus, consumers often spend a significant amount of time conducting information searches before purchasing these products (Kerin et al. 2006, Kuruzovich et al. 2008, Moorthy et al. 1997, Murray 1991, Viswanathan et al. 2007). Consumable products—such as groceries, books, and music CDs—are typical examples of lowinvolvement products (Kannan et al. 2001). For these products, the consequences of making the wrong purchase decisions are limited. Hence, consumers are less likely to conduct extensive information searches before visiting retailers to make the purchase (e.g., Dholakia 2001, Levy and Nebenzahl 2008, Mathwick and Rigdon 2004).

The differences in product type and information search behavior suggest external WOM may have greater influence on consumer purchase decisions for high-involvement products. The effect is strengthened by the growing popularity of search engines (Ansari 2009). A DoubleClick study reveals that over 50% of online shoppers use search engines during the information search process (Row 2006), and a large proportion of the search results refer consumers to external WOM sites. Our own analysis of a search for digital camera information on Google—which attracts nearly 70% of all online searches—showed that external WOM sites are prominently displayed in search results (see Table 1). The ease of access to external WOM sites and consumer motivation to conduct pre-purchase information for high-involvement products suggest that external WOM could have a significant influence on retailer sales of high-involvement products.

To investigate the role of internal and external WOM sources on retailer sales of high-involvement products, we collected a panel data of daily sales ranks of 148 digital camera products from Amazon between June 2007 and October 2007, along with the corresponding customer review information from Amazon, Cnet, DpReview, and Epinions. Digital cameras are considered high-involvement products and Amazon is a reputable online retailer with extensive consumer-generated internal WOM. A total of 14,751 observations were collected. We apply a fixed-effect model to control for product heterogeneity and brand effect, and used an instrumental variable approach to control for endogeneity between the WOM volume and product sales (Duan et al. 2008a, b). Our results suggest that external WOM sources (Cnet, DpReview, and Epinions) have a significant impact on camera sales at Amazon but the internal WOM has less influence, despite the fact that Amazon hosts more WOM messages on average than any of the external WOM sources. This finding is consistent with the proposition that consumers obtain product information from external WOM sources during information searches for high-involvement products (Kerin et al. 2006, Levy and Nebenzahl 2008) and are thus less influenced by retailer-hosted WOM.

In §4, we present the research context and variables, followed by research methodology in §5. Section 6 offers the results of our research and discusses the robustness of our models and findings. We conclude in §7.

## 2. Related Literature

A growing body of literature attempts to understand the influence of WOM on retailer sales and consumer purchase decisions. Past studies show that WOM has a significant influence on retailer sales of various consumer products (e.g., Chatterjee 2001; Clemons et al. 2006; Dellarocas et al. 2007; Duan et al. 2008a, b; Godes and Mayzlin 2004; Liu 2006; Zhu and Zhang 2010). As a result, businesses increasingly consider online WOM to be an important element of their business strategies (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Chen and Xie 2008, Dellarocas 2006). Most studies, however, are conducted on low-involvement products, and extant literature has yet to explicate the role of online WOM for high-involvement products (see Table 2 for a summary of WOM studies and their research context). Moreover, most WOM studies consider only a single WOM source, with the exception of Bickart and Schindler (2001) and Senecal and Nantel (2004). Bickart and Schindler (2001) analyze the differences between consumer- and seller-generated information, but not internal versus external WOMs. They show that consumer-generated information has a greater impact on consumer purchase decisions than seller-created information. The argument revolves around the trust consumers have in reviews provided by other consumers, relative to information provided by the seller. Similarly, Senecal and Nantel (2004) compare the influence of multiple sources of product recommendations (but not consumer-generated WOM) on consumer perceptions. They hypothesize that product recommendations on noncommercially linked websites are viewed as more trustworthy than those on seller-related websites, thus having more influence on consumer decisions. Different from these studies, our goal is not to compare seller-generated product reviews or recommendations with consumer-generated ones. Instead, we study the influence of multiple sources of consumer-generated product reviews where trust is of less concern. Our underlying theoretical argument is that the differences among multiple WOM sources are due mainly to consumer pre-purchase information searches, an important aspect of the consumer decision process that has not been considered in prior WOM studies.

Our research is related to prior studies of the variations in WOM influence on retailer sales. Chen et al. (2007) show that reviews perceived as more helpful have a greater influence on retailer sales. Forman et al. (2008) and Hu et al. (2008) found that reviewers who reveal their identities and have better reputations produce greater influence on sales. Duan et al. (2009) suggest that WOM has a more significant impact on less popular products than popular products. Awad and Zhang (2007) demonstrate that WOM has greater influence on product sales when the retailer does not censor negative consumer reviews. Clemons et al. (2006) point out that online WOM is more influential on highly differentiated products. These studies focus on variations of WOM influence within a single WOM source. We complement these studies by analyzing variations of WOM influence across different WOM sources.

This study also complements the literature on consumer information-search behavior. Nicosia (1966) argues that information-search behavior is one of the five essential stages of the consumer-purchase decision process: (1) problem recognition, (2) information search, (3) evaluation of alternatives, (4) purchase decisions, and (5) post-purchase behavior. Motivation for the product information search is both to reduce the perceived risk and to make better purchase decisions (Cox 1967, Hugstad et al. 1987, Murray 1991). Consumers, however, do not necessarily go through all five stages in making purchase decisions. In particular, studies reveal that a considerable number of purchase decisions do not involve an extensive information search because many products are of little importance to consumers (Kassarjian 1981, Olshavsky and Granbois 1979). The concept of low-involvement versus high-involvement products is thus introduced to help distinguish consumer behavior for the two types of products (Engel and Blackwell 1982). Consumers conduct fewer information searches for lowinvolvement products, but search extensively for high-involvement ones (Dowling and Staelin 1994, Mitra et al. 1999, Zaichkowsky 1985). This study contributes to the information search literature and studies on product involvement by analyzing the relative influence of internal and external WOM on retailer sales of high-involvement products.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

Consumer information search is an important part of the purchase decision process (Engel and Blackwell 1982, Nicosia 1966). Although consumers can obtain product information from a variety of sources, usergenerated content such as WOM is considered more trustworthy than that generated by sellers (Bickart and Schindler 2001), and plays an important role in the purchase decision-making process.

Before the Internet, the dispersion of WOM occurred among friends and relatives in private oneon-one interactions or social exchanges. The emergence of online WOM sites transforms the once private and fleeting WOM interactions to public online postings that are permanently archived and viewable to all future online consumers (Dellarocas 2003). Wide access to the Internet and improvements in search technology (e.g., Google) further reduce consumer search costs (Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Lynch and Ariely 2000, Ratchford et al. 2003). Furthermore, the proliferation of third-party infomediaries provides consumers unprecedented access to product information and WOM postings. A simple experiment using Google shows that the search result often brings out popular external WOM sources in the first few links (see Table 1).

Involvement theory suggests that consumers engage in limited pre-purchase information searches and processing in situations of low involvement, but extensive pre-purchase information searches in situations of high involvement (Schiffman and Kanuk 2004, Mathwick and Rigdon 2004). A main driver of consumer involvement and information search behavior is the level of risk perceived by consumers (Kerin et al. 2004). Extant research has identified six types of perceived risks: performance, financial, physical, social, psychological, and time risks (Cunningham 1967; Hoyer and MacInnis 2008, Peter and Tarpey 1975). These studies suggest that consumers are more concerned about durable products with complex functionality, high price, and long life. As such, consumers are more engaged in the purchase decisions of these products. As thorough pre-purchase information searches reduce the likelihood of consumers making wrong purchase decisions (Murray 1991), consumers are more likely to obtain product information from multiple information sources, especially external WOM sites that provide access to hardto-get information such as usage experience, quality, and performance.

Hence, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1.</sup> External WOM sources have a significant influence on the sales of high-involvement products at online retailers.

Although both external and internal WOM sources could influence consumer purchases of highinvolvement products, their roles in the consumer purchase decision process are different. During the information search process, a consumer’s goal is to obtain product information for objective comparisons across multiple products (Dholakia 2001, Kerin et al. 2006). As dedicated and independent sources for product information, external WOM sites have a number of advantages over retailer-hosted WOM sites in this process. First, external WOM enjoys better reputation and recognition as product information sources for consumers than retailer-hosted WOMs (Tedeschi 2006). Second, external WOM sources contain a significant amount of product information. They are often more specialized (e.g., Cnet focuses on consumer electronics) than online retailers, who sell products in thousands of categories. Hence, external sources generally offer greater depth and more insights. Third, many external WOM sources provide not only product reviews but also professional reviews, thus allowing consumers to obtain more product information. Table 3 shows that two of the three external WOM sources used in our analysis— Cnet and DpReview—provide expert reviews and other product information. These advantages suggest that consumers are more likely to use external WOM sources during the information-search stage. We therefore propose:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.</sup> External WOM has greater influence on the sales of high-involvement products at online retailers than retailer-hosted internal WOM.

## 4. Data

## 4.1. Research Context

We analyze the hypotheses using a panel data of daily product sales rank from Amazon.com, and WOM data from Amazon.com and three external WOM websites. We choose digital cameras as the research context for this study because they are a typical example of high-involvement products. Moreover, the digital camera market is extremely competitive. More than one hundred unique models are available during our study period. The number of choices available, the product complexity, and the high price require consumers to conduct extensive information searches before making purchase decisions.

We collect data daily on all Canon and Nikon digital camera models sold by Amazon.com for the period June 2007 through October 2007 (the data include sales ranks, consumer review ratings, number of reviews, release dates, and prices). We choose Canon and Nikon because they are the most popular brands of digital cameras. These companies also produce a wide spectrum of digital cameras, from under \$100 entry-level point-and-shoot cameras to over \$5,000 high-end professional DSLR cameras. We choose Amazon because it is one of the most popular online retailers and is well known for the influence of its extensive consumer reviews. A finding of less influence by Amazon-hosted WOM would provide a convincing argument for the limitation of internal WOM. It would also allow us to generalize our results to other lesser-known retailer-hosted WOM systems.

For external WOM sites, we collect daily consumerreview data on digital cameras from three well-known WOM sites—Cnet, DpReview, and Epinions—for the same period. Cnet is a well-known specialized product information provider that offers product information, descriptions, professional reviews, and user reviews on electronic products. It is also one of the largest product information sites, with more than six million unique visitors per month.<sup>1</sup> Cnet does not sell products on its own website, but it generates revenue by referring customers to online retailers through its price comparison services. DpReview<sup>2</sup> is also a specialized product information provider but with a narrow focus on digital cameras. It offers extensive camera information, including expert reviews, customer reviews, user forums, educational materials, newsletters, and a user gallery. The main revenue sources for DpReview are advertising and customer referrals to online retailers. The site attracts seven million unique visitors per month.

Epinions is a general product information provider that elicits customer reviews for a wide range of products, including digital cameras. Epinions also supplies extensive product information and descriptions. Unlike Cnet or DpReview, however, it does not offer professional reviews. Using Amazon Standard Identification Numbers and product names, we match the Amazon product list with those from Cnet, DpReview, and Epinions. We also routinely update our data sets to include products introduced during the study period. Furthermore, we drop products with sales ranks greater than 100,000, since the rank of these products was not updated frequently (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). In total, we obtain 148 products for the analysis.

## 4.2. Variables

4.2.1. Dependent Variable. Our objective is to analyze the influence of retailer-hosted (internal) and external WOM information on relative retailer (Amazon) sales of high-involvement products reflected through sales ranking. Prior studies have identified a power law relationship between a book’s sales rank at Amazon and its actual sales (Chevalier and Goolsbee 2003, Ghose et al. 2009). Similar power law relationships between sales and sales rank have also been identified for other products, such as DVDs and software (Ghose and Sundararajan 2005, Smith and Telang 2008). Although the relationship between sales and sales rank has not been identified specifically for electronics products, the power law relationship has been used in related studies (Archak et al. 2011). We take a similar approach and use Amazon sales rank as the proxy for Amazon sales. Of the several Amazon sales rank measures available, we use product sales within the camera product category for our analysis. Amazon calculates and updates product sales rank hourly, based on both the current and recent product sales volume.<sup>3</sup> Although the exact algorithm for the calculation of sale rank has not been disclosed, it is reported that Amazon sales ranks for a product often change significantly from day to day, indicating that it is heavily influenced by current sales (Robinson 2008).

4.2.2. Independent Variables. The key independent variables of this analysis are WOM information from Amazon and the three external WOM websites. WOM is known to influence product sales in two ways: WOM volume and WOM valence (Liu 2006). WOM volume represents the amount of WOM disseminated. The more people talk about the products, the more people become informed about them, which in turn lead to higher sales. WOM valence represents customers’ opinions (positive or negative) about product quality. WOM valence influences consumers’ evaluations of products, which then influences their purchase decisions. To estimate these WOM variables, we use the number of reviews and the average rating values as WOM volume and WOM valence, respectively. In addition, we use Amazon product prices, product age, product fixed effect and the interaction terms between product age and product fixed effect as control variables (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Li and Hitt 2008). We also obtain product search volume data from Google Trends for each of the cameras to control for exogenous demand shocks (e.g., product advertising), which may influence product sales at Amazon (Ghose 2009).

## 5. Research Methodology

## 5.1. Empirical Model

For the analysis, we use a log-linear model, which has been widely used in prior studies on the relationship between WOM and retail sales $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). The log-liner model allows a percent change in WOM variables to contribute to a certain percentage change rather than a level change in product sales. This assumption is appropriate because popular products are likely to see higher changes in sales level than niche products due to scale effect. In addition, the log-linear model allows us to leverage the power law relationship between product sales and sales rank, as it implies a negative linear relationship between the log values of product sales and the log values of sales rank. We can thus use the negative log value of Amazon sales rank in place of Amazon sales in the log-linear model. We start with a base model with only Amazon WOM variables:

The dependent variable, −Ln(AmazonSalesRank 5, denotes the negative log value of the sales rank of product i on day t at Amazon. AmazonRating<sub>it</sub> represents the average WOM rating on Amazon for product i on day $t ,$ while AmazonNumReview indicates the number of Amazon reviews for product i by day t. Prior research shows that individual products often diffuse at different speeds, with some reaching market saturation faster than others (Mahajan et al. 1995). This set of control variables allows the diffusion coefficient to vary across products (Duan et al. 2009).

$$

In the equation above, coefficients on WOM rating and volume variables capture the valence and volume effects of internal and external WOM sources on Amazon sales of digital cameras. Hypothesis 2 suggests that the coefficients on Amazon-hosted WOM $( \beta _ { 1 }$ and $\gamma _ { 1 } )$ will be smaller than the corresponding coefficients on external WOM sources $( \beta _ { 2 } , \bar { \beta } _ { 3 } , \beta _ { 4 } ,$ $\gamma _ { 2 } , \gamma _ { 3 } ,$ and $\gamma _ { 4 } )$

Prior studies find that online WOM not only drives retail sales but is also an outcome of retail sales (Duan et al. 2008a, b; Godes and Mayzlin 2004). This dynamic relationship raises concerns about endogeneity between WOM and sales. A major contributing factor of the endogeneity problem is product characteristics and demand shocks unobserved by researchers that could influence both product sales and WOM. We address this problem in Equations (1) and (2) by using product fixed effect and Google search interest data.

To address the remaining endogeneity, we first note the timing difference between Amazon’s sales rank and customer reviews on Amazon and external WOM sources. Although the Amazon sales rank represents the current sales of a product and is updated hourly, customer reviews on Amazon and external WOM sources reflect customer experience with products in the past. In the case of electronics, Amazon.com typically ships products within two or three days, but it takes customers days or weeks to learn about the products before they post reviews online. Therefore, today’s sales have limited influence on today’s WOM (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). We also note that endogeneity could arise if there are common factors affecting both WOM volume and product sales (e.g., newspaper articles on a product may motivate both product sales and postings of product reviews at the same time). Such common factors could create endogeneity between product sales and WOM volume beyond what is captured by Google search interest data. To ensure the robustness of our approach, we use seven-day lagged review-volume variables<sup>6</sup> for both Amazon and the three WOM sources as instrument variables in our regression analysis. The instrument variables meet two requirements. First, they are highly correlated with current review variables. Second, lagged review volume variables are not correlated with the shock in current Amazon sale ranks, as the shock cannot influence review variables in past periods (Wooldridge 2001). Table 7 reports the correlations between the current review volume variables and seven-day lagged review volume variables and shows that they are highly correlated. We include the instrumental variables in our model and perform the Durbin–Wu–Hausman test for endogeneity. The test reveals the presence of endogeneity in the original specification and indicates that it is necessary to use the instrument variable approach (Duan et al. 2008b, Joshua and Krueger 2001, Lemos 2005).<sup>7</sup>

## 6.2. Robustness Check

To assess the importance of three external WOM sources on Amazon sales of digital cameras, we conduct a likelihood ratio test. We first obtain the loglikelihood of the regression model that does not include any of the external WOM sources (i.e., the regression equation for column 1 of Table 8). We then obtain the log-likelihood of the regression model that includes both Amazon and the three external WOM sources (i.e., the regression equation for column 4 of Table 8). To conduct the likelihood ratio test, we note that the test statistic is twice the difference in the log-likelihoods (=153.43). The test statistic has a chisquare distribution with a degree of freedom of 6, resulting in a p-value of less than 0.001. This test indicates that the addition of three external WOM sources (i.e., six WOM variables) significantly improves the model.

To ensure robustness and generalizability of our result, we assess whether the influence of external

WOM sources is driven by information searches on a few high-end cameras or is a more general occurrence that applies to the entire product category. To evaluate this question, we split the data and conduct separate analyses on the upper and bottom quartiles of camera products based on retail price. We re-estimate Equation (2) for each quartile, and report the data in columns 1 and 2 of Table 9. The result again shows that Amazon WOM has little influence while external WOM has a significant impact, although the impact varies among the three external WOM sources. The result holds for both low-end and high-end cameras.

We also check the robustness of our models for possible multicollinearity, serial correlation, and heteroskedasticity. To assess potential multicollinearity for Equations (1) and (2), we calculate VIF for all independent variables. The values are all below or close to 5, indicating no significant amount of multicollinearity to affect the regression estimation (see column 5 in Table 8). Besides VIF values, our regression results also suggest that they are not likely to be driven by multicollinearity (Kennedy 2008, Greene 2007). First, our regression results do not change substantively when we add or remove independent variables (see columns 1–4 in Table 8). Second, a typical multicollinearity symptom is that coefficients have very high standard errors and low significance levels. Coefficients of our independent variables, however, have relatively low standard errors and high significance levels when we incorporate all WOM variables (see column 4 in Table 8). These findings indicate that our results are not likely to be influenced by multicollinearity.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusions

The finding of this study has a number of important implications for research on electronic commerce and WOM. First, prior WOM studies have often focused on retailer-hosted internal WOM information. These studies provided early evidence of the influence of retailed-hosted WOM while shopping at the retailer. This study goes a step further and compares the influence of external WOM against that of retailer-hosted WOM in the context of high-involvement products. Industry surveys and marketing research have found that consumers often conduct information searches long before they visit retailers. In the informationsearch process, consumers focus on collecting product information from a variety of sources, and external WOM could offer significant advantages over retailerhosted internal WOM. Our finding supporting that thesis has the potential to help researchers develop a more comprehensive understanding of the online WOM effect.

Second, the results also highlight the role of product involvement for ecommerce research. Our findings based on high-involvement products are markedly different from those of prior WOM studies based on low-involvement products. We suggest that the difference is due to consumers engaging in extensive prepurchase information searches for high-involvement products, and our results support this proposition. This finding highlights the importance of considering product involvement in understanding online consumer information search behavior and its influence on retail strategies.

Third, our results suggest exposure does not equal influence. Each Amazon customer is exposed to retailer-hosted WOM before making any product purchases. Despite the exposure, however, Amazonhosted WOM has less influence on consumer purchase decisions on Amazon than do external WOM sources. Our findings mean that when consumers reach the retail site for purchase of high-involvement products, most have probably made their decisions and are less likely to be influenced by retailer-hosted information. This finding could have considerable implications for both retailers and manufacturers in determining their online marketing and advertising strategies.

The results also have valuable practical implications. First, they indicate that external WOM sources could be valuable to retailers for sales forecasts. Many high-involvement products such as digital cameras are expensive. Retailers of these products face a dynamic and competitive environment that requires the retailer to develop optimal operations and business strategies to manage the risk. Our study shows that incorporating external WOM sources can potentially improve sales forecast accuracy, allowing the retailer to manage operations better and thus improve profit margin. Second, the results suggest that retailers should consider carefully the influence of external WOM and understand how such information sources affect consumer purchase decisions. Many online retailers believe hosting customer reviews on their sites can help consumers make purchase decisions and increase product sales (Econsultancy and Bazaarvoice 2007, eMarketer 2007). Our results, however, indicate that the influence of retailer-hosted WOM is somewhat limited for high-involvement products, for which consumers often conduct information searches before making purchase decisions. This result suggests to retailers the limitations of their internal WOM. Third, our results demonstrate that the influence of internal and external WOM sources is likely to depend on product involvement. Retailers need to be cautious in generalizing WOM effect from one product category to another. An effective WOM strategy for one group of products may not work well for another group of products. Retailers need to perform product-specific analysis to identify optimal WOM strategies.

This research has several limitations. First, since we only collected Canon and Nikon data, our analysis is limited to these well-known products. Extending the study to lesser-known products would be helpful in assessing the influence of online WOM on products in the long tail of sales distribution (Ghose et al. 2006). Second, we analyze only the influence of external WOM sources on Amazon sales due to data availability. Amazon, however, is known as a general online retailer that sells a wide variety of products and does not specialize in electronics. Therefore, our results may not generalize to specialized electronic retailers. Third, while our findings are consistent with the idea that consumers engage in information searches before arriving at retailers, we do not directly observe consumer information search behavior. It will be valuable to combine WOM data with click stream data to develop a more in-depth understanding of information search behavior and the role of external and internal WOM sources in the process. Fourth, we did not analyze the information content of online WOM. Text-mining techniques could be used in the future to identify the extent to which information contents contribute to differences in WOM influence. Fifth, our results reveal that there are significant variations in the influence of the three external WOM sources on Amazon sales of high-involvement products. These external WOM sources could differ in information quality, specialization, reputation, and online reach. It will be valuable for future studies to analyze factors that contribute to such variation. Sixth, given the presence of multiple sources of WOM information, it would be interesting to assess how information from one source moderates the influence of information from another source. The empirical challenge is that the analysis would require introduction of interactions among closely related information sources, which lead to multicollinearity issues. Developing an approach to assess the interactions between two sets of closely related information could prove beneficial. Finally, our study highlights the influence of WOM from a major online retailer and three external WOM providers. Consumers may also be influenced by other media though, such as online recommendations, blogs, promotional emails from retailers, or discussions in online forums (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2007, Garfinkel et al. 2006, Haubl and Trifts 2000). Although incorporating Google search interest data can partially control for their influence and missing variables, including more product information factors in future studies will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the influence of online WOM information.
