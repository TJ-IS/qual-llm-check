# A Process-Oriented Perspective on the Alignment of Information Technology and Business Strategy

PAUL P. TALLON

PAUL P. TALLON is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the Wallace E. Carroll School of Management, Boston College, and a Research Associate at the Center for Research on Information Technology and Organizations (CRITO) at the University of California, Irvine. He previously worked as an IT auditor and a chartered accountant with PricewaterhouseCoopers in Dublin, Ireland, and New York. His research has appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Global IT Management, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Information Technology and Management, and Communications of the AIS. He recently coedited a volume in the Advances in Management Information Systems series (M.E. Sharpe) on advanced empirical methods in economics of information systems and e-commerce. Dr. Tallon’s research interests include the economic, social, and organizational impacts of IT, strategic alignment, real options, IT portfolio analysis, and the economics of information management.

ABSTRACT: Even after a decade of research and discussion, strategic alignment, denoting the fit between information technology (IT) and business strategy, remains an enduring challenge for firms worldwide. In this paper, we go beyond the dominant firm-level alignment paradigm by utilizing a value disciplines perspective on strategic foci to conceptualize alignment at the process level. Theory would then suggest that alignment should be tightest in processes that are considered critical to each firm’s strategic focus. Using data from matched surveys of IT and business executives at 241 firms, we detect support for this locus of alignment argument when alignment is identified using profile deviation or moderation. We also find a positive link between alignment and perceived IT business value in each of five primary processes in the value chain. By bringing a process-level view to the study of alignment and its impacts, we go beyond a discussion on the extent of fit—a cornerstone of the literature—to whether firms are pursuing the right type of fit for the particular mix of processes underlying their strategy. In this way, a process-level perspective can foster a deeper and more meaningful understanding of how alignment affects firm performance. Our results also show a need for managers to reconsider the steps taken to align IT and business strategy by looking more closely at how IT can support individual processes rather than at how IT can support an entire strategy.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: IT business value, IT–strategy fit, perceptual measures, process-level analysis, process perspective, resource-based view, strategic alignment, value chain, value disciplines.

IN THE AFTERMATH OF THE PRODUCTIVITY PARADOX, as evidence points to a positive relationship between information technology (IT) spending and firm performance [22, 36, 45], research has turned its attention to the broader issue of what management practices or idiosyncratic firm characteristics affect the intensity and scope of IT impacts [8]. From a host of candidate practices such as total quality management (TQM), reengineering, and flexible work practices [6, 9], research has identified strategic alignment—defined as the interaction or fit between IT and business strategy—as a key ingredient in the search for greater value from IT [15, 30]. Besides fit, the information systems (IS) literature has also described alignment in terms of linkage [30, 61], harmony [41], and integration [7, 81]—the common thread among these terms being that firms gain when business strategy is supported, stimulated, or enabled by IT [7]. From a practical viewpoint, the importance or value of alignment is further evident in multiple executive surveys that, over the past decade, have repeatedly ranked alignment among the most critical and complex IT issues facing firms worldwide [18, 23, 42].

Despite a sense that strategic alignment is key to business success, researchers continue to wrestle with issues of conceptualization and measurement. These issues are affected, in part, by the unobservable nature of alignment and by the fact that the measurement of IT and business strategy—the building blocks of alignment—is highly complex [76]. As research reveals, measuring business strategy by, for example, assigning a firm to a group in any of the better-known strategy typologies, such as Miles and Snow [48] or Porter [54], yields a less than perfect picture of how that firm is running its business [24, 29, 67, 69]. One way that the earliest alignment research avoided having to force-fit firms into a typology was to use cross-referencing between IT and business plans as a proxy for alignment [33, 34, 61].<sup>1</sup> However, later research shows the value of using typologies to dissect the activities performed by firms and how IT supports these activities. For example, the strategic orientation of business enterprises typology creates rich insights into how firms compete and how IT supports or enables their efforts [15, 77]. While much can be learned from considering the resulting fit between actual business activities and actual IT use [15, 63]—how alignment affects overall performance, for example—in this paper, we propose an alternative conceptualization that calls for a more micro-level approach. Rather than visualize alignment at the firm level as the IS literature has traditionally done, we propose conceptualizing and evaluating alignment at the process level.

There are several reasons why such a reconceptualization is desirable. Because business strategy is implemented using a series of processes or activities as a value chain, shop, or network [70], differences in strategic foci could mean that some processes are more important than others [55]. For example, a low-cost firm may have little use for processes that treat each customer as a stand-alone market; instead, their focus is more on mass production or standardization as a way to reduce costs. In contrast, a niche provider may see customer-facing processes or marketing as key. As a practical example, Costco and Whole Foods Market sell similar foodstuffs but each employs a different value proposition and has different uses for IT. To focus on alignment in a firm-level context, as earlier research has done, ignores the fact that for a low-cost leader such as Costco, the issue is not whether alignment is tight in all processes but whether alignment is tight in areas such as operations that matter to the success of low-cost leaders [55]. For niche firms such as Whole Foods, alignment is important in operations but more so in customer relations, an area that defines success for niche firms [55]. Hence, alignment is not just about tight fit but whether firms are pursuing the right type of fit, given the specific mix of processes or activities that comprise their business strategy.

This change in emphasis requires firms to identify key processes where alignment between IT and business strategy should ideally be tight and other processes where misalignment may pose less of a threat to firm performance, competitiveness, or firm survival. Because it is not entirely clear what the right type of fit means for different firms if alignment is conceptualized at the process level or, indeed, whether it even makes sense to talk about alignment outside a firm-level context, our goal in this paper is to conceptualize alignment at the process level with a particular emphasis on what process-level alignment means for firms with different strategic foci. As such, two research questions need to be addressed. First, do differences in strategy or strategic foci translate into differences in alignment at the process level? This question speaks not just to the extent of alignment but the locus of alignment; quite simply, for a specific strategic focus, is alignment tightest in processes that are key to that focus and less tight (perhaps even misaligned) in other ancillary areas? Second, is the link between alignment and firm performance that has been observed at the firm level also valid at the process level? This question is important when we consider that in studies that highlight differences in strategic foci, alignment is not a universal predictor of firm performance [51, 63]. Thus, while researchers might say that alignment is beneficial for performance, this result does not apply equally to all firms. Examining alignment at the process level—something that past research has not done—could yield key insights into why alignment is selectively beneficial. To the extent that our results—like Chan et al. [14] or Sabherwal and Chan [63]—show that cost-driven, operationally excellent firms receive little or no gain from alignment, a process-level approach could point to key processes where alignment is weak and in need of remedial managerial attention. At a time when executives are being asked to reaffirm and increase further the value generated by IT [11, 42], such insights could prove highly valuable.

Our approach to answering these questions uses the value disciplines typology devised by Treacy and Wiersema [75]<sup>2</sup>—a typology that parallels Porter’s typology [54]—to gain a greater understanding of where alignment matters most vis-à-vis specific processes in the value chain. We further use this typology to operationalize alignment at the process level. Based on earlier work by Venkatraman [76] that explains different empirical methods for measuring fit, we use both profile deviation and moderation in a way that conforms to the multitrait multimethod approach developed by Campbell and Fiske [10]. By using two different yet complementary sets of measures, we can have greater confidence in our results. In Figure 1, we present our process-level research model—alignment in each process is modeled as the fit between intraprocess activities and IT use; alignment in turn drives IT business value within that process. We also identify the five generic processes where alignment is considered and the three value disciplines denoting possible strategic foci; alignment is separately examined in each process for each of these strategic foci.

## Theoretical Background

CHANDLER DEFINES STRATEGY AS “THE DETERMINATION of the basic long-term goals of an enterprise, and the adoption of courses of action and allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals” [16, p. 13]. Prior research has used this definition to examine strategy from two angles—namely, content (what is our strategy?) and process (how do we implement that strategy?) [31, 69]. While the content of strategy can be similar across firms—for example, multiple firms in an industry can have a low-cost strategy—the unique mix of processes or activities through which strategy is executed is the ultimate arbiter of superior performance [55]. This argument is echoed in the literature on the resource-based view where it is argued that rare, immobile, nonsubstitutable, and causally ambiguous resources–of which the fit between IT and business processes is a quintessential form—provide a basis for superior performance [1, 17, 28, 60].

While the resource-based view remains relatively underutilized in IT research, the theory affords a unique perspective into the way that IT influences performance [80]. Theory would suggest that if IT is susceptible to replication by competitors, it is unlikely to create a sustainable advantage. Rather, it is only when IT is combined with complementary resources such as unique skills or knowledge-based assets and embedded within a process that IT can deliver a superior result [44, 57, 58, 59]. Similarly, the integration of IT and business activities at the process level can be causally ambiguous to the extent that it is difficult for others to identify how alignment is achieved. Knowing which IT resources a firm has is necessary but not sufficient for replicating alignment. For example, while firms in the PC industry can learn from media and vendor reports what IT Dell is using, this has not allowed competitors to execute an equally effective business model by simply copying Dell’s IT resources [37]. Consistent with Sambamurthy et al., who suggest that the “business value of IT emerges primarily through its complementarity and integration with business strategies” [65, p. 243], Dell’s success is perhaps less a function of its IT resource endowment than a reflection of how effectively IT is integrated into the fabric of its business processes [37].

## Conceptualizing Alignment at the Process Level

This last example is useful in that it helps us understand how idiosyncrasies in Dell’s strategy can lead Dell to concentrate IT resources in certain processes in the value chain. Specifically, a build-to-order strategy calls for IT to pay particular attention to supporting and enabling supply-chain activities and their broader links to production [37]. Dell’s focus on alignment in these processes contrasts with firms such as eMachines, whose use of IT in production and retail distribution has allowed it to dominate the low end of the market, and SGI, whose use of IT in R&D has propelled it to the forefront of the high-end workstation market. Each of these three firms has a different value proposition that is protected (at least in part) by the complex way that IT is integrated into their business processes. A firm-level study along the lines of past research might show that IT is aligned with strategy in each firm but yet the focus of IT and the processes where tight alignment is desirable are clearly different. For example, tight alignment is critical for SGI in R&D but since neither eMachines nor Dell is seen as an innovator, tight alignment in R&D is less critical. In contrast, tight alignment in inbound logistics and operations is critical to Dell, but as neither eMachines nor SGI rely on JIT (just in time) to the same extent as Dell, tight alignment in this area is not as important. Meanwhile, tight alignment in retail sales and logistics is vital for eMachines, whereas SGI and Dell (neither of whom uses traditional retail channels) do not face the same alignment pressures or constraints in these activities.

What this example shows is that there is variability in process-level alignment among firms with different strategic foci; variability not just in the extent of alignment as measured on a process-by-process basis but in the implications of failing to achieve tight alignment in core processes. There may be little or no variability in terms of firm-level alignment between firms with different strategic foci, as noted in past studies of alignment among specialty retailers by Palmer and Markus [51] and in a broader multi-industry study by Chan et al. [14] and Sabherwal and Chan [63]. However, if individual processes are considered, firms may feel less threatened by misalignment in noncore areas whereas others cannot accept anything less than perfect fit in those same areas; such insights can only come from a process-level analysis. It can also be difficult to detect process-level variability when using some of the better-known strategy typologies as they focus more on labeling the content of strategy rather than specifying how strategies are executed.

To formulate a conceptualization of alignment at the process level, we use this idea of the process of strategy to assess the scale and scope of activities or competitive actions undertaken by firms [65]. This bottom-up approach reveals what Mintzberg [50] calls an emergent strategy—highlighting what firms are actually doing rather than what they plan to do. Scale indicates the intensity of an activity that, as noted earlier, can vary by strategic focus [49]; scope indicates whether firms have a single focus or multiple foci—the latter being a potentially hazardous situation that Porter [54] describes as being stuck in the middle.

Rather than trying to gain a sense for the scale and scope of business activities by building a list of all activities in a firm (akin to a process map in process reengineering parlance), a simpler approach is to recognize that activities cluster into processes such as order processing, marketing, and so on, and so process headings can be used to collect data on a diverse set of interconnected activities and similarly on how IT is used to support these activities [21, 55]. Hence, conceptualizing alignment at the process level calls for a multiprocess profile to address the extent of alignment in each of several business processes that taken together span the firm. With reference to the right type of fit, a profile simply means that for each strategic focus, firms may want tight alignment is some processes only. Unlike a firm-level conceptualization that emphasizes the extent of fit, a process-level conceptualization is sensitive to the extent of fit within each process and the locus of tight fit within the value chain. Since the locus of fit can vary by strategic focus, as seen in our Dell, SGI, and eMachines example, we turn next to a prominent strategy typology to complete our conceptualization; only then can we derive our initial hypotheses regarding the right type of fit.

## Value Disciplines, Business Processes, and the Locus of Alignment

There has been a significant amount of research in recent years around Porter’s typology of low-cost leadership, niche, and differentiation [54] but there is some concern that the labels are confusing for those who are asked to identify their strategy as part of an academic study, while it is also difficult to map each strategy to processes in the value chain [69]. Recent research around the concept of value disciplines—which closely parallels Porter’s typology—uses more meaningful labels and, more importantly, maps to the processes in the value chain. While the value disciplines typology may seem new and untested, it has been the subject of much confirmatory study. For example, Weill and Broadbent [81] and Weill and Ross [82] use the typology in empirical work on IT infrastructure and IT governance types. Palmer and Markus [51] also use the typology in their study on alignment, although the retail nature of their data did not cover all areas of the typology (product leaders were not a clearly identifiable category in their sample).

As argued by Treacy and Wiersema [75], firms create value for their customers using one of three unique value disciplines. As summarized in Table 1, operationally excellent firms create value by offering customers a low-cost product or service while customer intimacy denotes a strategy of doing whatever it takes to meet the needs of customers who favor variety, availability, and service quality ahead of cost. The third strategy, product leadership, signifies firms who create value through groundbreaking innovation.

What is especially interesting about these value disciplines from an alignment perspective is their focus on certain processes. As empirical research shows, operationally excellent firms emphasize supplier relations and production, customer intimate firms emphasize sales and customer relations, while product leaders focus on product and service enhancement [51, 81, 82]. This assessment is helpful to our process-level conceptualization of alignment as it shows, for each value discipline, what processes are candidates for tight alignment. Extant theories of alignment that use a matching perspective would say, for example, that IT use should be more supportive and enabling of activities that form the basis of strategic differentiation [51]. Consequently, whatever processes are deemed essential to the successful implementation of strategic foci should be those processes where IT is used intensively and where alignment is tightest. Therefore, for the three value disciplines representing distinct strategic foci, we propose the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1(a): For operationally excellent firms, the primary locus of strategic alignment (i.e., where alignment is tightest) is in supplier relations and production and operations processes.

Hypothesis 1(b): For customer intimate firms, the primary locus of strategic alignment (i.e., where alignment is tightest) is in sales and marketing, and customer relations processes.

Hypothesis 1(c): For product leadership firms, the primary locus of strategic alignment (i.e., where alignment is tightest) is in product and service enhancement processes.

## The Performance Effects of Strategic Alignment

The earliest research on strategic alignment contains an implicit assumption that tighter alignment is somehow more beneficial for firm performance [30]. As shown in the top panel of Table 2, a significant body of empirical research appearing over the past decade confirms a positive link between alignment and firm performance. There are two points of interest regarding this body of research. First, performance has usually been operationalized using perceptual measures; this could produce spurious results if perceptions are distorted by measurement error or bias. Second, differences in strategy are not considered. When such differences are considered, as seen in the lower panel of Table 2, a different and more contradictory set of results emerge. Of the six studies that consider differences in strategy, only two found that alignment uniformly leads to higher firm performance for all strategy types. Specifically, Zahra and Covin [86] find for various strategy types based on Porter’s typology [54] that alignment has a positive effect on margin, while in a university setting, Sabherwal and Kirs [64] find that alignment uniformly contributes to perceived IS success and to education-specific success factors.

Snow typology [48], for example, Sabherwal and Chan [63] reveal a positive correlation between alignment and perceived measures of firm performance for prospectors and analyzers but not for defenders. In their discussion, Sabherwal and Chan argue that, “the importance of aligning IS strategy with business strategy may not be as universal as previously believed” [63, p. 25]. Using the same typology, Croteau and Bergeron [19] find that IT facilitates or supports strategy in prospectors and analyzers but, curiously, has a degenerative effect on strategy in defenders. In a study of specialty retailers, Palmer and Markus [51] find no significant differences using the Treacy and Wiersema typology [75] in five retail-specific performance metrics between firms that are aligned and those that are not. Palmer and Markus view this as evidence that “alignment has become institutionalized [to the point where it has ceased to be] a differentiating factor in firm performance” [51, p. 257]. Last, in combining data from prior research by Sabherwal and Chan [63] and Sabherwal and Kirs [64], Chan et al. [14] find that defenders again fail to realize any benefit from alignment. This result is troubling, as it confirms that “not all firms are equally well served by allocating scarce resources to improve IS alignment” [14, p. 39]. As these contradictory results emerge from firm-level studies, we ask whether a process-level conceptualization can help to explain why alignment is only selectively beneficial as repeated studies find defenders unable to profit from alignment; these studies have not tried to analyze why defenders are so disadvantaged. Instead, we can use a process-level perspective to show how alignment creates value within firms and to discover where alignment may have failed to create value. To develop this argument, we turn to the resource-based view and an evolving perspective on IT business value that argues that the first-order impacts of IT arise not at the firm level—as implicitly assumed in firm-level alignment studies identified in Table 2—but at the process level.

The resource-based view enables a process-level conceptualization of alignment to deliver further insights into the link between alignment and performance—insights that could be useful in understanding the contradictory results seen in the last paragraph. The theory recognizes that firms are different not only in the content of their strategy but in how those strategies are enabled by IT. The causal ambiguity of how alignment is created is, in particular, a key factor in whether tight alignment yields higher performance or not. Alignment may be causally ambiguous if business strategy and IT use are considered at the firm level but ambiguity grows even more if competitors or third parties try to unravel how IT is used at the process level or how processes evolve through IT. What this means is that the value created by IT inside a process can be difficult to replicate if alignment is difficult to replicate. Recalling our earlier discussion, we can see how Dell’s ability to integrate IT with its supplier base has produced significant benefits within its supply chain such as faster order handling, reduced errors, and more accurate capacity planning—benefits that other firms cannot easily match because the exact nature of Dell’s supplier relations is difficult to grasp. Process-level alignment can, therefore, be a source of superior and sustainable business value from IT.

Over time, a substantial body of work has developed within the IS literature in favor of evaluating the performance impacts of IT at the process level. Barua and

Mukhopadhyay [2] and Barua et al. [3] use theory and empirical analyses to make a compelling case for why it is appropriate to use the process level as the locus of first-order IT impacts. Studies that consider IT impacts at the firm level do so by implicitly aggregating impacts across various processes [2]. If we say that alignment is conceptually a process-level concept in that it represents the fit between processlevel activities and IT support for these activities, we can argue that the first-order impacts of alignment will likely occur at the process level also. For example, if in the area of inbound logistics or supplier relations, activities such as order processing, error handling, and delivery verification are enhanced or facilitated by IT, the ensuing alignment is likely to contribute to a reduction in errors, faster deliveries, and fewer instances of miscommunication. As argued by Barua and Mukhopadhyay [2], process-level impacts can lead to higher-order impacts at the firm level such as lower operating costs or higher profit, but at a base level, the primary impacts from tighter alignment in a given process will materialize first inside that process. Even then it can be argued that spillover effects can flow to downstream processes in the value chain. For example, in a manufacturing context, improper use of IT within the supply chain can cause delays in production and delivery, leading—especially for niche firms—to a fall in customer satisfaction. Equally, tight alignment in the supply chain can have a positive effect on downstream processes if, for example, sales staff can promise shorter delivery times, which leads to higher customer satisfaction and lower turnover. We summarize these arguments in the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: There is a positive relationship between alignment and IT business value at the process level, with the relationship being strongest when alignment and IT business value are considered within the same process.

## Measuring Alignment at the Process Level

VENKATRAMAN [76] DESCRIBES SIX PERSPECTIVES on the operationalization of fit, three of which apply to research involving a criterion or dependent variable (mediation, moderation, and profile deviation) and three that apply to research that is criterion free (matching, covariation, and gestalt). Because this study uses IT business value as a dependent variable in H2, we focus on the former three perspectives only. We also exclude mediation from consideration because a conceptualization of alignment as an interaction between IT use and business activities expressly omits an ordering of relationships such as IT use predicting business strategy, or vice versa. Accordingly, we model alignment at the process level as moderation (or a series of process-level interaction effects between IT use and business activities) and profile deviation only.

As Venkatraman [76] explains, even though the conceptual interpretation and functional form of profile deviation and moderation is different, both measures are complementary. Profile deviation as used in past studies by Chan et al. [14], Sabherwal and Chan [63], and Sabherwal and Kirs [64] considers alignment in the context of a deviation or difference from an ideal situation. For example, for a given business strategy, how different is a firm’s actual IT use from its ideal IT use where ideal is reflective of perfect alignment? In Sabherwal and Chan [63], ideal IT use is theoretically derived for each of six dimensions of the strategic orientation of business enterprises (STROBE). Moderation, as used in research by Chan et al. [15], is both conceptually and empirically different from profile deviation. Moderation means that the effect of a predictor variable on a dependent variable is conditional on the strength of a third moderator variable. For alignment, moderation means that the effect of business strategy on firm performance is conditional on IT use or, alternatively, the effect of IT use on firm performance is conditional on business strategy. There is no recourse to an ideal level of IT use and so it could happen that a moderating relationship exists but that IT use is less than ideal. Equally, moderation effects can be especially difficult to interpret [12, 15], while profile deviation has been criticized for how ideal profiles are constructed [63].

Recognizing that neither is a perfect measure of fit but that there are important complementarities when both measures are used side by side, we opted to use profile deviation and moderation but, in order to reduce the possibility of single-respondent bias, to apply a matched survey design. The development of dual sets of measures using data from different sources conforms to the multitrait multimethod approach to construct validation given by Campbell and Fiske [10]. As this is the first time that alignment has been examined at the process level, the use of multiple methods is a useful validation step. Yet it must also be noted that common measures of IT use apply to each method and so there is still some area of overlap. If each method yields varying results even with different respondents, our process-level conceptualization of alignment must be regarded with some suspicion, meaning alignment is better conceptualized at the firm level, but if there is consistency in the results from each method, we can be more confident in our overall findings. ## Measuring Alignment as Moderation

In previous research, Tallon [71] developed a 25-item survey to assess the extent to which certain business activities had been implemented at the process level. Based on validity and reliability analysis in that research, we were able to collapse the 25 items down to a five-item construct—one item per process. Respondents—namely, strategic planners—were shown a wide list of activities in each process and asked to evaluate, for each item, the extent to which these activities had been implemented inside their firm on a seven-point Likert scale anchored on “not implemented” and “fully implemented.” Similarly, to ascertain the extent of IT use in supporting these activities, we developed a matching five-item construct–one item per process–aimed at IT executives who were also given the same list of activities. Item responses were rated on a seven-point Likert scale anchored on “low IT use” and “high IT use.” In this way, alignment as moderation is modeled as a five-item construct: one item per process as the product of each process-level measure of IT use and its equivalent process-level measure of implemented business activities.

## Measuring Alignment as Profile Deviation

Of the two alignment methods, profile deviation poses the greatest challenge from a measurement perspective. The primary issue involves deciding what constitutes an ideal profile. Research has tended to apply either of two approaches. Sabherwal and Kirs [64], for example, use the top 10 percent of their sample (in terms of performance outcomes) to build an ideal profile that is then used to evaluate deviations between critical success factors and IT capabilities. A concern with this approach is that high-performing firms in a sample might not be high in the overall population and so generalizability of research results could suffer. In lieu of this data-driven approach, Sabherwal and Chan [63] base their ideal profiles on an interpretation of the literature. However, a concern here is that not all researchers will interpret the literature in the same way and so there may still be some lingering doubts as to what exactly an ideal profile looks like.

In this study, we opted to use a third approach based on a Delphi technique—an approach that can address some of the concerns raised in Sabherwal and Chan [63]. Accordingly, we contacted nine experts in the area of alignment (two IT directors at IBM Global Services, a BearingPoint senior manager, five IS academics, and an IT director at a Fortune 50 firm).<sup>4</sup> Each expert was first given a short tutorial on value disciplines specifying definitions and including some exemplar firms taken from Table 1 and from Treacy and Wiersema [75]. Each expert was then asked to review five process headings derived from the generic value chain (supplier relations, production and operations, product and service enhancement, marketing and sales, customer relations) and to determine for each process whether, based on differences in strategic foci or value disciplines, a firm’s use of IT ought to be above average, below average, or just average. This meant that for each process, an expert had to ask if any of the three value disciplines was an above average or intensive user of IT in that process, if they were a less than average user, or if they were average when all firms are considered together. It was not required that experts identify an above or below average user of IT in each process. If they felt that all firms—regardless of value discipline—made equal use of IT in a particular process, they could simply report “average IT use” for each of the three value disciplines.

Consistent with Sabherwal and Chan [63], experts’ ratings of above average, below average, and average were assigned scores of 1, –1, and 0, respectively, by the author—values that can be interpreted as the number of standard deviations away from a mean level of IT use. Thus, if an expert felt that within the area of supplier relations, operationally excellent firms were the most intense or above average users of IT relative to other focus types, this was scored as “1.” Then, if customer intimate firms were considered the least intense (below average) users of IT in supplier relations, they were scored “–1” with product leaders receiving an average or “0” score. This exercise was repeated for each process with scores being recorded in a 5 × 3 table (five business processes, three value disciplines). In Table 5, we report the modal scores for the overall panel (nine experts and the author). Analysis indicated a high degree of agreement among the panel, particularly as to the locus of above average ratings where nine of ten panelists were in agreement. For below average and average ratings, there was over 70 percent agreement in each process. Given this extent of interrater reliability, we opted to use modal ratings rather than an average of all individuals’ ratings.

The data in each column of Table 5 reflect an ideal level of IT use for a specific value discipline. In reality, firms are unlikely to pursue just one value discipline but rather some combination of value disciplines. Indeed, Treacy and Wiersema [75] suggest that although firms should endeavor to focus on just one value discipline, they should remain competent in all areas. Accordingly, each firm’s ideal IT use profile is a combination or weighted average of the data shown in Table 5—where weights are determined by how much emphasis is placed on each value discipline. To identify this weighting, IT executives were asked to allocate 100 points across all three value disciplines with their primary value discipline receiving the highest number of points. Past research has found this to be an effective way of gaining insight into a firm’s business strategy, while evidence of interrater reliability among executives in the same firm points to a shared understanding of strategy [72]. Finally, short descriptions were added to the survey to clarify the meaning of each value discipline. As strategic planners had already answered questions on strategy at the process level, it was felt inappropriate to ask them to respond to questions on value disciplines and so IT executives were used instead. Admittedly, it may seem unusual or risky to ask IT executives to assess value disciplines. To assuage fears that data supplied by IT executives may be faulty, we engaged a senior consultant at a boutique strategy consulting firm to do an independent assessment of value disciplines for 80 firms—33 percent of our sample. Using data from press releases, analysts, investor reports, or direct personal knowledge, the consultant was asked to decide a similar allocation of points for each value discipline. We then use paired sample t-tests to compare responses given by survey respondents with that of the consultant. As seen in Table 6, insignificant differences for each value discipline allow us to feel confident using IS executives as a source of data on value disciplines as their responses appear to be accurate and reliable.

## Measuring IT Business Value

Although literature reviews have identified a wide variety of firm-level metrics of IT impacts [22, 36, 45], the relative absence of objective process-level measures has complicated the task of evaluating IT impacts at this level [2, 74]. As an alternative, researchers have designed perceptual measures that employ managerial insight or intuition regarding IT impacts in various parts of the firm [43, 66, 68, 74]. Although perceptions are often regarded as biased or open to distortion [52]—a belief that has spurred suspicion of perceptual measures and qualitative methods more broadly [13, 47]—Tallon and Kraemer [73] report that executives’ perceptions of IT impacts at the firm and process level correlate with reality to such an extent that perceptions can be used as a surrogate for actual IT impacts when there are no directly observable or accessible objective measures of IT business value. Accordingly, we identified 25 process-level measures of IT business value (five per process) in Tallon et al. [74], measures that were found to be accurate and valid in previous studies. These items were included in the business executive’s survey with the following instruction: “How much impact has IT had on your firm’s performance in each of the following areas? Please limit your appraisal to value already realized rather than value expected in the future.” Data were collected on a seven-point Likert scale anchored on “low IT impact” and “high IT impact.”

## Construct Validation

To assess the robustness of the survey items distributed across both the IT executive and business executive survey, we first did a confirmatory factor analysis on 35 items measuring business strategy (five items), IT use (five items), and IT business value (five factors: five items each). We used EQS (a structural equation modeling tool) to conduct this analysis with reflective indicators for each factor and allowing all factors to correlate with one other. The fit of the resulting measurement model gives a somewhat mediocre set of fit statistics (χ<sup>2</sup>/degrees of freedom $[ \mathrm { d f } ] = 2 . 6 3 ;$ comparative fit index $[ \mathrm { C F I } ] = 0 . 8 3$ ; normed fit index $[ \mathrm { N F I } ] = 0 . 8 2$ ; root mean square error of approximation $[ \mathrm { R M S E A } ] = 0 . 0 8 )$ . Part of the reason for this, as we found in reviewing the path estimates in the measurement model, was that IT use and business strategy items did not load cleanly on their respective factors; some path estimates (or loadings in factor analysis parlance) were less than a suggested minimum cutoff of 0.6 [27]. What this shows is that IT use and business strategy do not form single factors; each item represents a single process and each process should be treated separately. If we remove IT use and business strategy items from the measurement model, leaving 25 IT business value items, the fit of the reduced model is more than adequate to justify retaining the five-factor structure.<sup>5</sup>

Descriptive statistics for all survey items appear in Table 7. We also reviewed all survey items for convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity identifies if the indicators of a factor correlate higher among themselves than with indicators of a different factor, whereas discriminant validity determines if the indicators of a specific factor load higher on that factor than on any other factor. To assess both forms of validity, the correlation between each factor-pair must be less than the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each factor. As reported in Table 8, all factors pass this test, confirming that convergent and discriminant validity are present. We note, however, that the square root of AVE for IT use and business strategy is below a recommended minimum of 0.71 [27], the reason being that each factor is not entirely unidimensional and so business strategy and IT use can be treated as separate process-level items, consistent with what we observed with the measurement model results. We also find a high degree of correlation between IT use and IT business value in Table 8, reminiscent of the known link between IT use and IT impacts [25, 40]. We also assess composite reliability; as seen in Table 8, composite reliability in each IT business value factor exceeds a minimum of 0.8 [84]. However, in the case of business strategy and IT use, composite reliability is less than 0.8, again indicating that these factors are not unidimensional and that each process-level measure of business strategy and IT use should be treated independently.

## Confirmation of Findings from Firm-Level Studies

As previously noted, a key motivation for attempting to reconceptualize alignment at the process level was to provide further insights into the anomalous results noted in past research where alignment did not appear to impact performance for firms espousing a defender strategy within the Miles and Snow [48] typology. An essential question is whether our analysis (if somehow reconstituted at the firm level) might find similar anomalous results. For this test, we first created a proxy for firm-level alignment as the mean of the five process-level alignment measures devised for each firm. Since we created two sets of process-level measures (namely, profile deviation and moderation), this step yielded two proxy measures for firm-level alignment in each firm. As our sample comprised publicly traded firms, we next extracted data from Compustat for return on assets (ROA), return on sales or net profit margin, markup (sales divided by cost of goods sold), and net income to assets ratio for 2002 (the year of the survey) through 2004.<sup>6</sup> These data were then averaged over this three-year period to create a more balanced view of performance. In order to find if there is a link between alignment and firm performance for each strategic focus—and so facilitate a comparison with prior firm-level studies—we next needed to classify each of the 241 firms in our sample as operationally excellent (OE), customer intimate (CI), or product leaders (PL). Thus, if respondents gave 50 or more points to any one value discipline, the firm was labeled as such. Firms that remained unlabeled at the end of this step have mixed foci. In this way, we classified our sample as follows: 96 (OE), 38 (CI), 33 (PL), 74 (mixed). Discriminant analysis on the raw value disciplines items validates this simple rule by correctly classifying 99 percent or 238 of the 241 firms.

## Discussion

WE BEGAN THIS PAPER BY ADVOCATING A RECONCEPTUALIZATION of alignment at the process level. Because strategy is executed through a web of interconnected business activities as a value chain, shop, or network [70], it makes sense to think about alignment as a bottom-up construct that identifies how IT supports and enables such activities. The approach historically favored by past firm-level research is more top-down in its use of broad brushstrokes to classify firms under an established firm-level typology. This approach has borne a legacy of useful insights as shown in Table 2 but its continued relevance is in doubt as researchers start to consider differences in alignment between strategies. Research shows that business strategies vary in ways from resource requirements to sustainability or profit potential [85, 86]. As such, it is misleading if not meaningless to compare alignment between firms when their strategies entail different foci. There is nothing wrong per se with continuing to see alignment as matching an IT strategy with a business strategy but once there is a conscious decision to consider differences in strategic foci, it is important to recognize that alignment does not mean the same thing to all firms. All firms might desire tight alignment but they do not all want the same alignment. As the results of our first hypothesis reveal (Table 11), tight alignment may be critical in some processes but not in others. The primary locus of alignment, meaning the process or processes where alignment is tightest, is, therefore, of paramount importance given the need for firms to execute their foci around specific processes. As seen by the results of our second hypothesis (Table 12), if alignment is tightest in these key processes, there is potential for higher IT business value, but if alignment is weak, firm performance could falter as firms struggle to execute their chosen strategic focus.

Our point is not to trivialize firm-level research on alignment but rather to build on it. In the same way that IT business value research has come to accept that the first-order impacts of IT materialize at the process level (making this a preferred level for studying IT impacts), our analysis views alignment as the link between IT use and business activities—process-level manifestations of how firm-level strategies are executed. Doing so not only allows us to evaluate alignment in different processes but to think about how firms with different foci might place more or less emphasis on alignment in certain processes. This allows us to fuse firm-level strategy typologies (namely, Treacy and Wiersema [75]) with process-level thinking, something that is clearly important when we consider the contradictory results found in the literature in recent years [14, 19, 51, 63]—seen in Table 2—results that we now review in more depth.

One of the problems emerging from recent research is the exhortation that some firms, principally defenders in the Miles and Snow [48] typology, may be better off ignoring alignment as it has zero impact on firm performance. This finding comes at a time when there is a renewed debate as to whether IT even matters [11]. While we use a separate typology, we find that operationally excellent firms—like defenders in their emphasis on low cost, efficiency, and standardization—fail to realize any performance gains from tighter alignment (Table 10). However, unlike past research, by conceptualizing alignment at the process level, we can move closer to saying why alignment is so insignificant for these firms. From the results of our first hypothesis (Table 11), we identify that operationally excellent firms minimize alignment in three areas of the value chain—product and service enhancement, sales and marketing, and customer relations. This might not seem unusual since low-cost firms neither innovate nor customize products and services to suit individual clients or markets.

Allocating scarce IT resources to support these activities—if they even exist—may appear wasteful. Instead, a focus on supplier relations or operations is—at least in theory—the path to differentiation. The problem, as seen earlier, is that customer intimate and product leadership firms are not above creating tight alignment in these areas also. Using alignment to cut cost or to enhance operating efficiency is something that all firms desire, regardless of focus. Tight alignment might not seem to have any effect on operationally excellent firms but the reality, perhaps, is one of survival rather than strategic differentiation. Firms should not interpret an apparent lack of benefit from alignment as evidence that IT no longer matters or that alignment has become irrelevant. We would caution, therefore, against an overly liberal interpretation of Palmer and Markus’s argument that “alignment [has ceased] to be a differentiating factor in firm performance” [51, p. 257]. Our results note that alignment matters very much as a source of differentiation among customer intimate and product leadership firms.<sup>11</sup> Furthermore, as reported in Table 12, process-level alignment continues to be associated with increased IT business value for all firms.

## Theoretical Implications

While Palmer and Markus [51] call for renewed thinking on the theory of alignment—given that for the retailers in their study, alignment had no impact on firm performance—the point is not so much to declare alignment irrelevant but to recognize that certain nuances need to be incorporated into the theory. Consistent with earlier research, we find that firm-level alignment (proxied by taking the mean of all five process-level measures) is related to firm performance in all firms (see Table 10). It is only when we look at differences in strategy that cracks begin to appear in the theory that alignment is always beneficial—we find that alignment is positively associated with firm performance for some strategic foci but not for all. It is only when we consider the processes through which strategy is executed that a process-level conceptualization can offer much-needed insight into why alignment is not a universal source of value for all firms.

If alignment is seen through the lens of the resource-based view, value comes not from replication but from uniqueness. If all firms are forging tight alignment in the same areas of the value chain, process-level alignment is unlikely to emerge as a driving factor in firm performance. This would appear to be the case for the firms in our sample as regards alignment in supplier relations and production and operations. Our results find that these processes are consistently outside the primary locus of alignment for all groups. Empirically, this implies that alignment in these two processes is not a source of differentiable value if all firms are equally aligned in both areas. Knowing this, we can forge a better theory of alignment by saying that while tight fit between IT and business strategy allows firms to have higher value from IT, the reality is that alignment in some areas of the value chain may be more susceptible to replication than others. The strategic management literature has frequently referred to the risks facing firms that compete solely on the basis of cost—irrational price wars, price sensitive customers, and declining margins [46, 54, 56, 85, 86]. Perhaps, therefore, as firms try to differentiate themselves, IT should not only support core operations but seek out and support unique areas of value as in firms such as Prosper (www.prosper.com; a leader in peer-to-peer banking), Zafu (www.zafu.com; a customer intimate designer of custom-fit jeans), and Netflix (www.netflix.com; online video rental). While it is outside the scope of this study, innovative business models and the IT supporting them reveal unique instances of process-level alignment that can be difficult to copy. From the perspective of theory refinement in future work, it may not be sufficient to think of alignment on an individual process-by-process basis, as in this study, but to think of a web or cluster of processes that together form a business model and to then use a gestalt approach to simultaneously evaluate alignment across the entire web of processes [70, 76].

As researchers call for more process-level research on IT business value [3, 59, 74], an expanded theory of alignment not only calls for alignment to be considered at the process level but also for management practices that are said to impact alignment to be evaluated at the process level as well. For example, research indicates how closer links between business and IT executives leads to greater fit between business and IS goals [33, 61]; a process-level conceptualization of alignment permits consideration of how IT executives interact with process-level executives or function leaders. Research on the role of IT in a turbulent setting may also help to explain why alignment is tighter in some areas than others [65, 80]. A flexible, adaptable IT infrastructure can be especially important to sustaining alignment in areas that are vulnerable to market change or that are under competitive assault [81, 83]. In summary, what this reveals is that a process-level conceptualization of alignment allows for a more detailed analysis of the role of alignment and the factors that shape alignment. Given recent studies on the need for agility and adaptability, a process-level view of alignment could also help to show how IT can enhance process responsiveness [3, 65].

## Research Contribution

This research contributes to the growing body of conceptual and empirical literature on alignment and IT business value in four ways. First, this is the first time that alignment has been conceptualized and evaluated at the process level. If IT impacts are “more likely to be detected at the process level than at the firm level” [35, p. 25], it is reasonable to expect that the first-order impacts of alignment will also arise at the process level. One of the questions posed at the start of this study was whether it was possible to even consider alignment at the process level when the literature was dominated by firm-level thinking. Besides arguing why it is appropriate to assess alignment at the process level, we outline a multimethod approach to measure alignment as profile deviation and moderation—methods that are taken from earlier firm-level research. We apply each method to a series of five primary processes taken from the generic value chain; consistency in the results obtained from each method attests to the validity of our process-level measures.

Second, we provide a way to reconcile “unusual” findings from prior research that contradicts the received view that more alignment is better. We argue that the goal of tighter alignment is still appropriate but that, in some cases, it may not yield superior results. Rather, tight alignment in some areas of the value chain is essential for competitive parity rather than competitive advantage.

Third, we find that the primary locus of alignment is related to each firm’s chosen strategic focus. Other researchers have used the Miles and Snow [48] typology to assess alignment but this is the first time that value discipline have been used to develop specific profiles or configurations of alignment in the value chain. In so doing, we build upon a growing body of research in the IS literature that recognizes the practical and academic value of the Treacy and Wiersema [75] typology. The freshness of this typology is not an affront to existing work but rather an opportunity for IS academics to include it in their research.

Fourth, we find that IT business value is positively related to alignment at the process level for all value disciplines. While value discipline choices can lead firms to concentrate resources in certain parts of the value chain, the fact that alignment and IT business value are closely related suggests that pockets of value can be created if firms pursue alignment outside the areas that define their primary value discipline. Furthermore, our results show that the effects of alignment can flow downstream in the value chain from one process to another; sadly, this can also mean that the damage from misalignment can be widespread.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are several limitations in the measures, methods, and data used in this study that restrict the generalizability of our results. First, the approach used to assess a firm’s value disciplines—allocating 100 points across all three value disciplines—is open to interpretation. Earlier research has nevertheless found this to be a reliable way to identify strategy although the use of multiple measures and respondents would provide a greater level of comfort [72, 73]. We did not use multiple respondents to confirm our measures. Instead, we verified our data using an external consultant. Future studies could use multiple measures and multiple respondents in order to provide a greater degree of confidence in how strategy is measured.

Second, our sample is based on firms with revenues ranging from \$100 million to \$3 billion. We purposefully omitted Fortune 500 firms from our population as they were the focus of a separate study. In theory, size may pose unique alignment challenges that are not reflected in our data or results. Therefore, we would caution against generalizing our results to firms outside this range. In addition, multidivisional firms can operate multiple strategies across different business units. To the extent that these units share IT resources, the link between alignment and IT business value (whether evaluated at a firm or process level) can become highly complex. Except for the fact that Fortune 500 firms were excluded from our study, we did not account for the number or type of business units of the firms in our sample, or indeed if there were industry effects present. This does not negate our findings necessarily, but it is useful for future research to consider the role played by shared IT resources if multidivisional firms are trying to simultaneously align IT with several (and potentially conflicting) business strategies.

Third, we use perceptual measures to capture data on process-level IT impacts in the value chain. The lack of objective measures of IT impacts at both the firm and process level poses a real challenge for IS researchers [2]. While perceptual measures have been found accurate and reliable in prior research [73, 78], our measures could diverge from reality. Respondent bias is an ever-present issue in survey research, especially when single respondents are used. Although matched surveys can alleviate the fear of common method bias [53], subjectivity and measurement error remain possible. Bias can be further compounded in multidivisional firms if IT business value varies across divisional lines. It may be possible to reduce such bias in future research by limiting analysis to single line-of-business firms or adding other controls.

Fourth, we measure IT use using just five items—one per process. Similar to business strategy, IT use is a complex construct that arguably should be captured by multiple measures. While past research and pilot tests suggest that single-item measures can be used, future research could benefit from the use of multiple measures per process, measures that would help to determine how IT is used to support different activities within each process rather than looking at a single IT use measure for each process.

Fifth, our data are cross-sectional. Although this does not refute our results, the fact that business and IT strategy are changing in many firms means that alignment is a moving target. There is a possibility that some of the firms in this study may have been in the midst of a change that led to, or gave the appearance of, misalignment, thus distorting the correlation between alignment and IT business value. Future research would benefit from studying alignment during periods of intense change or over an extended period.

## Conclusion

THE ALIGNMENT OF IT WITH BUSINESS STRATEGY remains a perennial concern for firms worldwide. The fact that alignment continues to perplex and frustrate executives after more than a decade of research and analysis highlights the true challenge of molding IT to fit the evolving needs of a business strategy. This research argues that firms meet this challenge by translating formal strategic plans into business activities and ways for IT to support the business. By evaluating alignment at the process level, we get a heightened sense for whether the locus of alignment is consistent with the goals of the firm. For example, a company that tries to differentiate itself on the basis of superior customer service is unlikely to achieve that goal if IT resources are focused on peripheral areas, starving the most critical activities of IT support. The locus of alignment—the process or processes where alignment is tightest—must reflect a firm’s strategic goals.

Using the resource-based view of the firm and the logic of organizational capabilities, researchers have begun to explore new ways of explaining how firms realize value from IT [5, 58, 65]. There is also a concern among researchers that some forms of alignment can create rigidities that lock firms into a certain way of doing business [5,

26, 38]. Future work could enhance our knowledge of rigidity traps by building on our process-level research to identify how configurations of alignment at the process level could cause or prevent such traps from occurring, or otherwise mitigate any adverse effects on firm performance.

Despite the cross-sectional nature of this study, alignment is not a static concept [7, 32, 79]. Much theoretical and empirical work remains to be done into how firms sustain alignment over time in response to shifts in the global business environment. The ideal alignment profiles described in this paper provide a useful framework for examining how firms can revise their IT use to reflect the realities of competing in a global market. We urge researchers to build on the process approach outlined in this study, perhaps with a view to longitudinal or longer-term analysis. The fact that alignment endures as a critical managerial issue shows that, even after two decades of research, executives need guidance and theory-driven explanations for how to translate their strategic foci and business goals into concrete actions and how to use new forms of IT innovation such as utility computing or Web services to support the business. Process-level research, we suggest, is a useful position from which to continue this noble endeavor.
