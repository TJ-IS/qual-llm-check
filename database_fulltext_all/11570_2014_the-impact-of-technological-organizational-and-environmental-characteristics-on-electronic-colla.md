The Impact of Technological, Organizational and Environmental Characteristics on Electronic Collaboration and Relationship Performance in International Customer-Supplier Relationships

## Abstract

The literature suggests that advances in information and communication technologies have been a major driver in restructuring multinational enterprises and their cross-border supply chain management. However, the role of information technology usage for collaboration, and its antecedents and performance implications in cross-border exchange relationships, has not been clearly specified. In response to this claim, this study examines determinants and outcomes, for suppliers, of electronic collaboration (E-collaboration) with their international customers. Drawing on an empirical foundation of 240 Taiwanese-based electronics equipment manufacturers, we test the effects of technological, organizational and environmental dimensions on E-collaboration, and its impact on relationship performance in international exchange. Findings on the pertinence of E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships are presented and discussed.

## Keywords

E-collaboration; relationship performance; cross-border exchange; international exchange; supplier-customer relationship.

## Introduction and motivation

Globalization has driven firms to outsource their key activities to external suppliers and extend the boundaries of the firm across borders. This trend toward outsourcing and offshoring is making the management of interfirm relationships and the enhancement of relationship performance increasingly important. While firms can achieve competitive advantage by delivering better relationship performance and creating greater customer value in global supply chains, they have increasingly relied on information technology (IT) for collaborative interfirm activities such as collaborative planning, forecasting and replenishment (CPFR).

Although the importance of IT usage for electronic collaboration (E-collaboration) is uncontested, its role in cross-border relationships is not well documented in the literature. In fact, empirical work on the role of IT in interfirm collaboration in cross-border relationships is limited and incomplete. While there are studies on E-collaboration conducted in domestic

#

market settings [33, 46], very limited empirical attention has been given to such collaborations in cross-border exchange relationships [31]. That is, the literature has remained mute on the potential benefits electronic interfirm collaborations can offer, despite globalization having resulted in a profusion of interfirm relationships across national borders [28] and E-collaboration having been shown to be an effective tool for enhancing interfirm relationships. Filling this gap in the literature, this study explores the impacts of electronic interfirm collaborations on firm performance in the context of cross-border exch relationships.

In order to address these issues in the literature comprehensively, this study empirically tests a model of the interrelations between the drivers and performance outcomes of E-collaboration in the context of cross-border buyer-seller relationships. Drawing on Kim and Lee [33] and Subramani [52], E-collaboration in this study refers to the extent to which interorganizational systems (IOS) between exchange partners are configured to carry out collaborative channel activities including interfirm forecasting, planning and product development. This study focuses particularly on supply chain communication systems (SCCS) [62], which are defined as the type of interorganizational information system shared by suppliers and their international customers in order to carry out supply chain activities ranging from operational-level activities such as tracking orders and sending invoices to strategic-level ones such as collaborative demand forecasting, planning and general forecasting [60]. This study tests the proposed model of E-collaboration empirically in the context of international customer-supplier relationships between Taiwanese original equipment manufacturer (OEM) suppliers and their multinational enterprise (MNE) customers in the electronics industry.

## Theoretical background

As defined above, we use E-collaboration to mean the configuration of IOS to support collaborative channel activities between exchange partners. Similar to Subramani and Venkatraman’s [53] conceptualization of supply chain management systems, E-collaboration in this study captures the intentionality and pattern of IOS use. In addition, we view Ecollaboration as a technology innovation that shifts away from traditional and routine electronic transactions and information exchange towards more strategic collaborative activities, through SCCS.

#

In order to develop an overarching framework that delineates the contextual factors driving E-collaboration and its value creation outcomes, we rely on the technological, organizational and environmental context (TOE) framework [56], contingency theory [58] and the resource-based view (RBV) [4] to develop the theoretical model. The TOE framework identifies three aspects of a firm’s context that influence its technology adoption/innovation: (1) Technological context describes both the existing technologies in use and new technologies that are relevant to the firm. (2) Organizational context refers to measures describing the organization, such as scope, size, organizational culture and the amount of slack resources available internally. (3) Environmental context is the arena in which a firm conducts its business: its industry, competitors and its dealings with governments. Like the TOE framework, contingency theory, from the information systems (IS) literature, stresses the importance of technology, organization and environment as key variables that explain the technology adoption of firms [27, 44, 57]. While contingency theory seeks to understand interrelationships and the “fit” between strategy, structure, organizational resources and the environment, to explain firm performance [58], it has also been applied to information systems research, where IT adoption is viewed as a function of strategy, resources, structure, context and environment [27, 44, 57]. Drawing on these two major streams of literature, in this study we adopt technological, organizational and environmental variables to explain interfirm E-collaboration in international customersupplier relationships.

In terms of the relationship between E-collaboration and firm performance, we rely on the RBV, which argues that E-collaboration can be seen as a valuable and rare IT capability for suppliers in dealing with their international customers. In the information systems literature, the RBV has been used to analyze IT capabilities and to explain how IT’s business value resides more in the organization’s skill at leveraging IT than in the technologies [6]. For example, in a systematic review, Wade and Hulland [59] discuss different sets of IT resources, such as outside-in and inside-out IT resources, and their association with firm performance. The most accepted notion of RBV in the analysis of the business value of IT is that IT per se cannot generate sustained advantage because it can be relatively easily commoditized through competitive imitation and acquisition [32]. However, the advantage of IT can be protected by embedding it in an organization through complementarity and cospecialization. Complementarity exists when the value of IT resources is enhanced by the presence of another resource [11, 55]; co-specialization exists if one resource has little or no value without another [11, 55]. According to the RBV, E-collaboration, which integrates various strategic activities between exchange parties in the supply chain, is a key outside-in IT resource that cannot easily be imitated and has the potential to create business value. Adopting this view from the literature, in the current study we expect that active Ecollaboration between an international buyer-seller pair enhances the performance of each.

## Conceptual framework and hypotheses

Combining the TOE framework, contingency theory and the RBV, we develop a conceptual framework that links E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships with contingent antecedents and relationship performance. As shown in Figure 1, a set of antecedents is identified as the drivers of E-collaboration in international customersupplier relationships. We identify these antecedents by connecting the TOE framework with contingency theory. As discussed earlier, the extent of E-collaboration is influenced by its technological, organizational and environmental contexts within the TOE framework. In terms of technological context, we include IT advancement in this study because IT-related dimensions such as IT infrastructure have been well documented in the TOE framework as key components of technological innovation [66]. Contingency theory also highlights the importance of technology capabilities in driving the adoption of a higher level of IT [54]. Ecollaboration, a higher level of business-to-business (B2B) IT adoption, requires advanced supply chain technology such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) as a platform foundation [66], and thus offers a strong rationale for the consideration of IT advancement in our framework. In terms of organizational context, we include customer orientation as an internal factor because the RBV argues that certain organizational intangibles such as customer orientation can complement IT-enabled value [6]. Customer orientation has received a lot of attention in the literature as a key strategic resource and a driver of firm innovation. Contingency theory has been used to link customer orientation with IT adoption to generate a strategic fit. Customer orientation requires the acquisition and dissemination of customer information, which creates information-processing needs. According to contingency theory, E-collaboration can supply strategic information such as customer preferences, thus facilitating information-processing capabilities that fit with the information-processing needs generated by customer orientation [34]. Thus, we include customer orientation as an organizational context in the framework.

#

In terms of interorganizational context, we include customer dependence in the framework because power-dependence plays a crucial role in shaping interorganizational relationships. According to contingency theory, customer dependence creates informationprocessing needs in relation to key customers. E-collaboration can provide strategic information such as planning and forecasting to fit with these information-processing needs. Environmental uncertainties have been identified as crucial variables in the prior literature, both in the TOE and contingency theory streams. According to contingency theory, environmental uncertainties create information-processing needs. E-collaboration can generate strong information-processing capabilities to better manage uncertainties. Therefore, we include environmental uncertainties as a key environmental context variable.

In addition, the communication culture is argued to be a unique feature of the international context, with the ability to influence firms’ innovation adoption in international customer-supplier relationships [29]. According to Hall [23], people in a high-context culture (e.g. Japan, China) rely on communication more than those in a low-context culture (e.g. the US, Germany). That is, people from a high-context culture try to obtain information mainly from their personal information networks. People from low-context cultures, however, seek information from a solid research base or use information sources such as databases and the Internet. Thus, the communication culture is another critical environmental factor that tends to affect E-collaboration. This is particularly important in interorganizational relationships that transcend cultures. However, the prior literature has paid little attention to the effect of communication culture on IT adoption. Thus, we include the communication culture as another environmental context in our model.

In addition, the RBV argues that electronic integration is a critical IT capability that leverages advanced technologies in order to conduct key activities in the value chain, which in turn enhance the firm’s competitive advantage [59]. Thus, drawing on the RBV perspective [6], our framework posits a direct link between E-collaboration and performance in international exchanges.

## Technological context

IT advancement measures the degree of proactive adoption and implementation of advanced IT to find customer solutions ahead of one’s competitors [62]. Specifically, we focus on the advancement of IT in SCCS, such as the eXtensible Markup Language (XML) that offers a more open and compatible platform for firms conducting systems integration and collaboration in the supply chain context [67]. Open, Internet-based IOS can provide a wider trading partner base than traditional electronic data interchange (EDI) and can facilitate collaborative channel activities as a result. For example, certain XML-based standards can enable indexing and searching for new buyers and suppliers [67]. Frohlich [21] notes that a web-based technology now permits upstream as well as downstream supply chain integration in areas such as inventory planning, demand forecasting and order scheduling.

Furthermore, advanced IT systems may offer superior analytic abilities, providing decision support, via forecasting and scenario-based planning for example, and thereby stimulating interfirm collaboration. Wu et al. [62] also argue that the use of advanced IT in SCCS, with built-in and advanced functions, can help to recognize and bridge the gaps between multiple information systems, making firms with diverse levels of information systems more ready to collaborate. In the context of international customer-supplier relationships, geographical and cultural distances between exchange parties make Ecollaboration more difficult. Thus, the facilitative effect of IT advancement on Ecollaboration is expected to be more salient in the international context. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H1: The supplier’s IT advancement positively affects E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships.

## Organizational context

## The influence of customer orientation on E-collaboration

Customer orientation is defined as the process and orientation of a firm in terms of gathering and tracking information about customer needs and market changes [41]. The extant literature has considered customer orientation to be a key factor in firms’ innovation adoption. Customer orientation involves the generation and dissemination of, and the reaction to, market intelligence and knowledge in response to market needs [39]. According to the customer experience literature [20], E-collaboration can create an Internet-mediated environment that facilitates frequent and rich interactions and coordination between

Hence, a customer-oriented supplying firm that actively engages in information generation and dissemination in its exchange relationships will be more willing to use IT for collaboration to create a better customer experience. In addition, a market-oriented firm will have a strong commitment to serve its customers and will thus be more willing to allocate resources to fulfilling its customers’ needs through the development of tech-based innovation [65]. Thus, collaboration to fulfill its international customers’ needs.

## H2: The supplier’s customer orientation positively affects E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships.

Customer dependence is defined as the extent to which a customer relies on a supplier to provide it with a large proportion of its purchasing volume [24, 42]. A customer is more likely to be dependent on a supplier if the supplier offers unique products and services. In the prior literature, interorganizational structure, incorporating elements such as power and dependence, has been viewed as one of the key antecedents to firms’ adoption of interorganizational systems [10, 24]. For example, Hart and Saunders argue that customer power has a positive effect on supplier volume and diversity of EDI use. Customer dependence can create uncertainty and information-processing needs [34]. A dependent customer may have a strong requirement for timely and frequent information such as inventory, forecasting and planning to be provided by the supplier. From a contingency theory perspective, E-collaboration can generate information-processing capability and a fit with the information-processing needs of the dependent customer [34, 43]. Thus, in more customer-dependent relationships, the suppliers have stronger incentives and will be more likely to conduct E-collaboration with international customers. Thus,

H3: Customer dependence positively affects E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships.

## Environmental context

The influence of the communication culture on E-collaboration

Hall [23] focuses on the characteristics of communication and classifies cultures in different countries into high- versus low-context cultures. According to Hall [23], people in high-context cultures (e.g. Japan, China) rely on communication more than those in lowcontext cultures (e.g. the US, Germany). That is, people from high-context cultures try to obtain information mainly from their personal information networks. On the other hand, people from low-context cultures seek information from a research base or use information sources such as databases and the Internet. Given that the main power of electronic integration is to facilitate communication between exchanges partners, the communication cultures of the two partners may be a critical determinant of electronic integration in international channel relationships. However, only a limited amount of research has applied Hall’s classification to the investigation of B2B innovation adoption, such as E-collaboration, in international channel relationships.

Based on Hall’s theory, it is logical to argue that businesses in high-context cultures are likely to prefer in-person contact to carry out everyday B2B activities, and such a preference will reduce the need for IT-supported coordination. In contrast, businesses in lowcontext cultures may prefer to use codified information sources, thus gaining from electronic coordination and collaboration in their international channel relationships. Consequently, we follow Hall’s classification of communication cultures and hypothesize the following effect of communication culture on E-collaboration:

H4: With a customer from a low-context culture, the supplier is likely to achieve a higher extent of E-collaboration.

The influence of environmental uncertainty on E-collaboration

Environmental uncertainty is defined as the speed with which the environment changes [35, 50]. An uncertain business environment is characterized by changing customer demand, unpredictable competitor actions or fluctuating sales volumes. In a competitive environment such as the global electronics industry, rapid fluctuations in demand and supply conditions cause a degree of uncertainty because of the difficulties associated with making accurate predictions.

The prior literature has discussed the impact of different dimensions of environmental uncertainty on the adoption of B2B technology. However, the empirical evidence is mixed. For example, Wong et al. [61] find that environmental uncertainty is negatively related to the impact of information integration and firm performance. However, Grover [22] finds no significant relationship between environmental uncertainty in terms of demand uncertainty, and market volatility and IOS integration. Information and coordination requirements go up exponentially in an uncertain environment because of the possibility of excess capacity or stock outs [22]. E-collaboration can leverage opportunities in a highly uncertain environment by providing timely forecasts, helping with planning, and maintaining flexibility in response to changing markets, all at a low cost. Thus, by deploying a higher level of E-collaboration, firms can access real-time information and offer flexibility and efficiency benefits to their partners. Thus, they are able to respond to the coordination needs created by uncertain environments [22].

However, a tight integration through a higher level of E-collaboration may cause adaptation difficulties and introduce barriers regarding supplier switching. Hence, Ecollaboration has the potential to create strategic costs [30]. Researchers suggest that greater environmental uncertainty is associated with looser organizational forms such as electronic brokerage, as this permits ongoing adaptation and the search for more collaborative relationships [22]. Further, a volatile physical and economic environment may present a barrier to the supplier’s reliance on electronic coordination and collaboration with the international customer. This is related to the difficulty associated with obtaining necessary and accurate information in order to effectively coordinate and monitor the partner’s activities electronically. Under a volatile environment, product and market information changes quickly, which makes it more difficult to exchange timely information [61]. Davis-Sramek et al. [14] argue that B2B e-commerce systems may create formalized routines, which may not cope well with environmental changes. Research also shows that firms may need other sources of information, such as fax machines or telephones, rather than relying on information integration alone [61]. In an international exchange relationship where environmental uncertainty is exacerbated due to the geographical and cultural distance between the exchange partners [50], the supplier may not be willing to conduct close Ecollaboration with the international customer, wanting to maintain its adaptability and in the volatile environment. The strategic cost may exceed the flexibility and efficiency consideration for suppliers employing a higher level of E-collaboration under a highly uncertain environment. Thus, we predict,

H5: Environmental uncertainty negatively affects E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships.

## The impact of E-collaboration on relationship performance

Relationship performance is defined as the extent to which the supplier’s relationships with MNE customers are productive and rewarding [50]. A well-performing relationship exists if both the supplier and the customer are satisfied with the relationship’s effectiveness and efficiency. E-collaboration provides better information-processing capabilities, helping suppliers with their operational and coordination processes to do with

#

their international customers. Therefore, it is expected that E-collaboration will enhance suppliers’ performance by reducing transaction costs and creating transaction value.

In line with the RBV, E-collaboration is regarded as a valuable outside-in IT source that can generate higher relationship performance by improving relationship effectiveness and efficiency. In terms of the efficiency of relationships, E-collaboration can help firms to exchange operational-level information such as inventory and warehousing information. This helps suppliers better deal with inventory and cost control, which results in lower overall costs [49]. Further, due to the nature of the open standards of E-collaboration, suppliers can reach a broader scope of customers for a lower cost. In terms of relationship effectiveness, E-collaboration helps suppliers to exchange strategic information about aspects such as planning, forecasting and new product development with their customers. This helps suppliers to develop better new products and services in these collaborative relationships. Further, E-collaboration may facilitate absorptive capacity in terms of the assimilation and generation of new knowledge in the collaborative relationships, resulting in better relationship value [38]. Kim and Lee [33] show empirically that E-collaboration is positively related to market performance in supply chain relationships. Therefore, despite the mixed results on IT’s business value in the extant literature, we argue that electronic integration is expected to reap benefits for suppliers in their relationships with their international customers:

H6: The supplier’s E-collaboration positively affects their relationship performance in their international customer-supplier relationships.

## Control variables

In testing the hypotheses, in our model we control for plausible relationships between the variables. First, we include the direct links between the antecedents of E-collaboration and relationship performance. Prior work has indicated the roles of innovativeness, trust, dependence and environmental uncertainties on relationship performance. In addition, previous studies have shown that cultural distance has a unique influence on the way a company governs their international partners [64]. Thus, we adopt Hofstede’s [25] conceptualization of culture and control for its effect on E-collaboration and relationship performance. We have no a priori expectations as to the directions of these effects.

## Methodology

## Research context and sample

We used the survey method to obtain data for our empirical test. It has been argued that survey methods may provide better reliability than qualitative approaches [45]. Survey methods are frequently adopted to examine organizational and technology innovation issues [67, 68]. The unit of analysis in this study is the specific subcontracting relationship between Taiwanese suppliers and their international MNE customers. The focus on Taiwanese suppliers as the unit of analysis is deliberate as their cultural context is significantly different from the majority of their international customers. Thus, there is cultural distance. Moreover, the industry context features environmental volatility, which makes it difficult for them to make accurate sales forecasts, monitor trends in the industry, or accurately predict business trends. Furthermore, the Taiwanese electronics industry is a pioneer in terms of the development of IT, and contributes significantly to the global industry structure and the world economy [15].

The key informants in this study are senior original equipment manufacturer (OEM) / original design manufacturer (ODM) international subcontracting relationships with branded firms, and have knowledge about IT.

The sampling frame for our survey comprises all electronic companies from the year 2007 directory of the Top 5000 Largest Firms in Taiwan, published by China Credit Information Service Ltd (a total of 1069 companies). All firms in the database were contacted to assess their eligibility and to locate appropriate informants for the study.

To maximize the response from the Taiwanese-based electronics companies, which have been ‘over-used’ as information providers for management studies in recent years, we followed Yu and Cooper’s [63] suggestion of combining telephone and personal contact approaches. We also sent questionnaires by e-mail for those respondents who preferred this method. For the telephone and e-mail surveys, each informant who agreed to participate in this study was faxed or emailed a questionnaire packet. In all, 240 useable questionnaires were returned, resulting in an effective response rate of 22.72% (240/1056). The respondents’ characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Non-response bias was assessed by classifying the responses into two groups, early responses and late responses [1]. Furthermore, independent t-tests on demographic variables such as revenue and number of employees were performed. No significant difference was identified. We also ran other t-tests between these two groups on key variables from the proposed conceptual model, such as IT advancement. Again, no significant difference emerged, hence we inferred that non-response bias did not present a problem in our database [1].

## Targeted sample size, sampling frame, and data collection

In determining the required sample size, a priori, for an adequate level of statistical power [47], we used Subramani and Venkatraman [53] and Kim and Lee [33] to predict our parameters, as their context of buyer-seller relationships offers reasonable grounds on which to estimate the parameters of our study. In Subramani and Venkatraman [53], the standardized estimates that are statistically significant range from .23 to .35. In Kim and Lee [33], the significant parameters range from .22 to .62. For a conservative estimate, we used the least effect size of .22 from those two studies to determine the required minimum sample size for our study at 5% for an effect size of 80% or greater, using GPower 3.1.4 [9, 13, 18]. According to the result, we required a minimum sample size of 123. Furthermore, the literature suggests that a minimum of five observations per parameter be used in structural equation modeling (SEM) [5, 8]. In our model, there are 30 parameters to be estimated, requiring a minimum sample size of 150 according to the latter guideline. Therefore, to exceed the minimum sample size requirement for SEM and the statistical power for the anticipated effect sizes, we targeted a minimum of 200 observations in our data collection.

## Measurement

Multi-item scales and seven-point response formats were used to operationalize all variables. The measurement approach for each theoretical construct in our model is described briefly below.

## E-collaboration

E-collaboration refers to the extent that a firm’s information systems are integrated with those of its supply chain partners so that they are ready for potential interfirm collaborative activities such as forecasting and planning. We adapted a scale from Wang et al. [60] for this.

#

## IT advancement

A three-item scale was used to measure IT advancement. It was taken from Wu et al [17, 62] and adapted to our context. These items measure the extent to which a firm has adopted the most sophisticated technology in the context of SCCS.

## Buyer dependence

Building on the work of Lusch and Brown [37], we used three items to measure the international customer’s dependence on the supplier.

## Customer orientation

The OEM/ODM supplier’s customer orientation was based on the work done by Deshpandé et al. [16] and modified to the customer-supplier context. It was operationalized as a construct comprising three measures that address the supplier’s orientation towards generating, disseminating and responding to market intelligence.

## Communication culture

According to Hall’s [23] approach, countries can be classified into high- and lowcontext cultures based on the way messages are communicated within the society. We formed high- and low-context culture groups of the suppliers’ international customers based on Hall’s classification.

## Environmental uncertainty

This four-item construct captures the changeability of market shares, the difficulty of monitoring trends, the stability of industry volumes and the accuracy of sales forecasts, and we borrowed it from Skarmeas et al. [50].

## Relationship performance

Relationship performance is defined as the extent to which the suppliers consider their relationships with international customers worthwhile, equitable, productive and satisfactory. We developed a scale consisting of eight items, based on previous work by Selnes and Sallis [49].

## Analysis and results

## Common method bias

When a survey method is used to collect data using a single source, common method bias is always a potential threat. To assess the extent to which common method bias might affect our results, we conducted a test for potential common method bias using a hierarchically nested covariance structure model offered in the literature [e.g., 12]. As recommended in the literature, we estimated three models: a trait-only model (M2), a method-only model (M3), and a trait-and-method model (M4). The results are reported in Table 2 along with the results of testing for the presence of trait factors and testing for the presence of a method factor. According to the results, variances from both construct items (or traits) and the method were found in the data. This suggests that a portion of the covariance originated from the method used to collect the data. To assess the presence of such method variances, we calculated the mean percentages of variance explained by the construct items and by the common method factor. The results show that the mean percentage of variance explained by the construct items is 68.2% while that of the common method factor is only 3.2%, indicating that common method bias is minor [12]. ## Discussion and implications

With the growth in international outsourcing activities, managing interorganizational relationships has become critically important. E-collaboration, an advanced form of IT innovation, has increasingly been adopted as an effective coordination and collaboration mechanism in interfirm relationships. This is particularly relevant in international exchanges because cross-border coordination is challenging due to the cultural and geographic distance involved. However, there is limited understanding of the role that E-collaboration plays in cross-border exchange relationships. We make a sound contribution by developing and testing an integrated, theoretically grounded model, delineating the drivers and performance outcomes of E-collaboration in the cross-border context. Drawing from the TOE framework and contingency theory, we investigate IT advancement, customer orientation, customer dependence and environmental uncertainty as key drivers of E-collaboration. Further, based on Hall’s [23] conceptualization, we examine the impact of communication culture on the degree of electronic integration in international exchange relationships. Moreover, drawing from the RBV, we examine the performance outcomes of E-collaboration in international exchange relationships. The results of this study have implications for theoretical development.

In terms of the antecedents of E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships, the results show that supplier IT advancement, the technological context in the TOE framework, has a significant positive effect on E-collaboration. This is consistent with prior findings that IT advancement sets a key foundation for a firm’s adoption of strategic information systems such as E-collaboration or electronic integration in supply chain relationships [28]. In addition, against our expectations, the results show that customer orientation, an organizational antecedent, has no significant effect on E-collaboration. One plausible reason may be in line with the debate on the impact of customer orientation on firm innovation in the marketing literature [2]. The logic is that the customer-oriented supplier may focus too much on current business trends, thus ignoring emerging opportunities and overlooking the potential for future radical innovation [51]. Our research focuses only on the reactive dimension of customer orientation and does not investigate the proactive dimension. Customer-oriented supplying firms may focus too much on current or expressed customer needs and fail to look into customers’ future and latent needs. E-collaboration is an explorative form of IT use and technological innovation, and a proactively oriented organizational culture is needed to increase its level of adoption. Future research should investigate the impact of proactive customer orientation [7] on E-collaboration.

Furthermore, the other organizational context, customer dependence, has a significant and positive impact on E-collaboration. While there are potential lock-in risks of E-

#

collaboration in interfirm relationships, suppliers are more willing to adopt it with more dependent customers. The findings show that, while the lock-in risks still exist with dependent customers, forward-thinking suppliers may be willing to adopt E-collaboration to build new customers or relationships through existing dependent customers. The findings also elaborate the arguments that Internet-based E-collaboration significantly lowers transactional specificities in exchange relationships, thus reducing transactional risks [67].

Regarding environmental uncertainty, the findings show that environmental uncertainty presents a barrier and thus hinders the willingness to utilize E-collaboration as a coordination mechanism between international suppliers and customers. The specific international context, which exacerbates the geographical and cultural distance between the exchange partners, reduces the willingness to integrate closely via electronic forms. While this could potentially allow exchange partners to maintain adaptive responsiveness and flexibility, it seems that environmental uncertainty reduces the adoption of E-collaboration.

Finally, this study verifies that the communication culture between the exchange parties in international customer-supplier relationships plays a crucial role in determining the degree of E-collaboration. Communication culture, which emphasizes the context in which the exchange parties communicate with each other, has a significant impact on Ecollaboration. Our research finds that a low-context culture, a communication culture characterized by explicit information and messages, can facilitate the adoption of Ecollaboration. In contrast, a high-context culture emphasizing personal contact and face-to face interaction may inhibit the adoption of E-collaboration in cross-border exchange relationships. Some earlier studies have argued as to the potential impact of culture on IT adoption. However, only a limited amount of empirical work has focused on the communication culture and empirically established its impact on E-collaboration in international exchange relationships.

In terms of the performance implications of E-collaboration, this study finds that there are positive significant relationships between E-collaboration and relationship performance. This is consistent with the RBV of IT’s business value. E-collaboration is a critical IT resource for suppliers in dealing with international customers, which can ultimately lead to higher relationship performance. Our finding demonstrates that E-collaboration does matter for suppliers in driving competitive advantage in international exchange relationships.

#

Our study has several managerial implications. First, managers should change their intentions regarding IOS use from traditional coordination tasks such as transaction and information exchange to advanced collaborative activities including planning, forecasting and new product development. E-collaboration, in this study, is shown to have a positive impact on firms’ relationship performance in international customer-supplier relationships. Second, managers should consider the effects of E-collaboration under different contingencies, including technological, organizational and environmental contexts. Our research shows that contingency factors have different effects on the adoption of E-collaboration. IT advancement has a strong effect on E-collaboration. Thus, firms should develop an advanced supply chain IT infrastructure to ensure a more advanced level of E-collaboration. Interorganizational interdependence also shapes IT use. A dependent customer can stimulate a firm to adopt a more advanced level of E-collaboration. In addition, managers should consider the conditions of their business environment when adopting different levels of E-collaboration. Ecollaboration can be better leveraged to drive firm value in less volatile and uncertain environments. Finally, cross-cultural differences in terms of the communication context also shape the development of E-collaboration. E-collaboration can be better developed when a firm is dealing with an international partner from a low-context culture. In contrast, a highcontext culture may inhibit firms’ IT usage in cross-border exchange relationships.

## Contribution of the study

This study makes the following contributions: First, we combine TOE, the RBV and contingency theory to examine the antecedents and outcomes of E-collaboration in crossborder relationships. Hence, we extend the TOE framework by integrating different relevant theories into it. Recent research has called for a synthetic approach to TOE framework application [3]. Second, the focus of the majority of the E-collaboration research has been either the relationship between E-collaboration and its antecedents or that between Ecollaboration and associated performance outcomes. Very few studies have attempted to model the entire relationship, while focusing on the role of E-collaboration as a mediator and discussing its drivers and outcomes simultaneously, particularly in an international context. Finally, we extend the environmental context dimension of the TOE framework and of contingency theory by investigating the impact of communication culture on E-collaboration. Prior work has paid little attention to the effect of national cultural differences on organizational technology adoption.

#

## Limitations and future research

This study addresses the role of E-collaboration in cross-border partnerships by examining its consequences and antecedents. The findings need to be evaluated with regard to the following limitations. First, the study employs cross-sectional data, which excludes tests for reciprocal causation between E-collaboration and its antecedents, and its influence on a supplier’s relationship performance. These reciprocal causal relationships are suggested in the literature, and therefore should be given attention by future researchers. Second, the conceptualization of E-collaboration captures the intentionality of IOS integration and does not capture the functionality and configuration of IOS integration [22]. Further research could extend our study to examine other dimensions of IOS integration. Finally, this study only focuses on examining the effect of communication context on E-collaboration in international exchange relationships. Future research could examine other cross-cultural difference effects in the literature, such as the effect of institutional distance [48] on IT use in international exchange relationships.

## Conclusions

Globalization is driving firms to develop different types of international exchange relationship with both upstream and downstream partners. Managing international customersupplier relationships has become a critical issue for firms’ competitiveness. Advances in IT have enabled firms to develop new forms of IT-mediated collaboration with their international partners. IT-enabled collaboration helps firms to reduce costs and enhance their competitive advantage in the international markets. Our study makes a unique contribution to this emerging field in the literature by developing and testing a model of the antecedents and outcomes of E-collaboration in international customer-supplier relationships. However, the field is only beginning to develop an understanding of how a supplier can develop specific interactions and dynamic capabilities with the customer in such relationships. Thus, we recommend that further work be carried out in this promising area of information systems research.
