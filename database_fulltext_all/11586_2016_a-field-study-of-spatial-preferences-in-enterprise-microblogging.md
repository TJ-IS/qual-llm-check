## Abstract

How does the introduction of enterprise social networking affect the way that geographically dispersed managers communicate with each other? We examine enterprise social network (ESN) usage data obtained from a community of store managers in a leading Australian retail organization, over a period of 15 months after introduction of the platform. Our interest in examining this data is in spatial communication preferences by the network users, that is, to ascertain who is communicating with whom and where. Our analysis of 12,000 messages exchanged between 530 managers shows that interactions can generally be characterized by individual preferences for local communication but also that two different user communities exist – ‘locals’ and ‘globals’. We develop empirical pro<sup>fi</sup>les for these two types of social network user communities, and we compare the empirical results against contrasting theoretical perspectives for spatial preference patterns in communication behaviour. We outline implications for theories on spatial in<sup>fl</sup>uences on communication behaviours on ESNs. (2016) 115–129. doi:10.1057/jit.2015.27;

## Introduction

ocial networking has become very popular, not only for<sub>social</sub> <sub>and</sub> <sub>private</sub> <sub>purposes</sub> <sub>but</sub> <sub>also</sub> <sub>for</sub> <sub>businesses</sub> social and private purposes but also for businesses (Turban et al., 2011). Enterprises are adopting social network sites to provide employees a medium for commu-Snication, collaboration, and knowledge sharing both within and across company boundaries (McAfee, 2006). There are many examples of successful organizational uses of social networking sites (e.g., Turban et al., 2011; Halale et al., 2015; Mithas and Arora, 2015), but there are also notable cases of challenges such as lurking (Ridings et al., 2006), cultural <sup>fi</sup>t issues (Koch et al., 2013), lack of interactivity (Larsson, 2013), or failed outcome achievement (Recker et al., forthcoming). Social networking in enterprises is thus certainly a new, unique, and potentially transformative communication tech nology (Leonardi et al., 2013), but how its affordances can fully be leveraged remains insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciently researched.

One of the emergent phenomena on social network sites (both public and private) is microblogging – a lightweight communication practice by which users share information about themselves, their activities, their thoughts, or anything else of interest to them (Müller and Stocker, 2011). When used in an enterprise context, microblogging facilitates informal communication and raises awareness among employees (Riemer et al., 2010). Especially members of dispersed teams could therefore bene<sup>fi</sup>t from knowledge sharing through such social media technologies when accomplishing similar tasks, albeit in different geographical places (Subramaniam et al., 2013).

We are interested in examining whether the prospects of knowledge sharing, organizational learning, and ultimately employee collaboration manifest over distances when organizations start engaging in enterprise microblogging. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we are interested in the use of enterprise microblogging for work tasks in geographically dispersed contexts – that is, the use of enterprise microblogging by employees that engage in similar tasks but who are geographically separated from each other. We examine such a case in a <sup>fi</sup>eld study of enterprise microblogging by retail managers. Our research is based on a study of managers in geographically dispersed retail store outlets in a vast geographical region – the continent of Australia. These managers have been using an enterprise social networking platform to communicate with one another over a period of 15 months after installation. Our objective is to provide an interesting, new, rigorous, and authentic empirical account (Avison and Malaurent, 2014) rather than to develop and test new theory. We do this because we believe empirical, pre-theoretical work can be salutary for research disciplines (Miller, 2007) and an important precursor to future research by providing empirical patterns that deserve further attention and theorizing (Hambrick, 2007).

Through our empirical analysis we set out to answer a seemingly simple question: Does geographical distance matter to the intensity and frequency of communication between managers engaging in similar tasks? We show that the answer is not simple.

We proceed as follows: First, we review relevant literature on computer-mediated communication (CMC), enterprise social networking, and spatial analysis. Next, we report on the <sup>fi</sup>eld study, data collection, and analysis. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a spatial preference communication model as the analytical vehicle for examining our data, after which we describe the results and discuss various contrasting theoretical perspectives to explain the results. We then present implications for future research. We conclude by reviewing contributions and limitations.

## Background

Our research relates <sup>fi</sup>rst to enterprise social networking as a form of CMC, and second, to spatial effects in communication behaviour, which requires an understanding of spatial analysis. We discuss each element in turn.

## CMC and enterprise social networks (ESNs)

CMC describes interactions between two or more human beings via the exchange of information through a communication channel mediated by an electronic system (e.g., Walther, 1996). A CMC channel can be established by different systems, such as telephony, video conferencing, electronic mail, or instant messaging, referred to as communication mediums.

Typically, CMC mediums can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed according to their capability for supporting communication with regard to the time and location of the users (Baecker, 1995). Email as a medium, for instance, can support temporally asynchronous as well as spatially dispersed communication, whereas telephony can support spatially dispersed communication only in temporally synchronous settings.

Social networking sites are technology platforms that can be seen as an emergent CMC medium allowing users to connect and share user-generated content with others through Internet technology (Ellison and boyd, 2013). Often, these are labelled as social software because they share a focus on creating and maintaining relationships with other people online (boyd and Ellison, 2007).

ESNs are a speci<sup>fi</sup>c class of social network sites that differ from public social networks, such as Facebook or Twitter, in that they are organizationally bounded, that is, implemented within an organization and, hence, are not reachable by others outside the organizations (e.g., Turban et al., 2011). ESNs describe organizational communication platforms in which employees: (1) have uniquely identi<sup>fi</sup>able, (semi-) public pro<sup>fi</sup>les within the organizational boundary; (2) can articulate organizational connections with other organizational staff, communication that can be viewed and traversed by others;

and (3) can consume, produce, and/or interact with streams of user-generated content provided by their connections to other staff on the site (Ellison and boyd, 2013). ESNs allow workers to communicate messages with speci<sup>fi</sup>c co-workers or to broadcast to everyone, not only linking themselves to coworkers as communication partners by posting, editing, commenting and sharing information and <sup>fi</sup>les with each other, but also by viewing the chronology of messages, connections, text, and <sup>fi</sup>les over time (Leonardi et al., 2013).

What differentiates ESN is that they combine affordances of all other communication mediums in the enterprise (such as supporting synchronous and asynchronous communication, direct or broadcast communication, <sup>fi</sup>le sharing, or pro<sup>fi</sup>ling), allowing these tasks at any point in time (Treem and Leonardi, 2012). Therefore, the key characteristics of ESN are affordances of visibility and persistence (Leonardi et al., 2013): the possibility of identifying previously private or invisible communications by colleagues (Cross et al., 2002), and the possibility of accessing communication and interaction records unbounded by time and space. Together, in theory, ESN should provide employees increased ability to learn from the experiences of others, identify and adopt best practices, which, in turn, should assist the eradication of knowledge boundaries that normally exist in localized, invisible, or inaccessible practices (Carlile, 2002, 2004).

ESNs thus provide an opportunity for members to be exposed to other members’ expertise (Ren and Argote, 2011) thus developing trust in each other’s knowledge (Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2007) and aiding the development of a transactive memory system (Kotlarsky et al., 2015). However, spatial characteristics such as geographical dispersion (O’Leary and Mortensen, 2010) and physical distance (Alavi and Tiwana, 2002) may also constrain the development and maintenance of a transactive memory in technology-mediated settings (Ren and Argote, 2011).

Attracted by their promises, many organizations are beginning to use enterprise social networking sites (Müller and Stocker, 2011; Turban et al., 2011; Leonardi, 2014). Yet, there is still a paucity of research on the use of enterprise social networking sites (Leonardi, 2014).

The existing literature, though scarce, suggests that ESNs – unlike public social networks – are often used to maintain connections with other employees that they do not know and with whom they do not regularly interact of<sup>fl</sup>ine (DiMicco et al., 2008). Some research also suggests that ESN use is successful if users can publish user-generated content quickly (Müller and Stocker, 2011) but that unfavourable noise-to-value ratios can diminish ESN adoption (Zhang et al., 2010).

Considering how to reverse the void of research on ESN, Kane et al. (2014) suggest, as an open research question, whether and how the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of different types and characteristics of connections between users, such as interaction, <sup>fl</sup>ows, or indeed proximities (such as geographical distance), on their behaviour and the formation of the network would be similar or dissimilar between of<sup>fl</sup>ine social networks and digital social networks. This is the question we attempt to address empirically through our study, as the literature suggests that the answer may not be intuitive. For example, interactions between participants of a public social network site re<sup>fl</sup>ect, to a certain extent, the structure of their existing social network in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world (Agarwal et al., 2008); further, a primary motivation for the use of public social network platforms is often to maintain and intensify existing relationships with people they already know of<sup>fl</sup>ine (e.g., Ellison et al., 2007).

In an enterprise context, however, these behaviours can be different. For example, some studies suggest that employees use ESN to engage with people in their company that they may not know (DiMicco and Millen, 2007). This, however, does not seem to be a universal phenomenon, as other studies report that ESN use is more frequently connected with existing and former contacts in the company (Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2009). This suggests again that the research question of how different types of connections between users (such as how interaction, <sup>fl</sup>ows, or indeed proximities, i.e., geographical distance) in<sup>fl</sup>uence ESN indeed remains unanswered (Kane et al., 2014).

To provide an empirical answer to this question, we <sup>fi</sup>rst need to examine how communication behaviours may be affected by spatial effects, and then determine how to analyse such effects.

## Spatial effects in communication behaviour

Our interest in communication behaviours on ESN platforms is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the geographical aspects of such behaviours, more precisely, in the formation of spatial proximity or preference. Our interest is motivated by the observation that, in theory at least, the value of CMC emerges especially in geographically dispersed contexts when it would literally be too complicated or costly to interact otherwise. We wish to explore whether this is truly the case.

Our research therefore relates to spatial analysis (Fischer and Wang, 2011). Spatial analysis covers origin–destination <sup>fl</sup>ow data, that is, any type of relationships between entities attributed with geographical coordinates (e.g., LeSage and Pace, 2008). A common approach for modelling spatial interaction is the application of gravity models (Sen and Smith, 1995). These models describe a spatial interaction function as the probability of a connection or interaction with respect to the geographical distance between involved entities. Importantly, gravity models can be used to describe the distance decay effect: the communication probability between two actors declines when the distance between their locations increases (Allen, 1977).

Distance decay effects have been studied in several enterprise communication settings, at <sup>fi</sup>rst of<sup>fl</sup>ine and later in various CMC mediums; for example, the probability of communicating declines with an increasing physical distance between work locations, even if CMC technologies allow employees to communicate at costs independent of distance (Cairncross, 2007). Similar effects have been found in email communication networks in organizational settings (e.g., Kossinets and Watts, 2006). Moreover, distance decay effects even occur when proximity is perceived, rather than actually existent (Wilson et al., 2008).

The question still remains whether communication behaviours in technology-enabled social networks experience similar effects. To the best of our knowledge, distance decay effects have not been studied in the context of social media communications, let alone ESN. And indeed, there is a reason to believe that communication behaviour on ESN may not be strongly affected by a distance decay effect because ESN, in theory, allow for visibility and persistence of communication (Leonardi et al., 2013), and thus one may perceive costs of communication because of geographical distance to be reduced and participants exposed to them to be virtually equidistant. This could be, for instance, because the effort required to locate information is reduced (Treem and Leonardi, 2012) and the communication channels are largely public rather than private (Cross et al., 2002). Leonardi et al. (2013) further argue that through affordances of persistence, information via communication remains available (traditionally, it is bounded in time and space) since participants can interact with communications rather than communicators. The question arises therefore whether the distance decay phenomenon still plays an in<sup>fl</sup>uential role on the intensity of microblogging communication on ESN.

We note that there has been some research on spatial properties in relation to social networks. For instance, research on Facebook shows not only a decreasing probability of friendship relationships over distance, but also that the information gained is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to approximate the geographical location of a user (Backstrom et al., 2010). Still, it remains unclear whether distance decay effects translate to ESN settings, especially against the backdrop of disparities in motivations and use between enterprise and public social networks. While on public social networks users tend to project their social relationships into the online world (e.g., Ellison et al., 2007), the social component may not be as strongly present in a work context in which the usage of ESN is more often driven by a desire for collaboration, information, knowledge sharing (Turban et al., 2011), and for the establishment of work-relevant social capital. In sum, we believe the available literature remains inconclusive regarding spatial effects in ESN communication, and thus empirical research is called upon to provide clari<sup>fi</sup>cation about potential effects.

## Method

## Research context

To develop an empirically grounded understanding of spatial preference in ESN microblogging, we conducted a <sup>fi</sup>eld study with employees in a large-scale retail organization that used the ESN platform Yammer. Yammer is a commercial ESN platform with over 100,000 organizational installations globally, and close to 8 million users in total (Lunden, 2013). It ranks as a ‘strong performer’ (Koplowitz, 2014) alongside other alternative platforms such as Jive, Socialcast, or salesforce.com.

The retail company, operating in Australia, adopted Yammer in July 2011. The company sought to increase the ability of their employees to collaborate, to exchange ideas (such as product lines or displays) and to innovate together, independent from the locations they were working at. A second motivation was to trial Yammer as a new communications platform between headquarters and stores.

In October 2013, the use of Yammer was discontinued because the company implemented cloud-based of<sup>fi</sup>ce software for all employees, which included an integrated enterprise social networking solution, which has been in use since. By October 2013, 7572 users had exchanged 145,401 messages, and were organized in 316 different groups.

A good description of how Yammer operates is provided by Riemer and Sci<sup>fl</sup>eet (2012). In short, Yammer provides typical microblogging features such as posts, comments, image and <sup>fi</sup>le upload, like and share features, which allow joining and redistributing messages of others. Its interface resembles public social networks such as Facebook or Twitter, with the message stream being the central element. Aside from broadcasting posts, users can also send and receive private messages. The platform also supports established microblogging functionality such as hashtags for threading or the Follower principle known from Twitter. A differentiating characteristic is that posts on Yammer are not length-restricted. In sum, Yammer provides all features nominally associated with ESN (boyd and Ellison, 2007; Leonardi et al., 2013; Kane et al., 2014), and allows for affordances of visibility, editability, persistence, and association (Treem and Leonardi, 2012). We thus consider our research context to be both representative and typical (Yin, 2009).

Yammer provides one feed for the entire organization (All Company), but also allows communities to be created as subunits that are either private groups (restricted to members of that community) or public groups (visible to all registered users in the company). In our research, we examined one private group reserved for management staff responsible for the operation of 897 supermarket stores across all Australian states, viz., the store manager group. We focused on one particular community on the ESN rather than the entire ESN because of our research focus: whether ESN usage overcomes one particular boundary, geographical distance. To isolate this boundary, we deemed it prudent to control for other speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of boundaries in the organization: in organizational boundaries we focused only on staff belonging to the supermarkets operations division and in functional boundaries only on users occupying the store manager role.

In the case organization, store managers are responsible for the operation of supermarket stores employing approximately 115,000 staff across the continent of Australia. The geographi cal location of the stores mirrors the population density of Australia, with most stores located in proximity to highdensity areas such as Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, or Perth, and most rural areas sparsely populated with stores.

The store manager group on Yammer was established in August 2012 with the purpose to connect all managers of all supermarket stores. This marked the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in the history of the company that store managers were provided with not only a digital pro<sup>fi</sup>le (viz., an email account) but also a medium to directly communicate bilaterally in order to exchange knowledge and share ideas. With the establishment of the store manager group, the managers were equipped with 3G-enabled tablets allowing them to access the ESN as part of their day-to-day operations. With the mobility of the tablets, managers were able to use the ESN while stationed on the store <sup>fl</sup>oor instead of only from desktop computers in their back of<sup>fi</sup>ces. This also marked the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in the history of the organization that individual stores had a unique digital pro<sup>fi</sup>le and were reachable through online communication – that is, each store had an email address and an ESN pro<sup>fi</sup>le. This was what motivated our research question of how store managers adopted and appropriated the newly available communication opportunities – interpreted speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, whether they would attempt to reach out to distant colleagues more so than to local ones.

Over the period of 15 months (between August 2012 and October 2013) 530 store managers actively participated in the store manager group, creating 4318 threads, with 5905 messages and 1579 replies. Although the store managers represented a minority group of ESN users overall in the organization, the store manager group became the second largest and most active community on the ESN, thus making it a suitable and interesting setting to study ESN behaviours.

## Data collection

Two sets of data were collected for this study. First, we extracted a complete log of the ESN platform, which included data about each single activity on the platform since the time of installation. From this data set, we extracted all activities in the store manager group in the time frame August 2012– October 2013 (after which time, the installation was discontinued as a part of an IT infrastructure overhaul). The complete log featured data such as thread, message, conversation and user IDs, content, attachment, and time stamps.

Second, we obtained access to a data set providing demographic information about all supermarket stores on the Australian continent. The information was current as of October 2013. This data set included information such as store ID, location (latitude and longitude, region, state), and store classi<sup>fi</sup>cation data such as suburb pro<sup>fi</sup>le (rural vs urban).

To combine the data sets, we matched the unique store IDs to the user IDs of the ESN data set. Store managers had a pro<sup>fi</sup>le that linked them to the store in which they were operating, allowing for unequivocal matching.

## Data analysis

To examine the data, we applied a graph theoretical approach for structuring the data and analysing the communication relations between employees. Graph theory studies discrete relational structures (Diestel, 2010) by examining network structures between objects (such as actors) and the structure of relationships between them (such as message exchange or distance). In the social sciences, graph theory builds the foundation for the analysis of social relationships between people (Borgatti et al., 2013).

As a prerequisite for the analysis of graphs consisting of several thousands of nodes and edges, we developed a graph database scheme to store and query the data set obtained. Graph databases have computational advantages over relational databases in the discovery of relationships that span multiple levels, such as <sup>fi</sup>nding friends of the friends of a friend, or repliers to the repliers of a message (Robinson et al., 2013). We used the open-source graph database Neo4j (www.neo4j.org), which stores a single, self-contained graph that may contain several isolated subgraphs, the nodes of which are not connected by any relationships. The database allows distinguishing different types and nodes, and allows assignment of one or more relationship labels. Figure 1 visualizes the graph database schema as a UML class diagram.

To study the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the geographical distance between users on the intensity of mutual communication on ESN as a function of the preference of users for communicating with other users in dependence on the distance between them, we introduce a spatial preference communication model, providing a formalization of actors communicating among each other, their locations, and the communication incidents within the boundaries of a communication network. Our model allows for the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a measure of preference to describe the extent to which users prefer either short- or longdistance communication. With this measure, we can compare different instances of communication networks concerning the effects of distance on communication behaviours.

We developed a new model because we found existing models not readily applicable. For instance, gravity models (such as those applied by Butts and Acton, 2011 that estimate the distance decay of messages in a communication network) only consider interactions on a network level and fail to describe the behaviour of individual participants. In particular, the model used by Butts and Acton (2011) lacks the ability to represent more than one relationship between participants, that is, it is suitable for modelling friendship relationships (0:1) but not communication relations (0:many). This problem also applies to the spatial measures introduced by Scellato et al. (2010).

Our spatial preference communication model combines a social as well as a spatial structure (Barthelemy, 2011). Communication incidents between actors determine the social structure, whereas their geographical locations de<sup>fi</sup>ne the spatial structure. As such, the model represents the <sup>fl</sup>ow of messages at geographically dispersed locations and thus belongs to the class of spatial interaction models (Fischer and Wang, 2011: 49–59).

Our model describes a communication network by a 5- tuple of the form (A, L, M, p, d) with A describing the sets of actors $( a 1 , . . . , a n )$ , L describing the locations $( { \check { l } } 1 , . . . , l n )$ of these actors, and M describing the messages (m1, …, mn) between actors. Thereby, each message represents an information <sup>fl</sup>ow from a sender as to a receiver ar at a point in time t.

To describe the spatial structure of the communication network, the bijective function p de<sup>fi</sup>nes the one-to-one allocation of actors to locations, and d provides the min–max normalized, absolute distance between all pairs of locations (li, lj), which allows de<sup>fi</sup>ning the distance between any pair of actors as the distance between the locations they are allocated to by p. We normalized the distances to a scale that is bounded by the distance to the nearest and the furthest actor in order to avoid interpretational confounding of data without measurement units. The ratios between absolute distances are preserved in the normalized measure (Han et al., 2011). The resulting actor distance matrix D captures the distances of all possible pairs of actors and thus fully re<sup>fl</sup>ects the spatial structure of the communication network.

To describe the communication structure of the communication network, our model allows for the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a communication incident matrix. Communication incidents always occur between distinct actors, and describe an interaction between these actors via the exchange of a message. In the communication incident matrix, the value of the cell in the s-th row and the r-th column denotes the absolute frequency of messages sent by actor as to actor ar, with as≠ar. The matrix is not symmetric, since messages have a direction; the matrix thus re<sup>fl</sup>ects the topological structure of the communication network and yields the communication graph in which edges exist between the actor nodes ai and aj that have at least one communication incident.

While the spatial structure of the communication network is determined by the distances between locations, no knowledge is available on the behaviour of actors, thus rendering the a priori communication structure unknown. We therefore model the occurrence of messages in the communication network as the probability P of an outcome of a random process of Si (Who sends the message?) to Ri (To whom does the sender address the message?). Since the sender chooses the receiver and each sender might have different preferences regarding this choice, Ri conditionally depends on the preceding realization of Si. Essentially, the model describes a decision tree in which the sender describes the root, and the hypothetically possible messages to n receivers describe n branches. A leaf denotes a message as a tuple of realizations of S and R at a point in time Ti. This model is useful because the theoretical probability distributions of S, R, and T are not speci<sup>fi</sup>ed a priori, but can be derived empirically from an observed communication incident matrix. It also allows formulating possible theoretical distributions ex ante or a posterior to examine the <sup>fi</sup>t of a theoretical model to the observed communication structure.

To illustrate our spatial preference communication model, we present Figure 2, which shows an example of a communication network between 5 actors in 5 locations exchanging a total of 12 messages between them (for illustration purposes, we do not consider points in time in this model).

Our model can be used to analyse different communication networks, such as private message networks (i.e., email), postreply networks (where users post-reply to a parent message), or common thread networks (examining participants contributing messages and the number of replies to a common thread). We are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally interested in post-reply networks, which can be interpreted as addressing the reply message to the author of the parent message (e.g., Hua and Haughton, 2012). Figure 3 shows an example of such a network. To obtain a post-reply network, reply messages from an ESN are transformed into communication incidents, such that their authors are the sending actors, whereas the authors of the parent messages resemble the receiving actors. The original post starting the thread to which others reply is not part of this network. We focus on post-reply networks because they accurately describe the process of microblogging in private or public networks.

As the last step in our data analysis, we developed a measure for spatial preference by network actors. This measure allows a comparison of the spatial interaction behaviour of actors in the post-reply (or other types) network. In essence, spatial preference measures the deviation of an individual actor from a spatially indifferent behaviour (where the probability of interacting with another actor is independent of the geographical distance between the two actors) towards either a preference for interacting with closely located, or distant, actors. Our measure is similar to node locality metrics that measure geographic closeness of actors in a spatial social network (Scellato et al., 2010); however, our measure also considers multiple interactions between a pair of actors, and it incorporates a baseline of spatially indifferent, communication behaviour, such that short- and long-distance preferences can be estimated.

To estimate spatial preferences, we <sup>fi</sup>rst determined the spatial preferences of each individual actor in the network. We modelled the probability of message distances as the likelihood of deviance from a spatially indifferent communication. In other words, we measured the likelihood of a deviation of the observed message distance distribution from the one which would be observed if any particular individual communication were to be spatially indifferent. Importantly, this measure does not incorporate the absolute, but rather the relative, distance between actors, thus allowing us to account for the different perceptions of ‘closeness’ between store managers in rural areas of the country compared with those of managers in urban areas. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned the range of the individual spatial preference Δas of an actor as as [−1, 1], with a negative result indicating a preference for short-distance communication.

## Findings

Following the data analysis approach described above, we now examine the data obtained from four perspectives. First, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe descriptive statistics of the network in order to contextualize the structural analysis of the network, our primary interest; second, we examine the topological structure of the store manager network; third, we report on the spatial structure of the network; and fourth, we examine spatial communication preferences of the store managers in the network.

However, we note that store managers did not primarily use the store manager group when posting content to the ESN – the majority of their postings occurred outside of the group (83.8% of postings).

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, within the store manager group, the communication behaviour of store managers followed, as expected, a power law distribution (Figure 4). As visualized by the dotted line in Figure 4, 20% of store managers were responsible for 83.1% of the messages on the network, and 44.0% of store managers were lurkers (Wang and Yu, 2012), that is, those who did not actively contribute to the community. Compared with other studies of microblogging, we note that participation is neither extremely active (Ridings et $a l . . _ { }$ 2006) nor extremely inactive (Walker et al., 2010); instead, it appears to be normal.

Over the period between August 2012 and October 2013, the communication with the community showed high volatility, with a maximum of 330 posted messages in one calendar week (November 2012), and an average of 92.2 messages (SD, 62.7) per week. In general, message peaks corresponded roughly to important seasonal events and promotions, such as the start of Christmas trading season (week 2012–45), Easter (week 2013– 11), or the announcement of quarterly <sup>fi</sup>nancial results (e.g., week 2013–7, week 2013–19, week 2013–33).

While it was not the focus of our data analysis, we scanned the data for content characteristics such as type of conversations and content of postings. We found a relative stable pattern of ESN usage habits. For instance, a common habit that emerged on the ESN was store managers exchanging displays of local best practices (such as promotional shelf displays on the store <sup>fl</sup>oor), which were then discussed and sometimes implemented in other stores. Similarly, local promotions and community engagements (e.g., local charity events) were posted to the community and received laudation from others. Finally, often individuals’ efforts and outcomes were posted.

## Topological characteristics of the ESN

As a next step, we examined communication behaviour in the ESN store manager community from a topological viewpoint (Borgatti et $a l . ,$ 2013; Kane et al., 2014). Figure 5 shows the structure of the network graphically. The network contains a total of 900 nodes with 466 reply relationships. The visualization in Figure 5 shows that the majority of nodes remain isolated, with the dense component containing only 264 nodes. This means that 66.4% of store managers never replied to another store manager.

Examining the network cohesion, the density (the number of existing edges by the maximum number of possible edges, see Borgatti et al., 2013) coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of 0.0006 shows an overall sparse occurrence of replies. The reciprocity measure (Wassermann and Faust, 1994) of 0.23 shows that almost one quarter of store managers mutually replied to one another. The transitivity coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Watts and Strogatz, 1998) of 0.08 indicates that message relationships were only weakly clustered, that is, that message replies between store managers did not usually lead to new interactions between involved parties other than the original post-reply relationship. Finally, the mean degree (Borgatti et al., 2013) of store managers was 0.52, indicating that, on average, store managers replied to 1.67 parent messages.

## Spatial characteristics of the ESN

Next, we examined the spatial structure of the store manager group communication network. Figure 6 describes the spatial structure of the post-reply network positioned according to the geographical coordinates of their corresponding stores. It is apparent that the majority of communication incidents occurred between stores located on the Eastern shore of Australia, which is the most densely populated part of the continent (also featuring the majority of stores). Remote stores (such as in the north-western region of the continent) appear isolated from both geographical and communication perspectives.

The most important characteristic with regard to the spatial structure of a network are the distances spanned by the edges. Figure 7 compares the spatial structure of the post-reply network using the cumulative probability distributions of the observed message distances in comparison with the geographical distances between stores (the largest distance between stores was 3978 km). On the whole range up to this distance, the curve of the post-reply distance distribution consistently remains above the geographical distance distribution, showing that store managers reply to store managers of geographically closer stores with a greater probability than with those of further apart stores.

Focusing on the range of distances below the 0.5-quantile of the geographical distance distribution, that is, 934 km (indicated by the rightmost dotted vertical line in Figure 7), we observe a different shape of the communication distance distribution and, in turn, an even stronger divergence from the geographical store distribution. The average message distance in the post-reply network is 595 km and even the 0.75-quantile of the post-reply network spans less distance (928.7 km) than the 0.5-quantile of the store distribution. Moreover, while the distance distributions of the observed communication incidents show an upward bulge and follow a concave downward, marginally decreasing curve, the shape of the geographical store distribution is mostly convex and marginally increasing.

To explore the concentration of messages within a radius around the senders, Figure 8 visualizes the spatial structure of the communication incidents in a centralized interaction map, showing the relative distance of a sending actor to a receiver. The visualization shows a densely scattered cluster of messages in the centre (500 km radius) and a decreasing density with increasing distance. Closer inspection shows that 50% of all replies occurred within a radius of 179 km around their sensors, roughly corresponding to the organization’s de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a region (such as south-east Queensland). Even within the centre, dense scattering and decreasing density persisted: 25% of all replies occurred within a radius lower or equal to 22 km. These results suggest that communications largely occurred between stores that shared an approximate location (such as a metropolitan area: Brisbane or Sydney) within a management region that these stores belonged to – thus indicating a shared awareness and familiarity in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world (e.g., through regional management meetings and conferences).

## Discussion

Our analysis of descriptive, topological, and spatial characteristics of the store manager ESN community yielded several results. At the outset, our analysis shows that geographical distance does appear to have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the communication behaviour of ESN actors. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that an ESN platform does not fully supersede the phenomenon of distance decay, despite its broadcast nature and despite the circumstance that all participants are fully aware of the ongoing communication. We showed that actors tend to reply to messages of relatively close actors more frequently than to those of distant actors. We also found that more active users also covered longer distances than did less active users, and that the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of globals vs local actors on the network was relatively independent of the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of their stores as rural vs urban.

What theoretical logic is available to explain such empirical results? Our intent was to provide an empirical contribution through a rigorous in-depth analysis of empirical data, and to provide an empirical account of a relevant and current information systems phenomenon that is interesting, new, rigorous, and authentic (Avison and Malaurent, 2014). Yet, we also recognize the potential of the empirical account to allow for re<sup>fl</sup>ection of theory, in the sense that the systematic, detailed, and authentic reporting of the data can be used as a baseline to contrast against ‘ideal types’ (Avison and Malaurent, 2014) of a phenomena that is embodied in one or several theoretical accounts. In turn, our detailed empirical account can provide rich implications about existing (as well as potential new) theoretical accounts and, in turn, assist original and fertile theorizing (Compeau and Olivera, 2014).

To that end, Table 4 describes three theoretical accounts offering propositions about potential spatial preference patterns in ESN communication behaviours. The <sup>fi</sup>rst proposition describes the null hypothesis that distance will have no in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the occurrence probability of messages between actors, while the other two propositions provide logic for short- and long-distance communication preferences, respectively. For each proposition, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y provide theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>catory logic. In addition, we provide an empirical re<sup>fl</sup>ection on the propositions based on our data analysis.

Table 4 highlights each proposition as an ideal-type theory (Johnson, 2000), that is, as a simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed, ‘extreme’ form of communication behaviour that does not necessarily occur entirely, nor to that extreme. Idealizations are useful, however, not only to identify boundaries for any empirical measurement but also to evaluate the internal validity of the proposed logic.

## Implications

## For research on ESNs

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our <sup>fi</sup>eld study have implications primarily for research on ESN affordances, network structures, and transactive memory system development: First, the potential of ESN technologies lies in overcoming distance, communication, and transaction costs – if appropriated correctly. Yet, our study suggests that without appropriate technology features and/or organizational interventions, the reduction in transac tion costs through actualization of visibility and persistence affordances of ESN may not automatically occur. While we did not examine local or individual reasons for communication behaviours, we did <sup>fi</sup>nd that the sheer existence of ESN technology does not automatically lead to spatially expansive, let alone intensive, communication behaviour. A potential explanation is that bene<sup>fi</sup>cial functional affordances of ESN technologies are not necessarily perceived as such by actors, in turn obstructing the realization of these affordances. Functional affordance theory (Markus and Silver, 2008) would suggest that the structural features of ESN (such as cost-free communication over distances) may not necessarily be exposed appropriately through relevant symbolic expressions that inform users about how to interact with technology to achieve certain goals, such as learning from distant colleagues. It may however be that symbolic expressions that focus on how geographical aspects of technology use can be messaged to user groups need to be developed and studied.

A second implication emerges when relating our analysis to existing work on the relevance of interpersonal ties (Hansen, 1999) and centrality (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993) of actors within a network. For instance, the ‘hubbyness’ of networks is often explained through preferential attachment (Barabási and Albert, 1999): we connect to actors already well connected. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings now suggest that preferential attachment – as well as networking power as a consequence – may also be a function of distance: we tend to connect to network hubs (individuals, groups, or topics) that are already powerful and that are close to us, distance wise. The conditions in which distance overlaps, mitigates, strengthens, or con<sup>fl</sup>icts with other social networking attributes is thus an important parameter to investigate in combination with established factors.

A third implication emerges for research on the development and maintenance of transactive memory systems and knowledge boundaries (Carlile, 2004; Ren and Argote, 2011; Kotlarsky et al., 2015). The literature to date provides arguments both that ESN as a medium for technologymediated knowledge exchange can aid the development of a transactive memory system (Kotlarsky et al., 2015) and that such platforms may not be effective for physically distant, or geographically dispersed, members (Alavi and Tiwana, 2002; O’Leary and Mortensen, 2010). Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that geographical dispersion as an organizational characteristic acts stronger and more negatively as a pragmatic knowledge boundary than do the affordances of visibility and persistence as boundary-spanning technological characteristics. However, our research was situated in unifunctional and independent, rather than cross-functional and interdependent, teams, and it remains to be seen whether the technology characteristics of ESN afford more opportunities for development of a transactive memory in differently composed networks, such as interdependent teams with different, rather than similar, knowledge specializations.

For research on spatial characteristics of communication behaviour Our key contribution in this <sup>fi</sup>eld is methodological. To analyse our data, we developed a spatial preference communication network model. Our model can be applied to any other data set of message exchanges for studying spatial preferences, and also allows for extension and triangulation with other data, such as strength of ties or interpersonal attributes of message contents. Application scenarios include other forms of social networks (e.g., Facebook) or microblogging (e.g., Twitter), especially those that correlate content with geospatial information (e.g., Instagram). The model is also applicable to examining behaviours in direct message exchange networks (e.g., email, telephone, Skype, etc.).

A second implication arises when contrasting our work against communication medium selection theories such as media richness or naturalness. As discussed in Table 4, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd strong evidence that these theories account for the behaviour we noted; however, the context we studied did not explicitly involve the selection between alternative communication mediums. It would be prudent, therefore, to revisit the context of store manager communication and collect data on communication behaviours outside of ESN (e.g., email and telephone or meetings and conferences) to see whether media selection for short- vs long-distance communication is a question of spatial preference or media characteristics.

A third implication arises from the extrapolation from the community of store managers to employee communication on ESN in general. Our analysis of a largely homogenous group of actors showed strong preferences for local and short-distance communication in general. Other research has shown that knowledge sharing in large (global) organizations expands when actor groups are structurally diverse (Cummings, 2004). Together, this would suggest that over entire ESN, spatial preference may tend towards long-distance communication when postreply networks involve structurally diverse roles (such as store managers, support teams, and frontline staff).

## For practice

The primary practical implication of this research concerns the adoption and effective use of ESN in organizations. The organization we studied set out to use ESN, particularly to connect the managers of dispersed stores to facilitate organizational learning, collaboration, and innovation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts do not automatically manifest without interventions, and thus the potential remains for improving the return on ESN investment. In particular, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that organizations need to improve both the overall adoption of ESN by store managers and the engagement of store managers with other managers, especially in distant stores. Both objectives could be reached through appropriate incentive schemes as well as through improved reporting on value and bene<sup>fi</sup>t gains from existing knowledge sharing. Both types of interventions could lead to: (a) a higher probability of reaching sustained critical masses and (b) members being assisted with guidelines on how to achieve gains from ESN participation.

## Limitations

We identify several limitations. First, like other models, our study has abstracted communication behaviours to one variable of interest: geographical distance. Further research needs to examine other issues (for instance, network hubbyness or interpersonal preferences, see Schilling and Fang, 2013) and how they complicate the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our analysis. Second, the model we develop to examine our data assumes linear communication behaviour over distances, and abstracts from behaviours where, for instance, medium-distance actors are neglected or favoured over either short- or longdistance actors. We note how participation behaviour follows a power law distribution (Figure 4), and that more active users appear to cover longer distances than do less active users. Our model does not explicitly account for the skew in the distribution or in the preferences. In particular, it does not allow identifying the exact root cause: whether closely distributed managers communicate less or whether more actively communicating managers prefer longer message distances.

A third limitation of our work is that we did not examine content elements, that is, we did not examine how characteristics of a message or a reply would impact communication probabilities or preferences. Semantic analysis techniques (Dong, 2005; Evangelopoulos et al., 2012) could be used to extend our study towards that direction, and provide more answers to an interesting question: Do we talk about different things with distant others? Fourth, we were not able to examine individual characteristics of store managers, such as their individual perceptions of ESN usefulness or their attitudes towards knowledge seeking and sharing. Fifth, we note that the data we consider in our analysis allowed us to identify lurkers in the community but did not allow us to study this quite substantial share of users in great detail.

Further, in our analysis we were not able to ascertain both whether ESN affordances of visibility and persistence (Leonardi et al., 2013) were actualized by users who were not active posters, nor were we able to conclude whether this type of lurking yielded any organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Lurkers can play important online roles, such as follower-feeder or connectorleader, and they can also play important of<sup>fl</sup>ine roles, such as mentor and knowledge manager (Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al., 2015). For instance, lurking store managers may still identify best practices from the store manager group communications (local or global), and then apply and distribute this knowledge of<sup>fl</sup>ine, but we did not have access to the data to examine these possibilities. The study by Crane<sup>fi</sup>eld et al. (2015) serves as a good example of how such research could be carried out. Sixth, for the data set, we selected only one group in the ESN community of the organization. We did this to mitigate bias stemming from the existence of multiple roles or organizational units in the communication.

## Conclusion

This paper sets out to provide an empirical account of enterprise social networking in use by geographically dispersed managers. In developing our account, we attempted to provide an analysis of the available data that is simultaneously authentic, plausible, rigorous, interesting, and timely (Avison and Malaurent, 2014).

Through this work, we provide empirical evidence that the geographical distance between participants of an ESN site negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the probability of mutual communication when engaging via microblogging. We found that while distance decay was present in the use of microblogging, there were also speci<sup>fi</sup>c communities of users that decisively expanded their communication reach to distant partners. We trust that the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of these empirical patterns is compelling to the reader, providing a rich empirical basis to stimulate future empirical and theoretical research on current and future practices of social systems.

Jan Recker is Full Professor of Information Systems and Retail Innovation at Queensland University of Technology. Jan’s research focuses on processes-oriented systems analysis and design, Green IS and organizational innovation. He has published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Information & Management, and European Journal of Information Systems. He is Editor-in-Chief for the Communications of the Association for Information Systems and an Associate Editor for the MIS Quarterly.

Dominik Lekse holds a Master of Science in Information Systems from the University of Muenster, Germany. His research interests concern spatial analysis and computermediated communication. He now works for an IT consul tancy company.
