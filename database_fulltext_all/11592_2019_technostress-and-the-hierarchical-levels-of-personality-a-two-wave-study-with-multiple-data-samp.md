# Technostress and the hierarchical levels of personality: a two-wave study with multiple data samples

Christian Maier <sup>a</sup>, Sven Laumer <sup>b</sup>, Jakob Wirth <sup>a</sup> and Tim Weitzel <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department for Information Systems and Services, Centre of Human Resources Information Systems (CHRIS), University of Bamberg, Bamberg, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Schöller Endowed Chair for Information Systems, Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Nürnberg, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Even though IS use has numerous bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for users and organisations, such as improved user performance and greater productivity, an increasing number of users experience technostress. Since technostress can result in decreased user well-being, it is important to understand what leads users to perceive it. Recent technostress research points to the relationship between personality traits and the perception of technostress as a research gap. Given that personality traits are structured hierarchically, we study how and which levels of user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perception of technostress. In developing our research model, we select personality traits from the three hierarchical levels of personality: neuroticism, personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT), and IT mindfulness. The results of 2 two-wave studies analysing data collected in an organisational setting (sample 1) and through mTurk (sample 2) reveal that all three personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perception of technostress, with IT mindfulness having the strongest impact. This study contributes by revealing that user personality and, primarily, IT mindfulness in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perception of technostress. Additionally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal an inverted u-curved in<sup>fl</sup>uence of techno-stressors on user performance, deepening our understanding of how the perception of technostress in<sup>fl</sup>uences user reactions.

## 1. Introduction

One challenge associated with the information system (IS) use in organisations is technostress, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as stress associated with using IS (Ragu-Nathan, Tarafdar, Ragu-Nathan, & Qiang, 2008). Among others, technostress decreases well-being and reduces user performance (Maier, Laumer, & Eckhardt, 2015a; Srivastava, Chandra, & Shirish, 2015; Tarafdar, Tu, & Ragu-Nathan, 2010). To avoid these dangers, management needs to understand the factors that lead employees to perceive technostress.

One theory is that technostress is grounded in users’ perception of di<sup>f</sup>erent technological characteristics, such as technological complexity (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). However, little is known about why a given level of such technological characteristics is perceived as stressful by some users, but not by others. Even though some research suggests that this might be grounded in users’ abilities and values (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011), recent research quali<sup>fi</sup>es that there are additional factors which might in<sup>fl</sup>uence that relationship (Tarafdar, Cooper, & Stich, 2017). It has been theorised that some users are more prone to perceive technostress than others, indicating that the personality of users in<sup>fl</sup>uence their perception of technostress (Ayyagari et al., 2011). To date, research has not shown whether and how user personality leads some users to perceive technological characteristics as stressful and other users to perceive the same stimuli as not stressful. If personality predisposes users to perceive technostress, organisations would have to acknowledge that some users perceive technostress regularly and might be more inclined to perceive job burnout (Maier et al., 2015a). This study dives deeper into the link between personality and perceived technostress by considering three hierarchical levels of personality (Allport, 1961; Thatcher, Wright, Sun, Zagenczyk, & Klein, 2018), including (1) broad traits, (2) stable, context-specific traits, and (3) dynamic, context-specific traits, which vary in terms of breadth and stability. We intend to determine the relative strength of in<sup>fl</sup>uence across these three hierarchical levels of personality on perceptions of technostress in order to draw both theoretical and practical conclusions. If, for example, dynamic, ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence technostress perception more strongly, organisations can train their employees, o<sup>f</sup>ering them experience in using IS to strengthen dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits (Thatcher et al., 2018) and thus potentially reduce their perceptions of technostress. If, however, broad, stable personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceptions of technostress more strongly, such perceptions would be guided by stable predispositions and less easily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by training and experience. We thus strive to understand whether the perception of technostress is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced more by broad, stable traits or more by dynamic traits linked to experience. In doing so, we close a research gap identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in recent technostress research (Tarafdar et al., 2017) and illustrate the analytic value of distinguishing among three types of traits identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in personality research and in IS use research as well (Allport, 1961; Thatcher et al., 2018). We begin by asking the following research questions (RQ):

RQ 1: To what degree does personality predispose users’ perception of technostress?

RQ 2: To what degree do personality traits across the three hierarchical levels of personality vary in terms of their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the perception of technostress?

The theoretical underpinning that guides us in answering these research questions is twofold. First, we rely on research into technostress as a transactional process (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Second, we build on previous research suggesting three disparate hierarchical levels of personality (Allport, 1961; Thatcher et al., 2018): (1) broad traits; (2) stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits; (3) dynamic contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits. Based on these theoretical underpinnings, we develop a research model which investigates the role of the three hierarchical levels of personality on the perception of technostress.

This paper is structured as follows. We next summarise related research in the strands of technostress and personality to derive speci<sup>fi</sup>c research gaps. We then develop a research model with nine hypotheses. After describing our research method, which includes 2 two-wave studies with employees working in a single organisation (sample 1) and based on data collected via mTurk (sample 2), we provide our empirical results. Finally, we discuss these results, explain the limitations of the study, and draw implications for theory and practice.

## 2. Theoretical background and research gaps

To develop a theoretical understanding of how the perception of technological characteristics is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by user personality, we summarise related research and identify relevant research gaps in the strands of technostress and personality, focusing on the hierarchical levels of personality. We then use this knowledge to develop a research model in the next section.

## 2.1. Related research on technostress and research gaps

Technostress, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users’ experience of stress associated with using technologies (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008), has been the focus of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant recent research (Fischer & Riedl, 2017). Many scholars draw on psychological stress research (Cooper, Dewe, & O’Driscoll, 2001; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) and focus on chronic or on episodic stress (Galluch, Grover, & Thatcher, 2015). Chronic stress while using IS is ongoing and typically measured with surveys (e.g., Ayyagari et al., 2011; Maier et al., 2015a), whereas episodic stress while using IS is punctual and contextual and measured with surveys or neuroscienti<sup>fi</sup>c equipment (e.g., Galluch et al., 2015; Maier, Laumer, Weinert, & Weitzel, 2015c; Riedl, Kindermann, Auinger, & Javor, 2012, 2013; Tams, Hill, Ortiz de Guinea, Thatcher, & Grover, 2014). Episodic stress is therefore relevant to stress perceived while performing a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task, for example, in the IS context, when a user experiences a system breakdown. On the contrary, chronic stress is relevant to general and ongoing situations, rather than speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks, for example, in the IS context, when a user experiences general stress while using di<sup>f</sup>erent IS over an extended period of time (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Research into chronic stress when using IS theorises an entire transactional process.

Initially, a user encounters a technological condition, which is typically a characteristic of the IS (Ayyagari et al., 2011), which lead to techno-stressors re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the individuals’ perceptions of the IS (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). Next, the user may respond to the situation, for example, with growing dissatisfaction or poorer performance, which are examples of techno-strain, (Ayyagari et al., 2011). This transactional process of chronic stress while using IS is described in detail below (Figure 1).

## 2.1.1. Conditions

Technology conditions or characteristics are considered the starting point in the transactional process of technostress and are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three groups (Ayyagari et al., 2011). First, usability features refer to the perceived usefulness, complexity, and reliability of a technology. Second, dynamic features refer to the perceived pace of changes in the technology. Third, intrusive features refer to the perceived monitorability, invasion of privacy, or high degree of reachability after working hours enabled by characteristics of the technology.

In response to these conditions, individuals evaluate whether the abilities and values are in equilibrium with the demands and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the technology (Cooper et al., 2001; Edwards, Caplan, & van Harrison, 2000;

## 2.1.2. Techno-stressors and their causes

Broadly speaking, techno-stressors are technologyinduced stimuli, events or demands (Ayyagari et al., 2011). While research on episodic technostress has studied some speci<sup>fi</sup>c stressors (Eckhardt et al., 2013; Galluch et al., 2015; Riedl et al., 2012; Tams, Thatcher, & Grover, 2018), research focusing on chronic technostress proposes that techno-stressors can be divided into <sup>fi</sup>ve categories (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). First, employees experience techno-overload when they perceive the expectation to work faster and longer if they use a certain IS. Second, techno-invasion is when employees perceive IS as blurring the boundaries between the work and private domains. Third, employees experience technocomplexity when they feel unable to use IS because they lack the necessary skills. Fourth, techno-insecurity is the fear of losing one’s job due to IS. Fifth, technouncertainty is experienced when IS is perceived as the source of many changes in the organisation.

Beyond the knowledge that technological characteristics cause techno-stressors (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Maier et al., 2015a), initial research indicates that organisational setting, user involvement (Tarafdar et al., 2010), organisational technology dependency (Shu, Tu, & Wang, 2011), and organisational centralisation or innovation (Wang, Shu, & Tu, 2008) are factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing techno-stressors. Some other research has focused on sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender or education as causes for techno-stressors (Riedl et al., 2013; Tams et al., 2018), with divergent results. While some results reveal that sociodemographic characteristics impact techno-stressors, others do not con<sup>fi</sup>rm these results (Shu et al., 2011; Tu, Wang, & Shu, 2005; Wang et al., 2008). Our review identi<sup>fi</sup>es a gap in research into “what factors increase the likelihood that the characteristics of an

IS are perceived as techno-stressors?” (Tarafdar et al., 2017). Some research suggests that user personality may be an in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factor beyond the factors studied in the previous research (Ayyagari et al., 2011), but no such <sup>fi</sup>ndings have been presented to date (see Table 3 in the Appendix for a review of how user personality has been applied in technostress research).

## 2.1.3. (Techno-) strain

In general, techno-strain is a psychological, physiological, or behavioural response to IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c stimuli (Tarafdar et al., 2010). Psychological strain includes psychological reactions such as feelings of exhaustion (Ayyagari et al., 2011) or job burnout (Maier et al., 2015a; Srivastava et al., 2015). Physiological strain, studied in research on episodic stress (Eckhardt et al., 2013; Galluch et al., 2015), includes bodily reactions, including cardiovascular, biochemical, and gastrointestinal symptoms (Cooper et al., 2001). These reactions are associated with the autonomous nervous system and the hypothalamus-pituitaryadrenal axis (Tams et al., 2014), and are measurable in terms of emotional sweating, pupil dilation, faster heartbeat or increased cortisol levels (Riedl, 2013). Finally, behavioural strain includes behavioural reactions such as discontinuance of IS usage (Maier, Laumer, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2015b) or poor performance at work (Tarafdar et al., 2010).

Research into the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing techno-strain indicates that strain is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by techno-stressors (Tarafdar et al., 2010). When IS users evaluate techno-stressors as a threat, they may develop intentions to change their behaviour in response (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), such as by discontinuing IS use (Maier et al., 2015c). As the result of using less IS due to the perception of technostress, their work performance may decline or they may demonstrate lower levels of innovativeness (Tarafdar, Pullins, & Ragu-Nathan, 2015).

Research in the stream of technostress has shown that involvement facilitation and innovative support in<sup>fl</sup>uence psychological strain such as user satisfaction (Tarafdar et al., 2010). Beyond that, the importance of user personality in the strand of technostress research has been demonstrated in two ways. First, it has been shown that the personality traits dispositional resistance to change, neuroticism and extraversion in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavioural strain, such as discontinuous usage behaviour (Maier et al., 2015c). Related research shows that rather neurotic users are more likely to perceive psychological strain, such as feelings of techno-exhaustion (Ayyagari et al., 2011), as illustrated in Figure 1 by the arrow from user personality to strain. Second, user personality a<sup>f</sup>ects how techno-stressors are translated into psychology and behavioural strain, which is also illustrated in Figure 1 as a moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of user personality on the stressor–strain relationship. Research has shown that some of the Big Five broad personality traits moderate the relationship between technostressors and strain (Srivastava et al., 2015). For example, research shows that when individuals are predisposed to be open and less neurotic, the technostressors are less likely to reduce job engagement. Similarly, being predisposed to be agreeable and less extraverted in<sup>fl</sup>uences the likelihood that users will su<sup>f</sup>er job burnout when they perceive technostressors.

## 2.1.4. Research gaps in technostress research

Figure 2 illustrates two key relevant gaps in and limitations to previous technostress research focusing on the role of personality traits in technostress.

First, previous technostress research explains that personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence the psychological reaction of individuals, having a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on psychological strain, and that personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence how techno-stressors are translated into strain, moderating the relationship between techno-stressors and strain. This leaves room for research explaining how personality traits directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence whether users perceive techno-stressors, as indicated by “Research gap I” in Figure 2.

Second, as indicated by “Research gap $\operatorname { I I } ^ { \dag }$ in Figure 2, previous technostress research has mostly concentrated on the Big Five personality traits (Srivastava et al., 2015). These traits are all from the topmost hierarchical levels, revealing gaps in research into the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of (1) broad traits; (2) stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits and (3) dynamic, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits on technostressors, given that previous research indicates that context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits explain a higher variance than broad traits (Paunonen & Ashton, 2001).

To address these research gaps, we study the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality on techno-stressors and begin by summarising existing research on personality with a particular focus on the hierarchical structure of personality traits.

## 2.2. Personality traits

Personality traits are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as dispositions leading to constant patterns of thoughts, feelings and behaviours across diverse situations that distinguish individuals from each other (McCrae & Costa, 2006). Personality traits are arranged hierarchically with broad traits at the topmost level and the narrower context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits at the lower levels (Paunonen, 1998) (see Appendix Table 3). We next explain this hierarchical structure of traits, which vary in terms of their breadth and stability (Allport, 1961) and afterwards explain how these are related.

## 2.2.1. The hierarchical structure of personality traits

Personality traits can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed along two dimensions. On the one hand, traits are either stable or dynamic. On the other hand, traits are broad or context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c. The three hierarchical levels of traits include (1) broad traits, (2) stable, context-specific traits and (3) dynamic, context-specific traits.

![](/api/attachments/P8K58XN7/fulltext/images/07aa892a02bb0e9a11b01646731bb411c7eb83084663003694295399802f0f0d.jpg)  
The role of hierarchical levels of personality on technostress and research gaps.

First, broad traits re<sup>fl</sup>ect personality at the highest hierarchical level. They are characterised as context-free and stable and have the greatest breadth. As such, broad traits less precisely explain a speci<sup>fi</sup>c belief or behaviour, and only explain behaviours such as IS use, employee turnover and privacy concerns (Barrick & Mount, 1991; Eckhardt, Laumer, Maier, & Weitzel, 2016; Junglas, Johnson, & Spitzmüller, 2008) to a limited degree (Gaur & Gaur, 2006). At this hierarchical level, the well-known Big Five personality traits (Costa & McCrae, 1997) such as neuroticism or openness to experience describe elements of an individual’s personality (Goldberg, 1981).

Second, stable, context-specific traits are narrower traits that are useful in explaining beliefs and behaviours related to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. An example of a stable IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait is personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) (Agarwal and Prasad 1998), which is useful in understanding IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c beliefs and behaviours such as perceived ease of use or perceived usefulness (Lu, Yao, & Yu, 2005) but not useful in understanding other contexts, such as change-speci<sup>fi</sup>c beliefs or behaviours (Polites & Karahanna, 2012).

Third, dynamic, context-specific traits are also narrower traits which are particularly useful for studying beliefs and behaviours related to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. These traits are more dynamic than stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits (Davis & Yi, 2012), meaning that they are enduring but also malleable via experiences in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts. Recently, IT mindfulness, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences related beliefs and behaviours, has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such a dynamic trait in the IS context (Thatcher et al., 2018).

## 2.2.2. Relationship among traits along hierarchical levels

Given the hierarchical structure of personality traits, these three hierarchical levels of personality traits are connected by theoretical and statistical relationships. For example, previous research shows that the broad traits neuroticism and openness to experience are related to the stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait PIIT (Davis & Yi, 2012; Venkatesh, Sykes, & Venkatraman, 2014), which, in turn, is related to computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, an example of a dynamic, computer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait (McElroy, Hendrickson, Townsend, & DeMarie, 2007). Another example is that the dynamic, computer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait computer anxiety is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by broad traits such as trait anxiety and negative a<sup>f</sup>ect, as well as the stable ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c trait personal innovativeness in IT (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002). These examples are in line with the hierarchical structure, which assumes that broad traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence stable, speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uence, dynamic, speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits. The rationale for this is that a few broad traits (e.g., Big Five personality traits) in<sup>fl</sup>uence a host of narrower traits (e.g., PIIT, IT mindfulness) (Eysenck, 2013), which are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c. Some narrower traits are particularly relevant for IS, such as PIIT and IT mindfulness, and represent IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, whereby others are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to other contexts, such as dispositional resistance to change, and represent change-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits.

## 2.2.3. Summary and research gaps

Research classi<sup>fi</sup>es personality traits into three hierarchical levels which in<sup>fl</sup>uence beliefs and behaviours. The three di<sup>f</sup>erent levels vary with regard to their breadth and stability, a measure of how likely they are to change during the course of one’s life, and malleability. Broad traits such as the Big Five personality traits are highly stable (Costa & McCrae, 1997), whereby more speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits change over time (Roberts, Walton, & Viechtbauer, 2006). Particularly dynamic, contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits are malleable and can be changed to some degree through normative experiences (Lodi-Smith & Roberts, 2007), especially when individuals aim to change their personality (Hudson & Fraley, 2015). Most applicable IS research studies the impact of broad traits but conclude that the explanatory power of broad traits on beliefs and behaviours is low (McElroy et al., 2007). Psychology research recommends focusing on rather dynamic, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits that lead to more accurate results in line with the context and the research objective (Paunonen & Ashton, 2001) because such traits have the highest explanatory power. However, it is not known if that also holds true for IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c personality traits in comparison with broad traits in terms of understanding user beliefs.

## 3. Research mode

Using the research strands of technostress and personality, we next explain how personality traits from the three hierarchical levels in<sup>fl</sup>uence technostressors. While the selection of variables (here: personality traits) is essential to providing contributions, parsimony of selected variables is also recommended (Whetten, 1989). In developing a research model for this study, we aim to identify which traits are frequently used in related and recent IS research (see Appendix Table 3) and related to stress. Previous personality trait research stressed the importance of choosing personality traits relevant and related to the domain of interest to explain individual beliefs and behaviours (Paunonen & Ashton, 2001). The following section discusses our choice of personality traits for each of the hierarchical levels.

## 3.1. Selection of personality traits

In selecting speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits for each of the three hierarchical levels, we considered how frequently di<sup>f</sup>erent traits have been used in recent research (see Appendix Table 3) and how relevant they are in relation to stress. After considerable consideration, we selected neuroticism as a representative broad trait, PIIT as a representative stable, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait, and IT mindfulness as a representative dynamic, ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c trait.

## 3.1.1. Broad traits

Existing research into broad dispositional factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting IS use has either used the Big Five personality traits or Cognitive Style (McElroy et al., 2007). In the past 20 years, the focus has shifted gradually to the Big Five personality traits (Devaraj, Easley, & Crant, 2008; Venkatesh et al., 2014), as these are superior in explaining beliefs and behaviours, which also holds for IS-related studies (McElroy et al., 2007). Of the Big Five personality traits, neuroticism has received the most attention in investigations into stress (Bolger & Zuckerman, 1995), which is also the case in the strand of technostress (see Appendix Table 3; including Ayyagari et al., 2011; Maier et al., 2015c; Srivastava et al., 2015). One rationale for this might be found in rather general stress research indicating that, in addition to reporting higher perceived stress, neurotics also have greater levels of salivary cortisol (Portella, Harmer, Flint, Cowen, & Goodwin, 2005), a physiological reaction speci<sup>fi</sup>c to stress (Riedl, 2013). Due to that, we selected neuroticism as the broad trait most relevant for technostress.

## 3.1.2. Stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits

When focusing on stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, previous research has focused on two speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits: 1) personal innovativeness in IT (PIIT) and 2) dispositional resistance to change. In terms of the frequency of both in IS related research (see Appendix Table 3), PIIT (Agarwal and Prasad 1998; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002) has more often been used than other stable traits, such as dispositional resistance to change (Laumer, Maier, Eckhardt, & Weitzel, 2015, 2016; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). In addition, PIIT was used to understand concepts similar to technostress, such as computer anxiety (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002), which determines whether a user considers the use of an IS as complex (Lewis, Agarwal, & Sambamurthy, 2003; Lu et al., 2005). Moreover, from a contextual perspective, PIIT is rather an ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c trait whereby dispositional resistance to change is rather a change-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait. For these reasons, we selected PIIT as the stable, contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c personality trait most suitable for this research.

## 3.1.3. Dynamic, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits

IS use research has focused on various dynamic, contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits. While the earlier focus was on computerspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits such as computer anxiety, computer playfulness or computer self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy (e.g., Agarwal and Prasad 1998; Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002), more recent research has shifted attention to IT mindfulness (Thatcher et al., 2018). One reason might be the high popularity of general research studying the causes and consequences of mindfulness in a wide range of di<sup>f</sup>erent research streams. In that context, organisational and psychological research has revealed that the personality trait mindfulness is relevant in stress perception (e.g., Brown, Weinstein, & Creswell, 2012; Shapiro, Brown, Thoresen, & Plante, 2011) and well-being (Brown & Ryan, 2003; Langer, 1989b). Even more importantly, a strong relationship between mindfulness and the level of stress hormone cortisol has been proven, indicating a strong relationship between the trait mindfulness and stress (Brown & Ryan, 2003; Brown et al., 2012; Shapiro et al., 2011). Based on these developments and the strong relationship between mindfulness and stress, we selected IT mindfulness as the most well-suited dynamic, ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c personality trait for this study.

## 3.2. Hypothesis development

Using the research strands of technostress and personality, we next explain how personality traits from the three hierarchical levels identi<sup>fi</sup>ed above in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perception of technostress. We build on user involvement, job burnout and user performance to develop and overall research model and hypotheses, which are summarised in Figure 3.

## 3.2.1. Neuroticism and techno-stressors

Neuroticism represents “individual diferences in the tendency to experience negative, distressing emotions and to possess associated behavioral and cognitive traits” (Costa & McCrae, 1987, p. 301). Typically, individuals scoring high on neuroticism tend to experience negative emotional states (Costa & McCrae, 1987), including vulnerability to stress, anxiety, depression, hostility, and self-consciousness (McCrae & Costa, 1986; Suls, 2001).

Based on psychological research, we know that rather neurotic users are susceptible to daily stressors (Bolger & Schilling, 1991; Suls & Martin, 2005). With the high dissemination of technologies in organisations, IS use depicts a daily stressor for the working population (Maier et al., 2015a), and we argue that techno-stressors are perceived more strongly by neurotic users. This is grounded in the previous research informing us that people infer knowledge about their beliefs based on their judgments of their own past behaviour (Bem, 1972). Neurotic users tend to be less self-conscious and more likely to consider their abilities insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to meet the demands required to use the IS (Cooper et al., 2001). The resulting imbalance implies that the user perceives technologyinduced stimuli as stressful because they don’t feel able to handle the stimuli (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Moreover, neurotics tend to experience negative stimuli (Costa & McCrae, 1987) and more commonly perceive challenges while using IS, such as technooverload, techno-invasion (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008). Furthermore, neurotic users hold onto those experiences longer than less neurotic users. When then forming beliefs, such as perceptions about technostressors, the neurotic user is biased by her negative experiences (Bem, 1972) and thus forms rather higher perceptions of techno-stress. Thus, neurotic users are more vulnerable to perceiving technological characteristics as stressful, which lead them to develop negative perceptions towards IS, such as perceiving IS as rather complex, uncertain or insecure. We thus hypothesise that:

H1a: The higher the individual’s neuroticism, the higher the techno-stressors.

## 3.2.2. Personal innovativeness in IT and techno-stressors

IS-related research on narrower, stable, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits emphasises the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of personal innovativeness in information technology (PIIT). Research de<sup>fi</sup>nes PIIT as a user’s willingness to try out new IS (Agarwal and Prasad 1998) and theorises as well as validates that it is related to stress-related perceptions. Among others, PIIT in<sup>fl</sup>uences computer anxiety (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002) and whether a user considers the use of an IS as complex (Lewis et al., 2003; Lu et al., 2005), which is similar to the techno-stressor techno-complexity. We argue that individuals high in PIIT are less likely to perceive technostress. The rationale for this is that individuals high in PIIT tend to experiment with IS and are rather spontaneous, <sup>fl</sup>exible, and creative when interacting with IS (Agarwal and Prasad 1998). This has two consequences for the suggested relationships with techno-stressors. First, the characteristics of individuals high in PIIT are contrary to those characterising techno-stressors. Among others, such individuals are <sup>fl</sup>exible in using IS, which is contrary to the techno-stressor techno-uncertainty, which is when individuals react negatively to IS upgrades or changes, or to the techno-stressor techno-overload, which is when individuals do not want to adapt their habits to <sup>fi</sup>t a new IS. Further, as individuals high on PIIT are rather creative in using IS (Agarwal and Prasad 1998) and less likely to perceive complex IS as hindering, which would be an example of techno-complexity. Furthermore, such individuals are typically among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to use an IS and thus less likely to perceive a new IS as a threat, which would be techno-insecurity. Through a theoretical lens, individuals high in PIIT consider themselves as having the abilities to meet the demands required to use the IS (Cooper et al., 2001). On the contrary, individuals low in PIIT perceive an imbalance between their own abilities and the demands set by the IS, which lead them to perceive techno-stressors as a threat. Second, individuals high in PIIT are engaged in using IS and are frequently in a state of cognitive absorption (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Thus, individuals high on PIIT form beliefs, including perceptions of technostress, in a state of cognitive absorption, which positively shapes their perceptions (Bem, 1972), making it less likely for such users to perceive technostress. Thus, we assume that:

H1b: The higher the personal innovativeness in IT, the lower the techno-stressors.

## 3.2.3. IT mindfulness and techno-stressors

In general, mindfulness represents an individual’s state of alertness and dynamic awareness (Langer, 1989a, 1989b). As it is easier for some individuals to attain such a state than for others (Dane, 2011), mindfulness is considered a trait rather than a learned skill. In terms of IS research, IT mindful ness can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a “dynamic IS-specific trait, evident when working with IT, whereby the user focuses on the present, pays attention to detail, exhibits a willingness to consider other uses, and expresses genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures” (Thatcher et al., 2018, pp. 833–835). Thus, it re<sup>fl</sup>ects a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait relevant to understanding IS-related beliefs and behaviours. Based on general mindfulness research (Langer, 1997, 2014), we identify four dimensions of IT mindfulness (Thatcher et al., 2018). First, alertness to distinction re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which users understand the capabilities as well as the potential of an IS. A mindful IS user will more quickly recognise di<sup>f</sup>erences between technology features as well as the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a new or updated IS feature. Second, orientation in the present re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which a user is involved in a given context, so that a rather mindful IS user constantly adopts new features to increase her productivity while performing a task. Third, awareness of multiple perspectives re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which a user takes di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives on how an IS is used and what value these perspectives might bring to the user, so that rather mindful IS users hold di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives and can develop innovative solutions. Fourth, openness to novelty re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extent to which a user is willing to explore new potential or features within an IS, such that mindful IS users are curious and <sup>fl</sup>exible in their IS use and open to new features of an IS.

In addition to the conceptualisation of IT mindfulness, some IS-related research has used IT mindfulness to understand beliefs and behaviours. Among others, in the context of IS use, mindfulness causes a reduction in uncertainty and an increase in perceived usefulness and a higher perceived task-technology-<sup>fi</sup>t, thus fostering intentions to use IS (Sun, Fang, & Zou, 2016). Due to that, mindful IS users tend to be rather innovative and use many di<sup>f</sup>erent features of IS (Thatcher et al., 2018).

We posit that IT mindfulness in<sup>fl</sup>uences the degree to which IS use, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally complex IS use, is perceived as a techno-stressor. An IS user is considered mindful if she is highly alert to distinction (Langer, 1989a, 1989b) and tends to create new and e<sup>f</sup>ective ways to accomplish tasks by using IS (Thatcher et al., 2018). This leads the mindful IS user to experience less stress when an IS requires her to adapt her work habits (techno-overload) or when IS undergoes constant changes and upgrades (techno-uncertainty). Second, the mindful IS user’s orientation to the present (Langer, 1989a, 1989b) makes her more likely to get involved when using IS and to keep an eye on the big picture (Thatcher et al., 2018) allowing her to better distinguish between private and work domains. This makes the user less likely to experience IS as invading her personal life (techno-invasion). Third, the awareness of multiple perspectives enables mindful IS users to be open to new ways of using IS (Thatcher et al., 2018) such that she is less challenged by constant IS changes (techno-uncertainty). Moreover, such a mindful IS user is eager to learn new ways of using an IS (Thatcher et al., 2018) so that she seldom does not know enough about a technology (techno-complexity) and is seldom afraid to learn new skills to avoid being replaced by technology (techno-insecurity). Fourth, the openness to novelty enables rather mindful IS users to explore new potential or features within an IS (Thatcher et al., 2018). This means that even though techno-stressors are often grounded in perceptions of uncertainty due to IS use, e.g., frequent updates (techno-uncertainty), a mindful IS user is more willing to explore these new, updated IS features and is less challenged by constant changes and updates. Theoretically, this means that a rather mindful IS user considers oneself to have the abilities to <sup>fi</sup>t the demands (Cooper et al., 2001), which positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the formation of beliefs (Bem, 1972), such as reducing perceptions of techno-stress. Based on that, we assume that:

H1c: The higher the IT mindfulness, the lower the techno-stressors.

## 3.2.4. The relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of neuroticism, personal innovativeness in IT, and IT mindfulness on techno-stressors

From a general point of view, we propose that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the personality traits on techno-stressors varies depending on the hierarchical levels of the personality.

First, we suppose that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of neuroticism on techno-stressors is comparatively lower than for PIIT and IT mindfulness. The rationale for this is that broad traits such as neuroticism are, unlike PIIT and IT mindfulness, not speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the IS use context. That means that even neurotic individuals are vulnerable to perceiving stress, the relation to IS use and the perception of technostress is rather low (Maier et al., 2015c; McElroy et al., 2007). In contrast, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits such as PIIT and IT mindfulness more strongly a<sup>f</sup>ect use perceptions and behaviours (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002; Thatcher et al., 2018). Consider techno-complexity, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects whether users are able to use IS. Individuals with strong IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits PIIT and IT mindfulness tend to experiment with and use IS, a tendency not in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by neuroticism. Theoretically speaking (Cooper et al., 2001), individuals with strong PIIT traits or who are mindful users are more likely to perceive themselves as having the ability to meet the demands required by using IS than neurotic individuals, which is not per se relevant to IS use. Likewise, the strength of PIIT traits or mindfulness in<sup>fl</sup>uences the formation of beliefs (Bem, 1972), including techno-stressors, more strongly than neuroticism does.

Second, we assume that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of PIIT as a stable, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait on techno-stressors is lower than the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IT mindfulness as a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait. This is grounded in the fact that dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits are enduring but also malleable via experiences (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002; Thatcher et al., 2018). Individuals’ consideration of abilities developed by using the IS (Cooper et al., 2001) is thus more relevant for malleable traits, such as IT mindfulness, than for stable traits, such as PIIT. Hence, everyday experiences of using IS are re<sup>fl</sup>ected more by IT mindfulness than by PIIT. As the user gains experience handling IS or with the degree of invasion of IS in the daily work, this is re<sup>fl</sup>ected more in dynamic IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, such that IT mindfulness more strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceptions about the invasion (techno-invasion) or complexity (technocomplexity), among others.

These arguments re<sup>fl</sup>ect a di<sup>f</sup>erent relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the three personality traits on technostressors. This is in line with psychological research positing that narrower personality traits have a higher explanatory power of and stronger relationship to beliefs and behaviours than broad traits (Paunonen & Ashton, 2001). In the IS context, that means that PIIT and IT mindfulness have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on techno-stressors than neuroticism, and IT mindfulness has a stronger relationship due to PIIT, because it is malleable, so that we assume that:

H2: IT mindfulness has a stronger impact on technostressors than PIIT, which in turn has a stronger impact than neuroticism.

## 3.2.5. Techno-stressors, job burnout, and user performance

A wealth of research posits that the techno-stressors cause psychological and behavioural reactions among IS users (Tarafdar et al., 2010). A typical psychological reaction to techno-stressors is job burnout, which is a user reaction to perceived chronic stressors at work (Maslach, Schaufeli, & Leiter, 2001). The rationale for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of techno-stressors on job burnout is that cognition causes psychological reactions. This means that after evaluating the use of an IS as adverse and challenging, e.g., if the user perceives IS as forcing her to work faster (technooverload) or as an invasion into her personal life (techno-invasion), the user may grow frustrated with and emotionally drained by her work and feel exhausted by work (Maslach & Jackson, 1986). We therefore assume that:

## H3: The higher the techno-stressors, the higher the likelihood of job burnout.

Second, a typical behavioural reaction to technostressors is lower user performance, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which individuals use IS to improve their work e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness (Compeau, Higgins, & Hu<sup>f</sup>, 1999; Tarafdar et al., 2015), an important variable for organisations to remain successful over time. Even though previous research has emphasised a negative relationship between techno-stressors and user performance (Tarafdar et al., 2010), we align with the renowned Yerkes and Dodson (1908) law positing an inverted u-relationship between arousal and performance. The rationale for this quadratic relationship is related to stress hormone levels and adrenergic activity in the prefrontal cortex, which are relevant for individuals’ working memory and executive function processes (Arnsten, 2009; Arnsten & Goldman-Rakic, 1998; Hains & Arnsten, 2008; Lupien, Maheu, Tu, Fiocco, & Schramek, 2007; Mizoguchi, Ishige, Takeda, Aburada, & Tabira, 2004). Thereby, a moderate degree of arousal is considered to increase cognitive processes, which are crucial for alertness, attention focus, or memory capacity or consolidation (Arnsten, 2009; Diamond, Campbell, Park, Halonen, & Zoladz, 2007; Roozendaal, 2002; Sauro, Jorgensen, & Pedlow, 2003; Sharot & Phelps, 2004). In that line, recent IS research posits that both too much and too little email use compared to a user’s preferences might be stressful (Stich, Tarafdar, Stacey, & Cooper, 2019) and thereby have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user behaviour. Notably, the optima level of arousal varies for individuals and depends on a variety of contextual factors including task complexity and degree of knowledge.

De<sup>fi</sup>ning techno-stressors as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c user’s arousal while using IS, we posit a likewise inverted u-relationship between techno-stressors and user performance.

The inverted u-relationship means that there is an individual tipping point up to which techno-stressors might have positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on user performance and beyond which the perception of increased technostressors are likely to have negative e<sup>f</sup>ects. Psychological research has provided theoretical and empirical evidence that perceptions of stressors might be appraised as potentially promoting individuals’ personal growth and achievements (Cavanaugh, Boswell, Roehling, & Boudreau, 2000; Crawford, Lepine, & Rich, 2010; Podsako<sup>f</sup>, LePine, & Lepine, 2007). Among others, workload or complexity issues have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as stressful but also as stimuli that challenge individuals to perform better (LePine, Zhang, Crawford, & Rich, 2016; Zhang, LePine, Buckman, & Wei, 2014). As stressful technological characteristics also include an increased workload and a high complexity, these might challenge IS users, resulting in improved user performance. Among others, a user may perceive tighter time schedules due to IS use as an opportunity to optimise their daily work routine, resulting in improved user performance. However, there is a tipping point after which an increase in perceived technostress impairs user performance. The tipping point is individual and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the point at which additional stressful stimuli perceived by the individual results in lower user performance. This is grounded in the fact that the user concludes that she lacks the ability to handle additional techno-stressors appropriately (Ayyagari et al., 2011). Accordingly, the higher the techno-stressors, the more a user appraise the stimuli as constraining their personal development and work accomplishments (Ayyagari et al., 2011). This part of the inverted u-curve relationship is in line with previous research suggesting an inverse relationship between technostressors and user performance (Tarafdar et al., 2010), resulting in poor user performance.

We therefore assume that techno-stressors have an inverted u-curve, i.e., negative quadratic, e<sup>f</sup>ect on user performance. The general relationship between techno-stressors and user performance is aligned with the previous technostress research, suggesting that individuals consider techno-stressors as a threat and Yerkes-Dodson law suggesting an inverted u-relationship, so that we hypothesise that:

H4: Techno-stressors have a negative quadratic e<sup>f</sup>ect on user performance.

## 3.2.6. User involvement, techno-stressors, job burnout, and user performance

User involvement re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which organisations allow and encourage users to participate in the planning, development, and implementation of IS (Tarafdar et al., 2010). It is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an external factor because employees have no control over it and part of the organisational culture. Previous research focusing on user involvement has focused primarily on the relationships between developers, managers, and users (Henfridsson & Lindgren, 2010), as a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the culture and norms prevailing in organisations. In this case, high user involvement indicates a communicative organisational culture as developers integrate users’ needs during IS implementations. User involvement impacts how IS is perceived and used. As high user involvement leads to better acceptance (Olson & Ives, 1981), user involvement reduces the techno-stressors and how users react to them (Tarafdar et al., 2010). The rationale for this is that when users are closely involved, they are accustomed to working with the IS, can state their needs and control the processes while the IS is developed and adapted. Hence, high involvement is the basis for creating a working environment in which using IS is not considered a source and symbol of stress, e.g., di<sup>fi</sup>cult to use (techno-complexity).

Moreover, an environment that facilitates high user involvement also bu<sup>f</sup>ers negative consequences and sets the stage for positive work outcomes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2015). With respect to job burnout as psychological and user performance as behavioural strain, we posit that user involvement decreases the likelihood of job burnout because it creates strong communication paths for users to seek support. It also increases user performance because it enables users to accomplish tasks by using IS e<sup>f</sup>ectively and e<sup>fi</sup>ciently (Bala & Venkatesh, 2015). Based on that, we hypothesise that:

H5: The higher the user involvement, the lower the techno-stressors.

H6: The higher the user involvement, a) the lower the likelihood of job burnout and b) the higher the user performance.

In the next section, we explain the research method we designed to evaluate our hypotheses.

## 4. Research method: 2 two-wave studies in an organisation (sample 1) and via mTurk (sample 2)

As expressed in the hypotheses, this research aims to provide a theoretical explanation for how user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences techno-stressors. Since it is necessary to capture techno-stressors and (techno-) strain at di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the underlying causality between stressors and strain (Ayyagari et al., 2011), we collected survey data at two di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time. Further, empirical research has often been challenged as it does not provide reproducible results due to a systematic or random error. One proposed countermeasure is to replicate results in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts (Dennis & Valacich, 2014), so we also collected data via Amazon Mechanical Turk (mTurk), which is an online crowdsourcing market (OCM). mTurk has been successfully validated by previous research (Steelman, Hammer, & Limayem, 2014), and it is also considered to be at least equivalent to other research methods (Lowry, D’Arcy, Hammer, & Moody, 2016) while it provides the possibility to collect a cross-industry and cross-organisation sample (Chen & Karahanna, 2018). This gave us two data samples to evaluate the research model, sample 1, in which we collected data in one organisation and sample 2, in which we collected data via mTurk.

## 4.1. Organisational characteristics and data collection

For sample 1, we had an agreement with an organisation in Germany to survey some of their employees. The organisation produces drivetrain and brake system applications for the automotive industry, has more than 3,500 employees, and a sales volume of around 500 million euros. The organisation has factories around the world including, among others, the USA, Germany, China, Brazil, and Mexico. Employees in that organisation use SAP (e.g., SAP BP, BP CRM, BP ECC, MM, SD, PP, FICO) as part of their daily working process but also a variety of other technologies, including mobile (cell phone), communication (e-mail), collaborative (instant messaging), and network (VPN) technologies. Based on previous research, we know that these might result in technostressors (Ayyagari et al., 2011). To survey employees, we worked together with the CEO o<sup>fi</sup>ce of the organisation, and set up two surveys with six weeks between the surveys. The number of invitations was limited by the CEO o<sup>fi</sup>ce and employees were selected randomly, but all of them were Germanspeaking, work in the same branch and use the above-mentioned technologies for their work. The invitations to participate in the survey were distributed in one factory in Germany. In total, we invited 150 employees to participate in both surveys. In those surveys, we measured the three personality traits neuroticism, PIIT and IT mindfulness, technostressors and user involvement in survey 1 and the dependent variables job burnout and user performance in survey 2, six weeks later.

We then repeated the same procedure for sample 2, publishing a link to our study in mTurk. We also included a number of safety check questions (e.g., “I use IS for my work”, “I am employed”, “I earn less than 25% of my monthly income with mTurk”, “Please indicate if you have a job outside of mTurk”, “Please indicate your highest educational level”, “Please click on ‘strongly disagree’”) to ensure that participants’ main job was not mTurk, use IS for their work, successfully passed at least secondary education, and read the items carefully. Further, only individuals living in the USA were able to participate in the survey. Similar to sample 1, we also collected personality traits, techno-stressors and user involvement in one survey and job burnout and user performance in a second survey, six weeks later. We paid USD 0.50 for each participant in survey 1 and USD 1.00 for participating in the follow-up survey and had a <sup>fi</sup>nal data set of 408 employees after removing 34 participants who fail at least one of the safety checks.

## 4.2. Measures

In both samples, our survey measures were based on widely used measures that have been validated in the previous research. Our measure of neuroticism included three items used in recent technostress research (Srivastava et al., 2015) and based on psychological research (Gosling, Rentfrow, & Swann, 2003; Lang, John, Lüdtke, Schupp, & Wagner, 2011; Saucier, 1994). In sample 2 (see post-hoc analysis), in addition to using the same measure for neuroticism, we also used an additional measure with eight items (John & Srivastava, 2001). PIIT was measured with four items (Agarwal and Prasad 1998). For IT mindfulness, we used the second-order construct validated and described by Thatcher et al. (2018). To measure, the second-order constructs techno-stressors we used the items developed by Ragu-Nathan et al. (2008). To measure the two techno-strain constructs, we relied on Maslach and Jackson (1986) for job burnout and Torkzadeh and Doll (1999) for user performance. Finally, we measured user involvement using the items identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Karimi, Somers, and Bhattacherjee (2007). We provide an overview of all items used in our research model in the Appendix (Table 4). Except for IT mindfulness (5-point Likert scale), we use a 7-point Likert scale to measure the items with 1 as “strongly disagree” and 7 (5 in the case of IT mindfulness) as “strongly agree”.

By using these measures, we also compared the mean values with those from previous research. We see that neuroticism has a mean value comparable to those used in recent IS research (Maier et al., 2015c). The mean value of PIIT di<sup>f</sup>ers in both samples, but this is also seen in the previous IS research (Agarwal and Prasad 1998; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). For IT mindfulness, the mean values of the whole construct as well as those for the four <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs are rather similar to those from recent IS research (Thatcher et al., 2018) and it aligns with previous research in that the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs awareness of multiple perspectives always has the highest mean value. Finally, the mean values of the <sup>fi</sup>ve technostressors are also similar to those known from previous research when a 7-point Likert scale was used (Maier et al., 2015a).

## 5. Research results

We analysed the results of both samples following the partial least squares (PLS) method and SmartPLS 3.2.4 (Ringle, Wende, & Becker, 2014). We choose PLS because it is less restrictive for skewed variable distributions, which is relevant for technostress research.

## 5.1. Sample characteristics and required sample size

In response to the 150 invitations in sample 1, 126 employees participated in both surveys. On average (see Table 1 for more details), more men participated in our study than women, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects the gender distribution in that organisation. The average participant was 37 years old and one out of three individuals worked as IS professional as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the previous research (Eckhardt et al., 2016). Finally, most of the participants had a full-time employment contract, and all employees with a part-time employment contract worked at least 20 h per week.

For sample 2, we had a <sup>fi</sup>nal data set of 408 employees. On average, slightly more women participated in that sample and the average age was 33, whereby most of the participants had a full-time employment contract and almost two out of three participants did not work as IS professionals. While collecting the data via mTurk, we ensured by including control questions that all participants are above 18 years old, have an employment outside mTurk and live in the USA.

Generally speaking, 126 (sample 1) and 408 (sample 2) employees participating in the two samples is su<sup>fi</sup>cient to evaluate the proposed research model with two distinct samples. Two facts suggest that both samples are large enough to evaluate them separately. First, the rule of 10 suggests that we need at least 70 participants to run PLS technically (techno-stressors, job burnout, and user performance have all seven antecedents while calculating latent variable scores, which is the highest number in research model). Second, we followed Kim’s (2005) suggestion that a given level of power depends on a) the number of variables/degrees of freedom, b) the relations among the variables, c) choice of <sup>fi</sup>t index, and d) the value of the <sup>fi</sup>t index and proposes a range of di<sup>f</sup>erent <sup>fi</sup>t indexes. Using Steiger’s gamma (with γ = 0.95; α = 0.05; Power = 0.90), the proposed sample size is 113.8 and using RMSEA (α = 0.05; Power = 0.90) reveals a minimum-proposed sample size of 41 for the proposed research model. The sample size of both samples exceeds these thresholds.

## 5.2. Common method bias

To determine the extent of common method bias (CMB), we <sup>fi</sup>rst ran the Harman’s single factor test. The results indicate that one factor explains less than 30% in sample 1, respectively, 32% in sample 2. In a second step, we ran the test proposed by Williams, Edwards, and Vandenberg (2003), who suggest determining the extent of CMB with the help of PLS by including a CMB factor into the model. All remaining factors were transformed into several single-item constructs, and the ratio of $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ with a CMB factor to $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ without a CMB factor was compared. This indicates ratios of 1:368.5 for sample 1 and 1:243 for sample 2, i.e., no observable signs of CMB in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

## 5.3. Measurement model

Each construct in the proposed research model is measured by re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators, so that we focus on content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity to ensure the validity of the measurement model (Bagozzi, 1979). We operationalise techno-stressors and IT mindfulness as a superordinate second-order construct, which manifest in <sup>fi</sup>ve respective four re<sup>fl</sup>ective <sup>fi</sup>rstorder dimensions. We followed recommendations of previous research (Wright, Campbell, Thatcher, & Roberts, 2012) to calculate latent variable scores, which are used as indicators of the second-order construct.

## 5.3.1. Content validity

To ensure content validity, we only used items from prior IS use and technostress research. Details of our items are provided in the measures part of the section “research method” and in the Appendix (Table 4).

## 5.3.2. Indicator reliability

The indicator reliability indicates the rate of the variance of an indicator that has its origins in the latent variables. To explain 50% of the variance of a latent variable by the indicators, the value has to be at least 0.707 (Carmines & Zeller, 2008). This is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled for all indicators. Moreover, the loadings are highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (see Appendix Tables 5 and 6), as proven using the bootstrap method with 500 and 5,000 samples.

## 5.3.3. Construct reliability

To determine the quality at the construct level, composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) are used (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). AVE should be higher than 0.5 and CR higher than 0.7, and both criteria are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled (see Appendix Tables 5 and 6).

## 5.3.4. Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity describes the extent to which measurement items di<sup>f</sup>er from others (Campell & Fiske, 1959). Since the square root values of AVE (see Appendix Tables 5 and 6) are greater than the corresponding construct correlations (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Hulland, 1999), we can state that this requirement is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. As the heterotrait–monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations criterion detects a lack of discriminant validity more reliably than the Fornell-Larcker criterion, it is also used to assess discriminant validity (Henseler, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2014). Using the absolute $\mathrm { H T M T } _ { 0 . 8 5 }$ criterion indicates that discriminant validity is not an issue in our research. The highest correlation exists in sample 1 between IT mindfulness and PIIT: 0.847, and the bootstrapping approach indicates that HTMT is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erent from 1, so we conclude that the measurement model is valid.

We also tested for multicollinearity in both samples. We used the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) as indicator and results reveal that in both studies, each VIF value is lower than the recommended maximum VIF value of <sup>fi</sup>ve (Rogerson, 2001) with the highest value of 3.786 between PIIT and the second-order construct techno-stressors (sample 1). When focusing on the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order constructs, all values are even lower than three.

In addition, we provide a mediation analysis in order to understand whether personality traits have an indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence on job burnout and user performance via techno-stressors. Figure 4 indicates that the three personality traits neuroticism, PIIT, IT mindfulness and user involvement, explain 28.0% respectively<sup>2</sup> 22.9% of the variance of techno-stressors. Techno-stressors and user involvement, in turn, explain 19.4% respectively 35.6% of the variance of job burnout and 47.5% respectively 20.3% of the variance of user performance.

Concerning the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance levels of the path coef-<sup>fi</sup>cients, our results indicate that the three personality traits cause techno-stressors in both samples.

With a value of 0.074 for sample 1 and 0.099 for sample 2 that is less than the recommended values of 0.10 (Hu & Bentler, 1999), a good <sup>fi</sup>t can be concluded.

## 6.2. Including the Big Five personality traits as a whole

In section 2, we explain our choice of the broad trait neuroticism because it has a high correlation with cortisol and has been used in the previous technostress research. With knowing that neuroticism is one speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspect of the Big Five personality traits, we also collected the other four traits in sample 2. We measured each of those with the short version (Saucier, 1994) and then included the constructs in our research model. Our results indicate that extraversion (the tendency to seek the stimulation of others) $( \beta = - 0 . 0 6 5 , \ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , openness to experience (the tendency to prefer new experiences over routines) $( \beta \ = \ - 0 . 0 6 2 , \ \ p \ > \ 0 . 0 5 )$ , agreeableness (the tendency to cooperate with others) $( \beta \ : = \ : 0 . 0 7 7 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ } >$ 0.05), and consciousness (the tendency to act in a planned and duty-oriented manner) $( \beta \ = \ 0 . 0 3 3 ,$ $\mathrm { ~ p ~ } > \ 0 . 0 5 )$ all do not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with techno-stressors.

## 6.3. Distinguishing part-time from full-time worker

Within sample 1 for which we collected data in one particular organisation, we included a question to control whether participants work part-time or full-time. This di<sup>f</sup>ers from sample 2, where we only allowed mTurk workers with a full-time contract to participate. This enabled us to distinguish the e<sup>f</sup>ects of user personality on technostress for both groups by using multigroup-analysis procedure. Our results indicate that the mean-values are rather similar for neuroticism (fulltime workers 3.3 vs. part-time workers 3.4), whereby signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences (t-test result < 0.05 for each signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence) exist for techno-stressors (full-time workers 3.7 vs. part-time workers 3.8), PIIT (full-time workers 5.1 vs. part-time workers 4.8) and IT mindfulness (full-time workers 3.5 vs. part-time workers 3.3). The correlations between each of the personality traits and technostress di<sup>f</sup>er only slightly (di<sup>f</sup>erence of in<sup>fl</sup>uence of neuroticism on technostress for full- vs parttime workers: −0.029; a di<sup>f</sup>erence of PIIT: 0.024; a di<sup>f</sup>erence of IT mindfulness: 0.032). In short, the results did not vary, meaning that the relative importance of the personality traits are stable. This means that in both groups, IT mindfulness has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant higher e<sup>f</sup>ect on technostress than neuroticism and PIIT.

## 6.4. Respecting di<sup>f</sup>erent generations

Previous technostress research provides inconsistent results concerning the e<sup>f</sup>ect of age on technostressors, meaning that some <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that there is a correlation (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008), whereby others failed in con<sup>fi</sup>rming that (Maier et al., 2015a, 2015b). However, we also want to check the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of age and thereby decided to study the e<sup>f</sup>ect of di<sup>f</sup>erent generations and how they perceive technostress. We therefore assigned each participant to one of the following groups of generations<sup>3</sup>: baby boomers (born 1956–1965), generation X (born 1966–1980), generation Y (born 1981–1995) and/or generation Z (born after 1995). By using four groups and the need to have enough employees in those groups, we did this analysis with the larger data set, sample 2.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst performed an ANOVA to test for di<sup>f</sup>erences and then used post-hoc analyses to see which groups di<sup>f</sup>er signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. We thereby see that individuals from the generation Z have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant lower perception of technostress (mean-value of 3.5) than the other generations, baby boomers have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant higher perception of technostress (mean value of 3.9) than the other generations, and there is no di<sup>f</sup>erence between individuals from the groups of generation X and Y (both have meanvalues of 3.7). Concerning IT mindfulness, results indicate that individuals from the baby boomer generation have the signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lowest values (meanvalue of 3.2), generation X has the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant highest value (mean-value of 3.8), and there are no di<sup>f</sup>erences between the generation Y and Z (both have mean-values of 3.6). For PIIT, we only see that baby boomers are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent to the three other groups (mean-value of 4.1; the others have meanvalues of 4.3 or 4.4). For neuroticism, we cannot see any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences among the four groups of generations.

We afterwards recalculated the model using the multi-group analysis approach implemented in SmartPLS. We thereby see that the results (strength of relationship between each of the personality trait and techno-stressors and relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence) are similar to the results when using the full data set for the two groups of generation Y and baby boomers. For the group of generation Z, we additionally see that PIIT has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant stronger relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on techno-stressors than neuroticism. For the group of generation X, we see that both neuroticism and PIIT are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for techno-stressors.

## 7. Discussion, contribution, limitations, and future research

Motivated by the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of technostress as a downside of IS use resulting in decreased wellbeing and poor user performance, this research aims to explain why some users perceive a certain stimulus as a techno-stressor but others do not. We reveal two gaps in research – how user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences techno-stressors (Tarafdar et al., 2017) and how personality traits typical of di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels di<sup>f</sup>erently impact user beliefs (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002) – and close those gaps by answering our research questions. We aim to understand how personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors. In acknowledgement that personality traits can be categorised on di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels, we chose one of each based on incidence (see Appendix Table 3) and its relationship to stress (Paunonen & Ashton, 2001): neuroticism (broad trait), PIIT (stable, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait) and IT mindfulness (dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait) and studied their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on techno-stressors. With two samples, each representing a two-wave study with data collected in an organisation (sample 1) and via mTurk (sample 2), this research contributes by providing a theoretical understanding of how and which personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors. The following outlines our speci<sup>fi</sup>c contributions.

## 7.1. User personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences techno-stressors

Previous technostress research explains that personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceived psychological and behavioural strain (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Maier et al., 2015c) and how techno-stressors are translated into strain (Srivastava et al., 2015). Tarafdar et al. (2017) recently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a gap in research on the relationship between user personality and technostressors, which limits our understanding of how and why users perceive techno-stressors. We thereby contribute a theoretical explanation of how personality traits interrelate with other aspects studied in the previous technostress research, postulating that personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which users perceive technostress. We reveal that personality traits directly in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors. Therewith, we contribute in terms of how (Whetten, 1989) techno-stressors occur by theorising and empirically validating that this is grounded in user personality, uncovering the psychological dynamics of perceptions of techno-stress.

Furthermore, we align with psychological research positing that there is a hierarchical structure of personality traits including (1) broad traits; (2) stable, contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits; and (3) dynamic, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits. Even though previous IS research has explained how traits from di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels are interrelated (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002), no previous studies have shown how these in<sup>fl</sup>uence user beliefs di<sup>f</sup>erently. We provide a theoretical argumentation to explain that the impact of personality traits varies depending on their hierarchical level. Based on empirics from 2 two-wave studies, we contribute that, next to technological characteristics (Ayyagari et al., 2011), personality traits also in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors.

Based on all that, we contribute to previous research on technostress by showing that some part of whether individuals are prone to be stressed by IT use is grounded in their personality. Even though we still know how user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences how technostressors impact techno-strain (Srivastava et al., 2015), there was no indication that more than 20% of the variance of techno-stressors is predetermined by user personality. While this initially seems to be bad news, the good news is that individuals can do something against it, as the dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait is more responsible than a broad trait, which is explained next.

## 7.2. IT mindfulness as a dynamic, is-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait has the strongest in<sup>fl</sup>uence on techno-stressors

Besides the general in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality traits on techno-stressors, we provide theoretical arguments that explain that narrower traits have a stronger e<sup>f</sup>ect than broader traits. In particular, we provide evidence that IT mindfulness, as a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait, has a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on techno-stressors than PIIT or neuroticism. This theoretical argument and empirical result contribute to the literature as previous technostress research has concentrated on the Big Five personality traits to understand technostress (Srivastava et al., 2015). We, therefore, recommend that future research studying techno-stressors should focus on IT mindfulness when considering the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality on techno-stressors, as this has the strongest in<sup>fl</sup>uence in our study. This is in line with the general argument that dynamic, contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c and especially mindfulness are more important when studying individuals stress.

More general, this research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst in the stream of IS use that hypothesises a distinct relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality traits depending on the hierarchical level to which they belong. Hence, this research is a response to and provides an answer for recent research considering that the explanatory power of user personality traits is disappointing (McElroy et al., 2007). In that respect, our results recommend using rather narrow and dynamic traits to understand beliefs and behaviours related to IS research in general. This is grounded in the fact that dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits explain beliefs far stronger than broad traits. However, it is essential to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c personality traits relevant for the context under consideration. For our purpose to study a relationship between personality traits and stress, we selected IT mindfulness, as mindfulness is related to stress (e.g., Brown et al., 2012; Shapiro et al., 2011). This research can be used for other research streams than technostress by identifying dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits and studying their in<sup>fl</sup>uence.

## 7.3. Techno-stressors have a negative quadratic in<sup>fl</sup>uence on user performance in a two-wave study

Even though the main focus of this research is to study the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of user personality on technostressors, we also include user performance and job burnout in our research as two strain variables re<sup>fl</sup>ecting employees’ productivity and well-being. Based on that, we revealed that personality traits have an indirect mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect via techno-stressors.

We also focus on the relationship between technostressors and user performance. Here, previous technostress research indicates an inverse correlation resulting in poor user performance (Tarafdar et al., 2010). Our results agree with that negative relationship but we also provide theoretical insights that go beyond that. We suggest and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support an inverted u-curve relationship between techno-stressors and user performance. This means that while low or high perception of technostress result in a poorer user performance, our study shows that a moderate level of techno-stressors cause an improvement of user performance. Hence, we contribute by arguing and demonstrating that there is a non-linear relationship between the techno-stressors and user performance.

From a methodological point of view, an increasing number of research articles study quadratic e<sup>f</sup>ects (Haans, Pieters, & He, 2016) to better understand real-world phenomena, but this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst research to prove an inverted-u relationship between technostressors and user performance. In contrast to previous technostress research, we performed two-wave analyses, collecting techno-stressors and user performance at two di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time, thus acknowledging the causality underlying the stressor–strain relationship, which has not been done in the previous research (Ayyagari et al., 2011). As the data analysis reveals a rather high e<sup>f</sup>ect size following recommendations of previous research studying e<sup>f</sup>ect sizes in moderations (Carte & Russell, 2003), we recommend theorising and studying quadratic e<sup>f</sup>ects in technostress and related IS use research.

## 7.4. Replication of research results with two samples

Recently, empirical research was criticised for not being replicable due to a systematic or random error. One proposed countermeasure is to replicate results in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts (Dennis & Valacich, 2014). In the IS context, this recommendation gave life to a new journal named AIS Transactions on Replication Research with the mission of replicating studies and publishing them until scienti<sup>fi</sup>c consensus is reached. To address the replicability of this research, we collected two data samples; one within an organisation among employees with similar working environments (sample 1) and one via mTurk among employees working in di<sup>f</sup>erent organisations (sample 2). Even though employees working in an organisation might use di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies, due to working in di<sup>f</sup>erent departments, the technologies used in sample 2 vary even more, because the ERP or ECM systems di<sup>f</sup>er between organisations. Nonetheless, we see that the main results in terms of the relative e<sup>f</sup>ect of personality traits on technostressors are stable and results reached consensus. This provides insights in that di<sup>f</sup>erent contextual factors do not in<sup>fl</sup>uence whether and how personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors. These contextual factors – grounded in the characteristics of both samples – include whether participants a) work in one (sample 1) or di<sup>f</sup>erent (sample 2) organisations, b) use similar (sample 1) or di<sup>f</sup>erent (sample 2) technologies, and c) are embedded in similar (sample 1) or di<sup>f</sup>erent organisational environments (sample 2). Beyond that, we also provide empirical evidence that the used measures – with respecting that there are several measures for neuroticism – do not change results and thus that the results can be replicated with di<sup>f</sup>erent measures.

## 7.5. Generational- and employment contract-speci<sup>fi</sup>c di<sup>f</sup>erences

In addition to the theorised and validated e<sup>f</sup>ects, we see two further results contributing to literature.

First, we consider generational e<sup>f</sup>ects as an individual-level contextual factor. Even though IS use research has demonstrated the importance of age, technostress research provides mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the role of age re<sup>fl</sup>ecting that some articles indicate that age in<sup>fl</sup>uences techno-stressors (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008), whereby others could not replicate that (Maier et al., 2015a, 2015b). In our study, we grouped individuals with respect to their generational a<sup>fi</sup>liation determined by their year of birth. Findings indicate that individuals from the generation of baby boomers have the lowest values in PIIT and IT mindfulness. This might explain why these individuals have the highest perceptions of technostress. In contrast, individuals from generation Z have the lowest perceptions of technostress. This represents empirical evidence that age has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on techno-stressors, which becomes more apparent when employees are grouped by generation. Second, we focus on whether employees are full- or part-time worker. Findings suggest that part-time workers are more stressed by using IS, which may be because they have less experience. With both analyses, we provide a deeper understanding of who experiences techno-stressors and thereby provide two relevant contextual factors beyond those studied in the previous research (ISvs. non-IS professionals; Maier et al., 2015a).

## 7.6. Implementing mechanisms to increase IT mindfulness to decrease perceived techno-stressors

The research also has practical implications. The bad news is that personality has an in<sup>fl</sup>uence on whether employees perceive stress when using IS. This means that employees are predisposed to perceiving technostressors to some degree. The good news is that IT mindfulness has the strongest impact on whether employees perceive techno-stressors and this person ality trait can be changed to some degree, e.g., through normative experiences (Lodi-Smith & Roberts, 2007). This is the particular characteristic for dynamic, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, such as IT mindfulness (Thatcher et al., 2018) and has been shown and tested via multiple experimental studies arguing that particularly individuals who want to change their personality can actually do so (Hudson & Fraley, 2015). Having that in mind and building on our results that IT mindfulness has a stronger impact on techno-stressors than PIIT and neuroticism, organisations might implement mechanisms that initially increase employees’ awareness that one’s personality might threaten one’s own well-being, and then develop and provide mechanisms, including trainings or communications, to give employees the tools to change their personalities. Among others, mindfulness research has shown that training can foster mindfulness (Dane, 2011). In addition, research informs us that curiosity is related to mindfulness (Brewer, Davis, & Goldstein, 2013) so organisations might stimulate users’ IS-related curiosity to increase their IT mindfulness, such as by continually introdu cing new features of IS (Sun, 2012). As our research provides evidence that IT mindfulness has the strongest in<sup>fl</sup>uence on whether techno-stressors are per ceived, mechanisms including trainings fostering IT mindfulness might help employees perceive less tech nostress and thus increase their well-being.

## 7.7. Limitations and future research

The results of this paper are limited in several ways but also o<sup>f</sup>er possibilities for future research.

First, we use the conceptualisation of IT mindfulness as developed by Thatcher et al. (2018). Related research in the stream of IT mindfulness also suggests other conceptualisations (Sun et al., 2016). We chose Thatcher et al. (2018) because it considers IT mindfulness a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait, which has a high <sup>fi</sup>t to our research objectives. Other conceptualisations de<sup>fi</sup>ne IT mindfulness as an individual’s state instead of a personality trait. De<sup>fi</sup>ning IT mindfulness as an individual’s state and using an accordingly measurement would mean that IT mindfulness might then in<sup>fl</sup>uence other relationships in the stress process or that the reasons it in<sup>fl</sup>uences technostressors di<sup>f</sup>er from those reported in this research.

Second, the empirics are based on selections of speci<sup>fi</sup>c personality traits of the three hierarchical structure of personality traits. Even though the selection is based on incidence (neuroticism, PIIT) and in line with related research suggesting a strong relationship with stress (neuroticism, mindfulness), future research might focus on other personality traits. Thereby, future research might treat personality traits rather consistent with the broader stress literature instead of being consistent with IS literature, which we focused on (see Table 3 in Appendix). By using two samples and several post-hoc analyses (e.g., considering the Big Five), we are able to provide stable results, but focusing on further traits might provide additional insights.

Third, within this study, we aim to explain how user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences techno-stressors. We also report that the personality traits have a mediating e<sup>f</sup>ect on psychological and behavioural strain. In that respect, previous research has also focused on physiological strain including bodily reactions (e.g., Riedl et al., 2012; Tams et al., 2014). As we did not test for that type of strain, our results do not show whether and how personality traits are related to physiological strain, which may be a fruitful area of future research. Similarly, we focus on chronic stress but pay little attention to episodic stress caused by IS use. Episodic stress can be measured using surveys or neuroscience equipment. Given the limitation of not focusing on episodic stress, we cannot draw conclusions about whether and how personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors experienced episodically.

With regard to future research, our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a quadratic relationship between techno-stressors and user performance, which is also in line with organisational research indicating that stress might be a positive challenge as well as a negative hindrance stimulus (LePine et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2014), indicate the need for future IS research. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it would be important to understand how experience, ability and environmental factors might change or determine the tipping point up to which stress improves user performance.

In this study, we treat techno-stressors as a combination of <sup>fi</sup>ve stressors, which leaves room for future research. Among others, future research might study whether con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of these <sup>fi</sup>ve stressors exist which lead individuals to su<sup>f</sup>er job burnout or lead to reduced job performance. This would break up the conceptualisation of technostressors as a second-order construct and might provide further insights, e.g., whether some reactions (i.e., job burnout, poor performance) are triggered by speci<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of stressors but not by others. In other words, it might be that job burnout is grounded in di<sup>f</sup>erent con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of stressors than poor performance or other reactions.

Moreover, this research aims to study the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of traits from di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical levels on techno-stressors, which leaves room for future research. First, our results indicate that there are some correlations between the personality traits, so future research might study interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects, meaning that broad traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence stable and dynamic ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits, which then serve as a mediator. This then might better explain the mechanisms by which personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence techno-stressors, which di<sup>f</sup>ers from the objective of this research, which was to identify the relative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality traits from di<sup>f</sup>erent hierarchical level on technostressors. Second, while the explanatory power of user personality traits has been called “disappointing” and technostress research has revealed that only four out of 10 relationships between techno-stressors and job burnout or job engagement were moderated by the Big Five traits, future research might use the insights provided here and study how dynamic, ISspeci<sup>fi</sup>c traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ ability to cope with technostress.

Finally, bearing in mind that IT mindfulness is a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait (Thatcher et al., 2018) and considering our <sup>fi</sup>ndings that IT mindfulness is the trait with the highest relative e<sup>f</sup>ect on technostressors, future research might study speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways to increase IT mindfulness. This would be a valuable avenue, because this in turn might reduce the amount of technostress perceived by employees. Even though some initial coping research addresses mitigating techno-stressors (D’Arcy, Herath, & Shoss, 2014), there are no speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines for how to foster IT mindfulness – beyond tools derived from general mindfulness research, such as training – and, in turn, mitigate perceptions of technostress.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper contributes to previous technostress research by proving that and indicating how user personality in<sup>fl</sup>uences whether individuals perceive IS use as stressful. This is relevant for organisations because an increasing number of employees identi<sup>fi</sup>es techno-stressors as one cause for a decrease in their well-being (Maier et al., 2015a; Srivastava et al.,

2015). Building on research suggesting a hierarchical structure of personality traits, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from 2 two-wave studies with two samples reveal that traits of all three hierarchies in terms of neuroticism, PIIT and IT mindfulness determine techno-stressors and, moreover, have an indirect e<sup>f</sup>ect on job burnout and user performance. Further, we posit and our data provide empirical evidence that IT mindfulness as a dynamic, IS-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait has the strongest impact on techno-stressors. In addition, we extend current research by providing evidence of an inverted u-curve relationship between the techno-stressors and user performance.
