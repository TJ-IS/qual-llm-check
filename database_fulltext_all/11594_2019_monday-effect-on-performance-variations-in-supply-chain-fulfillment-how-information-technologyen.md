# “Monday Effect” on Performance Variations in Supply Chain Ful<sup>fi</sup>llment: How Information Technology–Enabled Procurement May Help

Abstract. Although there are both process-related and human-related grounds for systematic performance variations across days of the week, such a phenomenon has not caught the attention of information systems or operations management scholars. Using transaction-level data from the U.S. Government’s General Services Administration, we study three layers of effects: the Monday Effect, the Technology Effect, and the Produc Effect. First, we assess whether performance varies across days of the week in supply chain fulfillment, measured by order cycle time, complete orders fulfilled, and short shipmen percentage (i.e., the Monday Effect). Furthermore, we assess how information technology (IT), notably an electronic market, can mitigate such variations (i.e., the Technology Effect), and how product characteristics moderate such effects (i.e., the Product Effect). Our findings show that there indeed exist significant, systematic performance variations across days of the week, with Mondays having poorer performance than other days of the week, after controlling for workload and other factors. Further, we find that much of the per formance variation can be reduced when an IT-enabled electronic market is adopted Specifically, the performance gaps in order cycle time, complete orders fulfilled, and short shipment percentage are reduced by 94%, 71%, and 80%, respectively. We further find that IT improves not only the mean level, but also the variance, of operations performance. Finally, we find that the electronic market is most effective at mitigating the Monday Effec for less frequently transacted products, such as products of high value, products that are slow moving, and specialized products with small numbers of buyers. These findings suggest that measures can be taken to reduce fulfillment deficiencies, including using information systems to mitigate variations in operations management, leading, potentially to operational continuity and higher service levels in the supply chain.

## 1. Introduction

Variation in operations performance may be considered a key component of operations management, in that it impacts the level of safety stock firms in the supply chain must carry, the demand forecasting function, marketing results, and many other areas of operations. Even though firms can take steps to improve the (mean) level of their operations performance, they still may be worse off owing to higher variance (standard deviation). Moreover, variations in fulfillment performance may cause either stockouts or excess inventory downstream in a supply chain.

One area that deserves attention is how performance may vary over time, such as across the days of the week—in particular, the Monday Effect, that is, possibly poorer performance on Mondays compared with other days of the week. If labor productivity is lower on Mondays than on other workdays, and if this lower productivity is not correctly forecast, organizations risk “underperforming” on Mondays compared with other workdays; for example, failing to meet required production levels or service deliveries The Monday Effect has been documented in a number of academic disciplines. For example, Monday has been shown to have worse performance than other weekdays in terms of lower stock returns in finance (e.g., Pettengill 2003, Bakar et al. 2014), more workers compensation claims in industrial relations (Card and McCall 1996, Campolieti and Hyatt 2006), and lower worker productivity in labor economics (Bryson and Forth 2007, Hamermesh and Stancanelli 2014). In addition, according to a recent report by the Bureau of

Labor Statistics,<sup>1</sup> more workplace injuries occur on Mondays than on any other days of the week.

The Monday Effect may be due to both processrelated and human-related (psychological or physiological) factors. With respect to processes, because a two-day weekend can interrupt functional workflow (e.g., shipping and receiving goods), processes may need to be reinitialized and employees reoriented to their tasks on Mondays. With respect to humanrelated concerns, employees may lack motivation to work on Mondays given the length of time until the next weekend (Bryson and Forth 2007), resulting in negative moods—the so-called “Monday Blues” (Pettengill 1993). Some employees may find it difficult to adjust from the relative freedom of the weekend to the more structured workplace on Monday, thus potentially diluting concentration and productivity. On the other hand, the weekend break may reinvigorate employees, leading to higher productivity on Mondays compared with other workdays (Fritz et al. 2013). Studies have shown that break times lead to increased worker productivity, because “refreshed” workers can work more productively than workers who have not had a break. Thus, both process-related and human-related effects may contribute to variances in productivity on Mondays compared with other weekdays.

A potential way to reduce operational variation (or the Monday Effect specifically) is to reduce the occurrence of disruptions in operations or supply chains (e.g., Tang 2006, Chopra and Sodhi 2014), which will lead to a more even flow of products (or services) over time. The Theory of Swift, Even Flow (TSEF) (Schmenner and Swink 1998) states that an even flow of materials through a production process leads to increased efficiency. The theory has also been used to explain how even flows in service operations and across firms in supply chains can improve service quality and reduce operating costs (e.g., Karwan and Markland 2006, Devaraj et al. 2013).

In particular, to mitigate variations in performance across days of the week—both process-related and human-related—firms may adjust work scheduling (e.g., increase the number of workers on duty on Mondays). However, given a fixed workforce or a lack of foresight into the Monday Effect, this type of scheduling may not be feasible to institute. A second way to mitigate the Monday Effect is through the increased use of technology. First, organizations may use technology to more efficiently perform work scheduling. For example, Salesforce has a software module on workforce field scheduling that is designed to allow agents to quickly respond to unexpected situations.<sup>2</sup> Second, organizations may use technology to help order processing. For example, an automated order-processing system may place orders via computer link on weekends, even if humans are not working during those off days. Thus, order bottlenecks on Mondays can be mitigated. Moreover, computer-to-computer links avoid potential human effects (either negative or positive) resulting from the weekend break. Thus, one would expect to see a more even flow of work as processes are automated, reducing performance variation and (for example) the need for safety stock to meet fill rate expectations.

Fluctuations in business activity may be planned or expected; for example, expected fluctuations would include increases in toy sales in November and December. To the extent that companies can hire part time workers or keep facilities open during the weekend to accommodate the extra workload, there may be no performance decreases that result from the varied workload. We highlight the Monday Effect not because weekends are unexpected events, but because the weekend disruptions may result in unplanned performance effects. Rational inventory and production models may not account for work variance across days of the week, under the implicit assumption that Mondays are as efficient as other work days. Yet, this may not be the case for both process-related and human-related reasons. More importantly, highlighting the Monday Effect can allow organizations to manage the effect, much like managers can accommodate the increase in toy sales during holiday seasons.

Finally, information technology (IT) has been increasingly used in the workplace and can help smooth both process-related and human-related variations in order fulfillment (e.g., Bendoly and Kaefer 2004, Cotteleer and Bendoly 2006, Devaraj et al. 2013). As a result, IT may have a positive impact on mitigating the Monday Effect, even if reducing performance variation across days of the week is an unintended consequence of using the technology.

In this paper, using a large-scale transaction-level data set gathered during a 12-month period from the General Services Administration (GSA) of the U.S. government,<sup>3</sup> we study variations in operations performance by days of the week. Performance variations are assessed using variances in order fulfillment and order cycle time. We then examine whether an ITenabled electronic market (EM; i.e., GSA Advantage!) can mitigate these variations.<sup>4</sup> Moreover, we assess how characteristics of products transacted moderate the effects of the EM on performance variation. Public sector data are especially valuable for evaluating performance variation due to the Monday Effect, because the (U.S.) government workers process virtually no orders on the weekend, providing a clear-cut natural experiment.

Our major findings include the following: (1) After controlling for workload and order patterns, we find the Monday Effect in operations performance is prevalent. Mondays have poorer performance than other days as measured by both order cycle time and order fulfillment. (2) The use of an EM significantly reduces the performance gap for Mondays, thus reducing the performance variation across days of the week. Together, these results support the hypothesis that IT can smooth performance variations across days of the week, thus providing another dimension of IT value. (3) The results, although robust, vary by product type. We find that EMs are more beneficial for reducing performance variations for less frequently transacted products. These results suggest that EMs may help organizations overcome a learning effect; that is, EMs may be more beneficial at reducing the Monday Effect for products for which workers lack familiarity.

Our paper contributes to the literature in the information systems–operations management (IS-OM) intersection by extending prior literature on the drivers of fulfillment performance to a new, underexplored dimension. As far as we are aware, no prior research in the IS-OM literature has examined the impact of the day-of-week effect on operational performance. Furthermore, we demonstrate that IT can partially alleviate this operations variation (as well as improve the level of performance). Our results contribute by suggesting a new metric (i.e., reduction in performance variation across days of the week) to the literature on the business value of IT. Finally, our paper also contributes to the literature on government procurement (e.g., Goh and Hall 2013, Chen and Lee 2016, Calvo et al. 2017) by demonstrating that procurement in the public sector can be better managed by mitigating the Monday Effect. Although the government sector is not profit-driven, improving efficiency in operational processes may be an important public sector objective.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses the theory and develops the hypotheses. Section 3 describes our research setting, data, variables, and econometric models. Section 4 presents the analysis and results. Section 5 concludes our analysis and discusses limitations and potential future research.

## 2. Theory and Hypothesis Development 2.1. The Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c Basis

The scientific basis of performance variation across days of the week originates from two sources. First, performance may differ across days of the week owing to process-oriented issues. Second, the variations may be due to human psychological or physiologicaloriented grounds. The two bases are discussed in turn below.

The TSEF (Schmenner and Swink 1998) has been used by researchers in the operations field as a theoretical basis for their research. TSEF was initially used by researchers to explain operations performance in production facilities, where bottlenecks in one production process may lead to stoppages in a second, or variabilities in demand can contribute to bullwhip-effect inefficiencies (e.g., Cotteleer and Bendoly 2006, Kristal et al. 2010). The theory, however, has more recently been used to outline reasons for failures in service operations and to explain inefficiencies in supply chain management settings. For example, Devaraj et al. (2013) and Fredenall et al. (2009) both conduct their research in a hospital setting. In addition to achieving patient quality outcomes, hospitals are interested in speeding the flow of patients through various procedures and avoiding potential bottlenecks in patient care. On the basis of data from 567 U.S. hospitals, Devaraj et al. (2013) examine the effect of IT on streamlining hospital operations and find that IT is associated with swift and even patient flow and, ultimately, with improved performance. Fredendall et al. (2009) find that coordination of the various surgical units in a hospital leads to a reduction in potential bottlenecks and helps in speeding the flow of patients through their surgical procedures

Chen et al. (2013) apply TSEF to a supply chain setting. They note that according to TSEF, operational performance can be improved through a reduction in operational variations. These variations may be induced through demand-side risks or supply-side risks, as well as through internal process-related risks. Using a survey-based sample from the manufacturing industry, they find that both process-based risks and demand-side risks have a negative impact on operational performance (e.g., slower delivery speed, lower order fulfillment, and poorer customer dissatisfaction). Wang et al. (2006) also use TSEF as a theoretical basis for examining supply chain performance. They posit that supplier integration leads to cost reductions, based on the ability of integration to reduce bottlenecks in the supply chain. Using a structural model with data gathered from Taiwan-based manufacturers, the researchers do not find a direct relationship between supplier integration and cost savings but do find a potential indirect relationship, mediated by supplier responsiveness.

The setting for our research is concerned with a distribution service provider operating within a supply chain context. Thus, the TSEF laws of variability and bottlenecks are relevant to our setting. Given that the GSA does not process orders on weekends, weekends are equivalent to downtime in the system and may be considered as “bottlenecks” to the even flow of orders. Weekends, therefore, inhibit operational flow for a two-day period and, as a result, may have an impact on performance the following Mondays. Thus, recognizing the weekend bottleneck and taking actions to relieve the bottleneck may help reduce operational performance variation and improve service outcomes.

Although performance variations on Mondays may be due to weekend stoppages in operations, these stoppages may not be the only reason for the Monday Effect. A second theoretical basis for performance variation on Mondays concerns human factors, which may result in better or poorer Monday performance. In behavioral science, it has been documented that people may have mood swings during the week (Pettengill 1993). Individuals working a traditional Monday-to-Friday schedule may have a low point on Monday because it is the first workday (Fritz and Sonnentag 2005, Bakar et al. 2014). The relationship between human factors, the Monday Effect, and business performance has been studied by several researchers. For example, Pettengill (1993) examines how investor behavior changes by day of the week. In a controlled experiment, investors are found to put a higher proportion of investments into safe treasury bills on Mondays than on other days, suggesting more risk-averse behavior on Mondays. Fritz and Sonnentag (2005) survey German workers about their performance during the week and find that workers who experience non–work-related “hassles” over the weekend are likely to perform poorer after the weekend. The authors suggest that these weekend hassles consume resources (or attention) that would otherwise be devoted to the job.

On the other hand, there is considerable evidence that work breaks, ranging from a short coffee break, to a two-day weekend break, to a much longer vacation, may increase the psychological and physiological well-being of workers, leading to higher performance after the break (Gottselig et al. 2004, Fritz et al. 2013). According to Fritz et al. (2013, p. 274), breaks may allow workers to “replenish their psychological resources,” although the benefits from weekend breaks depend on how workers fared during the weekend. The authors distinguish between “good” weekends and “bad” weekends. Individuals who spend enjoyable weekends are more likely to be refreshed and ready to engage in work on Mondays, thus leading to performance improvements. On the other hand, individuals who had to spend their weekends dealing with hassles may not be refreshed, and their work could deteriorate from expected standards.

Smith et al. (1994) examine workplace injuries during a given workday. They find that injuries increase over the course of the day, presumably as workers become increasingly tired or bored. However, this increase varies depending on the type of work performed, with the most noticeable increase among those workers with a paced work schedule (e.g., assembly line workers). Along the same lines, Folkard and Tucker (2003) examine productivity over a single workday. They find that productivity will decline during a shift but temporarily rebound after a worker has a break, indicating that a break may help to refresh the worker’s psychological and/or physiological condition.

## 2.2. The Monday Effect

The scientific bases discussed above can be applied to our research setting because order fulfillment involves both process and human factors. As shown in Figure 1, order fulfillment processes include order placing, order taking, order picking, packing, and shipping. If the process flow is interrupted at any of these stages, performance may be adversely impacted. We consider Monday as a “warm up” day in terms of order processes, subject to both process- and human-related impacts. On the other days of the week, order processes reach a steady state. Because the weekend represents a process disruption at the distribution center (i.e., a bottleneck), performance on Monday may be lower than on other weekdays Moreover, because the order processing activities require human efforts by operational workers, mood swings or other behavioral effects may also contribute to variations in performance on Mondays.

The disruption in process flow from the weekend may reduce operational performance on Mondays; for example, increasing cycle times and reducing fill rates, as bottlenecks are overcome, and ordering and shipping processes correct to their normal pace. On the other hand, behavioral factors may lead to improved or lowered performance, depending on the psychological and physical conditions of workers following their weekend breaks. Assuming that, on balance, there are both positive and negative behavioral consequences from the weekend break, we hypothesize that the net impact of the weekend should be negative, given the analysis above, and subject to empirical validation. Therefore, poorer results in terms of order cycle time, complete orders fulfilled, and short shipments may be expected on orders processed on Mondays than on other days of the week. Hence, we formalize this as our first hypothesis:

## 2.3. The Technology Effect

The literature in information systems documents many benefits from the use of IT to facilitate the operations function (Zhu et al. 2006b, Dong et al. 2009, Yan and Zhu 2012) and enhance buyer–supplier relationships (Mithas and Rust 2016). For example, the implementation of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems was found to lead to improved order fulfillment and shorter lead times (Cotteleer and Bendoly (2006). Such improvements were attributed to contributions by IT to more efficient process flows at the firm (Dong et al. 2009). Moreover, it was also recognized that the introduction of this type of technology may change employee behavior and employee attitudes to work (Bendoly and Cotteleer 2008).

In our research context, we compare a traditional procurement channel (orders are telephoned, faxed, or delivered in person on paper forms) to an EM (highly computerized, greater extent of process integration, less human involvement). Key differences between the two channels include the degree of human interaction required to process orders, the use of technology in order processing, and the degree of system integration (Zhu et al. 2006a). Although both channels involve the same order placement and fulfillment requirements, the EM facilitates integration in terms of information exchange between buyers and sellers, thus resulting in less human interaction (e.g., rekeying data) (Zhu 2004) and less time (re)- initializing the order processing system (i.e., because order information is shared between buyers and sellers and across departments within the seller organization). Therefore, the electronic market may outperform a traditional market in order fulfillment owing to more accurate and timely information exchange.

On the basis of these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:<sup>5</sup>

Hypothesis 2. The performance in order fulfillment is better for electronic markets than for traditional transaction systems.

In addition to contributing to an overall improvement in order fulfillment, IT may also alleviate the Monday Effect. As noted above, the Monday Effect may be attributed to two broad causes—a process effect and a behavioral effect. The process effect is caused by disruptions in workflow due to the weekend break that need to be reinitialized on Mondays, as orders are processed, picked, staged, and shipped to customers. With EM, the order process may occur with minimal human intervention, thus reducing potential impacts from order bottlenecks. Human intervention required for staging and shipping the orders may occur sooner when the orders are processed through the EM. Thus, the EM should reduce the “process effect” and facilitate operating performance.

According to Germain et al. (2001), technology may be viewed as a subset of knowledge that can be used to reduce throughput variations and improve operational performance. In a series of articles, Bendoly and coauthors examine how the implementation of one type of technology application, ERP systems, can reduce bottlenecks in the production process and increase performance outcomes.<sup>6</sup> Their findings show that ERP systems are associated with increased operational efficiency and lower operating costs. Devara et al. (2007) and Devaraj et al. (2013) show that IT applications may contribute to improved integration across business functions, leading to faster process flows and enhanced operational performance in an organization. Finally, Karwan and Markland (2006) examine the use of IT to improve operational outcomes in a governmental setting. They argue that governments typically focus on effective delivery of services but that efficiency should also be a governmental goal. Using a data sample from motor vehicle offices, they find that IT applications reduce bottlenecks in the delivery of services—for example, shortening wait times in motor vehicle offices and increasing the percentage of transactions that may be accomplished without human intervention.

We propose that IT will not only improve the mean level of performance but can also lower the variance (standard deviation) of operations performance; that is, technologies can be used to mitigate performance variations or mitigate the Monday Effect in operations management. Hence, we put forward the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. The use of electronic markets helps to moderate the performance deterioration on Mondays (i.e., reduce the Monday Effect).

## 2.4. The Product Effect

The impact of IT on the Monday Effect may be moderated by product-related characteristics. Certain types of products may be more prone to improved performance on Mondays when EM is used. We differentiate frequently transacted products from lessfrequently transacted products. On the basis of learning theory, we hypothesize that workers will be more familiar with the processing of more frequently transacted products. Therefore, the additional benefits from the use of EM will be smaller for products that are frequently transacted. We use three metrics to measure product characteristics associated with the frequency of their transaction levels: total sales, product price, and number of buyers.

We argue that EM may be more important for reducing the Monday Effect for less frequently transacted products. According to learning theory, employee knowledge and experience increase when tasks are repeated (i.e., learning by doing) (Argote et al. 1990). As tasks are learned, performance tends to increase. The learning effect has been demonstrated in many industries; for example, in the shipbuilding industry (Thornton and Thompson 2001), in the semiconductor manufacturing industry (Macher and Mowery 2003), in aircraft production (Benkard 2000), and in online retailing (Cui et al. 2017).

As shown in Figure 1, order fulfillment processes typically involve order placing, order taking, order picking, packing, and shipping. These steps involve human efforts at the buyer’s location and at the seller’s distribution center. As the frequency of transactions for a given product increases, performance will likely improve owing to the learning effect.<sup>7</sup> Because more knowledge will be accumulated about frequently transacted products, performance outcomes may be better, in general, for these products, therefore resulting in fewer processing errors. Therefore, EM is less likely to be a factor, relatively speaking, in “correcting” for the Monday Effect for these more frequently transacted products than for less-frequently transacted products.

More frequent orders tend to be for products with lower prices, higher total sales, and greater numbers of buyers. Indeed, this is confirmed in our data. An examination of our data shows that the pairwise correlations between transaction frequency and product price, total sales, and number of buyers are <sup>−</sup>0.05, 0.55, and 0.77 (all significant at the 0.001 level). Because these products are transacted more frequently, employees will have accumulated more working knowledge and updated information about these products, and the EM may be less critical to the reduction in performance variation. In contrast, EM may be more beneficial at reducing the Monday Effect for less-frequently transacted products. Hence, we put forward our final hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. The moderating effect of electronic markets on the Monday Effect is greater for the less-frequently transacted products; that is, products that are characterized by relatively lower sale volume, higher prices, and fewer buyers.

## 3. Research Methodology 3.1. Research Setting and Data

The research setting is the GSA, an important supply channel used mainly by U.S. federal government purchasers but also by state and local government purchasers. The GSA connects government buyers with outside vendors in the governmental supply chain. The GSA purchases from outside vendors, stores products in its warehouses, and then fulfills orders from these warehouses. At the time data were collected, the GSA offered government buyers a choice in channels for purchases including: an electronic market, known as GSA Advantage!, and a traditional channel where orders may be faxed, telephoned, mailed in, or presented in person. Both channels were freely available to all buyers, and the buyers determined which channel to use for a purchase. According to our data, a large majority of buyers (98%) chose to use a single channel, and according to our interviews with procurement managers, buyers did not engage in optimizing their orders between channels and among days of week. Regardless of the channel used, orders were fulfilled at the same warehouses. Online and offline orders were placed and treated indifferently by the government buyers and by the five GSA warehouses located across the country.

GSA Advantage! can be distinguished from the traditional channel in several ways, according to Yao et al. (2009). Most importantly, it is an electronic market that facilitates information flows in a digita online format, whereas the traditional channel allows for the exchange of information using paper documents. For example, once a purchase order is entered into the online system, it can be digitally transmitted, without rekeying, to the warehouse operator for picking, packing, and shipping. On the other hand, the traditional channel needs more human involvement in transmitting orders from the purchaser to the fulfillment personnel.

Data were collected on all actual transaction records (more than one million) made through the GSA’s procurement channels during the fiscal year 2000, a key year for comparison purposes as the two channels coexisted. Each purchase record consisted of a purchase of one or more units of an item and fulfillment of that purchase order, including product, quantity, and date shipped. Given the size of the database, only the four largest product categories are used for this analysis, with a total of 890,131 observations.<sup>8</sup> These include: Hand Tools and Hardware; Office Supplies and Devices; Brushes, Paints, Sealers, and Adhesives; and, Containers, Packaging, and Packing Supplies. ## 3.2. Variables

We use three measures as dependent variables to assess fulfillment performance: order cycle time (CYCLE), complete orders fulfilled (COMPLETE), and short shipment percentage (SHORT). CYCLE measures the elapsed time, in weekdays, between when a purchase order is received and when it is shipped. SHORT is the percentage of units that were short shipped (zero, if no short shipment occurred). COM-PLETE is a three-dimensional measure of order fulfillment. An order is said to be complete if it is fulfilled with the correct quantity (i.e., no out-of-stocks or short shipments), with the requested product (i.e., no substitutes), and on time (i.e., no back orders). When an order is fulfilled on all three dimensions, COM-PLETE is equal to 3; when an order is fulfilled on any two out of the three dimensions, COMPLETE is equal to 2; when an order is fulfilled on only one dimension (any one out of the three dimensions), COMPLETE is equal to 1, and when an order is fulfilled by none of the three dimensions, COMPLETE is equal to 0.

We use a dummy variable for electronic market transactions and a series of dummy variables for days of the week. EM is a dummy variable representing orders placed using GSA Advantage!. Traditional channel orders are left as the base case. MON, TUE, WED, THUR, and FRI represent the days of the week that orders are processed. Although an order fulfillment cycle may span multiple days (e.g., order received on Monday and shipped on Thursday), our data show that approximately 35% of total orders are placed and shipped the same day, and this number increases to 68% for orders that are shipped by the next workday. In addition, on the basis of our discussions with GSA managers, much of the fulfillment work (e.g., order processing, determination of inventory status, recording of order, etc.) is accomplished on the day the order is received. If order fulfillment is worse on Mondays than on other days of the week, then these results should be reflected in the estimated coefficients for MON in our models, com pared with the coefficients for the other days of the week.

We use three variables to measure order patterns for the seller (GSA) and buyers (government agencies): number of orders received by a GSA warehouse on a given day (SORDER); number of orders placed by a buyer on a given day (BORDER); and order quantity, in units, for each order (OQTY). SORDER is the total number of orders from all buyers that are processed by one of the GSA’s warehouses on a given day, which measures workload in terms of order fulfillment for the GSA as the seller. BORDER is the total number of orders placed by a buyer for a particular day, which measures the workload in terms of order placements for a buyer on that day. OQTY is the quantity for each order record (which contains an order for a product). Increases in this variable may result in increased workloads for the buyer, the seller, or both.

## 3.3. Econometric Models

We construct three econometric models to test our hypotheses. The dependent variables are the three performance metrics for all three models, as defined above. The independent variables are the dummy variables for days of the week for Model 1, the in teraction term between the Monday dummy and the EM for Model 2, and the three-way interaction terms among the Monday dummy, the EM, and product characteristics for Model 3.

Model 1, as specified in Equation (1), tests whether the Monday Effect exists (Hypothesis 1), as well as the value of IT (Hypothesis 2). The Monday dummy is omitted as the baseline case for comparison purposes. An EM variable is included for testing for the value of IT.

We also include a number of control variables. First, we include the three workload variables. A potentially confounding problem in the measurement of the day-of-week effect is that employees may have an uneven workload over the days of the week; for example, heavier workload on Mondays, thus contributing to delays or errors in the processing of Monday orders. To control for this, we include the three variables on order patterns (i.e., SORDER, BORDER, and OQTY). Second, we include a dummy variable to control for the potential fixed effects related to transaction channel (EM), because the electronic channel facilitates order-taking activities. Third, we include variables for product-related characteristics: total sales volume for a product (TSALES), its average price (PRICE), and number of unique buyers for a product (NUMBUY). Fourth, we include a series of dummy variables to control for potential fixed effects related to product category (CATEGORY<sub>i</sub>). The GSA separates products into categories (e.g., office devices and supplies, hand tools, etc.), and certain categories may be ordered more often on certain days of the week $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , office supplies on Monday). Given that the fulfillment times may vary by product category, it is necessary to include these product category dummy variables in our model. Fifth, we include a series of dummy variables to control for potential fixed effects related to buyers’ government agencies or work units (BUYER ), because units have different sizes and needs. In our data set, there are 204 unique work units. Finally, we include a series of dummy variables to control for potential fixed time effects (MONTH ), because performance may vary by time of year (e.g., holiday periods, end of fiscal year, or seasonal demand).

Because buyers may choose between electronic channels and traditional channels when making purchases, this choice may introduce a selection bias whereby one channel is systematically used for certain products and the other channel for other products. To alleviate potential endogeneity resulting from channel selection bias, we use a standard instrumental variable (IV) approach (Greene 1997). We use the lagged channel choice of other work units within the same governmental department as the IV. A valid IV correlates with the endogenous variable (in our case the EM variable) but does not correlate with the dependent variable (performance metrics in our case). Our IV correlates with channel use in that the focal buyer’s decision to use a channel can be influenced by peers within the same department; for example, a work unit in the Department of Justice may learn from another unit about EM, especially because there is labor mobility between units.<sup>9</sup> Moreover, the use of EM by another unit should not affect the focal unit’s fulfillment performance. We estimate the models using the two-stage least squares method to incorporate the IV. The first stages from all three estimations show that the Cragg-Donald Wald F-statistics (9.0e+05) are much higher than the Stock-Yogo critical values (16.38), rejecting the null hypothesis that the IV is weak.

## 4. Results and Analysis

## 4.1. Order Patterns by Days of the Week

We first examine the order patterns by days of the week, as measured by the three variables described above. Table 4 presents the results. The mean for BORDER is 6.93 on Mondays, 6.46 on Tuesdays, 6.37 on Wednesdays, 6.43 on Thursdays, and 6.45 on Fridays The differences between the mean for Monday and those for other days are statistically significant. The Monday mean for SORDER of 779, however, is not statistically higher than the corresponding means for the other days of the week. Finally, the mean for OQTY is 17.07 on Mondays and is statistically higher than the mean for Wednesday and marginally higher than the mean for Friday (although not statisti cally different from the Tuesday and Thursday means). In summary, these results show that the Monday workload is statistically greater than the workload on other days of the week in terms of the number of orders for buyers, perhaps owing to accumulated orders during the weekend. Thus, it is important to control for workload when determining a potential Monday Effect.

## 4.2. The Monday Effect

Figure 2, (a)–(c), presents a descriptive view of our data. As the three figures show, performance is clearly better for the electronic market than for the traditional channel for all three performance metrics: the percentage of the orders completed is higher; the cycle time is shorter; and the percentage of short shipments is lower. The figures also provide preliminary evidence of the Monday Effect: performance seems to be worse on Mondays than on other weekdays for all three metrics.

In addition, performance variance seems to be greater for the traditional channel than for the EM, possibly indicating that the technology may serve to reduce performance variation by days of the week. However, to develop more definitive results, we need to control for the other potential variables (e.g., workload) that may influence the relationships.

To systematically analyze the Monday Effect, as well as the IT effect and the product effect, we now estimate the econometric models introduced in Section 3.3. Table 5 presents the estimation results for Model 1. The coefficients for all four day-of-week dummies (TUE, WED, THUR, and FRI) are significant for all three regressions (in three columns corresponding to the three dependent variables). The negative coefficients in the order cycle regression $( \alpha = - 0 . 2 5 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for TUE; α = <sup>−</sup>0.291, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for WED; α = <sup>−</sup>0.218, p < 0.001 for $T H U R ; \alpha = - 0 . 2 3 4$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for FRI) imply that the cycle time for orders received on other days is shorter than that on Mondays. Table 5 also presents the estimation results for the EM impact on the performance variables (Hypothesis 2). The coefficients for the EM variable are significant in all three Model 1 equations (and in all three Model 2 equations as well), as shown in the row marked as “EM.” Specifically, the EM coefficient is negative in the Order Cycle equations (i.e., IT is associated with shorter cycle times), positive in the Complete Orders Fulfilled equations (i.e., IT is associated with a greater percentage of completed orders fulfilled), and negative in the Short Shipment equations (i.e., IT is associated with fewer short shipments). These results confirm the positive impact that IT has on operational performance outcomes in our research setting for the public sector, thus supporting Hypothesis 2.

The results from Model 2 can also be used to estimate the impact of EM on the Monday Effect (Hypothesis 3). To simplify the interpretation of the results, we make the base case all the weekdays except for Monday. This specification allows us to key on the primary variables of interest. ## 4.5. The Economic Impact

To check whether our results are not only statistically significant but also economically significant, we conduct the following analyses using our estimated models. First, we compare Mond $\mathsf { a y } ^ { \prime } \mathsf { s }$ performance with the average performance of other weekdays, while holding other variables constant at their means. We find that, on average, complete orders fulfilled (0–3 scale) is 2.837 for Mondays, while averaging 2.843 for other weekdays, a 0.20% degradation; that order cycle time is 2.845 days for Mondays, while averaging 2.594 days for other weekdays, a 9.68% degradation; and that short shipment percentage is

7.26% on Mondays and averages 6.92% for other weekdays, a 4.87% degradation.

Second, we show the significance of EM at reducing the Monday Effect. From Equation (2), we take the partial derivative of the performance variables with regard to MON and obtain the Monday Effect:

$$
\frac {\partial P E R F O R M A N C E}{\partial M O N} = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {3} E M;
$$

$\beta _ { 1 }$ is the Monday Effect in the traditional channel (when EM = 0), and $\beta _ { 3 }$ is the impact of EM on reducing the Monday Effect. For example, using the results from the CYCLE estimation in Model 2, we have

$$
\frac {\partial P E R F O R M A N C E}{\partial M O N} = 1. 6 6 1 - 1. 5 7 7 E M.
$$

The results indicate that the Monday Effect is 1.661 when EM equals 0 and 0.084 when EM equals 1; that is, EM can reduce the Monday Effect in terms of order cycle time by 1.577 days, representing a 95% reduction (i.e., 1.577/1.661). Similarly, the EM can reduce the Monday Effect for complete orders fulfilled by 0.010 points, representing a 71% reduction, and for short shipment percentage by 0.012, an 80% reduction.

Third, we show the impact of the product characteristic moderators on the reduction of the Monday Effect through EM. From Equation (3), we take partial derivative of the performance variables with respect to MON:

$$
\frac {\partial P E R F O R M A N C E}{\partial M O N} = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {3} E M + \beta_ {4} E M \times P R O D;

$\beta _ { 1 }$ is the Monday Effect in the traditional channel (when $E M = 0 ) , \bar { \beta _ { 3 } }$ is the effect of EM on reducing the Monday Effect, and $\beta _ { 4 }$ is the moderating effect from a product characteristic where PROD denotes any of the three product characteristics. For example, using the estimates from the CYCLE estimation with PRICE in Model 3, we have

indicating that a unit increase in price enhances the EM impact in reducing the Monday Effect by 0.001456, or 0.09% (0.001456/1.558). On the basis of the mean price (\$13.89) and the standard deviation of the mean price (\$46.55), we can calculate that the EM reduces the Monday Effect by 1.578 at the mean price and by 1.646 at one standard deviation above the mean price; that is an increased effect of 4.29%.

## 4.6. Robustness Checks

We conduct robustness checks to further validate our results. First, we perform a falsification test. There were 10 federal holidays during our data collection time frame, 5 of which fell on Mondays. Hence, the Tuesdays following the federal holiday Mondays became the “new Mondays” as the first days of the week after the long weekends. We conduct our analysis by comparing the performance of these holiday Tuesdays with the normal Tuesdays and find worse performance for order cycle time and short shipment percentage (insignificant for complete orders fulfilled). Additionally, the EM effect is still there as well. The results are presented in Table A.1 in the appendix.

Second, to allay concerns that there may be selfselection bias for products in the electronic market, we re-estimate our models using the subsample of the common products transacted in both the traditional and electronic channels. Out of 8,599 products in total, 5,770 were common products. We obtain consistent results. Moreover, we check for multiple channel purchasers, even though our interviews confirm that buyers do not select one channel for certain products and the other channel for other products. According to our statistics, only 249 out of 34,198 buyers were multichannel users. We reran our model excluding these buyers and found consistent results. The results are presented in Table A.2 in the appendix.

Third, we re-estimate our model with a wider definition of Monday orders. Instead of our Monday dummy identifying orders that were received on Mondays, we create a new dummy variable to identify all orders that include Monday in their order processing cycle. Approximately 44% of total orders include a Monday between when the order was received and when it was shipped. We then use this wider dummy variable to replace the original Monday dummy in our main model. Furthermore, we limit the analysis to orders shipped within five workdays to exclude obvious backorders; that is, we compare orders that are made and shipped within five workdays that span a Monday with similar orders that do not span a Monday. The results are presented in Table A.3 in the appendix. They are consistent with the main findings reported earlier in the paper.

## 5. Additional Evidence

A concern for our results is whether the Monday Effect is still relevant in today’s environment. In theory, as long as weekend disruptions occur, the Monday Effect should still exist. The workload pat tern for government (and a significant segment of the private sector) remains as working during weekdays and off during weekends. The off days during the weekend provide a disruption to the swift and even flow of the work process, thus potentially leading to worse performance on Mondays.

To demonstrate that the Monday Effect is still relevant, as well as to show that this effect may be present in a different context from our main analysis, we analyzed a more recent data set from one of the largest supermarket chains in China. The supermarket chain has annual sales of \$4.37 billion and a network of distribution centers (DCs) and supermarkets throughout the country. We select a single DC that serves multiple stores as our research setting. Managers at the retail stores observe consumer demand, make their own order and inventory decisions, and place orders with the DC. We select 15 popular product categories that are supplied year-round. The 15 product categories yield 1,178 products and 27,619 store-product combinations.<sup>10</sup> We have order and fulfillment data for a seven-month period from April to October 2011.

Using these data, we construct a data set at the order level. The order-level data set includes all the orders placed by the stores to the DC, the corresponding shipments from the DC to the stores, and product and price information. In total, there are 18,565 orders. We also know the day of the week when each order was placed. The results are presented in Table A.4 in the appendix and show that order fulfillment performance on Mondays is lower than on other days, on average, in terms of complete orders fulfilled, although insignificant for order cycle time. The results reinforce that Monday Effect exists and is relevant in a more recent, private sector setting.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Although the literature has documented the Monday Effect in other disciplines, little research has examined this phenomenon in information systems and operations management. Using a data set of more than 800,000 actual transaction records, we first examine whether the Monday Effect exists in order fulfillment between sellers and buyers and then assess the effect on three performance metrics. Next, we examine whether an online procurement platform (EM) can be an effective tool in mitigating such effect, as a new angle to assessing the role of technology in reducing process variations in operations management. Finally, we examine how product characteristics moderate the impact of the electronic market on the Monday Effect.

After controlling for order patterns, workload, and other potential confounding factors, we find that the Monday Effect indeed exists in terms of order cycle time, complete orders fulfilled, and short shipment percentage. We find that performance on Mondays is the lowest during the week and that the Monday Effect is not trivial. Compared with other weekdays, for example, performance on Mondays has an 9.68% degradation in terms of order cycle time.

We next compare the Monday Effect between the traditional channel and the electronic market. We posit that the effect can be alleviated by the electronic market.

We find that EM indeed mitigates the Monday Effect for all three measures of order fulfillment. The performance gap in order cycle time, complete orders fulfilled, and short shipment percentage is reduced by 94% (i.e., 1.544 days on average), by 71%, and by 80%, respectively. This provides empirical evidence that EM facilitates information integration between buyers and sellers, thus reducing possible performance variations due to process- or human-related errors.

Although the EM is beneficial for reducing the Monday Effect, we find moderating impacts by product characteristics. The beneficial impacts of the EM at reducing the Monday Effect are greatest for more specialized products or less frequently transacted products. These include products that are high in value, slow moving, and that have high product specificity. For example, the impact of EM on the reduction of short shipments is 25% greater for products priced one standard deviation above the mean, 27% greater for products with total sales one standard deviation below the mean, and 27% greater for products with the number of unique buyers one standard deviation below the mean.

Our study makes several contributions to the IS-OM literature. This is the first study to provide systematic evidence on the existence of the Monday Effect in an operations context. Prior OM researchers examined factors that affect supply chain performance, such as information sharing (Cui et al. 2015), collaboration (Kulp et al. 2004), relationships (Subramani 2004), decision bias (Croson and Donohue 2006), and investment in IT (Mukhopadhyay et al. 1997, Mithas and Rust 2016). Hence, this paper addresses a phenomenon that has been underexplored in the IS-OM literature. Furthermore, we show that the Monday Effect is not trivial. Our results call attention to important deviations from the rational optimization models in the literature. For example, with respect to the calculation of safety stock, unless process performance variations are accommodated, calculations are likely to underestimate required safety stock for Mondays and result in stockouts.

Second, this study introduces the idea that future systems should be designed with days of the week in mind. For example, given our empirical findings on product characteristics, inventory ordering and shipping processes may need to be re-evaluated, especially for less frequently transacted products. Although businesses and the government have considered implementing new IT systems to minimize the impact of schedule and workload disruption, the IT systems designed with this new consideration could help supply chain performance, for example, by adjusting safety stock to account for the Monday Effect.

Third, we show empirically that information tech nology can be used to reduce the Monday Effect.

Moreover, we note that the Monday Effect is distinct from other process flow issues in that it is a problem that may not be identified or consciously addressed by management. IT is generally not used to smooth performance variations across days of the week but is introduced to address other concerns. Therefore, by examining the impact of IT on the Monday Effect, we have conducted a type of natural experiment to determine whether the Monday Effect can be reduced by using a technology designed to alleviate more general concerns. We show that the technology effect is not trivial—the electronic market can improve the performance on Mondays by as much as 90%. Furthermore, we add to the literature that IT is more beneficial at reducing the Monday Effect for less frequently transacted products—that is, products that are characterized by relatively lower sales volume, higher prices, and fewer buyers.

Fourth, our paper also adds to the literature that studies government procurement (e.g., Goh and Hall 2013, Chen and Lee 2016, Calvo et al. 2017). The central theme of the literature is on how to deliver effective and efficient public projects. Our study complements the literature by demonstrating that managing the Monday Effect using IT can be an effective way to improve government procurement performance. The public sector alone is comparable to many large industries. For example, in FY2016, the GSA reported \$20.5 billion in revenue.<sup>11</sup> As a point of reference, a country with this gross domestic product (GDP) figure would rank 110th in the world out of 191 countries.<sup>12</sup> In many other countries, the government sector is quite large, much larger as a percentage of GDP than in the United States. Therefore, the government sector itself is significant and, we believe, worthy of attention.

This study has several limitations and leaves open issues for future research. First, the goods considered in this research are generally overhead-type products (e.g., office supplies) and not direct materials for production. The Monday Effect and the impact of technology on the reduction of this effect could be different for direct materials. Second, our main analysis uses government procurement channels as a data source. Though the public sector itself is worthy of study, we need to be careful when generalizing the findings to the private sector. The government channels are more highly regulated than those used in the private sector—for example, requiring certification of buyers and sellers (Yao et al. 2009). As a consequence, the results may need to be revalidated in the private sector, although there are similarities between the purchasing function of the government sector and the purchasing functions of other large organizations (e.g., defense contractors). Third, although we did interview government employees about their purchasing activities, we were not able to conduct on-site oversight of the workplaces to determine the root causes of the Monday Effect and the potential for technology to mitigate such an effect. Direct observation would be helpful in revealing the scientific causes of the Monday deficit and the managerial or technological solutions to overcoming this performance gap. If direct observations are not feasible (e.g., owing to government security restrictions), laboratory experiments may be used for this purpose. Fourth, the paper relies on empirical analyses to examine the Monday Effect. Although the empirical analyses add value, future research may explore an analytical model that captures the dynamics of supplier and buyer behavior over the days of the week and informs the empirical analyses.

Despite these limitations, this research provides several implications for managers. We believe that by understanding the Monday Effect, managers will be better equipped to address potential performance issues through the use of IT (possibly in combination with staffing, training, and more nuanced practices in product-channel matching), with positive performance consequences. Firms will need to adjust their current practices; for example, adding staff on Mondays, reducing nonfulfillment activities/meetings on Mondays, providing better training, and speeding the migration toward online procurement. In fact, some leading firms are implementing new IT systems to minimize the impact of schedule and workload disruptions. This includes IBM’s business continuity projects<sup>13</sup> and the government’s IT disaster recovery and business continuity plan.<sup>14</sup> Salesforce is also implementing a flexible workforce assignment system to prevent scheduling disruptions toward maintaining business continuity. Knowing the presence and disruption of the Monday Effect can certainly help these organizations to better serve their clients and develop better IT systems to make preventive adjustments.<sup>15</sup> These changes could help organizations achieve superior procurement outcomes. As demonstrated in our analysis, the savings could be substantial.

In conclusion, performance variation is an understudied area and, therefore, ripe for future research. We hope that this initial study will stimulate further analysis, both empirical and analytical. We expect this area (particularly the role of technologies in miti gating adverse performance variations in supply chains) to become even more important as newer technologies (such as robotics, artificial intelligence) are adopted in operations.
