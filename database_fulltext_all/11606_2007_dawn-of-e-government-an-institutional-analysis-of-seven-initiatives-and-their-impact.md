## Abstract

Most countries have defined strategies for e-government. The objectives for implementing e-government are often defined but the means for fuelling the adoption and diffusion of egovernment are typically less well clear in the policy statements. The present study assesses the impact of latest Danish initiative implemented to stimulate e-government adoption. The e-Day initiative simply yet powerfully states that ‘one governmental authority has the right to demand that its communication with another authority must be in electronic format’ which is expected to create ripple effects both internally and externally. The e-Day initiative represents a drastic change in the former policy statements concerning IT adoption and diffusion in Danish government. The policy statements had previously been based on voluntary adoption focusing on visions and pedagogical intervention in governmental agencies, but the e-Day initiative marked a departure from that strategy and the carrot has been exchanged by the whip, and the voice is imperative. ## Introduction

he advent of digital government or electronic government (e-government) has rapidly spread throughout the world leading to a constant stream of policy documents on e-government visions from national and supranational agencies. Representing the supranational agencies, EU and OECD are among those that have launched visions for e-government (OECD, 2003; EU, 2003). Canada and the US are often claimed to be among the most salient examples of nations, which have gone beyond visions and actually implemented effective egovernment (Kaylor et al., 2001; Holden et al., 2003) and Asian countries such as Malaysia and Singapore have also been suggested to be in the lead with respect to effective implementation of e-government agendas (Tan and Pan, 2003). However, in a number of international rankings the Scandinavian countries are among the forerunners in egovernment (Accenture, 2005; Cap Gemini, 2004). Rankings of governments’ capabilities to implement e-government should, however, be received with reservation because, as observed by Muir and Oppenheim (2002), national governments tend to use hyperbole at government web sites claiming that they are world leaders in e-government without taking into consideration that not all can be world leaders.

A common characteristic of the e-government agendas and rankings is the citizen centric approach. It is an approach that focuses on how governments have or are supposed to adopt IT-tools in order to provide better and faster services to citizens and businesses. A number of maturity models for e-government have been developed in the slipstream of this discourse (Kaylor et al., 2001; Layne and Lee, 2001; Moon, 2002). The maturity models work on the assumption that there are different levels of interaction with users and different degrees of integration of data that can be benchmarked. A high score is then a surrogate of a high level of sophistication in e-government.

A much less evaluated aspect and less visible aspect of e-government is the internal facet. Namely that of the governments’ own use and reuse of data generated, handled, and stored with IT. Scholl (2005) claims that many e-government initiatives are subject to an iceberg phenomenon. The top of the iceberg represents the government to citizen and business interaction, which is very visible and evident whereas the governments’ internal effectiveness and efficiency is largely disregarded because it is hidden below the surface and therefore, much less visible. We concur with his observation and add that only with an effective internal processing of information can the citizen and business be better serviced. That is also in line with the public process reengineering model formulated by Andersen (2004). Andersen suggests that public sector institutions should focus on reorganizing their internal activities in order to achieve effectiveness and efficiency.

This article focuses on the internal aspect of e-government. Starting with a description on one decade’s visions and strategies for e-government in one of the Scandinavian countries, namely Denmark, the foundation for an assessment of what strategies yield what effects is established.

Inspired by a recently launched government wide e-Day, the review of one decade’s Danish policy statements is used to demonstrate how objectives and means for IT adoption and diffusion change over time. The e-Day initiative aimed at strengthening the intra- and inter-organizational interaction through electronic means among all government departments and at all levels of government. The objective of the article is to present the impact of the strategies the Danish government have applied to strengthen the diffusion of e-government in and among public sector institutions in Denmark. In order to go beyond an analysis of a single case, the case is used as a point of departure for a more general discussion of which types of instruments are available to fuel adoption and diffusion of IT in the public sector.

In the IS research domain, the impact of regulatory intervention has largely been neglected. One reason is a traditionally strong focus on private sector organizations where profit-oriented drivers such as market mechanisms and strategic considerations stimulate the adoption and diffusion of IT in and among organizations. Based on the figures from the years 2002–2005 on digital interaction, the internal digital handling of data, and finally uptake of technologies that support the digital handling, it is discussed if the agenda applied by the Danish government to stimulate the diffusion of e-government has proven to be successful.

When referring to e-government, the widely used definition developed by Layne and Lee (2001) is applied. They suggest that e-government is ‘government’s use of technology, particularly web-based Internet applications to enhance the access to and delivery of government information and service to citizens, business partners, employees, other agencies, and government entities.’ By using this particular definition, the external customers of the private sector as well as the public sector are seen as beneficiaries of e-government adoption and diffusion.

In this paper, we seek to distill from a single longitudinal case of seven e-government initiatives an answer to the following question:

What drivers applied by government to stimulate the diffusion of e-government has proven to be successful?

To tackle this question, we organize the remainder of the paper as follows: the next section provides an overview of the initiatives initiated by the Danish government to fuel the diffusion of e-government during the period 1994–2003. Then the stream of literature focusing on intervention and its use for fueling the diffusion of IS innovations are presented, and the impact of the e-Day is outlined and discussed. The last section provides some concluding remarks.

## History of institutional intervention leading to the e-day initiative

In the Danish context, the history of institutional intervention for the support of the diffusion of IT-innovations in government dates back to 1994. In the following, excerpts of the policy statements are presented in chronological order starting with year 1994 and ending with year 2003 where the e-Day initiative was launched. Not all initiatives have exclusively focused on IT adoption in the public sector. However, a particular focus on those sections concerning the public sector and its role in technology diffusion are emphasized in the presentation. All policy statements can be found at the web-site of the Ministry of Science, Technology, and Innovation (www.fsk.dk under the link publications).

## First initiative: 1994 – Info-society 2000

In 1994, the Ministry of Research published the report ‘Info-society 2000.’ The report was a consequence of the Bangeman-report (1994) prepared by the EU. The Infosociety 2000 report led to a continuous stream of initiatives, all suggesting how IT should be utilized in the public sector and how proper utilization would lead to benefits for the public sector, businesses, and society as a whole. The report explicitly stated that ‘yThe public sector shall be actively involved with the private one and be the leading force in the efficient use of information technology.’

The report provided a catalog of activities, which particularly focused on building a strong infrastructure for the benefit of all in the Danish society. A number of ITsupported networks were suggested, including an electronic service network in the public sector, a nation-wide electronic health network between GPs, hospitals, pharmacies, etc., research networks, electronic library networks, companies’ networks supported by EDI which could then be utilized in public procurement, and an open network of society, which should be accessible to ordinary citizens and businesses.

The Info-society 2000 report served as an eye-opener to all those opportunities that were created by the emerging Internet.

Second initiative: 1995 from vision to action – Info-society 2000 The statement ‘From Vision to Action – Info-Society 2000’ was published in 1995. The statement was used as a lever to create awareness of the great significance of the digital revolution. It was stated that this movement toward the Info-Society was a movement involving everybody. In a true Danish spirit, it was argued that if Grundtvig had been alive he would probably be connected to the Internet. Grundtvig (1783–1872) was a Danish poet, statesman, and church father. He was the driving force behind the establishment of the first Danish folk high schools. The folk high schools played a significant role in the education of the rural youth until the 1950s when the governmental education system took over. This rather strong symbol illustrates the expectations to the move toward the information society, which was seen as the means for everybody to get involved in democratic processes and education. The strategy should thus be based on a Danish model where market forces should not, similar to initiatives in Italy (Kumar et al., 1998) and Hong Kong (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1998) be allowed to be the only forces determining the development.

Although the statement primarily focused on building a strong infrastructure, the responsibility of the public sector to engage in the movement was stressed. The role of the public sector in the digitization of the Danish society was emphasized in statements such as: ‘Public administration (central, county and municipal levels) is to be connected in a comprehensive electronic service network, providing: better service to citizens, better service to trade and industry and support of their own use of IT, rationalisation gains, and more open decision-making processes.’ By implementing these electronic service networks, it was expected that: ‘Concurrently with the replacement of IT systems, public institutions are to change gradually from paper-based archives to electronic processing and filing over the coming years.

It was explicitly stated that the financing of the recommendations in the statement should be done through the ordinary IT budgets at state, county, and municipal level. The statement argued that the units already had a pool available for IT-investment and that these could cover the cost of implementing the recommendations, the argument was that the investments would pay back in the long run. The statement recommended that communication between businesses and the public sector including public procurement should be in a standardized format, namely EDI. To realize the vision of a network society it was stated that de-regulation of the tele-sector would take place according to the EU rules and that the minister would apply the necessary measures in order to lower prices for broadband. The aim was to obtain ‘The world best and cheapest tele-services.’

## Third initiative: 1996 – the info-society for all

In 1996, the next IT policy statement was published. The statement focused on EDI as the means to digitize the Danish society. The statement was named ‘The Info-Society for All – the Danish Model.’ In the 1996 statement, it was announced that: ‘y, this new technology [EDI] presents a number of opportunities and problems, which demand political consideration and action. A cohesive, aggressive strategy for how we wish to further the developments in Denmark is necessary.’

Included in this ‘aggressive strategy’ was the 1996-action plan for electronic commerce (e-commerce). It was found that, ‘y the importance of a fast, effective and consistent implementation of e.g. EDI could hardly be overestimated.’ First and foremost, it was claimed that technological landmarks such as EDI could give Denmark an international lead along with improvement in efficiency of working procedures and development of new products and production processes. The goal of the 1996-action plan was thus to provide the necessary conditions for companies, the public sector, and not the least, the consumers to reap the gains resulting from EDI: ‘The plan is to provide dynamism and accelerate growth. This will be achieved through the public sector joining forces with a large number of commercial organizations to create joint solutions. Thereby we will avoid a state in which everyone waits for everyone else, or in which the approaches chosen are not coherent.’

In order to create the necessary dynamics and consistency, seven initiatives were formulated. The initiatives primarily aimed at supporting the diffusion of EDIFACTbased EDI communication, thereby supporting electronic data transactions between private companies and the public sector. The expectations toward the ability of the public sector in relation to development and implementation were high. It was found that the public sector had to take the lead and show an example.

It was acknowledged in the introduction to the 1996- action plan that the project was ambitious and that it required involvement from several business sectors and institutions. One means to meet this end was to make the necessary arrangements to be able to execute public procurement via EDI. It was, however, the adopters who carried the main responsibility for acting according to the recommendations in the action plan: ‘One decisive feature of ‘‘the Danish model’’ is that, without grandiose plans, but precisely through dialogue and effective action, we are in a position to implement the necessary infrastructure quickly and to remove the barriers to it.’ This attitude was further explained by former experiences with the long Danish tradition of the establishment of the cooperative dairies and abattoirs, which successfully took place at the end of the 19th century and which led to a social and economic lift for a large number of small and often impoverished farmers. Again, strong national symbols were taken in use to promote the diffusion of IT in the Danish society. Again a vision was created and the actors were expected to act accordingly, but no concrete actions were taken to ensure the implementation.

## Fourth initiative: 1999 – focus on e-commerce

The outcome of the 1996 – action plan was less successful than expected and in 1999 – one year before the planned expiry of the action plan – a new initiative was launched. This initiative has been named ‘Focus on e-commerce.’ Focus on e-commerce was organized to meet the conditions imposed by the information society and the Internet. The aim was to initiate concrete activities, which could promote the development of e-commerce in Denmark.

The initiative was, similar to the 1996-action plan, an untraditional joint initiative launched by the Danish government and a number of important commercial and interest associations. Focus on e-commerce thus involved more than just the government. ‘Focus on e-commerce’ aimed at achieving fast, flexible, and efficient action. In recognition of the fast-changing technological environment of e-commerce, the main players of the action plan took new means into use for adoption of the action plan. ‘The option of adopting a traditional action plan has been abandoned because it would probably mean that the content and issues involved would become irrelevant or need to be modified before the plan could be put into effect.’ Instead, seven points of action were formulated, which could stimulate the adoption and diffusion of IT broadly: (1)awareness and sharing of experiences, (2) reliable and secure basic functions on the Net, (3) establishing an effective infrastructure for e-commerce, (4) contractual and regulatory frameworks, (5) research and training, (6) E-commerce in the public sector, and (7) social aspects of e-commerce.

In order to meet the multiplicity of e-commerce, the action plan focused on the above-mentioned seven points of action. There were, however, not any specific goals that had to be achieved within a timeframe in contrast to the 1996-action plan.

The involved partners in ‘Focus on e-commerce’ recognized that e-commerce was broader and more multifaceted than the exchange of EDI messages. Contrary to the 1996-action plan, the e-commerce agenda from 1999 introduced a dynamic definition of e-commerce where all possible actors, business processes, and technologies were included.

In line with the international trends, the Danish business environment adapted to the developments that took place during the period from the time the 1996-action plan was designed and launched, and until ‘Focus on e-commerce’ was introduced. The demanding EDIFACT standard was replaced by ‘whatever new technologies may appear’ and the scope of trading parties was expanded to include consumers. E-commerce was setting the agenda rather than EDI.

## Fifth initiative: 2000 – digital Denmark – conversion to the network society

In year 2000, the next initiative ‘Digital Denmark – Conversion to the Network Society’ was announced. The 2000 initiative emphasized once again that the road to achieve the goals stated in the initiative was dependent on the liberalization of the tele-sector, it was explicitly stated that cheaper mobile telephony was a goal in itself. Furthermore, it was acknowledged that new and promising standards (XDSL) would lead to better and faster Internet, which would be beneficial for suppliers and users of information provided on-line. The initiative was seen as a lever for education of all in the Danish society, terms such as lifelong learning and quality of life in network society were introduced.

Five objectives were highlighted as important to digitize Denmark. Among these five objectives was ‘More Effective and Cheaper Service via Digital Administration’, it was argued that: ‘Efficient digital administration means, among other things, ensuring optimal internal procedures. IT should contribute to the public administration by optimising its organisation and working procedures to produce results in the form of measurable rationalisation gains. These gains will also contribute significantly to the public sector, solving the demographic problem which will mean that in a few years it will have to function with a smaller workforce.’ To achieve the goal of e-government, the idea of a digital signature to all citizens was introduced.

## Sixth initiative: 2002 – IT for all – Denmark’s future

‘IT for All – Denmark’s Future,’ an IT and Telecommunications policy statement and action plan from 2002 was the next initiative. In its preamble, it was stressed that IT should be the means for promoting a reform of the public sector in Denmark. It was emphasized that the development should still be driven by private initiative and on market terms. One outcome of this strategy was the launch of the public procurement portal (doip.dk), which was privately owned and run. Again seven areas of importance to strengthen Denmark as an IT nation were outlined. Among these was ‘An IT-based public sector.’

The overall aim of the Government’s IT and telecommunications policy was to contribute to generating economic growth, reforming the public sector, and equipping the nation for the knowledge society of the future.

In order to achieve this goal, the action plan identified seven policy areas where good results will contribute to placing Denmark as a leading IT nation. These were:

\- More IT in Danish business and industry;

\- A competitive telecommunications sector;

\- Strong IT competencies in Denmark;

\- An IT-based public sector;

\- Increased IT security;

\- Useful content on the Internet; and

\- Danish impact on IT in the EU.

The recommendations to strengthen an IT-based public sector were: (1) the public sector to work digitally both internally and in contact with citizens and companies; (2) public service delivery to be coherent and user-centered, with greater re-use of public data and an increasing number of general portals being means to this end; (3) focus to be intensified including organizational upgrading of IT; and, (4) public-sector e-commerce to be initiated and substantially increased. Some resources were granted to implement the initiatives but it was emphasized that the policy statement should be seen as an invitation to dialog. Furthermore, a new body was introduced ‘The Digital Taskforce.’ The role of this Digital Taskforce was to ensure coordination and progress of the move toward e-government. It is a joint board including top managers from central and local government administration. One tangible outcome of The Digital Taskforce was the e-Day.

## Seventh initiative: 2003 – e-Day 1

The first of September 2003 was by the Danish government announced to be a special ‘e-Day.’ From that day, all Danish state, regional, and local governmental authorities have the right to demand that communication with other authorities to be exchanged electronically. The practical implication of this initiative is that authorities from that day can refuse paper-based communication. The e-Day initiative can be seen as a milestone in the Danish IT policy. It is the first time in the 10 years of IT policies that direct intervention is used as a means for fuelling the diffusion process by creating a network effect. The e-Day initiative explicitly states that public sector institutions have the right to demand that other units communicate electronically with them. Throughout the history of Danish IT policies, it has been claimed that digital communication would be beneficial for public institutions and that a shift should take place to make the Danish public sector efficient. There has been an implicit expectation that the diffusion of the necessary technologies would take place based on the recommendations, but no direct control or enforcement has been used to make diffusion and implementation happen. Another remarkable shift in the policy is the introduction of the digital taskforce as an active change agency, stimulating the diffusion process. The further section analyzes if the e-Day initiative actually has led to that egovernment, has seen the day of light in Denmark.

This section presented the Odyssey of one decade’s Danish IT policy. The presentation focused on the content and also to some extent, on the means for implementing the content of the policy statements. The next section focuses on impact of policy statements as observed in previous studies.

## Intervention as lever for diffusion

Adoption and diffusion of innovations can be driven by many forces and since decades, it has been an ongoing debate if it is possible to identify determining factors that stimulate uptake and diffusion of innovations or if there are no causal relationships at all (Markus and Robey, 1988). It has been suggested that regardless of methodological positioning, researchers should identify what type of innovation is investigated and what stage in the diffusion process is examined (Wolfe, 1994). In the present study, the innovation can best be characterized as a ‘policy innovation.’ A policy innovation is defined as a policy that is new to the governmental unit that adopts it (Walker, 1969). A policy innovation such as the e-Day is related closely to the concept of a technology cluster or an IT infrastructure. A technology cluster consists of one or more distinguishable elements of technology that are perceived as being closely inter-related (Rogers, 2003). With respect to the e-Day initiative, the technology elements include office systems capable of generating, storing, and processing data (e.g. electronic document handling systems), infrastructure capable of transferring data (e.g. broadband), and encryption technology (e.g. digital signatures) to ensure security. All these elements have to be in place before it makes sense to stimulate or even enforce adoption and diffusion of digital exchange of messages in the public sector. Furthermore, the e-Day initiative represents a managerial innovation. Managerial innovation refers to the organizational changes that the organization goes through when implementing the initiative (Zaltman et al., 1973). It is often argued that especially change in managerial practices in the public sector is constrained by the strong hierarchical structures, which characterize the public sector (Weber, 1922).

Walker (1969) argues that it is impossible to separate the nature of the innovation from the behavior of the adopters when studying diffusion of policy innovations. In the present study, emphasis is more on behavior of the adopters than on characteristics of the innovation, though as argued above it cannot be ignored that the features of the innovation have to be in place before it makes sense to discuss diffusion.

Concerning Wolfe’s second recommendation (Wolfe, 1994); the clarification of the stage in the diffusion process, which is under examination focus, is on the final stages in the diffusion process (Rogers, 2003). Rogers defines the final stages of the diffusion process in organizations as clarifying and routinizing. At that point in the adoption cycle, the characteristics of the innovation are less important compared to earlier stages where innovation characteristics might determine the decision to adopt. With respect to policy innovations such as the e-Day initiative, it becomes more relevant to analyze the mechanisms used to stimulate diffusion and implementation rather than to identify attributes of the innovation, which might influence the decision to adoption in the first place. The primary reason being that policy innovations are characterized by that they are issued by regulatory bodies. As such, they are part of a larger political system of priorities and the discourse of elected decision makers (Walker, 1969). Different rationalities than those that are applied in the private sector therefore influence the decision to adopt. Usually, elected persons want to stay in power and keep a majority of their political party. Therefore, elected persons might hesitate with enforcing unpopular decisions or large investments that do not pay-off at short term (Berry and Berry, 1999).

There are different opinions regarding the effectiveness of regulation and interventions as means for directing action related to adoption, and diffusion of IT innovations and policies that aim at stimulating the process. This raises the question whether regulatory initiatives can possibly result in voluntary changes in organizational behavior leading to adoption and diffusion, or whether incentives for adoption are to be found in mechanisms solely driven by economic and strategic interests. In the following, a number of considerations related to regulation are discussed. It should be emphasized that the initiatives presented above in the previous section, in a legal sense, are categorized as soft law. Soft law concerns rules of conduct that are on a legally non-binding level (Nielsen, 1999). An analogy is, however, made to considerations related to explicit rules and regulation in general.

Inherent in a set of rules is the means of regulatory intervention. Three types of regulatory intervention, the pedagogical, the economic, and the normative (Eckhoff, 1983) have been described as means for regulating organizational and human behavior. Eckhoff focuses particularly on environmental regulation. However, Andersen (1998) introduced the three types of regulation as mechanisms to stimulate IT adoption in the public sector.

Prohibition and commands characterize normative interventions. Normative interventions are means for enforcing the objective of the regulation. This type of interventions in relation to IT-adoption is, for example, to define standards (Andersen et al., 2000). The economic interventions are characterized by influencing what people find advantageous to do. Through the economic interventions, organizations are rewarded to perform certain acts or punished for certain other types of behavior. Direct subsidy of IT development projects can be considered an example of an economic intervention (ibid.). The pedagogical intervention is characterized by information campaigns initiated by governmental units and larger associations where the aim is to influence the opinion of a given group of potential adopters. Eckhoff (1983) argues that information is a must in most cases where the economic and normative regulatory interventions are brought into play, but information in itself can be used as a means of intervention. Through information, it is possible to influence opinions and values thus making individuals more motivated for certain types of actions wished for by the governmental units or associations. Knowledge building hence becomes an important aspect in pedagogical intervention (Andersen et al., 2000).

However, governments can also effectively employ institutional measures to obtain a desired change in behavior. Institutional measures are usually classified into influential or regulatory ones (King et al., 1994). Influential initiatives aim to change behavior of those under the institutions’ sway. This is achieved without direct reference to force, or the exercise of command, but mainly through an incentive structure. Regulatory actions on the other hand are expected directly to affect the behaviors of entities under formal government jurisdiction. This can take the form of directives or through limiting the choice of options. Overall, regulatory measures allow conflicting decentralized decisions to be compatible without the necessity of either centralized institutions or ‘key’ individuals to comprehend the whole system (Boyer, 1988). This is sometimes necessary as, for example, the use of common standards may yield more work for each node in a system, but the overall benefits clearly outweigh the sum of each node’s additional work.

Within the domain of IS research, the impact of intervention has often been subjected to discussion. The writings of Lai and Guynes (1997), Gregor and Johnston (2001), Johnston and Gregor (2000), and King et al. (1994) uncover a discrepancy in relation to the effects of coordinated efforts made by industry and trade associations, or governmental units in relation to diffusion of IT (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 2001). It has been stated that institutional (especially governmental units) interventions (Lai and Guynes, 1997) and change in the environment (e.g. regulation) (Gregor and Johnston, 2001) are among the most powerful causes for IT adoption and diffusion. Deliberate institutional interventions or refraining from interventions are thus found to play a vital role in technology diffusion (King et al., 1994). The opposite point of view has however also been stated by Johnston and Gregor (2000) who argue that, ‘y deliberative coordinated action by an industry as a whole, or units purporting to represent such a group position, may be severely limited in effectiveness.’ However, regardless of the attitude toward the impact of institutional intervention, different parties can address and have vested interests in the building of electronic infrastructures, which are a necessity for IT diffusion (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1998).

Damsgaard and Lyytinen (1998) provide a classification of government intervention, which is based on the King et al. (1994) dichotomy influence-regulation, combined with supply push and demand pull. The framework developed by Rogers (2003) suggests that diffusion of innovations is driven by supply push. The underlying idea according to this view is that an innovation is produced and then promoted by change agents and opinion leaders, who convince a potential adopter of the benefits of the innovation. Demand pull on the other hand is more driven by potential adopters, who search for innovations that can make a change (Abrahamson, 1996). King et al. (1994) suggest that the public sector may employ six types of institutional intervention to stimulate adoption and diffusion activities. The six types of institutional intervention range on a continuum from more subtle types of influence such as mobilization, knowledge building, and knowledge deployment to more concrete means of intervention such as innovation directive, subsidy, and standard setting.

Mobilization seeks to articulate a particular point of view in relation to innovation that is in accordance with larger goals. This is achieved by encouraging organizations and/or individuals to perceive an innovation in the ‘right’ way. An example of mobilization is when governments publish national e-government initiatives by making available through publicly accessible data bases or through organizing work-shops and seminars. Governments can also favor a certain point of view by simply paying attention to it and thereby, increasing the ‘demand pull.’ This can be done through edifying societal visions around future e-government initiatives, giving favorable interpretations of the possibilities of the technology, and so forth. When governments apply the strategy of knowledge building to stimulate diffusion of innovations, it is typically done by funding R&D programs. The assumption behind the intervention driven by knowledge building is that by funding research at universities, knowledge is created. The knowledge can then be applied in industry in the form of knowledge and solutions for the benefit of businesses and society at large. The knowledge deployment strategy is closely connected to and builds further on the knowledge building strategy. When applying the knowledge deployment strategy, the goal is to disperse new knowledge to individuals and organizations so that they are able to use solutions emerging through the innovation network. Initiatives that provide the work force with IT education falls into this category.

In the category of more direct means for stimulating the diffusion of e-government fall instruments such as innovation directives, subsidy, and standard setting. Norms that regulate the production or use of innovations in the governmental regime are here labelled innovation directives. Since e-government is an interorganizational issue, it exhibits the problem of ‘critical mass’ and associated ‘prisoner’s dilemma’ (Elster, 1989). In other words, the more adopters (potential partners) there are, the more valuable it is to join technology users. Consequently, no one is willing to adopt the technology in its early stages when there are only few potential partners. Government can take an active role showing the way of innovative behavior. Governments can create strong incentives for organizations to use e-government by directing its own agencies, such as customs, tax-officials, etc. to implement e-government in their interactions with outside partners – business and citizen alike. Another common way of using innovation directives is to require external parties to use e-government in their communication with government departments. For example, by mandating companies to use electronic invoices when dealing with government authorities. Another more direct instrument to stimulate diffusion is to use subsidy. Governments can subsidy activities that are critical for the diffusion of e-government. These can be targeted to any or all groups of actors that are involved in the diffusion process. Through subsidies, governments can lower the adoption barriers and thereby, relief the burden of some groups in carrying the otherwise disproportionate costs of their local involvement. They may also make the decision to launch such innovations more lucrative at the early stages of diffusion, when benefits are lower (due to critical mass problems and thus, limited network externalities) and risks are higher (due to scarcity of experiences and skills). Examples of the subsidy strategy is the public support for building an IT infrastructure and the development of digital signatures, which enables secure interaction with government. The sixth and final institutional diffusion instrument that King et al. (1994) suggested is standard setting. Standard setting formalizes practices and limits the space of choice options for organizations while participating in the technology selection. Governmental setting of standards can be targeted both at the demand side and the supply side. On the demand side acquiring standardized innovations creates confidence, since standardized innovations are more likely to be compatible with future innovations and they are in general, useful longer than non-standardized innovations. On the supply side, standardization reduces innovation risks and thereby, it encourages providers to use and produce innovations that comply to the standard. Standard setting is regarded as a necessary means to promote the diffusion of e-government, because e-government is targeted for communication between heterogeneous and incompatible systems that are under the control of different organizations. Therefore, organizations must agree on rules that unambiguously define transactions between them (Figure 1).

## Analysis of the seven initiatives

From the presentation of the seven initiatives, it is clear that in the Danish context, the indirect pedagogical intervention at the influence level has been the sole strategy pursued in the policy statements up to the e-Day initiative where more direct intervention – still at the influence level – is taken into use. Just from reading the policy statements, it is less visible if the policy statements have been driven by supply push or demand pull. The following section provides an analysis of how the seven initiatives presented in the previous section may be classified into the supply push/ demand pull–influence/regulation matrix. The classification is used to illustrate what type of intervention has been used and if there has been a shift with the e-Day initiative.

## First initiative: 1994 – Info-society 2000

The first initiative was mainly a Danish reaction to the publishing of EU’s Bangemann report (Bangemann, 1994).

The Danish initiative focused on mobilizing a vision around the information society, and it identified a number of issues that had to be dealt with before an information society would become visible. The intervention was to set the transition from the industrial society to the information society on the public agenda. It received much public attention and created a lot of debate in Denmark, and as such it fulfilled its purpose. This initiative is characterized as entry 2.

Second initiative: 1995 from vision to action – Info-society 2000 The second initiative was in many ways similar to the first one. However, in addition to mobilization, more attention was directed toward building general awareness (knowledge deployment on both the demand side and supply side). The initiative included in its rhetoric strong national symbols as a way to underscore the significance of the move toward the information society. It is noticeable that no subsidies were considered necessary. Innovation directives in the form of influence were applied internally within government to foster EDI adoption. For the telecommunication sector, innovation directives on the supply side were imposed to obtain the ‘the world’s best and cheapest telecommunication services.’ This initiative also falls in entry 2.

## Third initiative: 1996 – the Info-society for all

This initiative marks a radical departure from the two previous ones. Here, the government undertakes the role of leading technology provider, user, and advocator of a specific technology (UN/EDIFACT-based EDI). As such, it is involved with standard setting (EDIFACT), it issues an innovation directive to its constituents, and it seeks to mobilize a vision around EDI as a national challenge. This initiative is much more ambitious and also commanding: so, in addition to entry 2 also entry 4 measures are applied.

## Fourth initiative: 1999 – focus on e-commerce

In this initiative, the focus is back on knowledge deployment and much less on government as a technology user. The adoption is more voluntary as innovation directives are not employed. This initiative we therefore categorize as entry 2.

## Fifth initiative: 2000 – digital Denmark – conversion to the network society

In this initiative, the focus has shifted from the business environment to internal government processes. There is support for a specific standard (XML), and there was for the first time knowledge building as government was behind the creation of a digital signature. Similar to the previous initiative, the means for achieving the benefits were based on voluntariness. It was admitted that to reap the benefits of IT investments, it was necessary to face the organizational consequences of the technological investments. However, there is no radical shift in the means for fuelling the diffusion of technology and the initiative is hence placed in entry 2.

## Sixth initiative: 2002 – IT for all – Denmark’s future

This initiative marks a return to a wide approach to ICT and how it might adopted. The government has a more hands-off approach and seeks to support rather than control. Public sector e-procurement is encouraged but not directly subsidized, and a private company is granted an exclusive right to operate the public procurement portal and the government intervention is once again placed in entry 2.

## Seventh initiative: 2003 – e-Day 1

The e-Day 1 initiative is different from the previous initiatives in the sense that it delegates the right to make innovation directives to the various departments and boards. Contrary to the former six initiatives where recommendations but no specific suggestions to action toward the public sector institutions, the e-Day 1 initiative introduces the instrument of force; institutions have the right to demand information in digital format. It has a unilateral focus on internal government procedures and the initiative is hence placed in entry 4 (Figure 2).

The placement of the seven initiatives in the influence/ regulation – supply push/demand pull matrix demonstrates that influence – demand pull has mainly been used as an instrument to fuel diffusion of IT in government in Denmark. Compared to other countries, this strong emphasis on demand pull is striking. However, the incentives communicated in the influential initiatives have not been strong enough for the public sector to adopt IT. In their analysis of six initiatives, which aimed to stimulate the diffusion of EDI in Europe, Asia, and the Pacific region, Damsgaard and Lyytinen (1998) found a more even distribution of use of the four different types of instruments. It should, however, be noted that there has been a mixed outcome of the further diffusion of EDI in the different regions. The diffusion of EDI in Asia has been successful (Teo et al., 1997), whereas the success in the European setting has been assessed to be less successful (Krcmar et al., 1995).

The main reason for the absence of initiatives in the supply push dimension with respect to e-government diffusion in the Danish setting can be subscribed to the strong EU regulation on procurement of goods and services. All purchases in the public sector, exceeding the value of DKK 1.2 million, have to go through a strongly regulated tendering process. The incentive for suppliers to develop off-the-shelf software supporting processes in the public sector is therefore modest due to the strong competition from national and international players bidding on the calls for tenders issued by public sector institutions, when they are in need of new software products to support new work practices. The result is that there is a limited ongoing development of software products supporting e-government initiatives. It should, however, be noted that there are strong players in the Danish market, which supply governmental institutions with software supporting the basic procedures. Wellestablished software houses such as KMD and ScanJour supply municipalities and state, respectively, with robust software solutions.

## Impact of the seven initiatives

Statistical data were retrieved (Statistics Denmark, 2003, 2004, 2005, and 2006) to analyze the impact of the seven initiatives and e-Day1, in particular. The analysis and discussion includes the annual accounts of the public sectors’ use of IT in years 2002 – 2005. The reason for using this particular sample is that consistent and comparative data were available for that time-span. We believe that this data is the best option available. Another possibility would be to obtain data based on a survey instrument, specifically developed for the purpose of analyzing the impact of the six initiatives, leading up to e-Day in the diffusion and deployment of IT internally in the public sector in Denmark, but this approach would capture intentions and impressions only. Furthermore, there is a limit to the memory of people; it would be ambitious to expect that there would be a clear memory of exact deployment rates of IT 5 years ago. The actual use as reported to the national statistical bureau over time forms a concrete measure on which the impact of e-Day1 can be observed.

The series of data from years 2002, 2003, 2004, and 2005 show that there has been a growth in the exchange of data electronically between governmental units whereas the exchange of documents between citizens/businesses and the public sector has remained more or less constant. The growth in governmental units, receiving of documents electronically, during the period 2002–2004 at the 25% level is statistically significant (P is less than or equal to 0.001). Thereafter, the growth has remained almost unchanged. The growth is depicted in Figure 3.

The figures indicate that there was a modest use of IT in the public sector, and between the public sector and its customers in year 2002, before the launch of the e-Day 1. This could indicate that the impact of the six initiatives leading up to the e-Day 1 initiative have had limited impact on the uptake of IT in government. It is worth noticing that the adoption rates of Internet among Danish citizens and businesses were among the highest in the world in year 2002. There are therefore no reasons to believe that the limited interaction is due to lack of knowledge of how to use the Internet as a tool for interaction, given that, practically all Danes were familiar with using the Internet. In 2003, when the e-Day 1 was launched, the impact of the e-Day 1 was still insignificant but thereafter, the growth has taken off though still not with respect to the interaction with citizens and businesses. This indicates that the e-Day has had an impact on the diffusion of e-government with respect to the internal perspective. Public sector institutions have increased their digital interaction. That is especially the case in relation to the intra-governmental communication in 2004 and 2005, where more than 50% of the governmental units state that at least 25% of the documents they receive are in a digital format. Figure 3 indicates that citizens and businesses are still reluctant to send documents in a digital format to the public sector. By solely looking at Figure 3, one could state that the e-Day initiative has worked according to its objective because more documents are sent within governmental units in a digital format after the launch of the directive in 2003. However, adoption of technologies such as digital communication tools should go further than merely receiving digital documents. Use and reuse of data in the digital format is imperative if the benefits of the innovation are to be utilized (Massetti and Zmud, 1996).

Figure 4 illustrates that there has been only a small growth in the number of governmental units where at least 50% of the cases are handled electronically. Taking into consideration that there has been a substantial growth in the number of cases actually exchanged electronically intragovernmental (cf. Figure 3), which is not reflected in the statistics on electronic case handling, this could indicate that the public sector institutions do not have the IT-tools necessary for handling the cases electronically. One prerequisite for successful adoption and diffusion of an innovation such as the e-Day initiative is that the necessary technologies are in place (Rogers, 2003). In order to analyze this closer, the uptake of case- and document-handling systems in public institutions was analyzed.

Figure 5 depicts that the degree of sophistication on handling of electronic documents is rather low. There is a high level of deployment of electronic log systems at all levels – and especially at county level where the level has been 100% all years reported (one reason for the decrease in the level of electronic case handling at county level is a result of structural changes in the public sector in Denmark. From January 2007, all tasks of counties will be placed on either state or municipal units). When it comes to full electronic case handling, the deployment is rather low at state level whereas municipalities have increased its uptake substantially over the period, and has by 2005, surpassed the 50% level with respect to electronic case handling. Municipalities have managed to narrow the gap between the number of messages that are received in digital format (via electronic log-systems) and full electronic processing of data (via electronic case handling). However, in all cases there is a gap between the number of messages received in digital format and the number of messages that are processed electronically. It is therefore crucial to ask if this is due to issues related to incompatible standards across the different public sector units, which exchange data electronically.

The numbers from Statistics Denmark illustrates the impact of the e-Day in an exemplar manner. The diffusion rates are high at the surface, but the benefits from the adoption of the technology supporting digital government must be expected to be limited or even absent. Though Denmark is recognized to be in the lead with respect to egovernment, the gap between adoption and deployment is still large.

## Conclusion and putting the initiatives into perspective

One of the unifying principles of the Danish IT policy in the domain of e-government has been to reduce the paperbased case handling in favor of electronic processing and filing. In each of the initiatives presented in the section, History of institutional intervention leading to the e-day initiative, there are more or less pronounced expectations toward savings and efficiency gains, if the public sector changes its routines from manual paper-handling to electronic procedures. In a Social Democratic, spirit adoption and diffusion of the ideas exhibited in the policy statements have been fuelled by pedagogical intervention rather than more direct means, such as legislation or economic rewards for changing behavior. The initiatives have been solely driven by demand pull forces, which only in few instances have had the character of regulation. This path has not so far proven to be fruitful, if conclusions are made based on the figures on IT adoption in the public sector published by Statistics Denmark. Even though the rate of documents received in electronic format internally in government has grown significantly, there is still a big gap between the number of received electronic documents and the number of cases that are handled electronically. So, even though government has turned its language into imperative by stating that ‘one governmental authority has the right to demand that its communication with another authority must be in electronic format,’ this has not changed the work practices beyond the exchange of information.

One overall challenge in relation to assessment of the outcome of coordinated initiatives is the level of analysis when measuring the outcome. One way to evaluate the success or failure of a project is based on the projects’ rate of adoption among potential users. As observed by Kumar et al. (1998), this might be a less fruitful path since some or all adopters at a later state might reject the innovation (e.g. Kumar et al., 1998). To denote adoption rate as the dependent variable for success thus has to be seen in relation to time (DeLone and McLean, 1992). This approach is however not easy to apply to the e-Day initiative at this point in time, given the relatively recent inception. Furthermore, the time variable is a race against new developments in technology in the public sector setting. As demonstrated in the presentation of one decade’s IT policies, focus changes over time. The public sector is similar to the private sector, subject to phenomena stemming from new technology development and demands from customers/citizens. An eminent example is from the turn of the century where e-commerce was in demand. The Danish government aligned with the trend, deviated from its previous focus on internal processes, and defined its IT policy with special emphasis on secure e-commerce for Danish citizens.

Our analysis suggests that the e-Day initiative so far has led to mixed results. When looking at the intra-governmental exchange of electronic messages in isolation (cf. Figure 3), there has been a remarkable growth. This indicates that the e-Day initiative has had a positive impact on the diffusion of e-government. But a closer examination of the further deployment of the electronic messages provides a different picture. There has only been a slight increase in the number of government agencies using electronic document handling systems, which is a prerequisite for paper-less document handling as envisioned in the policy statements. This suggests that more traditional procedures are still being the norm in the governmental agencies. One problem appears to be the lack of basic systems supporting electronic document and case handling (cf. Figure 5). Ultimately, this points toward that Danish government agencies face what the business community has suffered from for decades – lack of common standards. One future challenge for the Danish public sector in order to be able to implement e-government is, therefore, to agree on a common standard for communication and interaction to make sure that the access to and delivery of government information and service to citizens, business partners, employees, other agencies, and government entities can be enhanced.
