# Pricing of Wireless Services: Service Pricing vs. Traffic Pricing

Atanu Lahiri

Michael G. Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle, Washington 98195, lahiria@uw.edu

Rajiv M. Dewan, Marshall Freimer

William E. Simon Graduate School of Business Administration, University of Rochester, Rochester, New York 14627 {dewan@simon.rochester.edu, freimer@simon.rochester.edu}

s the ability to measure technology resource usage gets easier with increased connectivity, the question whether a technology resource should be priced by the amount of the resource used or by the particular use of the resource has become increasingly important. We examine this issue in the context of pricing of wireless services: should the price be based on the service, e.g., voice, multimedia messages, short messages, or should it be based on the traffic generated? Many consumer advocates oppose discriminatory pricing across services believing that it enriches carriers at the expense of consumers. The opposition to discrimination has grown significantly, and it has even prompted the U.S. Congress to question executives of some of the biggest carriers. With this ongoing debate on discrimination in mind, we compare two pricing regimes here. One regime, namely, service pricing, involves pricing different services differently. The other one, namely, traffic pricing, involves pricing the traffic (i.e., bytes) transmitted. We show why the common wisdom, that discriminatory pricing across services increases profits and harms consumers, may not always hold. We also show that such discrimination can increase social welfare.

Key words: nonlinear pricing; second-degree discrimination; quasi-bundling; telecommunication; net neutrality; dumb pipe

## 1. Introduction

In the 18th century, navigation tolls depended on the cargo type, e.g., the toll for a ton of sand was different from that for a ton of timber (Odlyzko 2004). We see similar pricing practices today in the world of telecommunications: prices often depend on the traffic type and not merely on the cost of carrying it. Wireless carriers in the United States charge a much lower price for traffic generated by voice calls than they do for traffic generated by other services such as the short message service (SMS) text messaging or wireless surfing (Lahiri et al. 2010). Table 1 shows the typical rates that they charge for different services. The right-most column of this table shows a wide variation in prices charged per byte of data transmitted. Prices charged for SMS text messages, which are among the highest, have recently been scrutinized by the U.S. Congress (ABCNews 2009).

Major carriers in the United States claim that all prices are driven and determined by the competition among carriers. Experts and consumer advocates, on the other hand, argue that prices charged for SMS text messages far exceed the marginal cost of the wireless path (Keshav 2009), indicating the prevalence of monopoly-like pricing power in this industry. According to them, such pricing power results from a number of factors, including alliances between carriers and handset manufacturers, a lack of portability of handsets across networks, and network effects.

Many in the United States, including supporters of wireless net neutrality, are urging the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) and the U.S. Congress to ban all forms of discriminatory pricing, including those based on source, destination, and service usage (Lahiri et al. 2011). Writing in the Wall Street Journal, Mossberg (2007) claims, “We need a wireless mobile device ecosystem that mirrors the PC/Internet ecosystem, one where the consumers’ purchase of network capacity is separate from their purchase of the hardware and software they use on that network. It will take government action, or some disruptive technology or business innovation, to get us there.” Despite the popularity of this view against discrimination, the literature on pricing of information services has not critically examined such discrimination and its economic impacts—this void in the literature is what we intend to fill.

Our objective here is to understand how discriminatory pricing impacts (or a disruption of it would impact) carriers, consumers, and the society as a whole. Specifically, we compare two different pricing regimes—one that allows discrimination based on type of service and one that does not. One regime involves pricing different services differently; we name this discriminatory regime service pricing. Carriers in the United States use service pricing. For example, consider the pricing practice of a leading carrier: it currently offers consumers several options for voice calls, e.g., one can either choose a plan that costs \$80 a month and includes 1,000 minutes calltime or another that costs \$60 a month but includes only 700 minutes. The consumer can also purchase 300 text messages for a price of \$5 a month or 1,000 text messages for \$10 a month. Likewise, he can get a limited data plan for \$10 a month or unlimited access for \$15 a month. For each service, consumers have the option of picking a suitable plan from a menu of plans.

Service pricing is popular for smart phone “apps” as well. AT&T FamilyMap<sup>™</sup> costs \$9.99 per month for up to two family members and \$14.99 for up to five family members (AT&T 2011). This app helps AT&T users locate their friends or family members on the AT&T network. For users without a GPScapable phone, it generates maps based on the nearby tower information, a method that requires very little data transmission. AT&T also charges separately for third-party apps such as those offered by Loopt or Ebay—AT&T’s data plan does not include these apps. AT&T is not alone. Verizon charges separately for Skype Mobile<sup>™</sup> (Verizon 2011). Other major carriers also practice service pricing. They all believe that selling “dumb pipes” leads to a lower profit (Kim 2010). We critically examine this belief. It is useful to note here that the FCC accepts such pricing practices. The FCC requires neutrality only with respect to content delivered over Web browsers, including browsers on mobile devices (FCC 2010).

In the other regime that we term traffic pricing, a carrier simply prices the traffic consumed. Each consumer has the right to allocate the purchased traffic between services according to his or her preferences. As already mentioned, such pricing is not common in the United States. Exceptions include smaller carriers such as Boost Mobile, who offer consumers bundles consisting of voice, text, Web, and email (BoostMobile 2011)—their pricing practices are akin to traffic pricing. AT&T’s GoPhone<sup>™</sup>, which operates in the prepaid market like Boost Mobile, also offers a menu of bundles (AT&T 2011). Other examples of traffic pricing include broadband data plans for Android phones and tablets. Because users of open platforms can develop their own services, it is not possible for carriers to tag and meter every service separately. In this sense, open platforms entail traffic pricing (Lahiri et al. 2011).

We model a monopolist’s tariff design problem assuming that it faces a heterogeneous market. As common in practice, this monopolist is assumed to use nonlinear pricing (Mussa and Rosen 1978, Goldman et al. 1984, Maskin and Riley 1984, Sundararajan 2004). Nonlinear pricing is quite common in the United States. One example of plans currently used in the United States is: the charge is p dollars per month for up to n units of usage, and usage over n units is billed at r dollars per unit. Such “fixed up to” or “FUT” pricing has three components: a fixed fee of p dollars, a free call time allowance of n units, and an over-limit rate of r dollars per unit (Masuda and Whang 2006). However, Masuda and Whang (2006) show that no consumer in the equilibrium goes over the limit so long as the number of consumer types is finite and the monthly usage of each consumer type is deterministic. There is empirical evidence suggesting that this is largely true, i.e., only a minority goes over the limit (Lambrecht et al. 2007). Given this consumer behavior, we simplify the FUT pricing to a simple nonlinear price schedule.

For the purpose of exposition, we consider a setting in which the monopolist faces discrete consumer types and sells multiple services. When it uses service pricing, it offers a separate price schedule (or menu) for each service. Each schedule consists of a number of plans, one aimed at each consumer type; each plan provides a fixed amount of usage for a price. Consumers self-select from the menus offered.

On the other hand, when the carrier uses traffic pricing, it faces the task of designing only one price schedule to price the traffic consumption; each plan in the schedule provides a certain level of traffic usage for a price. Thus, in the case of traffic pricing, consumers have only one menu to choose from. However, the decision problem that consumers face under traffic pricing has two stages: in the first stage, each consumer picks a plan from the menu offered; in the second, the consumer decides how to allocate the purchased traffic between different services.

We model two kinds of consumer heterogeneity. In the first case, consumers differ primarily in their incomes and those with higher incomes are willing to pay more for every service. In this case, the consumer classes are uniformly ordered across services. In the second case, this ordering is nonuniform, just as one would expect when consumers’ tastes are primarily driven by their idiosyncratic tastes for different services—some consumers are willing to pay more for some services and the other consumers are willing to pay more for the rest.

For the two cases mentioned, uniform ordering and nonuniform ordering of consumer types across services, we compare all three surpluses—producer (carrier), consumer, and social—under service pricing with those under traffic pricing. Current research does not explain welfare implications of service pricing, an intriguing issue that we discuss in detail. We gain several counterintuitive insights from our model. We find that the prevalent view against discrimination holds in certain circumstances, but it does not in many others. We find that, in some cases, discrimination can surprisingly lead to a higher consumer surplus. We also find that, in some others, discrimination can benefit both the carrier and consumers simultaneously. We characterize conditions under which such unexpected outcomes occur and illustrate them using numerical examples.

Further, we find that the common wisdom, that discrimination increases profits, holds when consumer types are uniformly ordered, but it does not otherwise. We show that, when the order is not uniform, traffic pricing behaves like a bundle, leading often to a higher profit for the carrier. We further show that traffic pricing is also a very effective pricing strategy for the carrier in the sense that it dominates the purebundling strategy.

The major contribution of this research is, therefore, that it shows that common beliefs regarding the impact of service pricing on producer, consumer, and social surpluses often do not hold. Contrary to what consumer advocates and proponents of neutrality claim, discrimination is not always harmful for consumers just as it is not always beneficial for the carrier. We explain why policy makers need to be mindful that banning discrimination can reduce social welfare. In this sense, our research augments prior literature on economic benefits of price discrimination. The literature on second-degree price discrimination has shown that such discrimination can improve producer and social surpluses (Katz 1983, Roberts 1979, Spence 1977). Similarly, the literature on third-degree discrimination has found instances in which discrimination across different consumer markets leads to higher welfare (Schmalensee 1981, Varian 1985). We show that these insights, in a sense, carry over to this rather unique context of telecommunication services, where traffic related to different types of services are priced differently. Our findings, in a nutshell, explain why a detailed economic analysis is critical to understanding the consequences of banning discriminatory pricing practices in the wireless services industry. They, together, show how forcing wireless providers to offer dumb pipes can lead to lower social welfare and, in many cases, hurt the very consumers policy makers want to protect.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. After providing a brief literature review, we describe our model assumptions. Next, we discuss the following scenarios: a market with two types of consumers who are uniformly ordered, and a market with two types of consumers who are not uniformly ordered. We conclude by summarizing major contributions and limitations of this research.

## 2. Literature

This work belongs to the literature on price discrimination. Perfect price discrimination or first-degree discrimination is the one in which each consumer is charged his or her reservation price. However, firstdegree discrimination is often not practical (see Tirole 1988, pp. 135–137). More practical forms of price discrimination include second-degree and third-degree discriminations and bundling. In this paper, we discuss a new form of discrimination, which is discrimination across services (e.g., calling, texting, multimedia mails, etc.) that use the same resource (i.e., traffic).

Third-degree discrimination (Schmalensee 1981, Varian 1985) involves charging different prices in different consumer markets. Such discrimination is feasible if arbitrage cannot occur between different consumer markets, i.e., if consumers in one market cannot buy the product from another market at a lower price. In our case, this condition is not met: different consumer classes in our model are not located in different markets, and each consumer selfselects from the choices available. Therefore, we do not belong to this stream.

Second-degree discrimination (Katz 1983, Maskin and Riley 1984, Mussa and Rosen 1978, Roberts 1979, Spence 1977) involves offering consumers a set of options from which consumers self-select. Such discrimination has also received considerable attention from researchers working in the area of economics of information goods (Bhargava and Choudhary 2001, Sundararajan 2004). We, too, model second-degree discrimination in this paper. However, we are not interested in the implications of second-degree discrimination itself. We are interested in comparing two different ways of implementing second-degree discrimination. One approach involves implementing second-degree discrimination for each service separately—we call this approach service pricing. The other one involves implementing seconddegree discrimination to price the underlying traffic consumption—we call this approach traffic pricing.

Our work also overlaps with the literature on bundling. Bundling several goods into one bundle increases profits when consumers have differing valuations for individual goods but similar valuations for the bundle (Adams and Yellen 1976). The second pricing approach discussed in the preceding paragraph, i.e., traffic pricing, is akin to bundling. However, in the case of bundling (Adams and Yellen 1976), the seller chooses the bundle composition. In the case of traffic pricing, each consumer chooses his or her own traffic allocation. Traffic pricing is therefore best described as quasi-bundling (Lahiri et al. 2011).

There exists a long stream of research on bundling. Bakos and Brynjolfsson (2000) show that bundling is optimal for the seller when the number of goods is large and reservation prices for them are independently distributed. The intuition is that, because of the law of large numbers, all consumers would value a bundle comprising all goods the same. An important difference between Bakos and Brynjolfsson (2000) and our work is that we do not model discrete goods. In our model, consumers have a nonnegative reservation price for each service, which is increasing in usage. Our model, in this respect, is closer to the multiproduct pricing problem discussed in Armstrong (1999). Armstrong (1999) establishes the approximate optimality of a single two-part tariff from the seller’s viewpoint. We do not examine the case of a large number of products as we are not interested in approximate optimality results. We solve the quasi-bundling problem and find the impact of quasibundling on not only the profit but also on consumer and social welfare.

Interestingly, there exists research on quasibundling (also called customized bundling) of discrete goods (Brown and Alexander 2004, Hitt and Chen 2005, Wu et al. 2008). Brown and Alexander (2004) illustrate how cable TV service providers, who typically bundle several channels into packages, such as a sports package, could increase profits by offering customized bundles, such as “any 10 channels for \$20.” Hitt and Chen (2005) formally prove this result and also show that, under certain conditions, customized bundling is equivalent to mixed bundling, a form of bundling in which individual goods are offered along with pure bundles. Our twostage model of traffic pricing is similar to that in (Hitt and Chen 2005): in the first stage, consumers make the decision to buy, and in the second, they choose the bundle composition, i.e., they make the decision to allocate the purchased traffic between different services.

As do Hitt and Chen (2005) and Wu et al. (2008), we too find that quasi-bundling can be optimal from the seller’s viewpoint. We prove that this optimality holds only if consumer types are ordered differently across different services. Wu et al. (2008) find that customized bundling is typically welfare maximizing. We find evidence to the contrary; we show that traffic pricing, which is being advocated by the Wall Street Journal and the U.S. Congress because of its perceived neutrality, is not always welfare maximizing. Wu et al. (2008) attribute the higher welfare to a higher market coverage. We show that the market coverage often declines with quasi-bundling. The intuition is that quasi-bundling is akin to buffet pricing. Consumers who just want one or two items often balk at paying for the entire buffet, which reduces the market coverage. The reduction in the market coverage, in turn, lowers consumer and social welfare. This new result contributes to the economic research on policy making.

## 3. Model Preliminaries

Consider a monopolist carrier facing a market with two types of consumers who have different reservation prices for different wireless services. Let the consumer types be indexed by $i , i = 1$ or 2. Consumers of types 1 and 2 comprise fractions $1 - f$ and $f ,$ , respectively, of the whole population. The firm offers n services, which are indexed by $j , \ j \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , n \}$

The value of using t bytes on service j to a type i consumer, $v _ { i j } ( t )$ , is assumed to be strictly concave and increasing in t. Concavity of $v _ { i j } ( t )$ implies that the marginal value obtained by using an additional byte is lower at higher values of t. We also assume an infinite derivative at $t = 0$ to ensure interior solutions; many commonly used value functions, including those belonging to power families (Armington 1969), satisfy this assumption.

Assumption 1. $v _ { i j } ( 0 ) = 0 , ~ v _ { i j } ^ { \prime } ( 0 ) = \infty , ~ v _ { i j } ^ { \prime } ( t ) > 0 ,$ $v _ { i j } ^ { \prime \prime } ( t ) < 0 ,$ and $v _ { i j } ^ { \prime \prime } ( t )$ is continuous.

In §4, we assume that $\theta _ { j } \ge 1 , \ \forall j ,$ i.e., consumer type 2 gets an equal or higher value from every service. This is consistent with consumer type 2 having a substantially higher income (see Tirole 1988, Chapter 3, p. 143). Such uniform ordering thus reflects heterogeneity in income. In a later section, §5, we look at the other scenario, in which consumer type 1 gets higher values from some services while consumer type 2 gets equal or higher values from the rest. ## 3.1. First-Degree Discrimination

First-degree discrimination refers to the scenario in which the carrier charges each consumer his or her reservation price. Such pricing is not practical. The carrier may not know up front who is type 1 and who is type 2. Moreover, even if it knows the type of each consumer, it may not be able to prevent one type from buying the plan designed for the other type. However, the solution to the first-degree discrimination problem provides us with a benchmark. It tells us the maximum profit that the carrier can possibly make if it has full information regarding consumer types and the means necessary to restrict each type to the plan designed for it. Because the carrier can extract the entire surplus with first-degree discrimination, the prices are equal to the value created and the consumer surplus is zero regardless of the pricing regime.

We denote the optimal solution to the carrier’s problem for traffic pricing by $( p _ { i } ^ { F B } , t _ { i } ^ { F B } )$ and that for the service pricing (for service j) by $( \dot { p } _ { i j } ^ { F B } , t _ { i j } ^ { F B } )$ , for $i = 1$ and 2. In other words, in the case of traffic pricing the carrier designs two plans, one for each consumer type. In the case of service pricing, it designs two plans for each service.

To investigate the traffic pricing problem, we need to first consider the reservation price for traffic $v _ { i } ( t )$ the value a type i consumer gets by dividing t bytes of traffic optimally between the n services. For a type i consumer, this decision problem thus involves choosing his allocation for service $j ,$ denoted $t _ { i j } \geq 0 , \forall j ,$ , in a way that the total value, $\textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n ^ { \prime } } v _ { i j } ( t _ { i j } )$ , is maximized:

$$
v _ {i} (t) = \max _ {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i j} \leq t} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} v _ {i j} (t _ {i j}).
$$

We denote the solution to the consumer type $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ problem by $( t _ { i j } ^ { * } ( t ) , j \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , n \} ) ;$ ; i.e., $t _ { i j } ^ { * } ( t )$ is the traffic that a consumer of type i allocates to service j when he divides t units of traffic optimally between the n services. This solution satisfies the two conditions mentioned below as (1): the first one (which is essentially n − 1 equations expressed as one) says that the marginal value obtained from any service is the same at optimality; the second one says that it is optimal to use all t bytes because all $v _ { i j } ( t )$ functions are strictly increasing:

$$
\begin{array}{c} v _ {i j} ^ {\prime} (t _ {i j} ^ {*} (t)) = v _ {i k} ^ {\prime} (t _ {i k} ^ {*} (t)), \quad \forall   j, k \in \{1, 2, \ldots , n \}, \\ \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} t _ {i j} ^ {*} (t) = t. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

<sup>Lemma</sup> <sup>1.</sup> A type i consumer’s reservation price $f o r$ traffic, $v _ { i } ( t )$ , is continuous. It is also differentiable with respect to t with a continuous first derivative, as well as increasing and strictly concave in t. Additionally

$$
\theta_ {1} <   \frac {v _ {2} ^ {\prime} (t)}{v _ {1} ^ {\prime} (t)} <   \theta_ {n} \quad a n d \quad \theta_ {1} <   \frac {v _ {2} (t)}{v _ {1} (t)} <   \theta_ {n}.
$$

It follows from Lemma 1 that $v _ { i } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { i } ^ { F B } ) = v _ { i j } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { i j } ^ { F B } ) =$ $v _ { i j } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { i j } ^ { * } ( t _ { i } ^ { F B } ) ) = c$ (see the proof in the appendix for details). A consequence is that, when consumers are completely homogeneous with respect to their valuations, the two pricing regimes—service pricing and traffic pricing—lead to identical usage levels and profits. Mathematically

$$
t _ {i j} ^ {*} (t _ {i} ^ {F B}) = t _ {i j} ^ {F B}, \quad p _ {i} ^ {F B} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} p _ {i j} ^ {F B}.\tag{2}
$$

## 4. Model with Uniform Ordering

In this section, we consider the case in which preferences of the two consumer types are uniformly (or identically) ordered across services, i.e., $\theta _ { j } \geq 1 , \forall j \in$ $\{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , { \dot { n } } \}$ . In a later section, §5, we analyze the case in which this ordering is not uniform.

## 4.1. Traffic Pricing

In the case of traffic pricing, the carrier offers a single schedule for pricing traffic. The schedule consists of two plans, $( p _ { 1 } , t _ { 1 } )$ and $( p _ { 2 } , t _ { 2 } )$ , one for each of the two types of consumers. Consumers self-select from this menu $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the schedule) and then decide on their traffic allocations optimally. Because each consumer self-selects, their choices must satisfy the following constraints.

Individual rationality (IR). A consumer purchases a plan that offers nonnegative utility:

$$
v _ {1} (t _ {1}) \geq p _ {1},\tag{IR1}
$$

$$
v _ {2} (t _ {2}) \geq p _ {2}.\tag{IR2}
$$

Incentive compatibility (IC). A consumer picks a plan that generates the highest surplus:

$$
v _ {1} (t _ {1}) - p _ {1} \geq v _ {1} (t _ {2}) - p _ {2},\tag{IC1}
$$

$$
v _ {2} (t _ {2}) - p _ {2} \geq v _ {2} (t _ {1}) - p _ {1}.\tag{IC2}
$$

The firm’s problem is to maximize its profit subject to the IR and the IC constraints. The firm’s profit, with the plans selected as posited, is

$$
(1 - f) (p _ {1} - c t _ {1}) + f (p _ {2} - c t _ {2}).
$$

Because $\theta _ { j } \ge 1 \ \forall j$ in this scenario, it must be true that $v _ { 2 } ^ { \prime } ( t ) > ^ { \prime } v _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } ( t )$ and $v _ { 2 } ( t ) > v _ { 1 } ( t )$ (by Lemma 1). In other words, type 2 consumers value traffic more than type 1 consumers do, i.e., type 2 is the high type and type 1 is the low type. In a market with a high type and a low type, one of the following three solutions is possible (Mussa and Rosen 1978): no one is served, only the high type consumer is served, or both types of consumers are served. When only the high type consumer is served, the firm extracts all surpluses, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the price equals the value created for the high type. When both consumer types are served, the carrier’s profit is maximized when the low type consumer is indifferent between purchasing and not purchasing and the high type consumer is indifferent between purchasing the plan aimed at the low type consumer and its own plan. Hence, the IR1 and IC2 constraints are binding. Using the IR1 and IC2 constraints to substitute for $p _ { i }$ in terms of $v _ { i } ,$ for each $i = 1 , 2 ,$ we reduce the firm’s problem to

$$
\max _ {t _ {1}, t _ {2} \geq 0} (1 - f) (v _ {1} (t _ {1}) - c t _ {1}) + f (v _ {2} (t _ {2}) - v _ {2} (t _ {1}) + v _ {1} (t _ {1}) - c t _ {2}).
$$

Therefore,

$$
v _ {2} ^ {\prime} (t _ {2} ^ {S B}) = c.\tag{3}
$$

It follows from the discussion in §3.1 that $t _ { 2 } ^ { S B } = t _ { 2 } ^ { F B } =$ $\textstyle \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } t _ { 2 j } ^ { F B }$ . The derivative of the profit function with respect to $t _ { 1 }$ is as follows:

$$
- f (v _ {2} ^ {\prime} (t _ {1}) - v _ {1} ^ {\prime} (t _ {1})) + (1 - f) (v _ {1} ^ {\prime} (t _ {1}) - c).\tag{4}
$$

When $t _ { 1 } \geq t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } , v _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { 1 } ) \leq c$ and (4) is negative (because, by Lemma 1, $v _ { 2 } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { 1 } ) > v _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } ( t _ { 1 } )$ at all $t _ { 1 } > 0 )$ Therefore, $t _ { 1 } ^ { S B } < t _ { 1 } ^ { F B }$

It is possible that $t _ { 1 } ^ { S B } = 0$ . ## 4.3. Traffic Pricing vs. Service Pricing

Traffic pricing is analogous to selling a buffet consisting of all services. With traffic pricing, type 1 consumers either pay for and use all services, or use none, when they would have preferred to pay for and use only a subset of them. Type 1 consumers’ inadequate willingness to pay for traffic causes the carrier to forgo them too often when it uses traffic pricing. When type 1 consumers get dropped, it adversely impacts the surplus that goes to type 2 consumers. We summarize this important result as our first theorem.

<sup>Theorem</sup> <sup>1.</sup> In the case of uniform ordering, there exists a $\theta ^ { * }$ between $\theta _ { 1 }$ and $\theta _ { n }$ such that, if $1 / \theta ^ { * } \leq f <$ $1 / \theta _ { 1 } ,$ one consumer class (type 1 or the low type) does not get served under traffic pricing though it gets served one or more services under service pricing. Consequently, the consumer surplus is higher under service pricing than it is under traffic pricing at values of f satisfying $f \in$ $[ 1 / \theta ^ { * } , 1 / \theta _ { 1 } )$

Under service pricing, at values of $f$ satisfying $f \in [ 1 / \theta ^ { * } , 1 / \theta _ { 1 } )$ , type 1 consumers use services whose  parameters are smaller than $\theta ^ { * }$ . However, with traffic pricing, they prefer forgoing these services to paying for a “buffet” that consists of all services. Their lack of willingness to pay for the buffet causes the carrier to drop them. This shut out of type 1 eliminates type 2’s surplus under traffic pricing even though type 2 gets a positive surplus under service pricing in the same region.

Though we considered only two consumer types, our insights extend to situations where there are more than two types; imposing traffic pricing on every carrier is analogous to requiring every restaurant in a region to sell buffets only. Consumers interested in only a few food items would likely forgo expensive buffets comprising items of their interest as well as several others, just as type 1 forgoes all services when $f \in [ 1 / \theta ^ { * } , 1 / \theta _ { 1 } )$ . The resulting contraction in the market coverage would, in turn, adversely affect those not forgoing usage entirely, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , type 2. The main intuition is thus that, when a portion of the market is forgone, consumer welfare can dwindle. We further elaborate on this welfare issue in the discussion following Corollary 1.

One possible way to interpret Theorem 1 in the context of wireless services would be to ask what would happen if the carrier is prevented by the FCC or the U.S. Congress to practice service pricing. The carrier would be limited to plans that include everything. Such plans that include everything—voice calls, text messaging, and data—are expected to cost \$70 or more per line and \$130 or more per family, significantly more than those offering only voice calls (MyRatePlan 2011). All-you-can-eat plans, which do not impose usage limits, are expected to cost even more. Many consumers, who use their phones mainly for voice calls, would perhaps balk at purchasing such expensive plans, potentially leading to a lower market coverage, which is exactly what Theorem 1 points out in addition to establishing possible unintended consequences such as lower consumer welfare.

<sup>Corollary</sup> <sup>1.</sup> In the case of uniform ordering, the social surplus is higher under service pricing than it is under traffic pricing at values of f satisfying $f \in$ $[ 1 / \theta ^ { * } , 1 / \theta _ { 1 } )$

The existing literature on second-degree price discrimination also identifies instances in which discrimination is preferable (to uniform pricing across consumer classes) from the social planner’s viewpoint (Katz 1983, Roberts 1979, Spence 1977). Similarly, the literature on third-degree discrimination also finds instances in which discrimination across different consumer markets leads to a higher social surplus (Schmalensee 1981, Varian 1985). In this sense, our findings echo earlier findings. What is new here is the form of discrimination that we examine. We are examining discrimination across services $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ calling, messaging, etc.) that use the same resource $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ traffic). Though our setting is different, we get the same surprising results, such as that discrimination can have economic benefits. The intuition behind Corollary 1 is indeed similar to what one finds in the existing literature: when discrimination is not possible, the carrier feels constrained, and, in response, it prices its products in a way that causes certain consumer segments to forgo them. Consequently, the social surplus that could be generated by serving those consumers is no longer obtained. As already explained in $\ S 2 ,$ despite some overlap with earlier research, this result differs significantly from more recent research on quasibundling (Wu et al. 2008).

To sum up, Theorem 1 shows that traffic pricing could lower market coverage. Corollary 1 shows that the reduced market coverage, in turn, would lead to a lower welfare. Proponents of neutral pricing often overlook this unwarranted impact (Wu 2007, Mossberg 2007) and criticize the FCC for not ensuring wireless net neutrality (Miller 2010). Our research indicates that the $\mathrm { F C C ^ { \prime } s }$ recent ruling that preserves service pricing (FCC 2010) has merits. Forcing the carrier to sell “neutral” plans that include everything would cause some consumers to sit out, leading to a lower profit for the carrier and a lower surplus for consumers. Evidently, the social surplus would go down as well.

## 5.1. Traffic Pricing vs. Service Pricing

We now present a comparison of the two regimes, starting with the case of effectively separate markets. In this case, the carrier’s profit under traffic pricing equals that under first-degree discrimination, implying that traffic pricing leads to a higher profit when compared to service pricing at all values of $f .$ Further, the consumer surplus is zero under traffic pricing because both IR constraints bind. In short, traffic pricing surprisingly helps the carrier while it hurts consumers. We state this important finding as our next theorem.

<sup>Theorem</sup> <sup>2.</sup> In the case of effectively separate markets of nonuniform ordering, at all values of $f \in ( 0 , 1 )$ , traffic pricing leads to a higher profit and an identical or lower consumer surplus when compared to service pricing.

Example 3 points out that the two reservation prices for traffic of the two consumer types can cross even though their reservation prices for any service do not cross. The implication is that, traffic pricing can cause IR1 and IR2 to bind, although, under service pricing, the IC constraint of the high type and the IR constraint of the low type bind for each service. In Example 3, IR1 and IC2 bind for service 2 under service pricing, and IR2 and IC1 bind for service 1; thus, the usage levels of type 1 and type 2 are linked when both types are served. However, under traffic pricing, this link disappears as the IC constraints become nonbinding, making it possible for the carrier to serve each type its first-best plan and to absorb the entire surplus. An important implication is, therefore, that an efficient equilibrium outcome $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the first-best social surplus) is attained under traffic pricing even though the same is not possible under service pricing.

The existing literature shows that price discrimination always results in a higher profit when compared to the case of uniform or nondiscriminatory pricing (Katz 1983, Roberts 1979, Schmalensee 1981, Spence 1977, Varian 1985). However, here we find that the case of discrimination across services is different. The reason is that traffic pricing is essentially quasibundling all services as traffic. The difference between bundling and traffic pricing is that, in the case of the latter, the seller (carrier) does not play any role in determining the bundle composition. Each consumer class picks a different bundle composition based on its preferences. As Theorem 2 shows, it is possible that one consumer class gets a higher value from one composition while the other gets a higher value from another, making it optimal for the carrier to charge each class its full reservation price for the composition it values more.

## 5.2. Pure Bundling vs. Quasi-Bundling

It is indeed surprising that service pricing allows the carrier more flexibility with regard to how it prices various services but still leads to a lower profit when consumer preferences exhibit an inverse relationship. One ought to ask whether the carrier could leverage the inverse relationship by designing a bundle of services, say $\big \{ t _ { 1 } ^ { B } , t _ { 2 } ^ { B } , \dots , t _ { n } ^ { \hat { B } } \big \} _ { . }$ , and offering it at a price of $p ^ { B }$ to both consumer types.

The optimal pure bundle is obtained by solving the following problem:

$$
\max \left\{p ^ {B} - c \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} t _ {j} ^ {B} \right\},
$$

subject to t<sup>B</sup> ≥ 0 ∀ j1 p<sup>B</sup> ≥ 01 and

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} v _ {1 j} (t _ {j} ^ {B}) \geq p ^ {B},\tag{IR1}
$$

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} v _ {2 j} (t _ {j} ^ {B}) \geq p ^ {B}.\tag{IR2}
$$

To obtain an upper bound for the optimal profit under pure bundling, we first drop the IR2 constraint and solve. If IR2 is dropped, the maximum occurs when IR1 becomes binding and the maximum is

$$
\max \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} (v _ {1 j} (t _ {j} ^ {B}) - c t _ {j} ^ {B}) = v _ {1} (t _ {1} ^ {F B}) - c t _ {1} ^ {F B}.
$$

Similarly, by dropping IR1 and solving we can get another bound, which is

$$
\max \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} (v _ {2 j} (t _ {j} ^ {B}) - c t _ {j} ^ {B}) = v _ {2} (t _ {2} ^ {F B}) - c t _ {2} ^ {F B}.
$$

So the pure-bundling profit is at most min $( v _ { 1 } ( t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } ) -$ $c t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } , v _ { 2 } ( t _ { 2 } ^ { \bar { F } B } ) - c t _ { 2 } ^ { F B } )$ , which is less than the profit the carrier gets with quasi-bundling in the effectively separate case of nonuniform ordering. In the case of uniform ordering and also in the traditional case of nonuniform ordering (in which type 2 is the high type), the carrier can make at least as much in profits with quasi-bundling by offering the same traffic pricing plan $( p = v _ { 1 } ( t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } ) , \ i = t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } )$ to everyone. Therefore, quasi-bundling dominates pure bundling from the seller’s viewpoint.

## 6. Conclusion

We examine the economic impact of service pricing on the market for wireless services assuming a monopoly scenario. We consider a market with two consumer types, who may or may not be uniformly ordered with regard to their preferences for various services. We analyze how various surpluses behave when the carrier practices second-degree price discrimination using nonlinear price schedules. When the carrier uses service pricing, it announces a separate schedule for each service. When it chooses to not discriminate across services and uses traffic pricing, it offers exactly one schedule to price traffic.

Service pricing is commonly expected to lower consumer and social welfare. It is also expected to lead to a higher profit. We find that these expectations are often untrue and that the impact of discrimination is far more complex than often argued. Specifically, we show that the nature of consumer heterogeneity and relative sizes of different market segments play a pivotal role in determining which regime leads to a higher surplus.

Intriguingly, we find that, when the ordering of the two consumer types is uniform across services, the expectation that discrimination harms consumers is true only in situations that involve a few high type consumers and a large number of low type consumers. If the carrier is forced to use traffic pricing in a market with a substantially large fraction of the high type, lacking the ability to prevent low type consumers from using a subset of services, the carrier drops them completely. Dropping low type consumers, who can be profitably served some services under service pricing, leads to a lower surplus for high type consumers. A similar phenomenon can also occur when consumers have idiosyncratic preferences for different services, i.e., when their ordering is not uniform across services. The lesson, therefore, is that discrimination allows the carrier greater flexibility with regard to how it serves different market segments, which, in some cases, translates into gains for consumers.

Another insight that emerges from our study is that the common wisdom that discrimination is always beneficial for the carrier holds only in the case of uniform ordering. It does not hold in the case of nonuniform ordering. We find that, in the case of nonuniform ordering, traffic pricing may even lead to the first-best solution. Such a surprising situation occurs when the reservation prices for traffic of the two consumer types cross in a way that each consumer type gets a negative utility from the other type’s first-best plan. Therefore, each type prefers to stick to its first-best plan. In this situation, the carrier makes the maximum possible profit, and consumers end up with a zero surplus. Even when one consumer type gets a positive utility from the other type’s firstbest plan, we show that it is possible for the carrier to make more profits with traffic pricing by using it as an effective quasi-bundling strategy. We also show that such quasi-bundling is particularly powerful in the sense that it dominates pure bundling from the carrier’s viewpoint.

Also noteworthy is the finding that service pricing is not always detrimental to social welfare, as often believed. We show that service pricing can benefit the carrier and consumers at the same time—specifically, when the carrier forgoes a consumer segment under traffic pricing and lowers its output, it also eliminates the other segment’s surplus and lowers its own profit. In doing so, we establish that the implications of service pricing go beyond the usual argument of how it allocates a bigger portion of the surplus to the carrier. This usual “zero-sum” argument fails to recognize that service pricing, which offers the carrier more flexibilities, can improve the market coverage and output in certain situations, leading to a higher surplus for everyone.

The most salient contribution of this paper is thus that it explains why banning service pricing may not benefit consumers of wireless services as expected. Further, it shows that disrupting such discrimination does not always lead to a lower profit, or even a higher social surplus. All these findings have critical implications: consumers need to understand the circumstances in which service pricing may work in their favor before they extend unqualified support for a ban on discrimination; likewise, policy makers need to consider the likely negative impacts a ban on discrimination may produce, including the most unexpected impact of a lower social surplus. In this respect, our paper not only contributes to the ongoing debate on discrimination across services but it also sets the stage for further research on related debates, including the broader debate on net neutrality.

One possible direction for future research would be examining a model that considers third-party services and pricing issues that they present. Consider Skype Mobile<sup>™</sup> offered by Verizon for example: calls placed from Skype Mobile to phone numbers in the United States are billed separately by Verizon. For international calls, Verizon does not charge anything extra, but Skype requires that consumers sign up for a monthly plan. Skype-to-Skype calls, on the other hand, do not attract any additional charges. This complexity aside, third-parties often negotiate with carriers on revenue sharing. Another possible direction would be to investigate related issues that we have not considered in this work, such as substitutability and complementarity between services, which is relevant because services such as Skype are substitutes for voice and text messaging services offered by the wireless carriers.

A negative derivative, in turn, implies that if the optimal $t _ { 1 }$ is in the interval $( t _ { L } , t _ { R } )$ for a given $f ,$ then it would also be so at smaller values of $f .$ Now, note that, at $f = 0 , t _ { 1 } ^ { S B } = t _ { 1 } ^ { F B } \in ( t _ { L } , t _ { R } ) .$ , and that $t _ { 1 } = t _ { X }$ is strictly inferior to $t _ { 1 } = t _ { 1 } ^ { F B }$ . On the other hand, at $f = 1 ,$ the optimal $t _ { 1 }$ must be $t _ { X } ,$ because any choice in $( t _ { L } , t _ { R } )$ is strictly suboptimal. Therefore, there exists a threshold $f ^ { \ast } \in$ $( 0 , 1 )$ such that, at optimality, $t _ { 1 } \in ( t _ { L } , t _ { R } )$ if $f < f ^ { * }$ while it is $t _ { X } \mathrm { ~ i f ~ } f \geq f ^ { * }$ . Moving on to the consumer surplus, if $f > f ^ { * } ,$ , the consumer surplus is zero under traffic pricing (because type 2 does not get any surplus from type 1’s plan). The consumer surplus in the case of service pricing is always zero or higher, implying that service pricing leads to an equal or higher consumer surplus.
