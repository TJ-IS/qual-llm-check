## Abstract

Despite extensive research and practitioner literature on inter-organizational information systems (IOIS), our understanding of non-adoption still has some under-explored issues. This paper offers an explanation of non-adoption that focuses on the potential structural changes that IOIS adoption entails; in particular, we contend that the contradictions between the initial social structure and the structure enacted in the use of an IOIS can be a cause of nonadoption. This paper analyses adoption from a logic of opposition where nonadoption occurs because forces of transformation are offset by forces of persistence. Relying on the concepts of social structure, duality of structure, and structural contradiction from Structuration theory, we develop a framework that categorizes the underlying social structure where an IOIS is to be adopted, and formulate some theoretical propositions. From a practitioner perspective, this paper contends that the fact that IOIS management uncovers the contradictions in the social structure that result from IOIS adoption can help avoid dead ends. Accordingly, this framework, which can be used as a coarsegrained picture for anticipating adoption problems, can inform managers when devising an IOIS implementation strategy. ## Introduction

The proliferation of inter-organizational business data and process standardization initiatives (e.g. CIDX, RosettaNet, ebXML) and the subsequent implementation of inter-organizational information systems (IOIS) (e.g. Elemica, E2open, Inttra) poses many challenges to IOIS management in terms of adoption and use (Markus et al., 2006; Boh et al., 2007). Despite the significant number of theoretical frameworks in the IOIS literature that aim to provide explanations for adoption (Robey et al., 2008), some issues still remain under-explored; in particular, the potential changes in an institutional context that result from IOIS adoption.

We support this statement with the case of PortIC (http://www.portic .net), an IOIS that was set up by private agents – that is shipping agents (SAs), inland terminals (ITs), clearing agents, haulers (H), freight forwarders (FFs), etc. – and public agents – Port Authority and Customs – in the Port of Barcelona in mid-1999. PortIC provided two main interorganizational services. By mid-2001, a year after both services were launched, one system had been widely adopted by the various port agents, whereas the usage rate for the other system was far from satisfactory. However, both services had been developed by the same project team; both went live at the same time; adopters received similar training; some of the adopters were the same for both services; and both services were run under the same Internet-based technologies. Moreover, the core functional requirements for both services were based on a subset of the United Nations Directories for Electronic Data Interchange for Administration, Commerce and Transport (UN/EDIFACT) message standard, which was defined by a standardization consortium where all of the port agents – potential adopters – were represented. In short, even though IOIS management adopted the same strategies for the implementation of both inter-organizational services, and despite both services being run in apparently the same context, adoption of both services unfolded differently.

These differing results led to the following question: why does applying the same implementation strategy in apparently similar circumstances and with the same potential adopters not lead to similar adoption outcomes? The term implementation strategy refers to the set of management actions and decisions that govern or influence how the implementation effort is executed. To address this question we draw upon the concepts of social structure, duality of structure, and structural contradictions from Structuration theory (ST) (Giddens, 1984), and focus on the changes in the social structure that arise from IOIS adoption. From this perspective, we develop a categorization of IOIS based on the underlying social structure that can be used broadly to anticipate adoption problems, and accordingly, it can inform managers when devising IOIS implementation strategies.

This paper is organized as follows. We begin by reviewing prior IOIS research and search for studies that have dealt with our research problem; and we highlight several issues not addressed by the extant literature. This is followed by the presentation of our theoretical lens and the development of our theoretical framework. We then present a case that illustrates our theoretical arguments and addresses the research question, and then analyze the case results through the lens of our framework. Finally, we conclude with contributions and further research.

## Structure and adoption of IOIS

In this paper, we focus on IOIS that are set up, used, and controlled by organizations in the same sector. These organizations have standardized and agreed upon rules (i.e. the meaning of terms, procedures, sanctions) that govern their cross-company interactions. These IOIS are the result of a collective action and are embedded in a core business process (Markus et al., 2006).

The mainstream literature on IOIS adoption has proposed a number of predictive models for adoption (Chwelos et al., 2001; Zhu et al., 2003; MacKay et al., 2004; Gengatharen & Standing, 2005; Quaddus & Hofmeyer, 2007). These studies rely primarily upon the theory of diffusion of innovations and related theories of technology acceptance to suggest sets of factors that are expected to predict adoption. Although these studies develop variance models that have proven successful in explaining adoption, we contend that they pay insufficient attention to two aspects of IOIS adoption. First, these models focus on individual or organizational levels when depicting IOIS adoption, overlooking other levels of analysis – such as community and industry (Johnston & Gregor, 2000). However, IOIS usually underpin embedded inter-firm relationships, and therefore, adoption is not only an act of independent adopters, but also a coordinated act by multiple actors (Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001). Accordingly, the study of IOIS adoption at higher levels of analysis – such as community or industry levels – complements prior studies and enhances our understanding of adoption.

Second, mainstream variance literature employs a logic of determination in which adoption is regarded as the consequence of variation in a set of factors. We contend that because an IOIS may transform the social structure of the inter-organizational context – for instance, by changing data and procedures or shifting the balance of power (Webster, 1995; Markus et al., 2006) – so certain contradictions between aspects of the old and the new social structure may emerge. Those contradictions may prompt potential adopters not to use IOIS. The study of those contradictions in IOIS adoption requires a logic of opposition (Robey & Boudreau, 1999) rather than a logic of determination. From a logic of opposition perspective, non-adoption occurs because forces of transformation are offset by forces of persistence. As such, prior variance literature that draws upon diffusion of innovations and technology acceptance models is insufficiently supportive in terms of anticipating the contradictions that may trigger non-adoption. It may be possible to anticipate such contradictions if we are able to characterize beforehand the social structure of the inter-organizational context before and after the IOIS is adopted.

As part of our search for a way to characterize the institutional context in which an IOIS is adopted, we turn to another group of studies that have proposed taxonomies aimed at characterizing IOIS and supporting adoption. For instance, Kumar & van Dissel (1996) categorize IOIS according to the level of interdependency facilitated: pooled interdependency; sequential interdependency; and reciprocal interdependency. The authors argue that as firms may be involved in more than one type of IOIS, a particular IOIS may exhibit characteristics of more than one type of interdependency. With this taxonomy, Kumar & van Dissel (1996) recognize that inter-organizational structures shape management choices during IOIS design and implementation.

Choudhury (1997) proposes a taxonomy of three different types of IOIS (electronic monopolies, electronic dyads, and multilateral IOIS), and suggests that demand uncertainty (volume and technological) and market variability (fragmentation and volatility) influence a firm’s choice of IOIS. The author states that a firm may decide to develop an IOIS competitively or cooperatively, based on the strategic significance of the IOIS and the size and bargaining power of the firm initiating the IOIS (Choudhury, 1997).

Hong (2002) proposes four basic types of IOIS based on two basic dimensions: linkage (horizontal and vertical) between the different roles of adopters, and support (strategic and operational) provided by the IOIS. Hong (2002) argues that different types of IOIS need to be linked to specific business strategies: growth, differentiation, focus, and cost leadership. Boonstra & de Vries (2005) categorize IOIS based on the interests of the adopters (as perceived contribution) and power (the capacity to exert will over others in order to realize certain intended benefits). They propose three types of IOIS (unlikely, unbalanced, and balanced), and suggest that an IOIS may fail when management misunderstands or ignores the differences in interests and power between the parties.

Finally, Damsgaard & Truex (2000) propose a taxonomy for IOIS usage – binary trading relationships, market-like transactions, and hub-and-spoke model – based on the relationships between actors. The authors argue that ‘it is imperative to take these different classes of relationships into account because the differences have profound implications for the adoption unit and its use of EDI standards’ (p. 178).

These IOIS taxonomies support management in anticipating the potential adoption problems by focusing on aspects of the institutional context – that is, the type of relationship that exists between adopters, and the bargaining power of adopters. However, they do not consider potential changes in the social structure (which includes the systems of meanings, procedures, control and coordination mechanisms, reward structures, or power relations) that may entail the adoption of an IOIS, and the influence of that change on the management of an IOIS.

Several IOIS studies have acknowledged that adoption and use can reproduce the social structure where it is adopted – as well as challenge and transform the structure (Kambil & van Heck, 1998; Barrett, 1999). For instance, we increasingly observe industry initiatives aimed at spreading the use of IOIS beyond dyadic relationships (Boh et al., 2007). To achieve this goal these initiatives have to standardize the syntax and semantics of data, as well as the pragmatics of business processes among industry actors which, in turn, may result in the delegation of power to some of the agents to the detriment of others (Webster, 1995; Gerst & Bunduchi, 2005). If the social structure enacted in the adoption and use of these IOIS is inconsistent with the pre-existing structure and adopters have the capacity to do otherwise, then they will likely refuse to use the IOIS, or use it in only a perfunctory manner. Despite the empirical observations that support the contention that IOIS adoption may entail the transformation of the social structure, to our knowledge, no prior effort has apparently been undertaken in the IOIS literature to study the relationship between these transformations and adoption. To address this gap we draw upon ST (Giddens, 1984), which is regarded as an appropriate lens for examining change associated with the adoption of information systems (IS, Chu & Smithson, 2007; Jones & Karsten, 2008).

## Theoretical framework

## Structuration theory

ST is a general theory of social organization, developed by Anthony Giddens, which seeks to transcend the traditional dualism in sociology. There is a research stream that overemphasizes the influence of structure on human agency and leaves the development of structure unexplained. Other research streams put too great an emphasis on agency, thus omitting the influence of social institutions on behaviour and action. In solving this dualism, Giddens (1984) conceptualizes social practice as the mediation between structure and agency, and develops the core concept of ST or the ‘duality of structure’: ‘The structural properties of social systems are both medium and outcomes of the practices they recursively organize’ (Giddens, 1984, p. 25). According to ST, a social system can be understood as structure and interaction. Structure is constituted by ‘rules and resources, recursively implicated in the reproduction of social systems. Structure exists only as memory traces, the organic basis of human knowledgeability, and as instantiated in action’ (Giddens, 1984, p. 377).

Rules refer to ‘techniques or generalizable procedures applied in the enactment/reproduction of social practices’ (Ibid, p. 21). Giddens distinguishes between formulated and non-formulated rules. The former are those which can be verbalized or codified, for instance, conceptual schemes or laws. The latter, which constitute the bulk of rules governing social life, refer to those rules that have not been expressed verbally or in a written format; but are nevertheless enacted by actors in the interaction. For analytical purposes, Giddens splits the ‘rules’ into two dimensions: structure of signification, which relates to the constitution of meaning; and structure of legitimation, which refers to the sanctioning models of social conduct.

Resources, which constitute the structure of domination, are ‘fundamental to the conceptualisation of power (Giddens, 1984, p. 28), and can be authoritative and allocative. ‘Allocative resources refer to capabilitiesy generating command over objects, goods or material phenomena. Authoritative resources refer to types of transformative capacity generating command over persons or actors’ (Giddens, 1984, p. 33). On the other hand, interaction – which can be communication, power, and sanctions – is the activity instantiated by the agent acting within the social system. Social systems are produced and reproduced by actors drawing on structure (rules and resources) in their interaction.

Two other key concepts of ST are those of conflict (a property of interaction) and contradiction (a structural quality). Structural contradiction is the ‘disjunction of structural principles of system organization’ while conflict is ‘struggle between actors or collectives expressed as definite social practices’ (Giddens, 1984, p. 198). Conflict occurs due to the existence of contradictions of structure that negatively affect the various actors. In other words, conflict is the action that agents may develop from a particular contradiction.

## IOIS adoption and structural configurations

Through the lens of ST, IOIS adoption is not independent of the social structure where it occurs. The social structure enables and/or constrains IOIS adoption; that is, the rules and resources are the media of IOIS adoption. Conversely, IOIS adoption and repetitive use reproduces and/or changes the social structure.

When the social structure enacted in the adoption of an IOIS mirrors the social structure as it was before, then there are no structural contradictions and IOIS adoption will likely reproduce the social structure. In such cases, adoption will not generate much conflict as the rules and resources of the social system go unchallenged. On the other hand, when the social structure enacted in the adoption of an IOIS contradicts or differs from the initial structure (for instance, message fields change their meaning, or changes are made in the flow of activities of a process) and those changes harm the interests of adopters (for instance, more time is required to fill in certain information and execute a process, or misunderstandings arise in the interpretation of information which causes errors, or less control is held over a process) then conflicts are more likely and adopters will refuse further use of the IOIS. Therefore, considering that the contradictions between the existing social structure and the structure enacted in the use of an IOIS are a source of non-adoption, we propose to study structural contradictions by thinking of social structure in terms of ideal types. We have developed a framework that categorizes the underlying social structure where an IOIS is to be adopted (see Figure 1).

The first dimension in the framework are the rules, either constitutive of meaning or normative sanctions, that firms draw upon in their interactions (Giddens, 1984). The first type of rule corresponds to modes of signifying and refers to how the meaning and purpose are attributed to everyday activity – namely, the syntax and semantics of data and processes. These rules provide guidelines that actors use to define and make sense of actions and of the context in which they are operating. The second type of rule defines appropriate behaviour in particular circumstances, and hence these define the organizationally sanctioned way of executing work – in other words, the pragmatics of processes. We conceive the rules as being either standardized or non-standardized for all potential adopters of an IOIS. Rules are standardized when, in their interactions, all the organizations of an inter-organizational setting enact a common set of rules. The fact that rules are standardized in the inter-organizational setting means that they can be precisely and explicitly formulated and embedded in an IOIS (Reimers, 2001; Nelson et al., 2005). On the other hand, rules are non-standardized when they are not common to all the organizations in the inter-organizational context. Therefore, there is no one way to deal with interactions between organizations. This is the case when, for instance, each dyad in a supply chain has its own rules for governing interactions.

The second dimension in the framework is that of resources, either authoritative or allocative, through which the actors enact power (Giddens, 1984). IOIS research has long acknowledged the salient role of power as an antecedent to adoption and operationalized power with many variables – for example, dependence on trading partner, competitive pressure, industry pressure, importance of partner, enacted trading partner power (Hart & Saunders. 1997: Premkumar et al., 1997: Chwelos et al., 2001; Quaddus & Hofmeyer, 2007). ST views power as an elemental concept of social life, ‘directly implied in human action’ (Giddens 1984, p. 283). All social interaction involves the use of power, hence accounts of IS phenomena ‘need to give particular attention to the operation of power relationships’ (Jones & Karsten 2008, p. 135). ST defines power as the capacity of agents to achieve outcomes, and those agents draw upon authoritative and allocative resources when they exercise power: ‘Resources are the media through which power is exercised, and structures of domination reproduced’ (Giddens, 1979, p. 91). Accordingly, we label the second dimension in Figure 1 as distribution of resources. We conceive the distribution of resources as being either symmetric or asymmetric. In this paper, the fact that resources are symmetrically distributed means that there is not an organization that can exercise power over others to influence their adoption of the IOIS. On the other hand, when resources are asymmetrically distributed, there are organizations that have the capacity to exert influence over the behaviour of others and so influence the adoption of an IOIS.

Each cell of the framework represents an ideal type of social structure that we call ‘IOIS structural configuration’. We can distinguish four possible structural configurations, which we label for mnemonic purposes as ‘market’ (standardized rules and symmetric distribution of resources), ‘hierarchy’ (standardized rules and asymmetric distribution of resources), ‘hub’ (non-standardized rules and asymmetric distribution of resources), and ‘net’ (nonstandardized rules and symmetric distribution of resources). Graphically, the black circles represent firms (potential IOIS adopters). Equal size means symmetry of resources; the black lines represent the prevailing interaction between these firms; and the grey circles represent the set of rules that firms draw upon in their interaction. In the ‘market’ and ‘hierarchy’ configurations, the interactions between firms pass through the common set of rules governing interactions. In the ‘net’ and ‘hub’ configurations, the rules are not common and unique for all the adopters, but are standardized for a subset of firms; for instance, a dyad.

In a ‘market’ configuration, resources through which organizations in the setting can exercise power over others are equally distributed, and rules of meaning and norms are standardized. In economics a market usually connotes competition, although in our framework the inter-firm relationships in a ‘market’ can be competitive as well as cooperative. An example in the IOIS literature is the study of the Dutch flower market carried out by Kambil & van Heck (1998). The authors show how the introduction of ICT in the auction method, which has always been governed by common and public rules, not only creates efficiencies in terms of product search, quality control, and logistics; but also balances the distribution of resources between sellers (growers) and buyers by providing ‘dispute resolution mechanisms that mitigate against opportunism risk encountered by buyers and sellers’ (p. 8).

In a ‘hierarchy’ configuration, the rules are standardized for all the adopters, and the distribution of resources is asymmetric, and so an organization exists that can exercise power over others; for instance, an organization can force others to adopt certain rules that are embedded in the IOIS. Although the ‘hierarchy configuration is common within organizations, it also occurs in an inter-organizational context. This is the case when a dominant player imposes a common set of rules to govern his interactions with trading partners (Johnston & Vitale, 1988; Webster, 1995).

In the ‘hub’ configuration, interactions are dyadic between a central firm and other firms. The rules tend to be settled by the central firm for each of the other firms. The kinds of relationships these central firms establish with their partners are also usually dyadic. Examples of the ‘hub’ configuration can be found in the chemical (Christiaanse & Rodon, 2005) industry, which is characterized by dominant central firms interacting – exchanging structured information – with a host of smaller partner firms on the basis of pre-established agreements.

In a ‘net’ configuration, the rules are not standardized, but negotiated and settled by groups of firms that interact with each other. Any ‘net’ configuration may consist of multiple groups of firms. Therefore, as a whole, in a ‘net’ configuration there are no standardized rules or common patterns of interpretation governing interactions between the various groups of firms. Although in each group of firms there might be some degree of asymmetry, the distribution of resources at the ‘net’ level is symmetric. Kumar et al. (1998), in analysing the reasons for the failure of SPRINTEL in Prato, provide an example of a ‘net’ configuration. The authors show that the rules and patterns of interpretation governing the interactions in Prato were not common for everybody, and were established at the level of a group of firms adjacent in the value chain – the filiera tessile. Likewise, the authors note that there were no actors capable of imposing the adoption of Sprintel on the other firms in Prato.

In short, by drawing upon ST we contend that when structural contradictions exist (that is, contradictions between the initial social structure and the structure enacted in the adoption of an IOIS) and if adopters perceive that these contradictions are detrimental to them (i.e., adopters lose control of their operations) then conflict will arise. Conflict can be manifested, for instance, as a resistance to using the system, which leads to non-adoption if not managed properly. In our framework, one way to identify those contradictions is by examining the structural configuration of the setting before and after the IOIS is adopted. The following section illustrates this argument, and by doing so, the use of the framework.

## Research method and setting

## Methodology

This section illustrates the contradictions of structure and explains the adoption results by means of a single in-depth case study (Eisenhardt, 1989; Walsham, 2006) dealing with the implementation of an IOIS at the Port of Barcelona. In particular, we analysed two inter-organizational services developed simultaneously in the same place and with the same actors. In one service, adoption proceeded as the managers of the IOIS expected; while adoption was unsatisfactorily for the other service. Accordingly, we may regard our theoretical sampling approach as ‘polar type’, so that we can ‘observe contrasting patterns of data’ (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007, p. 27).

The data collection process was undertaken during a 5-year period (2001–2005) by means of interviews, field observations, attendance of meetings, meeting minutes, press articles, internal documents, and mailing lists. Forty-eight semi-structured interviews, averaging an hour in length, were conducted with individuals from 12 different types of organizations (SAs, FFs, ITs, haulage contractors, port authority, customs, etc.) and different levels (management, analysts, developers, and users). All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed by the authors. By combining various data collection approaches and informants, we tried to mitigate bias, and thus increase the validity and reliability of the information elicited. Moreover, data collection focused on retrospective as well as real-time phenomenon (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007).

The involved researcher role was adopted (Walsham, 2006). In addition to attending meetings and presentations, we provided participants with feedback in the form of presentations and reports after data gathering. Likewise, participant comments from the feedback were incorporated into the research findings in order to enhance confidence (Eisenhardt, 1989). Finally, we examined all the qualitative data gathered and analysed the data on the basis of the profiles of IOIS implementation that were presented in the previous section.

## Setting

The Port of Barcelona is the second largest Spanish port in terms of total goods traffic and the largest in terms of value. The port channels 24% of Spain’s foreign trade. It handled h28 billion worth of external goods traffic in 2006, and total traffic was in excess of 47 million tonnes, with 2.3 million containers.

The focus of this study is an IOIS in the Port of Barcelona known as PortIC. The board of directors of the Port of Barcelona approved the first strategic plan in July 1998. The plan identified three main challenges that had a major impact on the port: the process of economic globalization, the centralization of logistics, and the internationalization of port competition. The plan recognized that no individual port firm had the capacity to face these challenges alone. Therefore, the Port Authority and the main private agents in the port adopted a single, global, integrated vision for the future. This vision would enable them to set a series of goals and undertake the actions needed to achieve them.

One of the main lines of action of the plan was the establishment of electronic services to bring about a ‘paperless port’. The rationale for this metaphor of the paperless port was the reduction in the time and cost required for the passage of goods through the port. The chief information officer of the port authority argued for the need to replace the paper-based system with an electronic system, ‘the data exchange among the members of the transport chain is the real bottleneck in the transport of goods through the port’. Accordingly, 1 year later, PortIC was established as the first IOIS in the port of Barcelona and all the port agents became shareholders. PortIC brought together representatives of the associations of SAs, FFs, stevedoring companies, customs agents, chamber of commerce, and the port authority.

Despite the diversity of interests and motivations of each of these port agents, they all shared two main goals for PortIC. First, most of the port agents viewed PortIC as an opportunity to improve their competitiveness and that of the port of Barcelona. Second, PortIC was perceived as an entry barrier for other agents that could threaten their position.

To achieve these goals, PortIC had to integrate the message flows of the various port agents. The promoters of PortIC split these message flows into two groups of document exchange services: government-to-business (G2B) and business-to-business (B2B). The G2B service involved exchanges between private port agents and public bodies, for example cargo manifests, customs requests, dangerous goods declarations. The B2B service referred to exchanges between private agents, for example bookings, transport orders, shipping instructions, gate-in notifications. Both G2B and B2B followed a standard based on UN/EDIFACT, the development of which had been governed by a consortium representing all the port agents. The coordinator of the standards consortium argued for the choice of UN/EDIFACT in the following terms: ‘we considered the choice of EDIFACT as developed by the United Nations to be the best option because the port is only a link in the logistic chain. It seems natural that the more standardized our choice, the easier it would be for us to interconnect with other chains or sectors in the future’.

## Level of analysis

Given the networked nature of an IOIS, adoption is usually enacted through networks of multiple actors. In this paper, we term these networks of firms as ‘constellations’. That is, an individual firm’s decision to adopt the IOIS depends on and affects other firms in the constellation adopting it. Each constellation always includes at least two adopting firms (a dyad). In the case of G2B, the constellations involved public bodies (either the Port Authority and/or Customs) and private agents (e.g. SAs, customs agents, and ITs). In the case of B2B, constellations consist of only private agents and each must involve a FF, a SA, a H, and an IT. Moreover, each firm may participate in more than one constellation. For this case, we identify two constellations for B2B (see Figure 2). The first constellation includes FF1, FF2, SA1, H1, and IT1; whereas the second constellation includes FF3, FF4, SA2, H2, and IT1.

## Case results

## The case of G2B: Reproducing structure

In 1994, the Barcelona Port Authority started the development of a set of messages and procedures that constituted interactions between various private port agents (SAs, customs agents, and ITs) and public bodies (Customs and Port authority). In order to encourage a widespread, speedy adoption of those procedures, the Port Authority and the Association of Customs Agents and Brokers instituted several measures. The Port Authority facilitated the exchanges of SA manifests by acting as a one-stop shop with Customs. In this way, the Port Authority accepted from the SAs the manifests on paper or by EDI, and then, if necessary, translated the manifest to EDI before dispatching to customs. Accordingly, exchanges between public bodies and private agents took place through various mechanisms: EDI, fax, e-mail, or a proprietary application developed by the Port Authority. However, by 1998 the paper-based system still accounted for more than 50% of the messages received by the public bodies. Despite the dominance of paper-based systems over electronic systems, the standardization initiative had enabled the creation of a common set of rules for interactions between private agents and public bodies. As a result, message syntax and semantics, as well as procedures governing the interactions, were agreed upon by all the port agents despite the diversity of exchange mechanisms.

G2B was the first service that PortIC launched in mid-2000. It was adopted soon afterwards; and reached a considerable number of users a year after its launch. A significant percentage of the messages that the Port Authority and Customs received were generated through G2B. For instance, in 2001 the Port Authority received 93% of manifests from the SAs by EDI, most of which had been generated through the G2B service. Although the development of G2B entailed the replacement of the existing exchange mechanisms by a new Internetenabled system, it did not challenge the existing config uration of rules and resources in an inter-organizational context. G2B did not represent a major change to the rules, as these, in general, were already common and shared by public and private agents. Port agents had a common understanding regarding the content and flow of messages. As a SA CIO stated with reference to G2B:

PortIC offers a set of byproducts that the Port Authority of Barcelona was already offering; namely, cargo manifest, summary declaration, dangerous goods manifest, berth request, etc. The Port Authority transferred the exploitation of those services to PortIC [who embedded them in the G2B application].

## The case of B2B: challenging structure

The standardization process, included exchanges between private agents, started in the port of Barcelona in 1994. Nevertheless, the rules regarding the syntax, semantics, and pragmatics of these exchanges were traditionally fixed by constellations of firms that conducted business with each other. In these constellations, smaller firms usually work for one or two larger players who establish the rules (e.g., message content, frequency of exchanges, and price) for interactions in the constellation. Smaller firms, however, usually participate in other constellations. Therefore, while the rules were usually standardized at the level of constellations; the rules of the social system at the community level where B2B was to be implemented were not common to all the agents. However, resources in the social system of B2B were distributed symmetrically. In the case of the constellations, some firms held a certain degree of power over others as they were able to impose a series of rules. But, if we look at the social system of the whole set of potential adopters of B2B (community level), there is no actor that could impose rules, and so the distribution of resources was symmetric. EDI, fax, and e-mail exchange mechanisms coexisted.

B2B was launched in mid-2000 just after G2B, but 1 year later it had reached an almost insignificant number of users. For instance, before 2005 the percentage of messages at the main IT submitted through B2B still accounted for less than 2%. The reasons for such a marginal use of B2B were as follows.

First, potential adopters complained that the messages supported by the B2B application did not always fit their reality or entailed a change in work practices that potential adopters were not always willing to put into effect. For instance, an IS developer of an IT noted:

when I send a ‘gate-in notification’ message, if that message corresponds to an ‘acceptance order’ of a dangerous good, the CODECO guide defined by EDIFACT forces me to use a free text within the GID [Goods Item Details] segment. This irritates me, because I do not store free texts in our database. The fact that this free text field is compulsory makes no sense. In our business [inland terminal] this free text is of no use. Thus I have problems in generating the CODECO message.

Moreover, adopters interpreted certain fields of the messages differently from how the designers of B2B had intended. Designers, however, had implemented B2B following the standard, on which all the port agents had previously reached consensus. These contradictions of rules generated tensions between the adopters and the managers of B2B: as the latter had assumed that the former would adapt their systems and working procedures; while the former perceived that those adaptations harmed them.

Second, potential adopters also disagreed and felt uncomfortable with the uneven returns because not all port agents gained equally by adopting B2B. Port agents who only used B2B to submit messages gained less than those who used B2B to receive messages. The latter spared themselves having to type messages into their in-house systems. An IT manager of a FF, arguing for the need to incentivize adoption by FFs, noted:

I’ve always told PortIC management that it’s the shipping agent that gains the most out of this, because they get the document I submit through PortIC [B2B] without having to type it into their system. So, it’s the shipping agent that saves costs, not me. I save time, but very little y Then I ask them for an incentive for freight forwarders in the form of a discount y And, for those that don’t adopt, there’s the perception that this is because they expect to take advantage of the efforts of those who’ve invested in adopting PortIC, and, when this all works, then they’ll join.

Moreover, the messages supported by B2B did not affect all port agents equally. Initially, most of the messages were related to SA operations. So, other port agents (i.e. FFs, ITs, haulage contractors) felt undermined. For instance, the operations manager of an IT, commenting on the influence of SAs on the agreements taken by the standardization consortium, and which were later implemented by B2B, noted:

Sometimes there are dysfunctions in the processes, or they [members of the consortium] make decisions about the processes under pressure or influence from a certain group of users, namely shipping agents, while for other groups the implementation of those processes is inconvenient or unfeasible.

The B2B service benefited some structural types of adopters more than others. This was expected to be corrected mid-term once the B2B service supported all the potential interactions between the port agents. Finally, B2B was supposed to provide transparency to the processes between port agents and, as such, was expected to reveal inefficiencies. B2B integrated and centralized data regarding documentary formalities between all port agents. Any port agent could simply look up the documentary status of goods and find out who was in charge of that operation using B2B, without having to directly ask the port agent in charge of the operation. Then a competing rule became apparent. Agents perceived that in disclosing the information on their operations they might risk losing power in favour of others who could then control the full information flow.

## Analysis

This section analyses the results of the case through the lens of the theoretical framework developed in this paper. IOIS management adopted the same implementation strategy for both inter-organizational services – G2B and B2B. A set of applications was developed which followed the standard previously agreed upon and approved by the same potential adopters. The B2B and G2B services were based on the same IT – both services ran on the same application and data servers and had the same user interface. Management trained the potential adopters for both services. As with G2B, management expected that firms would start using B2B because, even though IOIS management was aware that port agents had different interests, all the port agents agreed that B2B was an opportunity to standardize and increase the efficiency of port operations. Indeed, by adopting B2B, firms were also supposed to benefit by integrating only once with the IOIS for communication with all partner firms within the port community; and firms could thereby avoid maintaining several interfaces with partners. Despite those similarities and the expected benefits, adoption results of both services differed. Whereas G2B was widely adopted, the adoption of B2B was far from satisfactory.

By examining both cases through the framework we observe that the settings where G2B and B2B were adopted, differed in terms of the initial and the target structural configurations (see Figure 3). In the case of G2B, the initial structure can be regarded as one in which rules were standardized and distribution of resources was asymmetric. In other words, the IOIS structural configuration where G2B was to be adopted was a ‘hierarchy’. In the case of B2B, many of the rules had historically been standardized at the level of the constellations. So as each constellation had its own rules, there was no common set of standardized rules for all the agents in the port – in other words, rules at the community level were not standardized. Moreover, while the distribution of resources in these constellations was asymmetric, the distribution of resources at the community level can be regarded as symmetric. Accordingly, the IOIS structural configuration where B2B was to be adopted was a ‘net’.

Second, whereas the adoption of G2B did not challenge the existing structure, the adoption of B2B entailed a change in the structural configuration (see Figure 3). In the case of G2B, the structure enacted in its adoption did not differ from the initial structure and therefore, there were almost no structural contradictions. The rules enacted in the use of G2B remained standardized, and the distribution of resources remained asymmetric. There were two focal firms, the port authority and customs, who had the authoritative resources to define the rules for interaction with the port agents and foster the adoption of these rules. Moreover, these public bodies, and especially the port authority, drew upon allocative resources in their attempts to support G2B adoption. These allocative resources included, for instance, economic incentives offered to those using G2B.

However, the adoption of B2B entailed a change of structural configuration. First, because B2B underpinned the standard developed at the port, its adoption was expected to standardize the set of rules at the constellation and community levels. Second, at the constellation level there were some shifts in the distribution of resources; for instance, because of information transparency regarding the passage of goods, some agents lost the control they traditionally had over those data; however, at the community level the distribution of resources remained symmetric.

In other words, at the community-wide level, the B2B service entailed a change in the structural configuration from ‘net’ to ‘market’. At the community level, the contradictions of structure mainly emerged because there were differences between the rules that governed the interactions between organizations in the port community and the rules enacted in the use of B2B. As many adopters felt these rules worked against their interests, and they had the capacity not to adopt because of the symmetry of resources in the structural configuration (‘net’), they opted for a marginal use of B2B. The following quotation from the chief information officer of the Port Authority, who compares the difficulty between developing the B2B standard and its adoption, illustrates this statement,

Reaching an agreement on how firms are going to work [standardization] is very difficult, but bringing it into reality [adoption and use] is even more difficult. The reason is that the agreement is reached by a dozen people who represent others in their trade associations. But in each association there are hundreds of firms y for instance, there are as many transport orders as haulage contractors. Moreover, what was decided in the standardization consortium regarding the messages and processes that involve private agents, was not binding on those port agents.

In short, even though firms in the seaport were committed to the new IOIS (all port agents being shareholders of the IOIS) and IOIS management employed the same strategy for both services (G2B and B2B), adoption outcomes for both services differed. G2B adoption did not entail any significant changes to the structural configuration and so there were hardly any structural contradictions. Moreover, in order to accelerate the adoption processes focal actors drew upon allocative resources to give an incentive to those adopters who lagged behind. Despite the contradictions between the rules enacted in the use of B2B and the existing rules, IOIS management adopted the same implementation strategy for B2B as well as G2B. Basically, IOIS management interventions targeted the community level in the sense that any decisions regarding both services were the same for all the users of the community. IOIS management changed their implementation strategy when it became aware that these contradictions harmed the interests of adopters – who were then unwilling to adapt their working practices to those supported by B2B. Management decided to intervene at the community and constellation levels. Specifically, management targeted several constellations and intervened to adapt some aspects of B2B to the reality of each constellation.

## Discussion and implications

The purpose of this case study has been to illustrate how the changes and contradictions of structure that arise as a result of IOIS adoption can help explain the adoption results. Our use of ST along with the development of our framework can be categorized in terms of what Lee & Baskerville (2003) refer to as generalizing from theory to theory. By relying in this paper on several concepts from ST – social structure, duality of structure, and structural contradiction and conflict – we have built a framework that categorizes the structural configuration of the social setting where the IOIS is adopted. From our theoretical framework and the findings of the case study, we have formulated three propositions that may guide future research in this area.

This case shows that the adoption of both services differed considerably – despite both being very similar in terms of the underlying server and client IT. Other similarities included the standard supported, date of implementation, potential adopters, expected benefits for adopters, training received, the fee that adopters paid for both services, etc. As seen with G2B, its adoption did not entail a transformation of the previously existing structural configuration and there were hardly any structural contradictions. B2B adoption entailed a transformation of structural configuration and this triggered the emergence of several contradictions of structure at the community and constellation levels. Potential adopters perceived these contradictions as detrimental to their interests and therefore refused the B2B service. In addition, given the symmetric distribution of resources at the community level, there were no organizations with enough power to command and impose the broad adoption of B2B; accordingly, potential adopters were able to keep their working practices and opted for a perfunctory use of B2B. This suggests:

Proposition 1: If IOIS adoption entails a transformation of the structural configuration and the contradictions between the enacted social structure and the initial structure harm the adopters’ interests, then adopters will resist the use of an IOIS.

Proposition 2: If adopters perceive that the structural contradictions that result from IOIS adop tion harm their interests, and the distribution of resources of the original structural configuration is symmetric (meaning that adopters have the capacity to do otherwise), then non-adoption will likely occur.

The case suggests that solving the adoption problems of B2B required IOIS management to change the target of their interventions. Given the agreement and support of all potential adopters in the development of the standard and the IOIS, management had initially assumed that adopters would adapt the B2B service; and accordingly, during the implementation IOIS management targeted all adopters in the same way they had done for the G2B service. Interventions by IOIS management were focused at the community level. Once IOIS management realized that it was not possible to standardize the multiple performances of work practices that were supported by B2B, they decided to intervene on the system – as well as with the adopters. This new intervention took place at a different level; specifically, IOIS management acted at the level of the constellation, which was where the adoption of B2B actually took place. This leads to

Proposition 3: The implementation strategy that IOIS management adopts is contingent upon the transformation of the structural configuration that IOIS adoption entails – as well as the level at which adoption occurs.

## Future research

In the framework (Figure 1), we treat the rules and the distribution of resources as dichotomical dimensions. However, in the real world, there is a continuum between standardized and non-standardized rules, and between symmetric and asymmetric distribution of resources. Future research might address this limitation by further dividing each of these dimensions. This can be achieved, for instance, by examining the two types of rules (systems of meanings and norms of behaviour), and the two types of resources (authoritative and allocative). Likewise, future research might develop a notion of likeness, or structural distance, in order to compare the contradictions of structure.

The case study does not claim to show all the structural transformations that can be analysed with the framework. We only illustrate the use of the framework with two adoption cases that experienced differing outcomes. Thus we consider that the framework presented offers possibilities for further research. For instance, by exploring IOIS adoption at other structural configurations; studying other possible transformations of structural configuration resulting from IOIS adoption; and identifying adoption problems related to these transformations.

Our categorization approach also complements prior variance approaches on IOIS adoption by providing a method to contextualize the lists of factors they suggest. These studies develop factor models that are acontextual. However, we contend that the relevance of these factors is contingent on the type of structural configuration. For instance, in ‘hierarchy’ or ‘hub’ configurations, powerrelated factors (Hart & Saunders, 1997; Premkumar et al., 1997; Chwelos et al., 2001; Quaddus & Hofmeyer, 2007) weigh more than in ‘market’ and ‘net’ configurations. The contextualization of these factors could be a promising line for further research. In addition, the identification of the types of contradictions and their influence on the adoption process, as well as the analysis of managerial tactics adopted to resolve these adoption problems, may be another relevant avenue for further research.

Our framework also complements existing studies that draw upon technology acceptance models by providing an explanation for some of the factors in these models. For instance, we contend that when there is a change of structural configuration that harms the adopters’ interests, then adopters will perceive the system as less useful.

We have developed a categorization of IOIS according to the underlying social structure where an IOIS is adopted. Our framework has demonstrated practical implications. By exploring in advance the potential changes of structural configuration that will result from IOIS adoption, management can anticipate various lines of conflict that may appear on the horizon and adapt their strategies accordingly. Our framework of structural configurations offers managers a coarse-grained picture of possible scenarios, which may help them to anticipate contradictions. Above all, this framework offers simplicity, which is needed in the initial formulation of the implementation strategy of an IOIS. The notion of structural contradiction enables us to explain and theorize on structural transformation and change. It illuminates the analysis of strategic conduct in studying IOIS adoption.
