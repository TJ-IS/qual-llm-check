Kieran Conboy<sup>a</sup>, Denis Dennehy<sup>b,⁎</sup>, Mairead O'Connor<sup>c</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Temporality Business analytics Business value Information systems development

## A B S T R A C T

Time is central to the purported business value of analytics. Yet, research has adopted a simplistic, ‘clock interpretation of time, ignoring its complex and socially embedded nature. There is also an overemphasis on analytics software and not on the people using them. Although analytics may be ‘fast’ to realise business value, it must cater to temporal complexities of organisations and people using it. Drawing on the temporality theory, this study develops temporal factors to examine the value of analytics. We also develop a research agenda that identi<sup>fi</sup>es opportunities to examine time, temporal personalities and other factors when people use analytics in the organisation.

## 1. Introduction

Business analytics is top of the corporate agenda [1], with claims that it can realise heretofore unprecedented value [2–5]. More than 85% of Fortune 500 companies invest in big data initiatives [6,7,10]. Estimates of the analytics market vary considerably (e.g. [8,9]). Gatner [10] estimate the global revenue at \$18.3 billion in 2017 and forecast growth to \$22.8 billion by 2020. Although sometimes dismissed as hype, it is predicated to change the way businesses are organised and run, by combining managerial insight with new forms of evidence to draw heretofore undiscovered conclusions and to partially or fully au tomated certain business processes or events [11].

Despite the exponential growth and interest in business analytics from academics and practitioners [12,13], it is a relatively new domain of research inquiry at an early stage of maturity. Although there is evidence that organisations could create value through the use of analytics [14–16], the proposition that ‘business analytics leads to value’ requires deeper analysis [17]. Business analytics and its associated value to organisations remains under-explored, under-theorized and empirically under-represented in business research [18,19].

Marchand and Peppard ([20], p. 109) highlight that ‘big data and other analytics projects require people versed in the cognitive and behavioral sciences, who understand how people perceive problems, use information, and analyze data in developing solutions, ideas, and knowledge’. Yet, the research conducted to date has tended to largely focus on analytics tools [21] and not enough on the people and their institutional and social environments [20,22]. George et al. ([23], p. 321) make the call for management scholarship that tackles the chal lenges of using such tools − or, better yet, that explores the promise and opportunities for new theories and practices that big data might bring about. The lack of academic focus on people and their organisational and social contexts has created a lack of socio-technical harmony that information systems (IS) implementation initiatives often need to succeed [22].

This study aims to strengthen the conceptual body of knowledge in business analytics by focusing on the concept of time. Time was chosen, as it is fundamentally important to both aspects of this special issue (business value and analytics). We argue that a greater scrutiny of time and temporality is necessary in the study of business analytics and business value for <sup>fi</sup>ve key reasons.

First, time is a diferentiating value feature of analytics. For some proponents of business analytics, the velocity or speed of the analytics is a bigger asset than the volume of data provided [19]. They are claims that organisations can achieve a strategic competitive edge by making better decisions through real-time data [13,14,24,25]. There is an emphasis on ‘fast moving’ data and ‘fast analytics’ – the ability to acquire and visualise data quickly [24,26–28]. Real-time or nearly realtime analytics makes it possible for a company to be much more agile than its competitors. For example, Massachusetts Institute of Technology Media Lab used mobile phone location analytics in Macy’s parking lots on day one of the Christmas shopping season, making it possible to estimate the retailer’s sales on that critical day even before

Macy’s itself had recorded those sales [19].

Second, time is an inherently complex, multi-faceted, subtle concept and is by nature socially embedded. While IS researchers are quick to highlight the impact of information and communication technology on the speed of organisational and social life, they can be slow to address the polymorphous, complex and nuanced nature of time in IS research [29–31]. Despite a strong consensus that richer conceptualisations of time are required [32–34], time remains theoretically elusive in contemporary studies of work [29], often included only as a ‘hidden dimension’ [35]. The temporality complexity will be discussed in Section 2.4.

Third, data typically have a time value. What is unique about data assets is the fact that they have no intrinsic value until the data are analysed to <sup>fi</sup>nd a business value. Very often this value has a temporal dimension. For example, an analysis report <sup>fi</sup>ve minutes before a meeting or before a share price announcement may be incredibly valuable; one second after that meeting or announcement, the value may drop to zero [36].

Fourth, business value is typically obtained under time pressure. There is a rarely questioned assumption that our ‘now’ is more ephemeral, evanescent than ever before, and that any organisation’s use of technology must help them survive and thrive in the seemingly neverending environment of dynamism and acceleration [37]. IS researchers often use speed as a methodological proxy and even synonym for IS value and success [29]. This is particularly true where analytics is concerned. Information is not static; hence, analytics must handle ‘data in motion,’ as the desired patterns and insights are a moving target [22]. The major concern of analytics implementations is the timespan between elicitation, analysis and subsequent action, with the goal of enabling organisations to react in a timely fashion with regard to changing business situations (c.f. [38] for an extensive review of realtime intelligence and analytics.

Finally, and fundamentally, there is a lack of temporality literature in business analytics. Despite the points above, we argue that temporality and certainly that the subtle, social and complex nature of temporality does not receive adequate attention in the business analytics literature. When it is acknowledged, there is usually an assumption that such complexity or ambiguity can be removed rather than embraced. For example, Clarke ([39], p.79) refers to temporal applicability as ‘the absence of ambiguity about the date and time when, or the period of time during which, the data content represents or represented a realworld phenomenon’.

## 1.1. Research objective and questions

One should be careful not to overemphasise the importance of time, nor should one consider temporal factors in isolation (see the conclu sions of this paper for elaboration of these limitations). However, given (i) the centrality of time to both business analytics and business value; (ii) the complex, subtle and socially embedded nature of time; (iii) the time value of data; (iv) the time pressured environments within which analytics typically operates and (v) the lack of research on these issues in business analytics, we argue there are a number of potential implications. First, over-simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation or omission of temporal considerations may mean certain claims regarding business value of analytics may be wrong or at least in<sup>fl</sup>ated. Even if the business value is in fact theoretically correct, the various temporal complexities and socially embedded factors may inhibit that value from being realised. Similarly, opportunities for analytics and business value may be missed because of over-simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation or omission of temporal considerations. To address this issue the over-arching objectives of this study are to

(i) develop and illustrate a set of temporal factors that researchers or practitioners can use to evaluate the temporal complexities of an analytics technology and process that may enable or inhibit busi ness value

(ii) develop a roadmap for future research on temporality in business analytics.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study seeks to answer the question:

What are the temporal factors that a<sup>f</sup>ect the business value of analytics?

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section presents the relevant business analytics literature used to structure this study. This is followed by a section on analytics and business value. Data governance is then explored, followed by the concept of temporality, along with a description of the [40] temporality framework, which will be used as the main theoretical lens. The research method, <sup>fi</sup>ndings and analysis of the study is then presented. An over-arching discussion and roadmap for future research is proposed. The paper ends with conclusions and limitations of the study.

## 2.2. Analytics and business value in information systems development

Software metrics have been studied for decades and several litera ture reviews [52,53] have been published [54]. Contemporary ISD methods such as <sup>fl</sup>ow use metrics such as lead time, cycle time and throughput rate [55–57]. Despite this abundance of data and metrics, development continues to be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict and risky to conduct [58]. There is a notable absence of ISD research on analytics, speci<sup>fi</sup> cally its impact on software development practice [59]. Microsoft Software Analytics reports that software analytics will enable software practitioners to perform e<sup>f</sup>ective and e<sup>fi</sup>cient data exploration and analysis to obtain insightful and actionable information for data-driven tasks in software development.

Business analytics is increasingly being used to assist systems developers and project managers to understand their projects, how they evolve as well as support them during their decision-making process [60]. Indeed, many ISD<sup>1</sup> stakeholders can draw on and utilize analytics, as software projects involve developers, testers, development leads, test leads, managers and researchers, all of whom have di<sup>f</sup>erent data and analysis needs [61]. Actors from di<sup>f</sup>erent parts of the organisation should be involved in the process of generating insights from data, as they use the data and analysis as a means to understand the phenomena that the data represent [16,62].

Despite large volumes of data and many types of metrics, projects continue to be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict and risky to conduct [58]. Despite previous attempts to make use of data collected during ISD, such e<sup>f</sup>orts are often of little use. First, the metrics, their use and their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are often not studied in context. Failure to do so limits the understanding and usefulness of results [52]. Additionally, Radjenović et al. [63] conclude that more studies are needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd metrics suitable to var ious contexts and determine which metrics should be used in a given context.

This is particularly true in contemporary methods such as agile [64] and <sup>fl</sup>ow [57,65,66] where people are valued over process; hence, the nature of metrics shifts to those that are more socially embedded, people related and subject to change. These are all characteristics suited to the use of analytics.

De<sup>fi</sup>ning ‘value’ is often challenging if not impossible, given fun damental gaps between the perceptions of analysts/developers and would-be users/customers because of ‘physical, conceptual and cultural distance’ between the two groups [67]. Each have their own agendas, and each bring an ‘unfamiliar language that is domain speci<sup>fi</sup>c’ [68], lying somewhere between an objective, rational view and a socially constructed view that embraces highly complex factors such as power, control, legitimacy, privilege, justice and equity [69]. However di<sup>fi</sup>cult it may be to de<sup>fi</sup>ne business value in a given ISD project, estimating value is more complex again. Analytics capabilities present an opportunity to inform and reconcile these di<sup>f</sup>erences and ambiguities.

Although early management research codi<sup>fi</sup>ed diaries and timemanagement techniques, big data enable us to study project teams in near–real time to predict behaviors, coordination challenges and performance outcomes [23]. Application of rigorous analytics to software project management can help managers to monitor a project, improve e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, manage risk, anticipate changes and evaluate past decisions [58,70].

Although business analytics has the potential to revolutionise project management [11,19], many businesses struggle to identify how to use analytics to take advantage of their data [71–73]. From a practice perspective, there are still challenges associated with the adoption of analytics into ISD processes, as well as the development and integration of analytics tools in practical settings [60]. Deploying analytics tools is relatively easy, but understanding how they might be used is much less clear [20]. A lack of understanding of how to use analytics to improve the business, as well as managerial and cultural issues, remain the key barriers to widespread adoption of big data analytics and techniques, rather than related data and technology [19,72,74,75]. Perceptions of data and results not being demonstrated in a timely manner have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as stumbling blocks in analytics projects [24]. With respect to analytic leadership, Davenport et al. ([70], p. 98) advocate that ‘leaders have a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on culture and can mobilise people, money and time to help push for more analytical decision making’. A related and emerging challenge that is impeding companies from realising the business value of business analytics is data governance, which is dis cussed in the next section.

## 2.3. Data governance

Data governance is essential to maximising value from business analytics [76]. Data governance is an organising framework for managing business analytics resources within an organisation and the assignment of decision rights and accountability to align business analytics initiatives with organisational objectives [77,78]. Analytics governance must also include mechanisms for identifying, communicating and resolving issues that are holding up analytics projects, as well as balancing priorities between analytics projects and other cor porate projects [54,79].

Yet, many organisations are still in the early stages of developing strategies and governance processes to leverage these new capabilities Mithas et al., 2013a. High-performing companies use analytics <sup>fi</sup>ve times more than low-performing companies [73]. A study by LaValle et al. [72] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed managerial and cultural challenges as impediments to analytics governance; [80] provide a comprehensive list of analytics governance challenges, including poorly de<sup>fi</sup>ned governance, fragmented organisational structures, poorly designed and understood de cision-making processes as well as di<sup>fi</sup>culty in linking analytics governance to business value and return on investment.

In terms of temporality – the focus of this study – the literature suggests that the speed at which data can be analysed is a major con cern for organisations [28,73,76,79,81]. In the past, having a reactive approach to global economic changes was adequate for organisations. Presently, they require a proactive and prescriptive approach to understand probabilities of external factors that may a<sup>f</sup>ect the organisation [73,79,81]. In fact, the standard requirement for organisations is now real time or near–real time insight [76,79]. Organisations continually fear whether their competitors can analyse data and generate intelligent information at a quicker rate [73]. Executive-level managers want a quick but insightful overview of company performance. They want instant solutions for any potential risks. Essentially, they want data-driven decisions based on simulations, which infer probabilistic measurements and best action options, in an instant [73].

If data governance is carried out appropriately, then the data can be acquired at a greater velocity [82]. In fact, data governance practices are peaked to be the instrumental force in utilising value from organisational data at a speed faster than their competitors [76]. Although it is evident that speed is an important time factor in data governance literature, research that explicitly examines speed, or any other timerelated factor, is lacking. The importance of speed is an indirect focus within the broad data governance literature. This is also the case for other temporal factors that are not studied in their own right. For example, the critical factor is controlling the data life cycle from the point at which data is developed to the point to which it is disposed [80]. However, although literature suggests managing the data life cycle is important, literature around this area is also lacking. There were similar time research gaps in the following factors, which were listed as essential to achieve e<sup>f</sup>ective data governance; these gaps include the following: (i) understanding the data; (ii) who uses the data; (iii) what the data are used for and (iv) and the data capabilities. No tably, there is no mention of a time-related factor, instead time is mentioned as an indirect measurement within the study [81]. Similarly, research tends to focus on the volume of the data. However, the fre quency at which the data are generated and then analysed was listed as one of the neglected but equally important dimensions of big data [28]. There are also a range of data governance literature which omit time completely [83–85]. Leveraging temporality as a theoretical lens to examine big data in the context of ISD is discussed in the next section.

## 2.4. Temporality

Temporality refers to how we think about time, experience time [86], our relationship with time [87] and how we interact with time [88]. Time has always featured prominently in the thinking of early philosophers from Augustine to Aristotle to Plato [89]. Yet it was not until the 20th century that discussions around the nature of time and our experience of time arose more prominently in philosophy (e.g. [87,90,91]. Around this time, natural sciences made revolutionary changes to the way in which we view and understand time [92]. The turn of the 21st century saw an increase in time discussions in social science, in particular, organisational literature [93–97]. Despite the importance and prevalence of temporality, researchers have lamented the under-theorisation of time across a range of business disciplines including project management [98], strategic decision-making [99] and organisation science [96,100].

The concept of time and its underlying variables has been represented in several frameworks (e.g. [40,95,96,101,102]. Such fra meworks have made a number of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to the temporality literature: (i) the concept of social constructions of time (c.f. [40,101]; (ii) how time is mapped to an event or activity (c.f. [103–105]; (iii) temporal structuring [96]; (iv) how the individual’s relate to time [96,101] and (iv) how individual’s perceive time [95,96,101]. Although many studies acknowledge how individual’s perceive time, this unique relationship with time remains understudied [40].

There are limitations with these frameworks, namely, (i) temporal variables di<sup>f</sup>er across the frameworks, (ii) temporal variables are dependent on the activity it is mapped to and (iii) the context in which the activity exists [103,106,107]. For example, in the context of evaluating temporal e<sup>f</sup>ects of IS, duration, sequence, temporal location, deadline, cycle and rhythm were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables [105]. In the context of organisation culture, [103] propose the following 11 temporal variables: (i) time boundaries between work and non-work; (ii) sequencing of tasks; (iii) punctuality; (iv) allocation; (v) awareness; (vi) synchronisation and coordination; (vii) variety versus routine; (viii) intra-organisational time boundaries; (ix) future orientation; schedules and deadlines; (x) work pace and autonomy of time use as well as (xi) quality versus speed. In an attempt to address these limitations, Ancona et al. [40] proposed a more comprehensive framework of temporal variables that is also useful for structuring how we view time, how time is mapped to an event or activity and how the individual relates to time.

This study adopts the Ancona et al. [40] framework (see Table 2) for two key reasons. First, while studies have looked at some of the tem poral components of the framework (e.g. [33,108,109,110], none have looked at temporality in such a broad, holistic manner. It provides the most comprehensive analysis of temporality, thus synthesising ‘a large swath of temporal concepts across diverse areas of temporal study and providing a common organizing framework for these temporal constructs and variables’ [30], p.3. Second, it places signi<sup>fi</sup>cant emphasis on the people, social and interpretive issues of temporality. As outlined in the introduction, such issues are relevant in the use of analytics, the application of analytics to systems development and in deed the attainment of business value through analytics in ISD. Therefore, a framework that places such emphasis on these issues renders the use of the Ancona framework particularly apt in this study.

Conceptions of time are the way in which organisations conceptualise time [106]. Time can be experienced in many ways using many types of time or socially constructed time [30]. Types of time may be linear time, clock time, uniform time, event time and cyclical time. The most popular and widely cited types of time are ‘clock time’ and ‘event time’ [95]. Socially constructed time may be work/family time and national holidays [40]. Mapping activities to time captures the temporal analysis of single activity mapping, repeated activity mapping, single activity transformation mapping, multiple activity mapping as well as comparison and meshing of activities [40,97]. It explains the commence ment, duration and trajectory of the activity [40,106]. Mapping an activity to time gives organisations awareness of scheduling, rate of completion, duration, cycles, frequencies, interruptions and synchronisations [40,103,104]. Actors relating to time explain the way in which each actor uniquely relates to time. Actors may perceive time in a multitude of ways [96,101,106]. The perception is based around the continuum of time, which <sup>fl</sup>ows from the past to the present and future. For example, actors who experience an event for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time may perceive it to last longer than a seasoned actor in the same position [106]. An actor's relationship to time deeply varies among di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures, sub-cultures and personalities [95].

## 3. Research method

The case study method is valuable for exploring ‘practiced-based problems where the experiences of the actors are important and the context of action is critical’ ([111] (p. 369). The rationale for adopting a case study strategy is that it enables the researcher to retain the holistic characteristics of real-life events while investigating empirical event [112,113]. Therefore, to ful<sup>fi</sup>l the objective of this research, studying cases facilitates engagement with those ‘living the case’ [114] to unearth the perceptions of time in the context of ISD business analytics and business value.

## 3.1. Case selection

This case study examined two ISD teams using business analytics at CompCo.<sup>2</sup> CompCo is an industry leader in technology solutions services and support; its global workforce exceeds 140,000 people, of which 1300 are located at the Irish campus.

The case study was purposefully selected for a number of reasons. First, the teams were using business analytics in live transaction environments that were complex and challenging. Second, decisionmaking was in some cases highly time-pressurised with short-time windows for identifying and correcting errors and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant repercussions if projects ran over time. Third, there was continued direct access to interviewees, which included on-going interviews, direct observations, participant observations and access to all project documents. This proved highly valuable when studying temporality, which, as stated earlier, is often subtle, nuanced and is highly context dependent; implicit and socially embedded. The pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the case is now described.

The company has a variety of data sources that underpin its core ISD analytics capabilities. Each of these data sources is explained below.

Team Foundation Server (TFS) is a Microsoft product that covers the entire application lifecycle. It provides source code management reporting, work item tracking, requirement management, project management, automated builds as well as testing and release management capabilities.

## 3.2. Data collection and analysis

In this study, interviews, direct observations, participant-observations and physical artefacts were used as multiple sources of evidence (c.f. [122]. The advantage of using multiple sources of evidence is the development of ‘converging lines of inquiry’, a process of triangulation, as this increases the precision of the empirical research, thus making the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and conclusion more convincing [121,122]. To remove the threat of researcher in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the responses provided by interviewees during the interview process, data triangulation was used to provide multiple measures of the same phenomenon (c.f. [122]).

To improve reliability and repeatability, an ‘audit trail’ was created from data collection to drawing of conclusions. Data were collected, primarily through purposefully selected, face-to-face interviews. This technique is well suited to case study data collection, and for exploratory research such as this, as it allows expansive discussion to il luminate factors of importance [122,123]. An interview protocol was prepared on the basis of the components of the Ancona et al. [40] framework. Two interviewers were present at all interviews, which were conducted in a responsive, re<sup>fl</sup>exive manner [124,125], thus al lowing the researchers to follow-up on insights uncovered midinterview and adjust the content and schedule of the interview accordingly. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, proof-read and annotated. In any cases of ambiguity, clari<sup>fi</sup>cation was sought from the corresponding interviewee, either through telephone or e-mail.

## 4. Findings

This section describes the temporal factors that emerged when the Ancona et al. [40] framework was applied to the analytics capabilitie volume, velocity, variety, and veracity. It explores how these factors a<sup>f</sup>ect business value. Seven factors arose from data analysis. Each is now presented and discussed in turn, with reference to the ‘Vs’ where appropriate.

## 4.1. Factor 1: appropriate use of clock and event time in analytics

Currently, the analytics tools use a combination of clock time (weekly and monthly reports) and event time (an automated email is sent every time there is an ‘incident’ (event) that convey incidents the teams must resolve. The clock-based reports (e.g. a weekly report on a

Monday morning) were predominantly used by sta<sup>f</sup>. However, they were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly weaker in terms of business value as a number of days may have passed between an incident occurring and appearing in a report. As mentioned previously, Team 1 is responsible for maintaining order automation of an application that manages in excess of \$450 m per quarter for retail segments. Referring to a recent event, a Software Tester explained that ‘an issue was found on a Friday and it had to be fixed and tested by Monday because there was \$11 m relying on it’. In this situation, because you move from delivering features to <sup>fi</sup>xing defects in those features, time gets condensed and there is a rapid pace of change, with very short deadlines.

In Team 2, although a team is noti<sup>fi</sup>ed once an incident or defect is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, teams tend to react in a clock-based and haphazard manner rather than a well-orchestrated event-based response. As one Scrum Master explained, ‘the right people with the right skills are not assembled to resolve the right incident’. This is akin to emergency or disaster response in the health and security industries, where sta<sup>f</sup> are trained in an eventbased manner (e.g. terrorist simulations), rather than training di<sup>f</sup>erent skills at di<sup>f</sup>erent times in the week.

The teams are now using predictive analytics to analyse potential incidents by monitoring spikes (i.e. potential defects) in Peripheral Component Interconnect (PCI) cards. This enables managers to assemble the most appropriate team to respond to an incident before a sequence of failures occurs in the process. This factor highlights the importance of data volume and variety to enable appropriate responses to incidents. A PCI card is a circuit board that plugs into the computer's main motherboard and contains the control electronics for a peripheral device (i.e. printer).

## 4.2. Factor 2: appropriate use of subjective and objective time in analytics

Currently, teams use a combination of subjective time (estimations) and objective time (billable h). However, the subjective estimates are almost always incorrect. A Scrum Master revealed that ‘the last couple of months we have been getting it drastically wrong and this has led to sig nificant unplanned overtime for team members’.

This over-reliance on subjective estimates undermines all the analytics that these data underpin. Consider Fig. 1 where 120 h of analyst time is estimated to result in cost savings of x for one factory. If analytics are working e<sup>f</sup>ectively, then the organisation should be able to accurately estimate business value. The ability of CompCo to respond quickly in an organised manner with the right teams will enable them to use time more intelligently and to generate increased business value from the project teams. By doing so, they can increase marginal returns and increase more incident <sup>fi</sup>nds (the curve in Fig. 1).

As both teams lack accurate and trustworthy data to estimate, they are then unable to quantify the amount of downtime they save to the factories and the business value delivered to CompCo. For example, each invalid order (i.e. con<sup>fi</sup>guration and defect), the team <sup>fi</sup>nds, it inevitably provides business value to CompCo in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial savings, resource allocation and customer satisfaction. ## 4.3. Factor 3: appropriate use of social construction of time in analytics

Both teams work around a social construction of time that is an 8-h day, of which 6 h are allocated for software development–related work and 2 h for administration-related work. A social norm at CompCo is that, regardless of the number of hours a team member spends working overtime, has their work day recorded as an 8-h day. This is a social norm, which hides the actual hours spent on projects as explained by a TPM, ‘in our time sheets, we weren’t allowed put in over time. You work an 8-h day regardless of what actually you do. Because the analytics tools missed to consider vital hours spent working on project-related tasks, extremely poor estimates were generated, and this resulted in business value being lost’.

In response to this loss of business value, a TPM con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the teams recently began entering actual hours worked on projects in order to provide better estimates’. He explained that by ‘using analytics to calculate hours into the time management systems, the Project Management Ofice (PMO) can now predetermine the cost of a project and decide the time line needed to complete upcoming projects’.

To a lesser degree, analytics calculations were often not considering national holidays across the distributed teams (e.g. India, Ireland, North America and Brazil). Each dependency in the development process has its own range of unique cultural events, and given the large scale of systems operating at once at CompCo, social constructions of time (public holiday and religious events) are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage. The teams expressed understanding about working during social constructions of time. However, there was consensus across both teams that analytics could help predict issues around setting a target release date around a public holiday or religious event. The teams are aware of anticipating how dependencies can impact one another and any interruption will have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nancial impact for CompCo. A Software Development explained that people need to know about release dates because they could be planning on taking annual leave when releases are due.

These challenges demonstrate the importance of data veracity and the appropriate use of social construction of time in analytics. Analytics tools could reduce this con<sup>fl</sup>ict between release dates and social constructions of time, whilst also enhancing the business value of analytics tools at CompCo.

## 4.4. Factor 4: appropriate management of multiple speeds of analytics

A di<sup>f</sup>erentiating characteristic of big data is the sourcing of data from multiple channels, each with its own speed of delivering data. At CompCo, both teams use TFS as one source to retrieve big data. Although the teams agreed, in general, that TFS provided business value, the speed of the system was an issue because team members would be ready to upload their code and current progress status, but TFS was slow to process the data. A Senior Principal Engineer explained that although TFS ‘adds a lot of value and you can associate your source code with your actual work but the problem is velocity as it is slow’. From a practice perspective, he described that ‘the stories for tasks and creating tasks is what takes time. Sometimes it’s down I receive a work error notification. I then must email system support requesting assistance to resolve this error which can take up to 48 hours to resolve the error’.

This was not an isolated event, as his colleague on the other team explained that ‘during release time, you are under pressure and. need to update the status of a story but TFS is down and your manager still thinks you are still on track’. As a result, decisions are being made by managers with information that lacks veracity, which impacts business value.

When information is not up-to-date, a TPM will have to wait until the daily stand up meeting to get actual progress updates from 7 to 10 people. This time delay leads to tasks ending up in production much later than had been scheduled or rolling over to the next sprint, which inevitably impacts business value. A TPM explained that, ‘it is very important for this information to be up-to-date as it allows people to help each other and create business value’. Referring to the virtual Kanban boards in TFS, he explained that by ‘updating the board, we can understand and identify current or emergent impediments and then allocate the right person or team to remove the impediment, rather than add it to the next sprint’. These challenges highlight the importance of velocity and veracity and why management of multiple speeds of analytics is critical to achieving business value. These challenges highlight the need for management to be cognizant of data velocity and veracity.

## 4.5. Factor 5: appropriate communication of real-time data in analytic

Both teams encountered multiple challenges dealing with the lack of real-time data in analytics. These challenges were found in the collocated (Team 1) and globally distributed team (Team 2) at CompCo. Both teams lacked the data required to adequately understand trends or patterns that arise (i.e. potential PCI con<sup>fi</sup>guration error). A Scrum Master revealed that, ‘these types of trends add more business value because as a team, we have a long list of defects. You don’t have an analysis of how far we are able to find the defects without going to production, how early are we finding it’.

When a high-priority defect arises, the TPM can use TFS to analyse the current state of a team member’s capacity. If the team member ha capacity, the TPM can then request that member to work on this speci<sup>fi</sup>c defect. However, without real-time data, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to manage an individual’s work load because ‘part of what analytics can bring is that we can see who is putting in the efort and we can make the right decision when assigning a person to a task (i.e. defect)’.

The teams are now using analytics to ensure the communication of real-time data (velocity) within and across teams at CompCo. A Scrum Master in Team 2 described how, ‘visualisations of spikes on my screen have alerts in red, which is the same on the screens for the team in India. We can now see the Kanban board and alert board’. The same interviewee further elaborated on how analytics is helping real-time communication across three continents, ‘we see orders every second, a tracker is updated every 60 seconds that shows the trends in the last 60 seconds. There is a graph for the last 5 hours. We see every region; Europe, ASIA and the Americas. It is updated every minute the factory is open’. This factor highlights the importance of velocity, variety and veracity to communicate real-time data appropriately and realise business value.

4.6. Factor 6: appropriate management of diferent perceptions of time in analytics

A TPM in Team 1 highlighted the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceptions of time in a distributed team. He noted the di<sup>f</sup>erent approaches his multicultural team members use to deal with request data: ‘instead of saying, let’s see how important this is to the business and maybe defer to another launch, it is a case of let’s get it done, even though we don’t have time. But while they get it done, it’s late’. His colleague, a Senior Software Developer stated that ‘diferent types of time can get confusing, especially when we have multiple launches (i.e. one major followed by a smaller launch) very close together, which brings a new level of complexity to the project’.

4.7. Factor 7: appropriate management of diferent temporal personalities of analytics

Decisions at CompCo are made on a minute-by-minute basis, depending on the criticality of the spike or severity of a defect. The Scrum Master in Team 2 explained that the behaviour of some people is to react to a spike with urgency (temporal personality) and others respond in a nonurgent manner (temporal personality). Referring to an event the week before the interview, he noted that ‘depending on the day of the week, some people would look at spikes and take immediate action, while other people would be much slower to respond’.

Similarly, a Senior Software Developer in Team 2 shared his ex perience when people with two di<sup>f</sup>erent temporal personalities conclude two di<sup>f</sup>erent decisions based on data from Kanban boards. He explained that ‘when a new priority comes in we don’t know which priority to remove. However, my Product Owner (PO) says we need to keep everything as everything is a priority. A solution to dealing with di<sup>f</sup>erent temporal personalities was proposed by the Scrum Master in Team 2. He noted that to drive positive behaviour and to counteract varying temporal personalities, we now colour code our spikes. This has helped us responding to spikes as we now know that red spikes must be investigated. This new approach has helped to change the behaviour of the team and increase business value’.

Although this factor was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only in Team 2, it, however, highlights the importance of being aware of how di<sup>f</sup>erent temporal personalities deal with velocity, variety and veracity in the context of analytics.

## 5. Discussion

In this section, we summarize our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings with a theoretical framework that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the main theoretical constructs and the relationships among them. We also present discussions about the theoretical and practical implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, limitations of this study and directions for future research.

## 5.1. Temporal factors afecting the business value of analytics

Based on the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we propose a set of temporal factors that may a<sup>f</sup>ect the extent to which analytics capabilities contribute, or indeed detract from, business value (see Table 5). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also demonstrate how the factors can be mapped to the various characteristics of the business analytics in question (volume, velocity, variety and veracity). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings revealed that some characteristics will contribute or be relevant to some factors and not others.

Having identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the temporal factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting the business value of analytics, the next section outlines the theoretical contribution of the study.

## 5.2. Theoretical contribution

Firstly, while time and particularly speed are seen as key strengths of contemporary analytics technology, temporality and certainly the subtle, social and complex nature of temporality, do not receive adequate attention in business analytics literature. This study contributes to this literature by drawing on temporal concepts from organisational and management literature. It then develops a set of temporal factors that can be used by researchers to examine the application of analytics.

Secondly, this special issue speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examines the business value of analytics. There are many claims regarding the transformative potential of analytics in terms of business value [14–16], and this link needs to be scrutinised [17]. Business analytics and its associated value to organisations remains under-explored, and under-theorized, parti cularly in business research [18,19]. This study contributes to the extant literature on analytics by examining the business value of analytics. The study shows how the 4 Vs of analytics can be used as structuring mechanisms to evaluate and optimise the use of analytics.

Third, it demonstrates the need to question many common assumptions regarding time and analytics and provides a structure for how this can be conducted. To date, the business literature and cer tainly the analytics literature has remained quiet on such assumptions. However, there are many conceptual studies (e.g. [40,95,96,101,102]) that address time complexity in organisations and management. One could also draw on studies such as Kavanagh et al. [37], which question over-arching, <sup>fi</sup>eld-de<sup>fi</sup>ning assumptions such as the most generally accepted assumptions that our ‘now’ is aggressively faster, more ephemeral, more evanescent, than any time period that preceded it.

Fourth, it provides another application of the Ancona et al. [40] framework. Other temporal researchers may re<sup>fl</sup>ect on this application for their own research. In particular, temporal factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting multiple layers (individual, team, organisation and industry) emerged from the study. Other researchers may re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the extent to which they are applying or should apply a temporal lens to multiple layers of analysis.

Fifth, it demonstrates that one should not automatically assume that better management or control of temporal complexities always leads to better temporal performance and thus business value. It could be argued that ignoring or at least taking a ‘loose’ approach to managing time may yield better results. This thinking is evident in other <sup>fi</sup>elds. For example, in the context of budgeting, tightening budgetary control does not always positively corelate to budgetary performance. Overly restrictive budgetary control can sti<sup>fl</sup>e a project and increase the chance of failure [132]. To date, however, little research has examined this notion in terms of time and analytics. Before this study, the prevailing assumption is that ‘faster’ analytics are better analytics.

Furthermore, it shows that we should not always assume that speed, velocity and acceleration are always a good thing. Consider a car journey. Although one may think that the faster the car is (the analytics technology), the quicker one will get to their destination (the business value), there are clear and present dangers to maximising that power. As speed increases, so does the risk and indeed likelihood of catastrophic failure.

Sixth, it highlighted the absence of studies that explicitly examine time in the application of business analytics, as well as its importance in data governance literature, which has not yet received adequate attention from the IS research community. Although examining temporal variables in data governance literature was outside the scope of this study, it does provide an opportunity for future research to advance the current body of knowledge speci<sup>fi</sup>c to data governance and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of temporal variables.

A <sup>fi</sup>nal theoretical contribution is the mapping of a research agenda to further knowledge on temporal complexity in business analytics and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to further thinking regarding the business value of analytics (Table 6). This agenda draws on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the analysis and the limitations of the study presented at the end of this paper.

Having presented the research agenda for examining temporal complexity in the business value of business analytics, the implications for practice are presented in the next section.

An organi sation may <sup>fi</sup>nd that overstretching to reach a certain velocity or veracity of data analytics may be causing a disproportionate set of temporal complexities and failings, and hence, may increase or decrease emphasis on that V accordingly.

(ii) Analysis of analytics tools or capabilities: The data showed that the temporal characteristics of the tools can enable or inhibit business value. Practitioners may consider the temporal complexities of the analytics tools or analytics capabilities.

They may consider how they currently ‘weave’ analytics into the ‘story’ of their team or organisation, thus ensuring temporal alignment between the volume, velocity, variety and veracity of the analytics and the corresponding volume, velocity, variety and veracity of the team or organisation’s activities. Across many factors in the study, it was clear that such alignment was absent and was negatively a<sup>f</sup>ecting business value.

A <sup>fi</sup>nal, yet important, implication is that, in addition to temporal issues, the study also shows the need for deep and deconstructed re-<sup>fl</sup>ection on the business value of analytics. It was clear from the study that there were analytics tools that provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant business value potential, yet sta<sup>f</sup> were unaware, or perhaps not motivated, to use the analytics capabilities for these purposes. Practitioners should re<sup>fl</sup>ect on what they mean by analytics and business value , and if in a leadership position they may consider what these terms mean to their sta<sup>f</sup>.

The factors mentioned above can be used as the structural basis for an action plan to address temporal complexities that a<sup>f</sup>ect value. This could be a large-scale action plan, where the entire processes of an organisation are refactored. However, in the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study, issues were often due to a simple lack of awareness of the existence or impact of temporal complexities or the e<sup>f</sup>ect these complexities had on business value. Workshops or training could identify potential shortcomings or misconceptions and could determine mechanisms to overcome these shortcomings.

## 6. Conclusions and limitations

Although the transformative power of business analytics is often discussed, the link between business analytics and business value remains under-explored and under-theorised [17–19]. In particular, little research has examined temporal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and what does exist tends to adopt an overly simplistic, ‘clock’ view of time, and neglects the complex, multi-faceted, subtle and socially embedded nature of temporality. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a set of temporal factors that may a<sup>f</sup>ect the business value of analytics in a particular setting. It demonstrated their application and provided a foundation for researchers and practitioners to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the temporal aspects of analytics capabilities. It encourages them to question commonly held temporal assumptions that may a<sup>f</sup>ect business value.

There are limitations of this study, which have to be considered. First, as stated earlier, although this study demonstrates the importance of considering time when evaluating the link between analytics and business value, one should not automatically assume that better management or control of temporal complexities always leads to better temporal performance. Nor should it be assumed that increased temporal performance is necessarily a good thing. Before assessing the temporal factors a<sup>f</sup>ecting business value, or taking any corrective action, it is important to determine whether time is an important dimension of success in that instance.

Second, time should not be considered in isolation. If we consider ISD as an example, time is one of three aspects of business value, with software cost and quality being the other two. The three are inextricably linked; an improvement of one may have a negative impact on the others. Trade-o<sup>f</sup>s and complementarities between these aspects need to be considered when creating a set of control mechanisms (c.f. [133–135]). For example, a Scrum or Kanban board may on one hand allow really fast delivery and instant co-ordination between team members but, depending on the temporal personalities and perceptions of that team, may cause a loss of productivity because of the resulting stress. The temporal factors developed in this study will need to be researched or applied with these trade-o<sup>f</sup>s and complementarities in mind.

The study adopted an exploratory, case-based approach. There are many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to examining temporal factors using quantitative, longitudinal and other research approaches. The roadmap in Table 6 highlights some of these opportunities. Longitudinal studies would be particularly desirable. In addition to the irony of examining the complexity of time using only one-o<sup>f</sup> short-term studies, the realisation of business value and the use of analytics are both highly dynamic activities. One often has to consider ‘data in motion’ because the data, the desired patterns and insights as well as the problem to be solved are all moving targets [22]. Longitudinal studies could examine these <sup>fl</sup>uctuations, the role temporal factors play in these <sup>fl</sup>uctuations as well as <sup>fl</sup>uctuations of the temporal factors themselves. For example, the business value of event-based analytics over a clock-based perspective may suddenly be eroded by the future occurrence of a particularly nasty or unique event that an event-oriented analytics capability may struggle to address.

There are also many contextual factors of this study that must be considered. This was conducted in an ISD setting, where a particular method (<sup>fl</sup>ow) was used. This issue of generalisation is true for much qualitative, exploratory research. However, readers of this study may think about temporality in not just a di<sup>f</sup>erent company using a di<sup>f</sup>erent software method in di<sup>f</sup>erent countries but may be applying temporality in the context of a completely di<sup>f</sup>erent application of analytics alto gether.

Finally, this paper was written considering analytics researchers and practitioners in mind. However, the study is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally set in an ISD context, using a speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of analytics functionality. Given, the diversity of tools, applications and contexts in the analytics domain, it is important that researchers consider the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. The research roadmap was developed considering novelty and impact on ISD in mind. Other analytics researchers may consider adding or editing questions or tailoring them to their own industry or context.

Kieran has published over 150 articles in leading international journals and conferences including Information Systems Research, the European Journal of Information Systems, and the Journal of the AIS. His research examines contemporary technology management and design including concepts such as temporality, <sup>fl</sup>ow, open innovation and agility. He is an editor of the European Journal of Information Systems and has chaired many international conferences in his <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Mairead O’Connor is a doctoral researcher with Lero at NUIG. Her research focuses on the application of temporality within the context of ISD. This research contributes to research and practice by (i) identifying conceptual issues in the application of temporal concepts, (ii) examining the complexity of temporality within ISD teams (iii) examining the impact that such complexity may have, and (iv) identifying strategies for resolving these temporality issues. Her research is informed through extensive engagement with Lero project partners. She has published this work in leading conferences and workshops such as the International Conference on Information Systems. She holds an MSc in Business Information Systems from University College Cork.
