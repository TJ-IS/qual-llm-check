## 1. Introduction

Improving outcomes by making better use of resources is a common goal in many decision environments. Accomplishing this goal is particularly challenging when a series of interdependent decisions is required, i.e., decisions made earlier constrain choices available at present, and when the environment changes in response to actions implemented on the basis of decisions as well as autonomously. Contexts that exhibit these features are referred to as dynamic decision-making environments (Edwards 1962). Prominent examples include healthcare, emergency management, piloting, and air traffic control (Brehmer 1992, Rochlin 1997). In all these contexts, information systems (IS) are used to collect data and support decision making. In this paper, we use data mining and control theory to develop an approach that improves decision-making outcomes and can be integrated to enhance existing IS. We demonstrate the feasibility of this approach using the examples of chronic disease care and manufacturing and mention possible applications in other dynamic domains.

Our focus in this research is the development of an approach to address complex, ill-structured dynamic decision-making problems, i.e., environments in which decision makers may (a) only see symptoms resulting from their actions instead of the underlying state; (b) have multiple, competing goals; (c) be faced with complex interrelationships among variables; and (d) receive time-delayed feedback (Funke 1991, Simon 1973). These problems stand in contrast to wellstructured problems to which significant literature is devoted (Simon 1973, Reitman 1965). For example, hundreds of articles have been written about inventory control techniques alone, from problem-specific methods, such as economic lot size models, to the application of general methods such as linear or integer programming (for an overview, refer to Axsäter 2006 among others). Well-structured problems typically have objective criteria, for example, there is an optimal number of units of inventory to meet a given objective and this number can be derived by use of an appropriate algorithm or technique. At any point in time, the current level of inventory on hand can be explained as the result of receipts, production, shipments, shrinkage, and adjustments. Ill-structured problems, on the other hand, do not have this transparency and often do not have a single best answer, i.e., different answers may have their own sets of strengths and weaknesses. For example, in chronic disease care, treatments that are effective for some patients are not effective for others based on some aspects of physiology that are not readily observed. If more than one treatment is used at a time, main and side effects cannot always be pinpointed to their source. Therefore, there is no global solution that works for all patients equally well.

Because it is burdensome and cognitively expensive to make decisions for complex, ill-structured problems again and again, individuals and organizations in dynamic environments adopt decision strategies, i.e., systematic ways of eliminating or choosing among alternative options (Beach and Mitchell 1978, Simon 1969). In practice, such decision strategies are encoded in policies, guidelines, and algorithms. This is consistent with the view of dynamic decision making as solving a process control problem, where a decision maker formulates and uses a control strategy in order to make specific decisions at every decision point (Lovett and Anderson 1996, Broadbent et al. 1986, Mackinnon and Wearing 1985, Rapoport 1975). Therefore, a possible approach to obtaining better outcomes in dynamic decision making is the improvement of control strategies. Frequently, the modification of a control strategy results in better outcomes for some decisions but fails for others. We are interested in what distinguishes the instances in which the strategy is successful from the instances in which it fails to achieve a desired outcome. Without this knowledge, a strategy can only be modified either for all decision instances or based on conjectures about the conditions of failures. In either case, the modification may also affect instances that are already controlled successfully, which may result in waste by expending additional resources unnecessarily, or by introducing actions that result in additional failures.

To identify opportunities for improvements in control strategies, we develop an approach based on machine learning and control theory named PROCEDO— PRediction of Control Errors in Dynamic Contexts. PROCEDO is a systematic approach to (a) determine conditions under which a given control strategy fails and (b) improve the control strategy under these conditions. Specifically, we state the determination of conditions that are predictive of failures as a data mining question: what information is contained in the state of the system at a given point in time that is indicative of a control failure occurring at a later time? This enables the application of data mining techniques, such as decision tree induction, to predict future failures based on present conditions. The control strategy can then be improved for the conditions under which failures are predicted. Using domain-specific knowledge of outcome and cost trade-offs, PROCEDO evaluates different strategy modifications for the purpose of selecting the most cost-effective one, which then is incorporated into an improved control strategy.

To demonstrate the viability of PROCEDO, we apply it to the environment of chronic disease care, more specifically, to the decision problems faced by physicians in the treatment of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Diabetes care is concerned with the prevention of adverse clinical events, such as heart attacks and strokes, by bringing a patient to evidence-based goals for health characteristics, such as blood sugar and blood pressure. The domain of diabetes care fits the definition of a complex ill-structured problem domain (Funke 1991, Simon 1973, Reitman 1965) in that it contains complex, nonlinear interdependencies and constraints, processes that cannot be directly observed or inspected (patient physiology), as well as delay of feedback with respect to decisions taken (treatment effects are not instantaneous).

In §2, we present the related literature introducing relevant concepts from dynamic decision-making and data mining literature as well as provide background on diabetes care. In §3, we provide a formal description of the proposed PROCEDO approach. In §4, we illustrate each step of the proposed approach using the diabetes care example. In §5, we discuss the outcomes from applying PROCEDO and demonstrate the flexibility of this approach by evaluating its performance on three very different initial strategies. In §6, we demonstrate the general applicability of PROCEDO by also using it in a manufacturing domain. In §7, we summarize and discuss the contributions as well as potential future work.

## 2. Related Work

We first define complex, ill-structured dynamic decisionmaking contexts and demonstrate the use of control theory as a metaphor for these contexts. These contexts do not typically lend themselves to formal analysis, therefore the ability to recognize patterns in empirical data is emphasized. Traditionally, the inability to deal with patterns is a limitation of applying control theory in a dynamic context (Bainbridge 1981). We address this issue by showing how data mining can extend the control theory metaphor used in prior dynamic decisionmaking literature by providing a mechanism for pattern recognition. Finally, we provide background about the diabetes care example and discuss its relevance and suitability for the demonstration of the PROCEDO approach.

## 2.1. Dynamic Decision Making, Control Theory, and Complex Problems

Edwards (1962) lists the following criteria for a decisionmaking environment to be considered dynamic: (a) a series of decisions is required to reach a goal; (b) the decisions are path dependent; (c) the state of the decision problem changes as a result of actions based on decisions as well as on its own. In addition, dynamic decisions are constrained by time, as seen, for example, in emergency management and patient care (Brehmer 1992).

All but the simplest of dynamic decision-making problems have not been found to be tractable to the analytical normative-descriptive approach, which yielded insights into static decision making by comparing decision-maker performance to optima derived from theory (Rapoport 1975). In complex, ill-structured contexts, decision-maker performance is impaired by the presence of delay of feedback, the inability to inspect the entire system to form a strong mental model, and the need to balance competing goals (Funke 1991). When delay of feedback is present, decision makers tend to apply more actions than necessary, with the result that the system is overburdened after the delay expires (Forrester 1971, Sterman 1989). The inability to inspect the system has also been shown to limit decision-making performance in dynamic contexts (Brehmer 1992). The complexity introduced by multiple competing goals can be illustrated using the example of chronic disease care, in which one goal is to reduce the risk of heart attacks, another is to reduce the risk of kidney failure, and yet another is to keep the cost of treatment manageable. Because of the inherent complexity of the context, an action may positively affect some aspects but negatively affect others in the long-term without a direct indication (feedback) to the decision maker.

The implication from the complex dynamic decision contexts mentioned above is that dynamic decision making can be considered as a discrete process. Therefore, Rapoport (1975), Mackinnon and Wearing (1985), and Broadbent et al. (1986) have all suggested that using control theory from engineering as a metaphor may provide useful perspectives for the study of dynamic decision making. Control theory is only used as a metaphor because—as both Bainbridge (1981) and Brehmer (1992) have pointed out—the mathematical formalism of traditional control theory (tracing back to Maxwell 1868 and in modern forms relying heavily on calculus of differential equations) stems from continuous process control and cannot be readily applied to the discrete nature and pattern recognition aspects of decision making in dynamic environments. We discuss later how data mining enables us to fill this gap with respect to pattern recognition.

We extend the control theory metaphor (Brehmer 1992) by viewing the state of the system to be controlled as observable and affected through actions. The system is governed by a control strategy, a set of rules that manipulates the system based on its current state. As shown in Figure 1, the state of a process control system can be characterized by three sets of variables: controlled variables, manipulated variables, and disturbance variables (Seborg et al. 2004). The goals of a process are defined in terms of its controlled variables, e.g., by giving a threshold or range for which the variable is considered under control. Control is achieved through “feedback” strategies that consider observable information from the system to be controlled or through “feedforward” strategies, which adjust their actions based on predictions about future states (Brehmer 1992).

A simple example of a dynamic control problem is controlling the speed of a car. The controlled variable is the speed. Manipulated variables are what the control strategy can affect in order to bring the controlled variables to their desired goals, for example, the amount of pressure applied to the accelerator or brake pedal. Disturbance variables are not influenced by the control strategy but do influence the controlled variables, for example, a driver may hold a car at constant speed on a flat road but the car will slow down when an incline is encountered. The slope of the road is a disturbance variable. The driving example also incorporates feedback: the driver is aware of the car’s speed. Most drivers adjust the speed of the car easily when encountering an incline. This particular context provides features that enable good performance: (i) instantaneous feedback; (ii) the fact that the driver has a mental model that enables control, in this case of the relationship between applying the accelerator and the brake and the corresponding reactions of the car; and (iii) a single objective criterion for success, namely, the speed of the car. Note that all these features are absent in the complex problems we are addressing in this paper.

Brehmer (1992) argues that some of the aspects of complex problems, e.g., delay of feedback and lack of transparency, reduce the performance of feedback control strategies but can be addressed by feedforward strategies that make predictions about the future state of the system. For example, if delay of feedback is anticipated, a decision maker can rely on the prediction of what his action will accomplish instead of either overreacting because no feedback is currently present or not reacting at all, i.e., waiting for the delay to pass. However, if it is not easy to inspect a system because one can only observe the system’s changes in response to actions, how can a mental model suitable for feedforward control be formed? The PROCEDO approach presented in this paper proposes that feedforward behavior can be introduced by empirically predicting the success or failure of a given decision strategy and making appropriate adjustments in anticipation of failures.

## 2.2. Data Mining

Modern processes are frequently monitored using information systems that record large amounts of information about the process. For example, in air travel, consider the flight recorder devices (“black boxes” on aircraft) that capture all pertinent flight data and record the voices on the flight deck. Such data can then be used to identify failure conditions in a process and make adjustments. Recording process information frequently results in very large data sets that are not necessarily tractable to analysis by individuals or usefully interpreted using common statistical techniques such as linear regression or t-tests. For example, consider how almost any difference is statistically significant when making comparisons on data sets with a million records or more. Data mining methods have emerged to provide ways to uncover interesting patterns or learn new information from very large data sets (Friedman 2001).

As pointed out above, a drawback of using formal control theory is its limitation with regards to pattern recognition (Bainbridge 1981, Brehmer 1992). By proposing to pair the control theory metaphor with a data mining technique, we incorporate a pattern recognition mechanism into process control with the aim to improve outcomes. Specifically, we use data mining to identify conditions under which a control strategy is unable to bring the controlled variables to their desired values, which we will refer to as control failure.

Sadoyan et al. (2004) have shown that data mining offers potential for improvement of process control by developing a data mining algorithm based on rough set theory specifically tailored toward improving an industrial context, namely, rapid tool making. We set out to develop a general, domain-independent approach that employs commonly used and proven data mining techniques to improve feedback control strategies for ill-structured dynamic decision-making contexts.

Traditional process mining approaches attempt to discover and document the nature of processes that generate an observed chain of events (van der Aalst and Weijters 2004). In contrast, the approach we propose in this paper aims to control the process using observations and available actions to affect the process without requiring the discovery of a model of the underlying process itself. This makes PROCEDO valuable in processes that are difficult to analyze but that react consistently to interventions; e.g., some aspects of human physiology are difficult to analyze, but can be affected through certain medications. However, some patients may have factors, such as genetics, that make medications ineffective for them. Given enough relevant observations, PROCEDO identifies these nuances, allowing the formulation of customized, more effective control strategies.

Parallels can be drawn between PROCEDO and reinforcement learning techniques since both represent approaches to explore different candidate strategies (policies in reinforcement learning). Reinforcement learning techniques are generally used for learning tasks where different policies can be explored by adopting a Markov decision process (MDP) view of the learning environment with the objective of estimating an explicit value function, i.e., an estimate of the return from taking different actions (Sutton and Barto 1998). To estimate the return, different actions have to be attempted, resulting in a trade-off between exploration and exploitation. In contrast, PROCEDO is designed for a specialized class of learning tasks—improving decision strategies in dynamic, ill-structured environments— and some of these environments might not be easily representable using the MDP-type models. In other words, PROCEDO does not require a Markovian view of the learning environment, but rather represents a data-driven heuristic approach that focuses only on the regions of the learning space that are prone to errors and modifies decision strategies through attenuation or augmentation for that region. Therefore, PROCEDO is essentially an exploration-oriented approach, which biases exploration in desirable directions by identifying shortcomings of the current strategy and incorporating compensating actions (making use of domain and expert knowledge) that are expected to improve outcomes. Exploitation is used by PROCEDO only to develop a sufficient database of the outcomes obtained with the current strategy, which will be used for inferences of where to explore next in order to potentially obtain further strategy improvements. Furthermore, PROCEDO is a semiautomated approach that can also incorporate simulation models of the underlying environment as well as domain expertise to reduce the cost of strategy exploration and evaluation.

PROCEDO employs established data mining techniques to recognize patterns that result in control failures, i.e., under-performance or excessive, wasteful actions. Thus, it must be able to provide a classification between successful outcomes and failures. Although many classification techniques are available, such as neural networks, support vector machines, naive Bayesian classifiers, and decision tree learning (Tan et al. 2006), the goal of PROCEDO is to output a set of conditions for which a control strategy can be improved. We have chosen decision tree learning algorithms, in particular the C4.5 algorithm (Quinlan 1987), specifically for their ability to partition instances to their outcome classes based on the most informative distinguishing criteria. They provide a set of readily interpretable conditions that can be incorporated into a control strategy using Boolean logic. Interpretability is a desirable characteristic for many real-world dynamic decision contexts; e.g., physicians can be reluctant to take treatment action based on a black box prediction technique (Avins 2010), such as a neural network. Furthermore, the interpretable nature of a decision tree node provides information that can guide the adjustment of the control strategy because the characteristics of the instances in which the strategy performs poorly are human readable and easy to encode as IF-THEN statements in computer programs or database selection criteria.

## 2.3. Example Context: Diabetes Care

In addition to the development of PROCEDO, we demonstrate its feasibility and flexibility by applying it to a specific problem, the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus patients.

Diabetes management is an instance of an illstructured, complex dynamic decision-making problem in the healthcare domain. The goal of treatment in diabetes care is to bring and keep a patient’s blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol at evidence-based goals in order to prevent adverse events, such as heart attacks and strokes, in the long-term. The treatment requires regular office visits, at which physicians monitor progress and adjust treatment regimens if necessary. The treatments interact in complex ways; for example, a treatment intended for lowering blood pressure can also negatively affect indicators for kidney function and consequently contraindicate a blood glucose treatment. The disease progresses on its own but is affected by the physician’s treatment. Physicians formulate strategies for the treatment of chronic diseases by adopting tools to facilitate the decision-making process, for example, by using lab tests, clinical guidelines, and the Physician Desk Reference (PDR 2011). Last but not least, decision makers in healthcare have to balance competing goals, for example, bringing patients to evidence-based goals, reducing the risk of adverse events, and managing costs.

The treatment of diabetes is a problem of high societal interest. In the United States in 2011, 25.8 million Americans were estimated to be diabetic and another 79 million to be prediabetic, i.e., have above-normal levels of blood sugar. Diabetes increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes two–four times, increases the likelihood of having high blood pressure, can cause blindness, kidney failure, and sometimes makes it necessary to amputate limbs. Diabetes also has a very high cost to society. In 2012, diabetes has been responsible for \$176 billion dollars of direct medical costs and another \$69 billion in indirect costs such as disability, work loss, and premature mortality (CDC 2011, ADA 2013).

Demonstrating that PROCEDO offers a way to identify improvements in diabetes care strategies contributes not only to the validation of the proposed approach, but also to making IS research relevant to healthcare. It has been argued that, in spite of the strong push for pervasive use of information systems, especially electronic health records, in the United States even backed by government mandate, diabetes patients have yet to see sustained significant benefits from this technology (O’Connor et al. 2005). Considering that electronic treatment records provide a record of the discrete process of treatment, PROCEDO can potentially identify conditions under which treatments have been insufficient or excessive and provide evidence to consider when formulating new clinical guidelines with the ultimate goal of improving outcomes for patients in a cost-effective manner.

To demonstrate the efficacy of applying PROCEDO to a clinical problem without a randomized clinical trial, we employ simulation. Simulation allows for the estimation of within-patient differences of various treatment strategies and enables the creation of a large number of treatment cases in a short amount of time. It is an idealized trial since perfect copies of a patient can be created for each intervention. Additionally, in this context, simulation makes it possible to investigate baseline treatment policies such as “no additional treatment” that would raise ethical concerns in real trials. To be of value, the simulation must be based on a plausible model of diabetes patients. For the diabetes care example used throughout the paper, we use the Simcare patient model described by Dutta et al. (2005)

and McCabe et al. (2010). This model represents individual diabetes patients and their reactions to clinical treatments. The model has been validated for coherence and correspondence against medical literature, randomized clinical trials, and observed treatment effects from individual patients in real clinical practice (McCabe 2012, McCabe et al. 2010).

The synthetic patients simulated using the Simcare model are treated based on a control strategy, which contains rules for the prescription of diet and exercise; the sequence in which medications can be prescribed; the sequence of doses that can be titrated for each medication; treatment algorithms, for example, for the administration of insulin; and decisions about how frequently patient visits are scheduled to adjust treatment. Medications for blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol are prescribed and titrated in a predetermined sequence (after considering contraindications). To demonstrate that PROCEDO can improve a wide variety of control strategies by making appropriate modifications, i.e., augmenting or attenuating where necessary, we have used three very different initial treatment strategies in our experiment, as will be discussed below.

## 3. Prediction of Control Errors in Dynamic Contexts (PROCEDO): An Overview of the Proposed Approach

The proposed approach is described below in general as well as using a specific example in the context of chronic disease care.

Consider a system that is to be controlled in a dynamic decision context as described above. For example, this system may represent a chronic disease patient. Consistent with Seborg et al. (1986), the state of the system is represented by the tuple $( c , m , d )$ where c is the set of controlled state variables and the goal of the process control is to bring these to desired values (for example, for a diabetic patient, blood sugar is a controlled variable); m represents the set of manipulated state variables that can be affected by the decision maker and which in turn affect c (the amount of medications, like insulin, that affect blood sugar is an example of a manipulated variable); and d denotes the set of disturbance state variables that affect c but cannot be affected by the decision maker. An example of a disturbance variable is a patient’s age, which correlates with increased blood sugar and affects the risks of diabetes-related complications but cannot be affected by a physician.

In dynamic decision contexts, the system can also change over time autonomously (Edwards 1962). The state of the system at a specific point in time t is denoted by $( c _ { t } , m _ { t } , \dot { d _ { t } } )$ . In complex, ill-structured contexts, the effect of time on the system can be an unknown function that modifies the state of the system for the passage of the amount of time ã. We represent this function as $T ( c _ { t } , m _ { t } , d _ { t } , \Delta )$ 5. If the system is uncontrolled, its state is determined by the following equation:

$$
(c _ {t + \Delta}, m _ {t + \Delta}, d _ {t + \Delta}) = T (c _ {t}, m _ {t}, d _ {t}, \Delta).
$$

The purpose of controlling the system is to bring the controlled state variables c to a designated goal. The decision-maker’s task is to manipulate m at discrete points in time so that, with the passage of time, the system will reach a goal state. For this purpose, the decision maker adopts a control strategy, a way of making and executing decisions at every decision point. In this paper, we consider the cases for which the decision maker adopts feedback control strategies, i.e., strategies that take the state variables $( c , m , \bar { d } )$ of the system to be controlled as inputs and output new values for the manipulated variables in m in order to bring the system toward a goal state. The initial control strategy (version $n = 0 )$ refers to the original strategy of making decisions based on the state $( c , m , d )$ about how to modify the set of manipulator variables m in order to control the system. The goal of PROCEDO is to define successive versions of the control strategy, each version representing an improvement over the prior one. Because of the complex, ill-structured, and dynamic nature of the problem domains, control strategies typically are not analytically tractable, that is, they cannot be improved using traditional optimization techniques, linear programming, or numerical gradient descent approaches (Nocedal and Wright 2006). Therefore, PROCEDO serves as a heuristic approach to improve control strategies based on empirical observations of outcomes and costs associated with a strategy, i.e., without knowledge of its inner workings.

To determine whether strategy x represents an improvement over strategy y, a strategy “effectiveness” metric is needed, which we denote as ê. In other words, two strategies x and y are equally effective when $\Phi ( x ) = \Phi ( y )$ . In complex domains, effectiveness is usually a function of more than one dimension where there may exist trade-offs between different dimensions. In many environments, this trade-off can naturally be represented in two dimensions in terms of costs and benefits (or outcomes), and we use this “cost-effectiveness” trade-off as the operationalization of ê in the proposed PROCEDO approach. Figure 2 provides a stylized illustration of the cost-effectiveness based strategy evaluation. In particular, strategies x and y are depicted to reside on the same “isobar,” the same dotted line representing equal performance in terms of cost-effectiveness. Please note that, in the examples used for this paper, the outcomes represent adverse clinical events (heart attacks, strokes, etc.) and, therefore, it is desirable to minimize ê. Thus, strategy z in Figure 2 performs worse than x and y because $\Phi ( z ) > \Phi ( x )$ and $\Phi ( z ) > \Phi ( y )$

As discussed above, one feature of complex, illstructured domains is that they typically feature multiple, competing goals. Therefore, the trade-offs between outcomes and costs are highly domain dependent and, consequently, ê is specified by a decision maker based on domain knowledge. For example, in diabetes care, cost-effectiveness is a function of the number of patients at goal, the cost of treatment, the expected number of adverse events and their estimated cost, and a cost-effectiveness threshold of the maximum amount of treatment cost to prevent events (Gold et al. 1996, Petitti 2000, Chapman et al. 2000, Bell et al. 2006).

A high-level overview of PROCEDO is provided in Figure 3 (on the left). PROCEDO is designed as an iterative process that attempts to improve an initial control strategy, control-strategy , in terms of cost-effectiveness as measured by ê. In each iteration of PROCEDO, the current version of control-strategy<sub>n</sub> is evaluated (Step 1), and the improvements to this version are generated to create candidates for the next version of the control strategy (Step 2). Each candidate strategy is evaluated using ê, and the candidate with the best ê is chosen as control-strategy<sub>n+1</sub> (Step 3).

Given this framework, the problem of strategy improvement is essentially reduced to the problem of intelligent generation of candidate strategies (Step 2), which constitutes a challenging task because of the ill-structured nature of the dynamic decision-making domains of interest. Thus, a key contribution of this paper is developing an approach for generating candidate strategies. This step is shaded in Figure 3 on the left and illustrated in more detail on the right. To generate strategy improvement candidates, PRO-CEDO uses the notion of control failures. Because of the two-dimensional nature of the cost-effectiveness based evaluation, where the performance of a control strategy can be measured by outcomes achieved and cost incurred, control failures can be of two types. Specifically, a control failure can reflect either an insufficient action, which adversely impacts outcomes, or an excessive action, which adversely impacts cost. We refer to the former as failures of type E<sup>−</sup> and the latter as failures of type E<sup>+</sup>. PROCEDO uses domain-specific knowledge of quality measures, which define whether a control failure occurred for some instance and, if so, of what type.

PROCEDO uses machine learning techniques to identify the conditions predicting these failures (Step 2a of the PROCEDO process). For the top k identified failure conditions (Step 2b), strategy modifications are generated as candidates for the improved control strategy (Step 2c). Choosing value k that is smaller than the total number of identified failure conditions limits the number of conditions for which improvements have to be generated and evaluated in order to increase the scalability of the proposed approach.

A key element of the proposed approach is that modifications are added so that improved control strategies resulting from the application of PROCEDO are always of the form depicted in Figure 4 and specified in Algorithm control-strategy. Every new version n + 1 of the control strategy forms a wrapper around the previous control strategy n and will only modify an action if a failure is predicted. For example, control strategy version n = 1 will take additional action only if the initial control strategy (version n = 0) is predicted to fail; otherwise the initial strategy is executed. In other words, PROCEDO is able to directly reuse the well-performing aspects of the original strategy and is applied only when it is necessary to prevent empirically predicted failures. This is an important aspect of the proposed approach, because it makes PROCEDO not only an effective tool for strategy improvement but also for strategy testing and diagnostics (for finding the problematic aspects of a given strategy that lead to control failures). It is also important to point out that every version of the control strategy takes the same inputs and produces outputs of the same format, making this approach independent from the initial control strategy. The original strategy can be a black box and yet PROCEDO can design incremental improvements to wrap around it, transparent to the user of the control strategy since the interface (inputs or outputs) does not change.

## 4. Step-by-Step Description of PROCEDO Using the Diabetes Care Example

As a starting point for PROCEDO, an initial control strategy must be selected. In our diabetes care experiments (as will be discussed in §5), we use three diverse treatment strategies, informed by healthcare research literature. The treatment strategies provide the order and dosages in which medications can be prescribed as well as rules governing the prescription of insulin and the frequency of office visits. Examples of actions available to these treatment strategies are “prescribe 500 mg Metformin<sup>1</sup>” and “schedule next visit in 90 days.”

Given the initial control strategy, our proposed approach, as represented by Figure 3 and Algorithm improve-control, can be applied to obtain improvements to this strategy. We will explain PROCEDO step by step below using the context of the diabetes care example.

## 4.1. Step 1: Run Process and Collect Data

First, data is collected by applying run-process (discussed in §3) with the initial control strategy control-strategy to the system to be controlled or a model thereof. At given points in time, e.g., patient visits, the observed state is recorded to build history set . In the example, we treat synthetic patients simulated using the Simcare patient model mentioned in §2.3. This model can be represented as follows: $X _ { t + \Delta } = P a t i e n t M o d e l ( X _ { t } , A _ { t } , \Delta ) .$ , i.e., it takes patient state $X _ { t }$ at time t and the prescribed treatment actions $A _ { t }$ (e.g., diet, exercise, oral medications, and insulin) as inputs and provides the resulting patient state $X _ { t + \Delta }$ at a future point in time $t + \Delta$ (Dutta et al. 2005, McCabe et al. 2010). The state vector $X _ { t }$ includes, for example, patient characteristics (blood glucose level, systolic blood pressure, etc.) and current doses of medication. $X _ { t }$ can be decomposed into controlled, manipulated, and disturbance variables $( c _ { t } , m _ { t } , d _ { t } )$ . For example, this decomposition will assign blood glucose and blood pressure to the set of controlled variables, medication doses to manipulated variables, and patient age to disturbance variables.

As mentioned earlier, a specific treatment regimen is translated into an algorithm to yield the initial control strategy control-strategy . This strategy defines what actions are applied to a patient at a given simulated office visit. Using the patient model and initial control strategy, we simulate one year of treatment for 10,000 diabetes patients. The population of 10,000 patients has the same distributional characteristics as a real population under care in a U.S. Midwestern healthcare system. run-process4	1 T 1 n5 can now be run, setting 	 to contain the 10,000 patients as instances for the process, T = 1 year, and $n = 0$ to use the initial control strategy. The result of this step is history set  containing the state of every patient at every visit.

In summary, this step is responsible for collecting the data about the performance of a given control strategy, which is then analyzed and modified in the subsequent steps, as described below. Note that, consistently with the lack of transparency of complex, ill-structured problems and in order to provide a truly domain-independent approach to strategy improvement, PROCEDO is not aware of the inner workings of either the Simcare patient model nor the treatment regimen. It only observes the actions taken by the treatment strategy and the resulting health outcomes.

## 4.2. Step 2a: Identify Preconditions to Failure

As discussed in §3, PROCEDO uses the domain-specific quality measures to determine whether a control failure has occurred for some instances. Specifically, a domainspecific quality measure E is required that specifies the conditions for insufficient performance (i.e., failures of type E<sup>−</sup>) and the conditions for excessive or wasteful performance $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ failures of type E<sup>+</sup>). In chronic disease care, the goal of the treatment strategy is to bring a patient’s blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol (controlled variables) to evidence-based goals (defined in Table 1) in order to minimize the risk of complications or adverse clinical events, such as heart attacks and strokes. At the same time, excessive treatment may be harmful to the patient or waste resources of the healthcare system. Therefore, let $\tau _ { i }$ represent the time when patient i reached evidence-based goals for the first time during the course of treatment, and let T represent the required time by which the patient should be at goal (in our experiments, T is equal to one year). We define the following domain-specific quality measure E for any given patient i:

In other words, if the patient, who is not at evidencebased goals in the beginning of treatment, is not able to arrive at evidence-based goals in the required timeframe, the actions taken by the treatment strategy were insufficient (failure $\dot { E ^ { - } } ) ;$ on the other hand, if the patient was able to arrive at goals in less than half the required time, the actions taken by the treatment strategy were overly aggressive or excessive (failure E<sup>+</sup>). In the chronic disease context, insufficient progress is often the result of errors of omission; for example, a physician may not have attended to a condition that needed to be treated because of other acute problems (Parchman et al. 2007). Excessive actions can occur in the form of overtreatment, which can adversely affect patients; for example, a medical safety concern was raised by a recent randomized clinical trial, which showed that bringing patients to evidencebased goals in very aggressive timeframes was associated with increases in patient mortality (ACCORD Study Group 2008). Excessive actions can also raise a cost-effectiveness concern in that excessive resources (e.g., scheduling unnecessary visits) are used to bring patients to evidence-based goals when less intensive treatment would be able to do the same. Applying modified strategies to such patients may eliminate these control failures. The ability to model control failures as insufficient (E<sup>−</sup>) or excessive (E<sup>+</sup>) actions within a cost-effectiveness framework makes the proposed approach applicable in many diverse settings. However, in some applications, control failures can be much more complex (e.g., prescribing an experimental drug that may improve the patient’s condition in one aspect but, at the same time, may have very serious side effects), requiring more careful and nuanced definitions of benefits versus costs as well as insufficient versus excessive actions. PROCEDO handles this complexity by providing interpretable results and relying on human experts to suggest appropriate improvement actions.

Each instance (i.e., patient) in history set  is evaluated according to the aforementioned metric E to assess control failures. The question at the core of PROCEDO is: What information at a given point in time predicts that a control failure occurs in the future? After recording the results of run-process and identifying control failures, data mining is employed to investigate this problem. Specifically, a data mining problem is set up to find a set of conditions  that have predictive value for future failures. PROCEDO generates  by applying a data mining algorithm, which is represented by identify-failure-conditions() in Algorithm improve-control, to the recorded history . Out of the set of conditions , a subset can then be selected as candidates for the predicate predict-failure (as will be discussed in §4.3). A high-level depiction of identify-failure-conditions is provided in Figure 5.

To identify the particular conditions under which a control strategy fails, a data mining technique with interpretable results has to be used for identify-failureconditions(). We chose a decision tree algorithm for this purpose, specifically the C4.5 algorithm (Quinlan 1993). (In other domains, where prediction of failures without interpretability is acceptable, other techniques such as neural networks may also be used.) Decision tree induction uncovers informative patterns relating patient state to future control failures. Each node in the decision tree represents components of system state that have predictive power for whether the control strategy will succeed or fail.

Boolean conditions can then be constructed by following the tree from the root to the leaves and conjoining each intermediate node with a logical AND (Quinlan 1987). For the diabetes care example, the C4.5 algorithm was used to build the decision trees based on 25 distinct patient state variables (including demographics, measurements of patient state, and medication dosages). The decision tree models were built and evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. A fragment of a generated decision tree is shown in Figure 6; in our experiments, the learned decision trees typically contained between 15 and 40 nodes.

In the tree in Figure 6, three conditions predict control failures for the initial strategy: if a patient has a systolic blood pressure (SBP) greater than 155 mmHg (node (1)); or if a patient’s SBP is less than or equal to 140 mmHg, $\mathrm { A 1 c \leq 7 . 3 \% }$ and LDL ≤ 153 mg/dL (node (2)); or if a patient’s SBP ≤ 140 mmHg, A1c > 803% and creatinine > 1035 mg/dL (node (4)). Patients in nodes (1) and (4) are not brought to goal within one year of treatment (failures of type E<sup>−</sup>), and patients in node (2) are treated too aggressively (E<sup>+</sup> failures). On the other hand, it is predicted that the initial control strategy is successful in bringing patients with SBP ≤ 140 mmHg, A1c ≤ 703% and LDL > 153 mg/dL (node (3)) to goal in the desired timeframe.

The goal of PROCEDO is to identify control failures, so the success nodes are disregarded. A subset of conditions (for which the candidate improvements will be designed) is then chosen from the resulting set  in the next step.

After enumerating all the conditions contained in the decision tree, k most promising conditions from  (denoted as $\mathbb { F } ^ { k } )$ are selected as candidates for predicate predict-failure , i.e., the failure condition for which the control strategy will be improved:

In the extreme case when k = , PROCEDO could take all failure subgroups identified by the decision tree and evaluate every possible modification for every possible such subgroup. Since an evaluation of one modification for one subgroup may involve a comprehensive and computationally costly simulation in some domains (e.g., simulation of treating 10,000 patients for one year in our case), and there could possibly be several dozens of failure nodes with several possible modifications for each, the scalability of such an approach becomes questionable. This step of PRO-CEDO is used as an intelligent heuristic to narrow down the possible conditions for improvement.

To make meaningful improvements, it is desirable that the selected conditions are of a certain predictive quality, e.g., in terms of predictive accuracy and support (Tan et al. 2006). Intuitively, to gain the most from adjusting the strategy, these conditions should have a large number of true positives Q<sup>+</sup>, i.e., correctly identified failure instances, and a low number of false positives Q<sup>−</sup>.

To illustrate this, we evaluate condition (1) “systolic blood pressure > 155 mmHg” in the example decision tree (Figure 6) described above. Here, a true positive is a patient who had an initial systolic blood pressure greater than 155 mmHg and did not reach goal within one year of treatment (since it is a failure of type E<sup>−</sup>), while a false positive represents a patient who was brought under control in this timeframe in spite of having an initial blood pressure greater then 155 mmHg.

The total number of instances for which the condition holds is given by the number of all positives, i.e., $Q ^ { + } + Q ^ { - }$ . Two common metrics to evaluate decision tree rules are accuracy and support. Accuracy represents the proportion of instances captured by the candidate condition that are in fact true positives, i.e., $Q ^ { + } / ( Q ^ { + } + Q ^ { - } )$ . Improving the control strategy based on the false positives would not be productive and may in fact be harmful, for example, in the context of patient care. Therefore, we consider accuracy as the most important metric in the diabetes care example.

Additional discussion about the importance of accuracy as a selection metric is provided in §6.3.

The support of a candidate condition is the absolute number of instances it contains, $Q ^ { + } + Q ^ { - }$ <sup>−</sup>. A minimum threshold for support is desirable to avoid the selection of a condition covering a small number of cases that may be due to an artifact of a particular data set rather than an underlying pattern in the process. If a rule accurately predicts a failure because it only applies to a single patient, it is questionable that it would generalize to a larger population. If more sophisticated selection criteria are desired, costs for true and false positives can be incorporated, in essence making the decision tree cost sensitive, for example, as discussed by Zhao (2007). Once the selection metrics have been decided, an algorithm select-failure-conditions can be implemented to choose rules from  in accordance with these metrics. ## 4.4. Steps 2c and 3: Create Strategy Modifications and Identify Best Improvement

Step 2b identified failure conditions $\mathbb { F } ^ { k }$ for candidate strategy modifications. Creating a set of possible modifications is the goal of create-modifications (Step 2c in our proposed improve-control algorithm). A modification is a set of rules to change the values of the manipulator variables m in a manner than will affect the system to be controlled more or less intensely than the previous control strategy, in order to eliminate or lessen the control failure.

The identification of an appropriate action in response to the selected failure condition can be highly dependent on the specifics of the application domain. PROCEDO is domain independent and, therefore, permits for each domain to apply their appropriate methods and domain expertise to identify and select improvements. This enables decision makers in a complex, ill-structured contexts to avail themselves of methods including, but not limited to, expert deliberation, use of documented guidelines, choosing from a formally specified set of candidate improvement actions, or simulation of possible alternatives to choose the most promising one. For the purposes of the diabetes care example, we use the simulation of incremental improvement alternatives that are vetted for medical safety. The importance of using machine learning techniques with interpretable results is further emphasized in this step—because the strategy improvements are being considered for the particular subgroup for which the failure condition holds, the subgroup characteristics (as identified by decision tree branches) must be available to the domain expert to be validated as medically appropriate for the subgroup. Also, note that simulation is just one (albeit convenient) way to evaluate candidate strategies. In some settings where simulation models are not available, the strategies can be tested using the actual process itself, in which case the additional cost of using the real process for candidate evaluation may need to be considered. We provide an example of applying PROCEDO in such a setting in §6.

The control strategy is augmented to eliminate type $E ^ { - }$ failures or attenuated to eliminate type $E ^ { + }$ failures. In diabetes care, intensification of a treatment strategy can be achieved in three major ways: (a) by treating more than one condition $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , blood glucose, blood pressure, or cholesterol) at a given visit; (b) by scheduling more frequent visits in order to have more opportunities to treat; or (c) by intensifying treatment itself, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , by administering a combination therapy of an oral agent and insulin in accordance with clinical guidelines (Mazze et al. 2005). Conversely, a treatment strategy can be attenuated by (a) treating fewer conditions at a given visit (if more than one is treated); (b) scheduling less frequent visits; or (c) using weaker treatments (single instead of combination medications, or smaller doses). We use this set of strategy modification approaches in our experiments; i.e., for each failure condition from $\mathbb { F } ^ { k } ,$ , we create three candidate augmentations or attenuations (depending on the failure type) with respect to the current version of control-strategy<sub>n</sub>.

## 4.5. Step 4: Iterate

After making an improvement to control-strategy<sub>n</sub>, the process $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ Steps 1–3) is run again, this time governed by the improved $_ { \mathit { c o n t r o l - s t r a t e g y } _ { n + 1 } . }$ . The states can again be recorded, quality outcomes assessed, conditions for additional improvement identified using data mining, and further improvements to the strategy can be made. If a point is reached at which no more actionable conditions are discovered, or the set of possible modifications has been exhausted, then PROCEDO has identified boundary conditions for this particular dynamic decision-making problem. For example, it may be possible that, even with all the available intensification options, a patient with a very high initial blood sugar cannot reach the evidence-based goal within one year of treatment.

In summary, we have proposed an approach to improve feedback control strategies that are employed in complex dynamic decision-making contexts. Although the initial control strategies may be pure feedback strategies, with each iteration PROCEDO introduces feedforward anticipation to the process by applying data mining to examine preconditions to control failures (Brehmer 1990, Seborg et al. 2004). Specifically, the approach identifies subgroups—in our example, subgroups of patients—for which the original strategy failed to achieve desirable outcomes, either by taking insufficient or excessive actions. For these subgroups, and only these subgroups, we propose the identification of an incremental modification that will eliminate the control failure so as to improve outcomes or avoid wasting resources.

Below, we demonstrate this iterative improvement process and present results from applying it to the example of diabetes care using three different initial treatment strategies.

## 5. Validation of the Proposed Approach:

PROCEDO-Modified Diabetes Care To demonstrate that PROCEDO improves control strategies by making appropriate modifications, augmenting or attenuating when necessary, we have created three scenarios with different starting points, i.e., three very different initial control strategies. Scenario 1 uses an extremely conservative initial treatment strategy referred to as MIN, informed by “usual care” research literature (Phillips et al. 2001, Selby et al. 2003, Shah et al. 2005). In stark contrast, the strategy in Scenario 2, referred to as MAX, is extremely aggressive (and consequently costly), informed by diabetes specialist guidelines (Mazze et al. 2005). Scenario 3 lies between the two extremes MIN and MAX and follows strategy EXP, informed by observations of experts in clinical practice (Ramsey et al. 2010).

In each scenario, we are interested in the strategy performance over one year of treatment. We evaluate the overall outcomes for each iteration in terms of (a) number of patients brought to evidence-based goals, (b) disutility of not being at goal, and (c) cost of treatment. The intent of bringing patients to evidencebased goals is to reduce their risk of adverse clinical events (e.g., heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure, etc.). Therefore, we quantify the disutility resulting from not being at goal by calculating the risk of such an event over a 10-year horizon using the UKPDS Risk Engine (Stevens et al. 2001) and applying Monte Carlo simulation to translate the risk estimate into events for the 10,000 simulated patients. Using cost estimates for adverse events and cost-effectiveness thresholds from healthcare research literature (Bell et al. 2006, O’Brien et al. 2003, Chapman et al. 2000, Gold et al. 1996, O’Brien et al. 1998, Petitti 2000), we compute costeffectiveness ê for each treatment strategy. Recall that function ê encodes the relevant domain knowledge to assess whether a strategy X performs better, worse, or the same as strategy Y in terms of cost-effectiveness. As before, in the graphs we will show a level of equal performance as a grey dotted line representing an isobar, i.e., each strategy on the same line is equally cost-effective. Better-performing strategies reside on isobars with intercepts lower on the y-axis.

All results presented in this section and Tables 4–6 are after one year of treatment. The conditions identified for predict-failure in Step 2b specify a subgroup of the patients for which the treatment strategy fails. In Scenario 1, the treatment strategy under-performs, in Scenario 2, the strategy tends to treat excessively, and in Scenario 3, the strategy is insufficient for some patients but may be excessive for others. The number of patients at goal represents how many patients reached evidence-based goals for all three conditions, i.e., blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol. The number of visits is the total number of patient/physician interactions. The number of glucose moves represents how many times a physician prescribed a blood sugar medication or increased the dose; blood pressure moves and cholesterol moves are analogous. The cost for treatment of all patients was calculated using estimates for the cost of office visits and medications provided by a U.S. midwestern healthcare system. Adverse events represents the number of adverse clinical events (e.g., heart attacks and strokes) experienced in the 10-year period following the treatment and was estimated using the UKPDS risk simulation (Stevens et al. 2001).

## 6. PROCEDO in Other Domains

PROCEDO is not limited to the medical domain but rather can be applied to any complex, ill-structured dynamic decision-making context that features ways to manipulate the system and provides feedback related to the variables to be controlled. PROCEDO gains considerable power and is applicable in many domains because of the fact that the underlying processes and initial control strategy do not have to be formally specified or explicitly modeled analytically. The incremental, wrapper-based approach can yield improvements even when the initial control strategy is a complete black box, as long as observations of the intermediate process outcomes are available. Importantly, this makes PROCEDO not only an effective tool for control strategy improvement but also for strategy testing and diagnostics, i.e., for finding the problematic aspects of a given strategy that lead to control failures. In the diabetes care example, PROCEDO was able to use simulation to evaluate candidate strategies. However, having simulation-based models of the underlying process is not a requirement; instead, PROCEDO can learn directly from the actual process itself, in which case the evaluation of candidate strategies may not be costless. To demonstrate this much broader use of PROCEDO, in this section we discuss its application to a proprietary real-world manufacturing task in the automotive industry, where PROCEDO does not have access to simulation models.

## 6.1. Manufacturing Example Context

In this example manufacturing context, a finished good is produced from a number of parts. One of these parts is a widget, a subassembly that itself consists of several parts. These parts are supplied by different manufacturers and the assembly process of the widget is nontrivial and requires manual labor. As a result, there is considerable variation in the quality of these widgets. If the quality is insufficient, the widget will fail in the final assembly step of the finished good and destroy the entire good (a very costly event). To reduce the likelihood of this failure, widgets can be reinforced (augmented, strengthened); however, reinforcement is time-consuming and costly. Therefore, it is desirable to reinforce only widgets that would fail otherwise. Each reinforcement action has different costs as well as different effects on the attributes of the widget. For example, a widget may be reinforced by adding an additional component, or by replacing a steel element with one of a higher grade steel. The initial quality of the parts provided by suppliers are disturbance variables, they affect the outcome (failures) but cannot be directly changed. Reinforcement affects variables that can be manipulated, such as dimensions of parts (e.g., by cutting or replacing a smaller part with a larger one) and how parts are joined to form the widget.

For this study, we only use simulation to represent the manufacturing process itself and determine failure rates. PROCEDO does not have access to the simulation and cannot use it for candidate evaluation. Instead, a limited pool of widgets is available, and these are consumed by the manufacturing process. PROCEDO will learn from these consumed instances, use means-ends analysis to identify potential improvements, consume additional instances to test these candidate strategies, and then incorporate them into the next iteration strategy.

In particular, each widget is described by 12 attributes $( A _ { 1 } , \ldots , A _ { 1 2 } )$ that reflect its quality. All attributes are standardized and represented by values between 0 and 1; however, the specific attribute values depend on the raw materials used to assemble the widget and the assembly process, and their distribution was modeled in consultation with a manufacturing domain expert. The widget failure function was also modeled in consultation with a manufacturing domain expert and is based on the values of $A _ { 1 } , \ldots , A _ { 1 2 }$ for each widget. More specifically, the failure function is a complex Boolean predicate that can include nonlinear combinations of the 12 attributes in its multiple conditions, e.g., $( A _ { 1 } \times A _ { 2 } < 0 . 0 7 5 ) \wedge ( A _ { 3 } < 0 . 2 ) \wedge \cdot \cdot \cdot$ . It is important to reiterate that PROCEDO is not aware of this failure function; in our experiments we use this function simply to mimic the outcomes of a realistic manufacturing process, and only these specific outcomes (i.e., successes or failures of the widgets) are what PROCEDO observes and makes decisions based on.

An initial pool of one million widgets is available. To establish a baseline, a first batch of 10,000 widgets is processed with an initial strategy of not reinforcing any widgets. For each widget, its attributes and the outcome (success or failure in final assembly) are recorded. If a widget fails, we consider this an error of type $E ^ { - }$ , i.e., a failure to reinforce a widget that should have been reinforced. We present two scenarios: one based on the empirical observations of the domain expert, and a hypothetical scenario of a switch to a supplier that delivers raw materials of lower quality $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ assuming different distributions of attributes $A _ { 1 } , \ldots , A _ { 1 2 }$ for the one million widgets).

## 6.2. Results for Manufacturing Scenario 1

In the first scenario, consistent with empirical observations of the manufacturing domain expert, 19.1% of the widgets fail in the final assembly step if additional reinforcement is not provided. This is a function of the quality of the raw materials that compose the widget. Based on recorded information, a decision tree is used to identify what combination of attributes predicts this failure. Three ways (i.e., candidate strategies) to reinforce the widgets are known in this manufacturing domain and can be broadly classified as (a) improve joins, (b) pretreat components before assembly (e.g., rethread), and (c) resize components, i.e., replace a part at the low end of a tolerance with a larger part or cut a component at or above the high end of tolerance.

For example, in the first iteration, based on the learned decision tree, PROCEDO found that widgets with $A _ { 3 }$ value below a certain threshold tend to fail. The aforementioned three candidate reinforcement strategies for this group of widgets are represented as (1a), (1b), and (1c) in Table 7. N represents the number of widgets that were used for any given iteration of the process, widgets remaining gives the number of widgets remaining in the pool after running the strategy named in the strategy column, failures (%) shows the failure rate of the widgets in this run. Mean reinforcement cost, i.e., average cost per widget spent on reinforcement, represents the cost aspect in the cost-effectiveness metric used for this domain. Mean failure cost is the sum of the cost of all failures (i.e., the cost of the finished good destroyed because of failure of the widget) divided by $N ,$ which represents the benefit aspect of the cost-effectiveness metric, where lower mean failure cost indicates higher quality of resulting widgets. Finally, mean total cost is the combination of the previous two metrics, i.e., the total cost resulting from spending on reinforcements and from remaining failures. It serves as the cost-effectiveness metric ê for this domain, which PROCEDO has the objective to minimize.

Each of the three candidate reinforcement strategies is tested with a batch of 4,000 widgets and the outcomes are recorded. For example, in the first iteration, candidate (1a) appears most promising based on its mean total cost and, thus, is selected as an augmented version of the initial strategy. The new strategy (Strategy 1) will now act exactly like the initial strategy, i.e., not reinforce any widgets, except when widgets have values of $A _ { 3 }$ below the identified threshold, in which case the reinforcement action (1a) is applied. There are 978,000 widgets remaining, and another 10,000 widgets are now processed with the new strategy. This yields row “Strategy $1 ^ { \prime \prime }$ in Table 7.

Note that there are no differences between Strategy 1 and candidate 1a. The differences in the results are due solely to the random widgets that were picked. More specifically, for the clarity and simplicity of presentation and for more efficient use of data, in our experiments we kept the following two steps conceptually separate: (i) test run of each candidate strategy for evaluation and selection purposes (4,000 widgets per each candidate strategy were sufficient to identify the performance differences between the strategies), and (ii) production run of the best candidate strategy for data generation and learning of the new failure prediction model (10,000 widgets were needed for the selected strategy to generate a sufficiently large and diverse data set for building a failure prediction model). Alternatively, we could have combined steps (i) and (ii) into one by running 10,000 widgets for each candidate strategy (i.e., for candidates 1a, 1b, and 1c) to begin with. Then, after determining that candidate 1a is the best performing strategy in the first iteration, we could have used the previously obtained 10,000 data points for this strategy to learn the new failure prediction model. However, the latter approach would use up 30,000 widgets for one iteration (10,000 widgets for each of the three candidate strategies) versus 22,000 widgets in our approach.

PROCEDO is then applied again in the same fashion, consuming widgets to evaluate candidate strategies in each iteration until they do not provide substantial improvements over the final strategy from the previous iteration. Again, at no point does PROCEDO have any knowledge about the failure function and is making decisions based on the observations of actual manufacturing failure rates. In this example, we observe four iterations of improvement, after which the final resulting strategy is applied to all the remaining 912,000 widgets. The final (iteration #4) strategy exhibits a failure rate of only 2.8% (compared to 19.1% for the initial strategy), while reducing the total cost by 69%. Even taking into account the cost of evaluating three different candidate strategies in each iteration, the overall failure rate for the entire pool of one million widgets is only 3.3%, with 67% total cost reduction as compared to the initial manufacturing process (row “total” in Table 7).

## 6.3. Data Size Considerations

In the above manufacturing scenario, over 90% of the widgets were treated with the final strategy, the performance of which, therefore, dominated the overall averages. In other words, we were implicitly assuming that we have a relatively large overall production time horizon (1,000,000 widgets) as compared to the smaller production time horizons needed for fine-tuning/evaluating the production process (e.g., 4,000 widgets), which is a realistic assumption in many industrial contexts. In traditional process control analyses, the assumption is often even more extreme, i.e., that the process will be run indefinitely (has an infinite horizon), in which case the total cost per widget will simply converge to the mean total cost for the final strategy.

To illustrate this more formally, let N be the total number of widgets, C the cost of one widget failure, and f the failure rate of the current strategy. Using the current strategy on all widgets, the total cost in the example is simply fNC, and the average cost per widget is fC. Now assume that we identify a prediction rule that identifies some fraction w of widgets that are predicted to fail. Also, assume that the cost of reinforcement (chosen by domain expert for this rule) applied to each selected widget is A. Therefore, the cost of reinforcement resulting from the application of the identified rule is wNA. Furthermore, since the failure prediction rule may not be 100% accurate, assume that some fraction t of these selected widgets does, in fact, fail $( \mathrm { i . e . , } t$ is the accuracy of the rule, as defined in the paper)—this fraction will be affected by the reinforcement. Finally, assume that the reinforcement will succeed (i.e., the widget will not fail after receiving this reinforcement) with probability s. In other words, wN is the number of widgets that are predicted to fail; among them, wtN is the number of widgets that will actually fail; and wtsN is the number of those widgets that will be fixed by the reinforcement action.

Therefore, wtsN widgets (that would otherwise fail) will no longer fail and, therefore, the total cost will decrease by wtsNC. In summary, the total cost for an adjusted strategy (i.e., the initial strategy plus the selected rule) can be computed by the following:

$$
T o t a l C o s t = f N C + w N A - w t s N C.
$$

Therefore, an additional PROCEDO iteration using the selected rule is worthwhile if it reduces the total cost of the original strategy, i.e., if $w N A - w t s N C < 0$ or, after simplification, if $s t > A / C$ . An iteration is cost-effective if the ratio of reinforcement cost and failure cost is smaller than the product of the accuracy of the chosen rule and the success rate of the selected reinforcement action.

The above calculations suggest that choosing the prediction rule with greater accuracy t provides more opportunities for a PROCEDO iteration to increase cost-effectiveness. At the same time, cost-effectiveness of a chosen PROCEDO iteration is independent of the number of widgets predicted to fail wN (i.e., independent of the support of the rule). This provides further motivation for our rule selection algorithm select-failure-conditions (Algorithm 4), which prioritizes failure conditions by predictive accuracy (while maintaining some restriction on support to avoid spurious rules).

It is interesting to note that widget failure cost C should be known from the domain expertise, and accuracy t can be estimated from building the decision tree. Widget reinforcement cost A and success rate s may not be known a priori, but can be estimated from a test run (candidate strategy evaluation). Therefore, a PROCEDO iteration could affect the total result negatively if it has a higher reinforcement cost, lower success rate, or lower accuracy than originally estimated, which would likely imply that the sample used to construct the rule was not representative. Using sufficiently powerful sample sizes would substantially reduce this possibility. Alternatively, a PROCEDO iteration could affect the total result negatively if all the candidate strategies turn out to be very costly; however, in that case PRO-CEDO can always revert back to the strategy from the previous iteration. In this case, the impact of the “wasted” iteration on the mean total cost would be extremely small, assuming that we have a significantly large overall production time horizon as compared to the test run sizes (which is a realistic assumption for many industrial settings, as discussed earlier).

To demonstrate the adaptability of PROCEDO, we set up a second manufacturing scenario, in which one million widgets have substantially different distributions of $A _ { 1 } , \ldots , A _ { 1 2 } .$ . In this scenario, the initial failure rate is 25.4%. Table 9 summarizes the PROCEDO process for Scenario 2. For example, PROCEDO is able to correctly identify that widget reinforcement action (c) is much more useful in this scenario (in contrast to Scenario 1, where it was significantly less cost-effective than other actions). In summary, even without a simulation model of the underlying process (i.e., using trial manufacturing runs with actual widgets), PROCEDO is able to adapt to different problem scenarios and achieve substantial improvements in cost-effectiveness.

Note that candidate evaluation can be further improved by (i) optimizing the sample size for candidate evaluation, (ii) optimizing the number of instances needed for model building, and (iii) evaluating strategies only on predicted failures. For (i), although we currently use the test runs with 4,000 widgets to evaluate each candidate strategy, design of experiment and statistical methods could potentially be used to identify a smaller yet sufficiently powerful sample size to identify significant differences between candidate strategies. For (ii), it is necessary to have sufficient instances to identify robust rules, otherwise PROCEDO might terminate prematurely because no additional rules meeting the accuracy and support requirements can be found. Each PROCEDO iteration eliminates more failures, thereby reducing the number of failures the decision tree can learn from. Therefore, an adaptive method that increases the number of instances for model building as errors become fewer could be used to improve the overall performance further. For (iii), if it would be possible to sort available widgets by their attributes a priori with very little or no cost, the candidate reinforcements could be evaluated only on the instances to which they will be applied. In our specific manufacturing setting, however, attribute measurement and reinforcement tasks are tightly coupled, and earlier sorting would be cost-prohibitive and problematic for lot control reasons (i.e., to make sure that raw materials provided by the manufacturer can be traced back to the exact production lot in which they were produced) according to the domain expert. Note that different domains may have additional, domain-specific constraints on sample sizes, e.g., based on minimum run quantities; in other words, it may simply not be economical to run batches smaller than a given quantity. In summary, further optimization of PROCEDO as described here remains an interesting avenue of future work.

Other domains to which PROCEDO could be applied include emergency management problems, such as firefighting. In this case, the control variables are characteristics of the fire (for example, coordinates to which it has spread, temperature, etc.); the manipulated variables could be methods of extinguishing, such as water and foam, and orders to send fire fighters to specific locations; and disturbance variables are wind speed and direction. The goal is defined as the complete extinction of the fire; metrics to assess success or failure could be the size of the area covered by fire at a given time or whether the fire expands rather than contracts (i.e., change in size over time). A conceivable outcome is that PROCEDO determines that a certain method of firefighting is inappropriate for a certain type of fire, or location, or type of building, or that a specific type of firefighting unit always fails to fight fires that meet certain initial conditions.

Traffic control at airports represents another possible domain in which PROCEDO could provide insights leading to improvement. Here, a general goal may be to reduce touchdown-to-gate time, however, during emergencies, other goals may override. A controlled variable is arrival at gate time, a manipulated variable is the gate assignment for a plane, and examples of disturbance variables are weather conditions, flight diversions, etc. PROCEDO may identify subgroups of flights that tend to cause delays before arriving at the gate, or show that there are consistently delays at certain intervals for which additional capacity would be necessary.

## 7. Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have developed PROCEDO, a principled approach to improve feedback control strategies, which guide decision making in complex, illstructured dynamic contexts, by drawing on literature from dynamic decision making, machine learning, and control theory. PROCEDO uses an inductive machine learning algorithm in order to identify areas for improvement and provides guidance in the selection of iterative improvements so that the resulting control strategy is more cost-effective, either by only consuming as little additional resources as necessary to achieve the desired goal, or by reducing the use of resources when a strategy overperforms. PROCEDO can be applied to dynamic contexts as long as the system to be controlled has observable variables and opportunities to augment or attenuate the control strategy. Observability is required to obtain feedback for the control strategy to be improved. To show the efficacy of PROCEDO, we have demonstrated its use in two application domains: chronic disease management and manufacturing. In diabetes management, PROCEDO was shown to be effective in (a) strengthening a very conservative treatment strategy, (b) attenuating an excessively aggressive treatment strategy, and (c) concurrently improving outcomes and reducing costs for a treatment strategy observed in clinical practice. In each case, PROCEDO has increased the cost-effectiveness of the control strategy. Starting from very different performance levels, all three initial strategies were brought to a similar, higher level of cost-effectiveness (see Figure 8). When applied to a manufacturing problem that does not allow for costless evaluation of candidate strategies, PROCEDO was able to achieve substantial cost-effectiveness improvements in the control strategy even after considering the cost of strategy exploration and evaluation. These results support that PROCEDO is a viable general-purpose method for identifying specific conditions for improvement and then iteratively fine-tuning the improvements in order to make cost-effective use of resources.

In addition to its effectiveness in improving a broad range of initial control strategies, PROCEDO can be customized in a number of ways to match the complex, ill-structured problem to which it is applied, such as by choice of different evaluation metrics (i.e., costeffectiveness trade-offs, as discussed earlier), different predictive techniques to identify failure conditions, different selection criteria for failure conditions, and different methods for modifying the control strategy.

In the diabetes care example, PROCEDO was shown to identify subgroups of patients for which treatment strategies needed to be modified. In this case, the selected quality metric assessed the overall treatment strategy, which did not differ between visits. In the same context, the proposed approach can be applied to a problem of finer granularity: improving the treatment patients receive at specific visits. Using an analogy to manufacturing, these two problems can be regarded as reducing the overall defect rate of the final good versus reducing errors during different phases of manufacturing. Rather than evaluating whether patients are at evidence-based goals at a given point in time, this formulation would consider whether patients make at least an expected minimum progress toward goal but, at the same time, do not make dangerously excessive progress. The range of acceptable progress for this formulation can be derived from randomized clinical trials and medication effect curves from drug studies.

Using the formal description provided in this paper, PROCEDO can be readily implemented in simulation environments to assess potential changes in control strategies and support process design. As we demonstrated with the diabetes care example, the proposed approach is easily paired with simulation technology for the modeling of process control improvements. Benefits of the pairing with simulation include the consistent availability of process data (no missing data), the ability to analyze and improve upon counterfactual scenarios (e.g., “no treatment” in medicine) to discover new strategies, and rapid speed of studies (e.g., no time and resources needed for subject recruitment, compensation, and related activities). Of course, for simulation studies to be of value, the simulation must accurately represent the system to be improved, for example, as indicated by the extensive validation work on the Simcare diabetes patient model (McCabe et al. 2010, McCabe 2012) used in this paper. Building such simulation models can be a challenge, especially in complex, ill-structured domains. The need for such a model to build a successful control strategy follows from the good regulator theorem, which states that every good regulator of a system is a model of that system (Conant and Ashby 1970). However, PROCEDO can be applied in some domains without a separate model, if the process to be controlled can serve as its own model. For example, as demonstrated in §6, in manufacturing, test (i.e., pilot) runs can be conducted directly on a production process without the need for building a separate model on how the process would behave. PROCEDO can then learn from the real-world observations to generate improvements.

It may be possible to make the approach even more powerful by using alternative data mining techniques or more fine-tuned decision trees than the C4.5 decision trees we have selected in the example for their interpretability. In addition, in special cases, when all the available adjustments to a control strategy can be formally specified and enumerated, it may be possible to formulate the approach in a way that an improvement is automatically selected and, therefore, a fully automated version of PROCEDO could be implemented (once domain-specific quality and evaluation metrics have been defined). However, such a fully automated approach could also prove to be costly in the domains without available simulation models, if the number of candidate adjustments that need to be explored in each iteration is large; this further emphasizes the usefulness of allowing domain experts to identify most appropriate strategy modifications in PROCEDO. The use of alternative data mining techniques and full automation possibilities constitute interesting and important directions for future work.

In conclusion, PROCEDO is a novel data-driven approach to aid decision makers operating in environments that require series of decisions, in which prior decisions constrain future choices, and the environment changes on its own as well as in response to actions taken by the decision maker. It may be especially useful in environments that tend to be challenging for human decision makers, namely, complex, ill-structured problems that feature delay of feedback, limited ability to examine the inner workings of a system, and multiple competing goals, such as treating chronic disease patients in a healthcare setting. In this particular context, PROCEDO has demonstrated the capability to produce significant cost-effective improvements to treatment strategies by capturing benefits from improved feedforward decision making while controlling costs by taking actions only where necessary. For example, in a real-world healthcare application, our proposed approach could be used to enhance electronic medical records and clinical information systems by providing actionable analysis of the data residing in these systems.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first approach for decision strategy analysis and improvement in complex, ill-structured, and dynamic environments. Additional research is needed to further our understanding of how decision performance in these domains can be improved and how information systems can support decision makers by extracting concise, interpretable, domain-appropriate, and actionable conclusions.
