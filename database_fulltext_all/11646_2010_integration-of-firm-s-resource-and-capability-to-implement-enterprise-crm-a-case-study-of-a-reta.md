## a b s t r a c t

This chronological in-depth case study describes that, although the integration of functional resources and capabilities including information resource, analytical capability, and operational capability might in<sup>fl</sup>uence retention process, those are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to bring the desired performances of acquiring and expanding customer relationships. Find-Equity Bank (a pseudonym) in this case validates that employee satisfaction must be a prerequisite of successful relationship acquisition and expansion processes, both which require employees to have more customer orientation relatively. Thus, this study emphasizes the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of tripartite resource and capability consisting of technology, process, and people for the successful implementation of an enterprisewide CRM strategy.

## 1. Introduction

The deregulation of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial industry, the expansion of the business area of global <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutes, and the diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation of consumers' needs for <sup>fi</sup>nancial services brought about intense competition in the retail bank industry [31]. Such keen competition made retail banks transform their resources and capabilities from being product- or service-centered into customer-centered. As a customercentered IT-driven strategy, Customer Relationship Management (CRM) has been increasingly adopted by corporations [4,30,31,50], including retail banks, and the <sup>fi</sup>rms have started to invest heavily in CRM [31,36,37]. Like Wells Fargo and Royal Bank of Canada, which are well known for their successful CRM strategies, Fine-Equity Bank in Korea is one of the big players that have implemented their enterprisewide CRM successfully.

In early 2003, Fine-Equity bank was concerned that decreasing the interest pro<sup>fi</sup>t rate on deposit and loan and the infringing on the banking business by other industries were threatening its position. Moreover, dichotomized customer management processes caused by the merger and acquisition with Seoul Bank in 2002 were yielding customer dissatisfaction, consequently resulting in customer defections. Enterprise-wide CRM [36], enabling the integration of its resources and capabilities along with its customer needs, was deemed to be a mission-critical business strategy to ensure that Fine-Equity Bank could distinguish itself from its competitors, win over new customers, and maintain the loyalty of its existing customers. By 2008, Fine-Equity Bank was the <sup>fi</sup>rst in pro<sup>fi</sup>tability per customer in Korea and awarded by Euromoney as the best private bank in Korea for four consecutive years. The change was the result of an ambitious effort to redesign its entire customer management process and link people with the new practice, such that customer satisfaction was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increased.

Fine-Equity Bank's efforts have been made up of two different phases: the phase of integration of functional resources and capabilities needed to innovate its business processes (from 2003 to 2006) and the phase of integration of human resource and capability to complete its mission of transforming itself into a CRM strategy driven organization (from 2007 to 2008). While the former consisting of integrations of information resource, analytical capability, and operational capability provided them a concrete base to run an enterprisewide CRM strategy, the latter brought them a CRM acculturation that could hardly be accomplished only with those functional resources and capabilities, consequently accepting CRM not as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c technology or technique but as a business paradigm in which people should involve. Fig. 1 shows the chronograph of Fine-Equity Bank's CRM implementation.

However, such two phased-implementation process was not intended from the outset. Similar with other organizations, Fine-Equity Bank also has been devoted to integrating apparent resources and capabilities to set up CRM foundations quickly. But the outcome at that time could not meet their original expectations; the result did not come from a fully-realized CRM strategy actually. After a corporatewide diagnosis, they found that they have been missing another critical factor: the people. Its impacts have been re<sup>fl</sup>ected obviously in the performance indexes, having them put the human resource into the second subject of integration.

This case illustrates which resources and capabilities companies should consider integrating to run CRM as a corporate business strategy. We show the limitations of CRM implementation driven by the integration of physical information systems (IS) through an empirical case, implying that the consideration of human resource and capability should be involved in the process of CRM implementation. To do this, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review several theoretical backgrounds of organizational resources and capabilities for implementing CRM strategy, then illustrate step by step the circumstances they met and their efforts to overcome. In the last section, we discuss the managerial implications and suggest a conceptual model of successful CRM implementation.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Resource-based view

This study is grounded on the resource-based view (RBV). The theory of RBV has been considered as a fundamental framework for measuring IT performance [22,25,34,40]. In the context of IT performance measurement, three underlying assertions have been made: (1) resource heterogeneity [1,12], (2) resource immobility [1,12], and (3) resource bundling [12,34]. Resource heterogeneity means that resource factors for implementing IT-driven strategies such as CRM may differ according to territories, industries, and <sup>fi</sup>rms. In addition, resource immobility and bundling also seem closely related to successful CRM implementation. For example, one of your competitors may try to replicate your CRM initiative by purchasing and deploying the same set of CRM hardware and software as yours. Can they deliver the same level of CRM performance as yours? The answer will be “no” since a <sup>fi</sup>rm's CRM resources consist of not only tangible hardware and software but also a number of intangibles such as customer-oriented people, process, organization, and culture. These intangible CRM resources are by nature inimitable and should be bundled with the rest of the tangible resources to create sustainable competitiveness for the <sup>fi</sup>rm [12].

## 2.2. Integration of organizational resource and capability for CRM implementation

Common to all RBV approaches in IS literature is that it provides a systematic framework to explain which resources and capabilities a company explores and reinforces for successful IS implementation. Drawing on the RBV, Melville et al. [25] provided a model of IT business value by integrating the various strands of research into a single framework. Their integrative model comprises three different domains: (1) focal <sup>fi</sup>rm (company level); (2) competitive environment (industry level); and (3) macro environment (territory or country level). In their conceptual model, they emphasize that a company should align not only IT resources such as technical IT resources (TIR) and human IT resources (HIR) but also complementary organizational resources including non-IT human resources, culture, policies, and rules to create value generating processes. Other major studies based on RBV have been on the same line with the study of Melville et al. [25]. Peppard and Ward [32] stressed that, to convert a company's IS into a core competency, business process and structure should be harmonized in organizing level, which competency would come from three components in resource level: business, technology, and people's behavior and attitude. It is because the expression of a particular competence in an organization depends on people applying their knowledge, integrating their knowledge, interacting with others and coordinating their actions [32]. Similarly, Hess et al. [14] suggested a tightly-linked system of data, people, and tools as complementary components to make marketing decisions effectively. In short, RBV provides a balanced view of technology, process, and people as critical components for successful IS implementation, which in turn results in a favorable organizational performance. Though CRM literatures have scarcely adopted RBV as their direct theoretical foundation, the tripartite approach addressed above has been considered as an indispensable condition for successful CRM implementation as well. Table 1 summarizes the critical resources and capabilities for CRM implementation in the perspectives of technology, process, and people discussed in RBV.

Fine-Equity Bank placed their focuses on information resource, analytical and operational capabilities, and employee satisfaction and customer orientation in terms of technology, business process, and human resource, respectively. In the early stage of their CRM implementation project, integrating information resources and analytical/ operational capabilities provided them a physical foundation to develop a company-wide CRM strategy. However, they learned before long that any CRM project neglecting human resource would not reach their organizational objectives because CRM is inherently a business strategy driven by not technology but people [7,41]. Their second efforts to engage people in CRM practices provided them a cultural motivation to internalize it.

## 3. Case context

## 3.1. Brief review of AS-IS context

Before the implementation of Fine-Equity Bank's enterprise-wide CRM strategy, its information systems for managing customer relationship had been based on an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient IT architecture. Fig. 2 illustrates the as-is CRM architecture model by 2002.

Fine-Equity Bank held seven different channels for customer contact, and three kinds of back of<sup>fi</sup>ce system, including e-<sup>fi</sup>nancing, strategic information system (SIS), and account management system, provided customer information to each channel. Data between the systems was pipelined by the message queues at a <sup>fi</sup>xed time. The previous version of the data warehouse was constituted with customer information acquired mainly from account management system, and the marketing system (MKS) and several data marts, which were connected with the data warehouse, were in charge of customer analysis in parts. However, many researchers have shown that customer satisfaction will be hardly achieved unless such company's resources and capabilities could be integrated in terms of customer perspective [11,20,24,47].

This AS-IS CRM systems have held critical problems in terms of both technological and strategic perspectives. From the technological perspective, it was dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to synchronize data acquired from various channels and required plenty of time to do it because every channel has operated by its own subsystems. Moreover, the different data format and schema of such heterogeneous systems meant that the integrity and consistency of customer information were rarely guaranteed. Secondly, partial and separated analytical functions supported by each subsystem have caused redundant targeting, resulting in ineffectiveness of marketing campaigns. Those technical problems have been frustrating in their customer management practices as well. The systems separated by channels forced Fine-Equity Bank to grade its customers by not their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts but their deposited amount, and manage them according to each product and channel. To make matters worse, since there was no systematic way or process to control and manage such separate initiatives, the responsibilities of CRM planning and execution activities had been left to each branch. Not only did it impose excessive workloads on the employees at the branches but also resulted in redundant and frequent marketing efforts to the same customers. Consequently, it led to increased marketing costs and diminished response rate, making the clerks feel that the CRM was not effective.

## 3.2. Organizational efforts to implement an enterprise CRM

Such problems had de<sup>fi</sup>nitely been caused by a series of thoughtless developments requested by ad hoc necessities. Since such situation contradicted Fine-Equity Bank's ultimate objective, which was to transform itself into customer knowledge-based organization enabling competitive advantage [4], the top management and board members reached a consensus to initiate a project integrating its capabilities and resources towards customer satisfaction in late 2002. Fine-Equity Bank's enterprise CRM project, which is called CRM4U, consisted of two different phases: The <sup>fi</sup>rst one was to integrate functional resources and capabilities, and the other one was to engage human capital in its CRM strategy. The functional resources and capabilities that Fine-Equity Bank focused on were information resource, analytical capability, and operational capability. The integration of customer information, one of the key resources for successful CRM strategy [26, 39], plays the role of bridgehead that would leverage Fine-Equity Bank's customer knowledge base. The newly designed customer information in multi-facets would allow Fine-Equity Bank to identify which customers are most pro<sup>fi</sup>table by grading and segmenting its customers sophisticatedly. Analytical capability was another integration area because they found that the AS-IS analytical practices had been carried out in terms of products and as ad hoc demands of branches, producing more than 2054 types of analytical reports, which indicate 72% redundancy rate. The <sup>fi</sup>nal component of the integration of functional resources and capabilities for CRM implementation was operational capability. An improved operational capability in Fine-Equity Bank would deal with how to deliver the CRM activities with customer knowledge created through the analytical capabilities, which use the information resources, and how to feed the customer responses back to the internal resources and capabilities (e.g., [24,44]).

Although surely the integration of functional factors above establish a tangible foundation of enterprise CRM strategy [38], it is imperative to integrate human resource to sublimate it in a true organizational core competency [8,17]. However, Fine-Equity Bank did not put the mission into their early CRM itinerary from the beginning.

The interim <sup>fi</sup>ndings had them notice that they must have missed something in the process of CRM implementation; it was people. At the end of 2006, as the efforts to integrate human resource with their CRM strategy, Fine-Equity Bank began to improve employee reward system, modify roles and responsibilities in CRM jobs, and rearrange CRM educations and trainings, which would contribute to personal CRM competency.

## 4. Integration of <sup>fi</sup>rm's resource and capability

To begin their efforts, the CRM4U team <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted extensive case studies and reviewed many published materials to gain a better understanding of best-in-class CRM initiatives (e.g., [9,11,31,46]). Based on the CRM knowledge from this case and literature studies, they entered the phase of integrating information resource, analytical capability, and operational capability.

## 4.1. Information resource

Beginning in May 2003, the CRM4U team analyzed the issues related to data resources in Fine-Equity Bank and formalized the directions for improvement on the issues. The CRM4U team worked with selected employees in cross-functional teams in order to surface and capture the knowledge needed to support their customer management processes. Intensive interviews and surveys with them revealed that not only physical integration but also logical integration of every piece of customer-related data would be important for providing consistent customer insights to every channel. Based on this <sup>fi</sup>nding, the CRM4U team derived six different subject areas as major components of the integrative data model. The redesigned integrative data model consisted of (1) customer, (2) product/service, (3) service management, (4) organization management, (5) contract, and (6) interaction management (see Fig. 3). These subject areas that each includes 15 to 24 detailed entities are not physical but logical divisions such that they are connected with each other systematically.

Those six subject areas have been built in a customer-centric information repository [9] called Enterprise Customer Data Store (ECDS), which enabled the event-based one-to-one marketing through interlinking customers' demographic data with their various behaviors and life-cycle event information [24]. Using the integrated information from ECDS, Fine-Equity Bank was provided with an opportunity to (1) ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently distinguish who are the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table customers and who or not [18,46], (2) improve customer management process by sharing consistent customer insights cross-functionally [20,24], (3) increase the hit ratio of marketing initiatives by identifying critical events that have impact upon company's pro<sup>fi</sup>ts [16], and (4) satisfy customer needs by presenting a customer single view to <sup>fi</sup>eld employees [23].

## 4.2. Analysis capability

To leverage the utility of the integrated information resource, it stands to reason that <sup>fi</sup>rm's analysis process also should be aligned with it [43]. However, the then analytical capability was not <sup>fi</sup>t for the integrated ECDS. Especially, the company faced a particular challenge in that too many kinds of reports had been yielded from the sporadic analyses. An in-depth review of the reports revealed that the problem was not only that such analytical reports were overabundant and redundant but also that most of them were nothing more than a naïve summary or simple descriptive statistics. It meant that the current analysis capabilities in Fine-Equity Bank did neither create any mean ingful customer knowledge nor share it, consequently becoming ex pensive yet hollow activities, which never pump bene<sup>fi</sup>ts into Fine-Equity Bank.

Given the main purpose that was to support effectively for settling the differentiated but consistent customer management process according to each customer segment [26,43], their efforts have been highlighted in developing a systematic analysis framework and setting the nine standardized themes for analytical CRM. Fig. 4 depicts the overview of the newly designed analysis framework. In this model, every analysis activity would be aligned according to each customer life cycle in banking, spanned from selection/contraction to expiration/ termination, and each analytical initiative is guided by systematic procedures consisting of customer understanding, strategy planning and building, execution, and result analysis [49].

Meanwhile, the integrated analytical needs from each department in Fine-Equity Bank have been categorized into nine analytical themes consisting of customer pro<sup>fi</sup>le analysis, customer pro<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, customer dynamics analysis, customer group analysis, product/service pro<sup>fi</sup>le analysis, product/service pro<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, product dynamics analysis, organization pro<sup>fi</sup>le analysis, and channel analysis. Besides the traditional analytical subjects that had usually been focused on product or service perspective, they added a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of customer-focused analysis features. Moreover, by considering the cost-effectiveness of each organization and channel, it could provide all facets of knowledge necessary to implement enterprise-wide CRM.

## 4.3. Operation capability

Though the integrative knowledge resource and analytical capability provide a favorable infrastructure for the effective customer management process, they do not necessarily mean the value-creating CRM strategy itself [43]. The integrated operational capability would be a medium in that it delivers CRM initiatives at the point of customer contact [5,15], which are derived from the internal knowledge resource and analytical capability (e.g., [15,19,43]). Reversely, the integrated operational capabilities enabling the acquisition of the right data by the right method in the right time would be a systematic funnel [20,24] in that it feeds high-quality inputs into the ECDS and the analysis mechanism. Based on these necessities, the CRM4U team concluded that event-based response system and sales force automation (SFA) were the key drivers in integrating Fine-Equity Bank's operational capabilities.

Before developing the event-based response system, the bank had only gathered naïve events (e.g., customer's birthday) daily by a batch processing at the end of the daily tasks, and delivered the prepared massages to the customers the next day. It meant that they could not provide agile responses against the quickly changing customer needs. In order to address this problem, they explored the current practices of responses to the mission-critical customer events. The CRM4U team found that 28 events, including opening a new account, expiration of account, capturing the signs of customer defection, etc., had significant in<sup>fl</sup>uences on Fine-Equity Bank's pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, it was surprising that although the 28 events were of such importance to Fine-Equity Bank, many of them had been prepared with no strategic response schemes or inappropriate responsive activities at that time. Thus, the CRM4U team should <sup>fi</sup>rst plan the optimized responsive schemes for each critical event, and then build them into a rule-based scheme, which could be referred to from everywhere in Fine-Equity Bank. With this newly designed event rule-based scheme, when Fine-Equity Bank perceives an important event from a customer, it <sup>fi</sup>rst derives the most appropriate response strategy for the customer and the event automatically, and delivers the derived response strategy to the customer through every channel, department, or branch consistently.

The sales force automation (SFA) system, the other axis of the integrative operational capability [42,43], was also considered as a tool for leveraging the event-based marketing strategy. With the addition of system engineers from Siebel to the CRM4U team, efforts began to customize Siebel's solution to integrate it with the event-based marketing capability. After identifying and following several system design requirements, Fine-Equity Bank's SFA was designed to provide high-degree customer knowledge and insights for the effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient sales activity. The reengineered SFA technology also provided learning opportunities for the internal resources and capabilities by feeding the voices of customers such as complaints, praises, and suggestions collected through various channels back to the internal resources and capabilities [42].

The reengineered SFA technology with the event-based marketing strategy provided a function of real-time perception of customer needs in terms of customer events, enabling the so-called immediate responsive system. It also solved the problem of, regardless of the customer contexts, forwarding a monotonous message to all customers who brought about an identical event. The integrated operational capability provided this support via an automatic responding function by offering appropriate messages dependent on the customer contexts. It means that the new operational CRM capability would be expected not only to support making decisions related to customers ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, but also to reduce the operational cost through the automation of preparing the responses to customers' ordinary demands.

## 4.4. Companywide propagation

In September 2003, the project team set forth to validate the concepts and the technologies of CRM4U strategy. For a pilot test, a series of cross- and up-selling initiatives was conducted with 1000 customers as an experimental group in <sup>fi</sup>ve different principal branches, including the head of<sup>fi</sup>ce, in using the CRM4U system. First, the project team segmented the customers in terms of lifetime value, which could be acquired owing to the ECDS's consistent and systematic management of information streamed from various channels (the support from the integrated information resources). Second, once the life cycles and pro<sup>fi</sup>les for the selected customers were analyzed, the most-possible-to-buy products or services were derived for each selected customer (the support from the integrated analytical capabilities). Finally, the customized cross- or up-sell strategies were automatically loaded into the SFA system such that each branch and channel could apply them when they faced the customers (the support from the integrated operational capabilities). Although the number of the experimental subjects was limited, the pilot test showed that the hit ratio of cross-/up-sale initiatives was enhanced signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly as compared with the previous ways. Moreover, the fact that the positive or negative responses from the customers were fed back into the kernels meant that such reactive information would be re<sup>fl</sup>ected in planning the next marketing campaigns, and consequently the effectiveness would be more improved. The positive outcomes of pilot tests began to spread throughout the bank by corporate news and inspired top managements to decide in expanding the CRM4U system company-widely.

## 5. Engaging people in CRM practice

## 5.1. The first yields and lesson learned

Adapting the CRM4U strategy in the entire Fine-Equity Bank had brought positive outcomes in both the operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance through preventing customer defections. First of all, CRM4U provided an opportunity to adjust its CRM business process to be more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. For example, contrary to the past custom of one-to-one marketing that had been conducted unsystematically and at great cost, its newly designed process allowed to standardize the relevant jobs as a series of request, planning, execution, and analysis, and then leave the planning and analysis to the head of<sup>fi</sup>ce and delegate the request and execution to operation divisions and branches (see Fig. 5). The project manager commented, “By minimizing the workload of each division and branch, and eliminating inef<sup>fi</sup>cient operational factors such as redundant and repetitive jobs occurred among them, it is expected to decrease the cost of internal operation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly.”

The CRM4U also favorably affected organizational performances. Fig. 6 shows the progress that was made in its efforts, with data from 2004 through 2006. Among its several efforts to enhance the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, we found that the customer defection rate has been significantly decreased. This favorable effect <sup>fi</sup>rstly has come from the fact that the integrated customer management processes through the CRM4U project has placated the customers' discontent, which had been caused by the past dichotomized CRM practices. Moreover, its customer satisfaction was greatly improved owing to the change from the past marketing communication practice where unilateral messages far from customer needs had been untimely pushed to unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed customers, to the new one where individually customized messages based on their events are now presented automatically in the right time.

Fig. 6 however involves another critical message as well. While the defection rate manifesting the effect of customer retention activities, the acquisition rate and X-selling rate indicating the effect of customer acquisition and expansion activities respectively did not show apparent improvement. How come this situation? In the letter half of 2006, we took steps toward exploring this situation. By using the CRM Scorecard [17], a CRM performance measurement framework, we diagnosed Fine-Equity Bank's seven infrastructural factors diagnosis that might impact on corporate CRM processes. A <sup>fi</sup>rm' external service (i.e., CRM process) quality is necessarily affected by its internal service quality, the infrastructural factors in other words such as CEO support, cross-functionality, employee satisfaction, information technology, etc [13,17,25]. Table 2 shows the results of the diagnosis.

We conducted AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process) analysis, survey, and quantitative KPI measurement for the seven infrastructural factors and standardized all numeric values in 10 point scale except importance score. Each importance score is an eigenvalue (λ) calculated from the matrix of pairwise comparison of factors, which was derived from AHP analysis [48]. As a result of the examination, though top management attitude and information technology for CRM were found excellent, employee satisfaction and organizational alignment (i.e., CRM education/training and CRM-compatible reward system) were quite poor. More surprisingly, employee satisfaction was the most important factor on which CRM-relevant staffs agreed. It might place an obstacle in the way of voluntary participation in CRM activities through internalizing the company's CRM strategy. Many studies dealing with CRM in both marketing and IS literatures have come to the common conclusion that people's commitment through employee satisfaction is a key of CRM success (e.g., [2,3,19,41,42]). In addition, acquisition and expansion of customer relationship among three CRM processes including customer acquisition, retention, and expansion [17,29,36] require relatively more personal efforts so-called customer orientation [6,17]. Therefore, low level of employee satisfaction might be connected to the weakened acquisition and expansion process, coming to disappointing results. Fig. 6 above shows that Fine-Equity Bank fell under the case. This <sup>fi</sup>nding let the bank acknowledge that they missed another critical resource in the process of integrating CRM resources and capabilities. Thereupon, they rushed into the second mission to complete CRM implementation, which was to engage people in their CRM practice through grasping the causes of employee dissatisfaction and solving them with systematic ways.

## 5.2. Employee satisfaction and customer orientation

From technical perspective of CRM, the degree of CRM adoption might be related to the company size [21]. Luneborg and Nielsen [21] asserted that customer-focused technology such as CRM would be more easily adapted to small-medium banks than large-scale one. It is because, while the ratio of IT-supportive jobs to whole business works in a small-medium bank would be relatively high, a big player usually has so complicated organizational structure and systems that IT-supportive area would be relatively small. Thus, a large-scale bank would rather introduce CRM not as an information technology or functional solution, but as an IS-based corporate business strategy. When a company regards CRM as a strategy, the company has to involve its human resource in the strategy to reach its ultimate objectives [2,11, 19,49] because diverse infrastructural factors would impact on employee attitude or behavior, consequently affecting the <sup>fi</sup>rm's external business process quality [10,13,17,42]. In other words, people play the role of interface between a <sup>fi</sup>rm's internal service quality and its external service quality, which is vital for managing customer relationship [2,17,25]. Moreover, lots of real experiences have shown that customer relationship might become vulnerable if a key contact employee is no longer available [2]. At these points, many relevant studies have asserted that companies should satisfy their employees <sup>fi</sup>rst as internal customers (e.g., [10,17,19,36,42]).

Notwithstanding the importance of human resource, Fine-Equity Bank could not address the issue at the early stage of CRM implementation, limiting its CRM performance to narrow one, which might be accomplishable by only several functional advances. Our investigation revealed that the bank's employee dissatisfaction had been re<sup>fl</sup>ected in two distinguishable symptoms. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was the private bankers' refusal to share their customer knowledge. Although one of the most important objectives of CRM4U was to change the customer management practices of private bankers (PB) who had managed the VIPs' assets and provided them with one-stop banking services, they had been trying to stick to their old custom of keeping their own private notes recording all details of their customers and refused to open them, and thinking: why should I share my customer knowledge with others? Not only did such behavior make it impossible to plan a longterm CRM strategy in terms of corporate perspective, but it also might cause the phenomenon of chain defection that if a private banker would leave Fine-Equity Bank, the customer whom the PB was in charge of would also defect.

The other problematic situation was that tellers at the front desk had not utilized the CRM system. Though the new system has provided the tellers with the customized information of marketing opportunities and communication messages to each customer, they usually neither followed the directions nor turn the system on at all. They just tried to handle the customers in waiting queue swiftly. Though this stereotyped working pattern can surely deal with operational jobs in hand, it could not seize even a very potential sales opportunity, eventually rendering the CRM system useless.

After a chain of in-depth interviews with the frontline employees, we could found the three suspected problems that might cause their refusal to accept the new initiatives. First, any CRM-oriented compensations or rewards were not provided to the employees for the new paradigm of doing business. It means that there were no differences of performance evaluation no matter if they adhered to the existing business practices. Second, there was an operational con<sup>fl</sup>ict with a related department when clerks execute CRM initiatives. While the CRM team urges the workers to identify the customer needs by making every customer feel like he/she is the only customer, the CS (customer satisfaction) team asks them to reduce the average queuing time of customers. Such duplicated organizational structure puts the employees under pressure, lowering job satisfaction. Finally, many employees felt that oppressive manner of spreading the brand-new strategy was prevalent in the company. Since the top-down approach for the CRM4U is sometimes regarded as a high-handed and demanding policy, it hinders the voluntary internalization of CRM. Therefore, to exploit the full potentials of CRM4U, they had to launch a new plan to integrate human resource and capability, which can solve the problems provoking employee dissatisfaction.

## 5.3. Integration of human resource and capability

Since the end of 2006, the company has been striving to internalize CRM4U into the deep side of the organization by various initiatives.

Fig. 7. Causes of employee dissatisfaction and its effects.

Their efforts could be summarized as follows: improving its incentive and reward system, reorganizing roles and responsibilities related to CRM jobs, and providing systematic education and training program. Such CRM-compatible organizational alignments could be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderators to control between <sup>fi</sup>rm's CRM strategy and its organizational performance [36] (Fig. 7).

The development of proper compensation schemes is a key determinant of a <sup>fi</sup>rm's capability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence CRM strategy [36]. To build a CRM-compatible compensation system, Fine-Equity Bank upgraded the previous incentive program called “CR-Do”. Although the previous program measuring the degree of CRM activity could be regarded as an additional enticement, it could not change the fundamental criteria to measure employees' job performance. It means that because the incentives provided by the CR-Do are not imperative to the employees, they cannot help following the evaluation criteria of the legacy system, which are mostly contrary to the CRM philosophy. However, their new CR-Do program was adjusted to evaluate the degree to which employees use the CRM4U in their daily operations and to apply the evaluation into the personnel performance ratings.

Their second effort was to make the <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward being a more customer-oriented organizational structure. To deliver differentiated treatments and products to different customer segments, rearranging people's roles and responsibility is inevitable. To do this, Fine-Equity adjusted several job areas which have been duplicated and con<sup>fl</sup>icted between departments. For example, the bank has operated its CRM team and CS (customer satisfaction) team separately before, causing incompatible evaluative criteria, i.e., the degree of CRM usability and average customer-waiting time, to employees in branches. Thus, customer-waiting time would be lengthened if tellers want to amplify communications with customers, or building a favorable relationship with customers would be unfeasible if they want to <sup>fi</sup>nish only customer-requested jobs as soon as possible to shorten the waiting time. Such dichotomized but incompatible criteria have been damaging the level of employee satisfaction seriously. To solve this dilemma, the bank decided to integrate CRM team with marketing department to place the both functions under the control of the department. This structural change took a new of<sup>fi</sup>ce regulation. According to the new regulation, while the low-desk tellers who interact with customers seated should use the CRM system, the high-desk tellers who handle routine banking tasks do not need to refer to the system. In other words, now that they are allowed to observe the role and responsibility adjusted to the task type, they do not need to be thrown into confusion any more.

Another effort to integrate human resource with CRM strategy was to establish a series of CRM education and training programs. Systematic educational or training procedures for helping employees adopt

CRM initiatives have been addressed as another critical success factor for CRM [17,36]. The programs had two objectives: the <sup>fi</sup>rst was to alleviate the coercive atmosphere of doing CRM, and the second was to provide practical guidelines to utilize the CRM4U system more ef-<sup>fi</sup>ciently. For the former, the education programs have taken a middleup-down approach. It means that the programs have been provided to middle managers <sup>fi</sup>rst, and then propagated throughout the company through the middle managers. This middle-up-down approach rather than top-down or bottom-up approach is more suitable for promoting knowledge-driven strategy like CRM in business organization [27]. They tried to share the concept, necessity, and future directions of CRM4U with all employees in the main and branch of<sup>fi</sup>ces of the country through repetitive trainings and workshops. For the latter, rather than just discrete and functional training, it was developed as a formal coursework involving regular curriculums. This education programs have let Fine-Equity Bank's people know better how the newly integrated resources and capabilities for CRM would assist them to share customer knowledge, make daily decisions, and enhance their performances.

These efforts to integrate human resource were aimed at not only accepting the CRM4U but also changing the perceived attitude to CRM initiatives by pacifying employee dissatisfaction about the new business practices. Once people in an organization are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed, they would take a positive attitude in communication with their customers [2,10, 41]. Beyond the bounds of what an information system itself could provide, the people-driven CRM strategy based on employee satisfaction started to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the performances of acquiring and expanding customer relationships, both which had been inactive relatively (see Fig. 8).

During these processes of Fine-Equity Bank's organizational transformation, its customer-oriented strategy started circulating through the academics and practitioners in Korea; consequently it has been regarded as one of the best practices for successful CRM implementation. Fine-Equity Bank is the best in pro<sup>fi</sup>tability per customer in Korea and awarded by Euromoney as the best private bank for four consecutive years from 2005 to 2008, which is the <sup>fi</sup>rst case in Asia.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Fine-Equity Bank is not the <sup>fi</sup>rst organization to implement an enterprise-wide CRM to improve its competitive stance, but the company is quite remarkable in the extent to which the company transformed and integrated its necessary resources and capabilities successfully. The most important implications we can draw from this study are that CRM would hardly be implemented successfully when it is considered as a technology, and even its successful implementation does not necessarily mean the success of the strategy. The study of Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien [33] also supports our assertion. They emphasized that an organization's ability to use IT to support its core competencies is dependent on IS functional capabilities, which, in turn, are dependent on the nature of human, technology, and relationship resources of the IS department. Since none of CRM practice can be generically applied into every organization [50], we believe that a company should take the implementation steps including identifying their own needs and issues before introducing CRM, selecting relevant resources and capabilities, and integrating them company-widely to address the needs and issues. Moreover, by combining appropriate CRM-compatible organizational alignments motivating people to engage in CRM with the critical resources and capabilities, an organization will likely be more successful in enhancing its organizational performance through the CRM strategy.

Of course, if Fine-Equity had grasped earlier the resources and capabilities needed to be integrated for successful CRM implementation, the present performance would have been accomplished earlier. Even though admitting that the company has spent lots of money and time to secure the present-level competency, there exists a clear lesson to be learned. It is the fact that they were not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the outcome from the <sup>fi</sup>rst implementation phase, but they have tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd the hidden opportunities continuously to be better. Since CRM is a continuous learning process rather than an information technology or analytical method [28], it should evolve permanently to respond to quickly and continuously changing customer needs. Therefore, the positive momentum, which is indomitable with failure or selfconceited with small returns, is quite a kernel of CRM. And Fine-Equity did understand it well.
