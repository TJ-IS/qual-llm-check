# Fighting Fire with Fire: Commercial Piracy and the Role of File Sharing on Copyright Protection Policy for Digital Goods

Tunay I. Tunca

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, ttunca@rhsmith.umd.edu

Qiong Wu

Bank of China International, Hong Kong, China, qiong.qw@gmail.com

n recent years, with the emergence and growth of illegal file sharing on the Internet, individual piracy of Idigital goods, i.e., consumers making illegal copies on their own rather than relying on purchasing copies from commercial pirates, has stirred substantial controversy. Threatened by this growth, the information goods industry took legal action by suing the file sharing peer-to-peer networks and the consumers who illegally share copyrighted material on these networks. In this paper we demonstrate that each one of these two actions aimed to fight individual piracy can backfire by providing strategic disadvantage to legal publishers of information goods. In particular, we show that in the presence of commercial piracy (i) a higher population of consumers who are capable of individual piracy can increase a legal publisher’s profits; and (ii) a higher detection and prosecution rate for individual piracy can reduce a legal publisher’s profits. Both effects can be observed in markets where commercial piracy is suppressed because the legal publisher can be coerced to take a price cut to minimize the loss of market share. The latter effect can also be observed in markets with active commercial piracy presence because the legal publisher can be forced to raise prices and concede market share to piracy. Our results suggest that information goods producers may be better off by considering their copyright protection policies concerning individual piracy from a more strategic point of view.

Key words: analytical modeling; competitive impacts of information systems; economics of information systems; strategic management of information technology

## 1. Introduction

Digital piracy has become a major issue for the global information goods economy. The threat and the damage caused by piracy are widespread and span the entire range of information goods from music and movies to software. Each year, the industry’s losses to piracy amount to billions of dollars. According to the BSA/IDC 2009 Global Software Piracy Report (BSA/IDC 2009), software piracy caused a revenue loss of more than 50 billion dollars in the world in 2008, and that for every two dollars’ worth of software purchased legitimately, more than one dollar’s worth was obtained illegally. Some 35% of all music discs sold worldwide are illegal copies costing the industry \$406 billion, and the movie industry’s annual losses to piracy reached \$601 billion (IFPI 2005, MPA-LEK 2008). Further, each year the affected industries spend millions of dollars in direct costs of fighting piracy through activities such as litigation and publicity campaigns.

There are two main types of piracy concerning digital goods producers: The first type is commercial piracy, in which a third party creates illegal copies of the digital products and sells them to consumers for profit. Especially rampant in emerging economies such as China, Eastern Europe, and South America, this type of piracy, usually takes place from illegal outlets that sell CDs and other physical media that store digital goods as well as illegal online outlets that sell copies of pirated content (IFPI 2005). The second type is individual piracy, where end users make copies for themselves by using copying technologies available to them. The exact method depends on the product and the medium it is stored in. Some examples are creating copies of movies by burning DVDs, and making illegal copies of digital music on computers and handheld devices. Naturally, not every consumer has the technology or the know-how to individually pirate. In fact, for certain products such as music or movies, historically, individual copying has not been very easy for many consumers and only a limited population of consumers have had the technology, equipment, and the ability for copying digital goods. Further, even in cases when it was relatively easy to copy, the individual copying activity was limited to relatively closed circles of acquaintances, and thus individual piracy had not led to the mass and rapid distribution of illegal copies of copyrighted material. However, in recent years, with the emergence and the growth of the Internet, coupled with increased digitalization of media, this situation has changed. The Internet, by its widespread and highly connected nature, enabled the formation of “digital communities” where people could not only socialize but also share digital files including copyrighted material. This capability peaked with the emergence of peer-to-peer (P2P) networks. Starting with the centralized Napster in the late 1990s and then morphing into decentralized networks such as Kazaa, Grokster, and BitTorrent, P2P networks provided an unprecedented number of consumers with ease of illegally sharing a wide spectrum and selection of digital goods such as movies and music and substantially increased the consumer population able to individually pirate both in the United States and around the world (see, e.g., OECD 2004, Mennecke 2005).

The emergence of P2P networks alarmed the information goods industry. The substantial activity of sharing of MP3 music files and movies on these networks generated a hot debate. Feeling threatened by the increasing Internet individual piracy, the recording and movie industries took legal action first suing the P2P networks, and even succeeded in ending the activities of some of these networks (Phillips 2005, Davis 2009, Sandoval 2010, Geuss and Daw 2012). The industry also turned its attention to increasing deterrence on the users themselves. In a sequence of lawsuits, trade associations and affiliates of recording and movie industries have sued a number of P2P network users who have been allegedly sharing copyrighted music on the Internet in the United States and all around the world in countries such as Sweden, Switzerland, Argentina, Hong Kong, and Singapore in addition to spending efforts by other means to suppress and control user activity on file-sharing websites (EFF 2007, Gonsalves 2009, Yoskowitz 2009). The lawsuits on individual users who illegally share movies on P2P networks sharply peaked in 2010 (Kravets 2010). The debate escalated as the consumer groups, P2P networks and Internet service providers challenged the action (legally or otherwise) from the points of view of privacy and fairness, some critics arguing that the industry was hurting itself with this action by alienating its own fan base (EFF 2007). Most recently, the debate escalated to the highest legislative levels in the United States, by the proposed introduction of the highly controversial Stop

Online Piracy and Protect IP Acts (SOPA and PIPA) in the U.S. Congress. These bills would impose restrictions on Web entities and search engines that link to websites that enable illegal sharing of copyrighted digital content. The provisions would range from banning advertisers and payment facilitators from doing business with such websites to requiring Internet service providers to outright block access to those sites in addition to increasing the punishment rates for individuals who stream pirated content (Kang 2011, McCullagh 2011). After a broad backlash from the Internet community and social media, the consideration of bills were indefinitely postponed in January 2012 (Weisman 2012).

The lawful publishers of digital goods naturally have a legal right to seek enforcement of their copyright protection by paying attention to Internet individual piracy activity. However, in most countries in the world today there is a varying degree of both individual and commercial piracy, and in a market with presence of both types of piracy, the role that Internet piracy plays is more complicated. In particular, from the legal publisher’s point of view, the effect of each one of the two types of piracy is different. First, when one considers the process of obtaining the good, commercial pirates provide consumers with a product that is in fact overall a closer substitute to the legal one: obtaining digital goods from commercial pirates does not require any special copying equipment, technological sophistication, or knowhow on the consumer’s part because it only simply needs buying a physical medium such as a CD from a pirate vendor to utilize this source. In every country, especially in developing countries, a substantial part of the population does not have Internet access or know-how or desire to learn to swap files on P2P networks (CIA 2006). Such consumers can still purchase the product of the commercial pirates and substitute it for the legal one. On the other hand, being sufficiently technologically literate is a prerequisite to have the capability to individually pirate digital goods, and most individual file sharers, such as P2P users, belong to an especially technologically savvy demographic group of consumers (FTC 2005).<sup>1</sup> As a consequence, individual piracy innately has a more limited potential population segment than commercial piracy. Second, there is a significant strategic side of commercial piracy in that commercial pirates set and adjust prices as a response to the legal publisher’s prices, and as a consequence when setting her price, the legal publisher has to take into account the price response of the commercial pirates (Kelly 2005). As a result, and as the music industry also admits, commercial piracy in many cases poses a more critical strategic threat for copyright owners (Smith 2005). Combining these observations, the existence of individual piracy, whether in the form of P2P file sharing or otherwise, may present strategic opportunities to consider for copyright owners.

Building on this premise, in this paper, our goal is to show that overrestricting individual piracy can in fact reduce a legal publisher’s profits by forcing her not to fully take advantage of potential strategic opportunities. Specifically, we construct and analyze a model of competition between the legal publisher and a commercial pirate in a market environment where the consumers also can copy individually. In our model, the legal publisher of a digital good is facing two kinds of piracy: (i) Individual piracy where customers can make illegal copies of the product themselves (e.g., through a P2P network); and (ii) commercial piracy, where a third party makes and sells illegal copies of the product for profit purposes. Consumers differ in their valuations for the product and their “technological savvy,” which indicates their ability to pirate the product themselves. For an individual user, the probability of being detected and prosecuted for the act of piracy varies between the two illegal channels of obtaining copies. In addition, the commercial pirate operates under a cost of entry, which encompasses the fixed overhead of operation as well as the risk of being caught producing illegal copies and being prosecuted and penalized. In the progression of the game, the legal publisher introduces the product to the market and prices first. Obtaining a copy of the product and observing the legal publisher’s price, the commercial pirate then decides to enter the market and if he chooses to enter, produces illegal copies and sets a price for them. Finally, each consumer chooses whether to buy the product and if so, which channel (whether legal or illegal) to utilize to obtain her copy.

Utilizing this model, we derive three main results showing that tolerating individual piracy can be beneficial to a legal publisher:

1. The legal publisher’s profits can increase with consumers’ increased technological ability to pirate individually. This implies that targeting and eliminating P2P sharing opportunities can, in fact, hurt a legal publisher.

2. The legal publisher’s profits can decrease with increased detection and prosecution probability of individual piracy even when the commercial piracy activity in the market is suppressed.

3. The legal publisher’s profits can also decrease with increased detection and prosecution probability of individual piracy with active commercial piracy presence in the market.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a literature review. Section 3 presents the model and derives the market equilibrium. Section 4 explores the effects of increasing consumers’ capability to individually pirate and the detection rate for individual piracy, respectively, on a legal publisher’s profits. Section 5 offers our concluding remarks. Mathematical preliminaries, proofs of propositions and mathematical derivations of applicability regions for the results are in the Online Supplement (available at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.1120.0430).

## 2. Literature Review

Digital piracy has long been discussed by researchers in the literature. Comprehensive surveys of economics literature on piracy can be found in Peitz and Waelbroeck (2006) and Varian (2005). Rao (2003) presents an overview of copyright issues for electronic information goods. Holsing and Yen (1999) provide an overview of ethical, technical, managerial and economic issues related to software piracy. Broadly, the literature consists of papers that examine the effects of piracy either from the perspective of social welfare or from that of a legal publisher (see Chen and Png 2003 for the lists of the main papers in the two categories). Our paper falls in the latter category as we explore the strategic effects of piracy on legal publishers’ profits. The traditional view on piracy is that it reduces a legal publisher’s profits (see, e.g., Novos and Waldman 1984, Johnson 1985, Hui and Png 2003). Empirical studies find that high prices, low income, cultural tendency to share and technological access and ability are the main factors that boost the piracy activity (Cheng et al. 1997, Marron and Steel 2000, Bhattacharjee et al. 2003). Specifically focusing on P2P file sharing, Bhattacharjee et al. (2005, 2006) show that sharing activity is positively correlated with subsequent market performance for digital goods, although one can also detect sales lost to pirating from the data. Bhattacharjee et al. (2007) find that P2P file sharing generally reduce survival rates of songs and albums with the exception of top performers.

Considering the downsides of piracy, one stream of literature examines policies to combat piracy (see, e.g., Gopal and Sanders 1997, Harbaugh and Khemka 2010, Wu and Chen 2008). Alvisi et al. (2003) suggest that piracy may cause a legal publisher to employ quality differentiation in its products in order to persuade consumers away from pirating the product and to purchasing it. Chellappa and Shivendu (2003) show that maintaining regional digital standards helps increase publisher profits and protects customers in high paying regions. Sundararajan (2004) analyzes the joint choice of price discrimination and technological protection through Digital Rights Management (DRM), finding the two can act as substitutes. August and Tunca (2008) show that restricting software security patches only to licensed users can reduce publisher losses from piracy but can be detrimental for social welfare under certain conditions by reducing the legal publisher’s incentives to invest in software security. Bhattacharjee et al. (2009) demonstrate that bundling of digital goods as a single product (such as having an album of songs) can help decrease piracy by reducing search costs for the users.

Chen and Png (2003) study the role of piracy prevention measures on welfare maximization. They introduce a model of consumer behavior with the possibility of piracy. In their model, the consumers can be one of two types: the ones that are capable of pirating the product given the benefits of copying outweighs its expected downside, and ones that are essentially incapable of piracy (labeled “unethical” and “ethical” consumers, respectively). The government sets the levels for three instruments it uses to combat piracy: fines imposed on users who have been detected to have pirated the product, taxes on the copying medium, and subsidies on legitimate purchases. Given the government’s policy, the legal copyright owner sets a consumer price and a level for monitoring piracy. They find that increasing detection is more detrimental to welfare than price cuts. They also show that the subsidy is optimal from the point of view of welfare maximization and a tax on the copying medium is welfare superior to fines for piracy. Our model builds on the base model of Chen and Png (2003). However, we take the point of view of the legal publisher and focus on the role of detection and the extent of the consumers’ capability to individually pirate. In addition, our analysis introduces and centers on the strategic interaction and price competition between a legal publisher and a commercial pirate. Overall, the nature of our analysis differs from Chen and Png (2003) and the other papers that examine policies to combat piracy by demonstrating that spending resources to suppress Internet piracy not only hurts the bottom line through direct costs but also may be unsuccessful in achieving its purpose in the first place.

Despite the traditional view on the negative effects of piracy, there is a sizable stream of literature in economics that discusses the potential beneficial effects of piracy for a publisher. A few earlier papers point out that the original publisher can recover some of her losses from piracy by indirectly appropriating these losses from the first purchaser (Liebowitz 1985, Besen and Kirby 1989) or through the sales of ancillary products that accommodate the functioning of these products such as CDs and radio (Curien et al. 2004). Gopal and Sanders (1998) show that if the software industry in a country is weak, then tolerance to piracy can help increase social welfare in that country. Givon et al. (1995) and Prasad and Mahajan (2003) show that piracy can help increase profits by facilitating the diffusion of the product into the consumer population and that tolerating piracy early in the product introduction can speed up the diffusion. A number of papers argue that under network effects, a certain degree of piracy can benefit a legal publisher (see, e.g., Connor and Rumelt 1991, Takeyama 1994, Slive and Bernhardt 1998, Shy and Thisse 1999). The main argument in these studies is that allowing certain users to pirate increases the appeal of the product because of the existence of positive network effects. However, also note that Poddar (2005) shows that when the legal publisher is facing commercial piracy, the benefits from network effects can be significantly weakened or eliminated. Bakos et al. (1999) and Varian (2000) argue that sharing of digital goods can create value for customers and consequently the pub lisher, and identify conditions, under which file sharing would increase the publisher’s profits. Peitz and Waelbroeck (2003) demonstrate that in a multiproduct environment, free file sharing can create value for a publisher by facilitating consumer learning and consequently increasing legal purchases. Banerjee (2003) examines optimal government enforcement to combat commercial piracy from a social welfare point of view. He shows that when it is profitable for a legal publisher to implement a protective device to prevent piracy, it is welfare maximizing for the government not to monitor and enforce piracy. Ben-Shahar and Jacob (2004) show that when there are two isolated markets for a product, it may be optimal for an existing firm not to enforce piracy detection in one of the markets to drive the price of the product in that market to zero, thereby keeping the market small for a potential entrant and preventing entry. Chellappa and Shivendu (2005) show that consumer sampling of the product through piracy can be helpful, especially for underrated products. They show however that unlike physical products, for digital products sampling is beneficial only under narrowly defined conditions. Gopal et al. (2006) and Bhattacharjee et al. (2006) further demonstrate that sampling can be a helpful byproduct of piracy but efficient search techniques are necessary for profitability in legal markets. Takeyama (1997) shows that by eliminating profitabil ity in the future, piracy makes future price reductions unprofitable for a legal publisher, which makes high early prices credible and hence can increase publisher profitability. Our results add a new potential reason why tolerating (certain kinds of) piracy can be help ful. We show that in a single market where the vendor sets a single price, decreasing consumers’ individual piracy capability or tightening control on individual piracy can have an unexpected effect without changing the market structure, purely because of strategic reasons. Table 1 summarizes the review of the literature. Our current paper can be viewed to be in the intersection of two branches of the piracy literature. It is in the branch that studies “potential advantages of piracy” because it focuses on the profit boosting effect of tolerating a certain type of piracy. It also can be viewed to be in the branch “methods to combat piracy” because we show that effectively boosting certain piracy activities can be a way to reduce the overall damage from piracy.

## 3. The Model and the Equilibrium

## 3.1. Model Description

Our model utilizes the basic level of the setup of Chen and Png (2003). We extend that setup by introducing strategic competition from a commercial pirate. We also modify the basic formulation in Chen and

Png (2003) by including non-unit opportunity costs of being caught for pirates. A firm, hereafter referred to as the legal publisher and denoted by L, produces a digital good and sells each unit at price, $p _ { L }$ , to maximize her profit. A commercial pirate firm, hereafter denoted by C, makes illegal copies of the digital good produced by the legal publisher and offers to consumers at a unit price $p _ { C }$ . The marginal cost of production is zero.

There is a continuum of consumers whose valuations for the digital good, v, are uniformly distributed on 601 17. Each consumer has a unit demand for the product and has a type $\theta \in \{ T , N T \}$ that indicates that particular consumer’s capability (technologically savvy to be able to pirate or not) of pirating the digital good by making individual copies such as using a P2P network to download and share files with other users. If a consumer has type T , she will individually copy if she chooses to, while if a consumer has type N T , individual copying is not an option available to that consumer. A consumer is of type T with probability  and is of type N T with probability $1 - \alpha .$ . For simplicity, we assume  is statistically independent of v. Without loss of generality, the cost of individually acquiring an illegal copy is assumed to be zero.

Following Chen and Png (2003), when a consumer illegally acquires the product (either from the commercial pirate firm or by individually copying in our case), there is a chance that she will be detected and prosecuted. Denote the probability that an individually copying consumer to be detected by $\mu _ { S }$ and that for a consumer who acquires the copy from the commercial pirate firm as $\mu _ { C }$ , where $\mu _ { S } , \mu _ { C } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . The total cost that a consumer incurs if detected to have pirated the product is

$$
\psi (v) = f + \beta v,\tag{1}
$$

where $f , \beta \ge 0 .$ . In (1), f is the fixed part of the costs of being caught and prosecuted; $\beta$ is the coefficient that links the costs of being prosecuted with the valuation of the consumer and is positive indicating the positive correlation between the consumer valuation and the opportunity costs of being detected and prosecuted. If a consumer is prosecuted for piracy she incurs a total cost that potentially includes a fine and other fixed costs of prosecution as well as opportunity costs that are incurred from being prosecuted. Such opportunity costs would likely be positively correlated with the consumer’s valuation for the product as the maximum amount that a consumer is willing to pay for the product is correlated with the consumer’s wealth and corresponding opportunity costs (see, e.g., O’Connell et al. 2005).<sup>2</sup> For simplicity, we assume that the correlation is of first order, i.e.,  is linear in v as given in (1).

To avoid trivialities in the analysis, we will focus our analysis on the region $0 < \beta \mu _ { c } , \beta \mu _ { s } , f \mu _ { c } , f \mu _ { s } < 1 ,$ because otherwise no consumer would ever choose to acquire the product from the commercial pirate firm or by individual copying. Note that, despite the low probability of detection, the high levels of punishment and opportunity costs $( \mathrm { i . e . , } f$ and ) bring the expected disutility from being prosecuted $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \beta \mu _ { C } , \beta \mu _ { S } , f \mu _ { C } ,$ and $f \mu _ { s } )$ to levels where the piracy decision for the consumers becomes nontrivial.

Commercial pirate’s operation is not entirely costless or riskless. He incurs a fixed cost, F , if he chooses to make and sell illegal copies of the product; F includes any fixed overhead, in addition to the expected penalty he pays in monetary fees or through disutility from prosecution and penalties if detected.<sup>3</sup>

## 3.2. The Description of the Game

The game between the legal publisher (L), and the commercial pirate (C) evolves as follows:

1. The legal publisher sets her price $\left( p _ { L } \right)$ and releases the product.

2. Obtaining a copy of the original product, the commercial pirate decides whether to enter the market by producing illegal copies.

3. If the commercial pirate decides to enter, he determines the unit price $( p _ { C } )$ he will set.

4. Given $p _ { L }$ and ${ p } _ { C } ,$ each consumer maximizes the net expected benefit she receives by making a decision on whether to acquire the product and, if so, from what source. If the consumer is of type $T ,$ she has four options: (i) purchase the product from the legal publisher; (ii) purchase the product from the commercial pirate; (iii) individually acquire a copy through technological means (such as file sharing through the Internet); and (iv) do not acquire the product. If the consumer is of type N T , she faces all these options except (iii). Each consumer makes a decision among the options available to her by weighing the costs and benefits of each option. Finally, based on the consumer decisions, the payoffs are realized.

The timing of the game is depicted in Figure 1. Note that the sequence of actions here plays an important role and needs a closer look: In the market for digital goods, the nature of the role of the commercial pirates is that of followers.<sup>4</sup> After the legal publisher produces a digital good, the commercial pirates obtain a copy and set their prices based on the legal publisher’s price and other market conditions specific to the product environment and in many cases the country of operation (IFPI 2005). Being aware of the fact that commercial pirates follow their prices strategically, the legal publishers set their prices to combat piracy and to curb commercial pirates’ entrance to the market as much as possible (Kelly 2005).

The sequential pricing structure we employ here commonly arises and is used in modeling pricing, especially when the competing firms have asymmetry in status, size, and strategic leadership (see, e.g., Gelman and Salop 1983, Scherer and Ross 1990, Hamel and Prahalad 1994, Porteus et al. 2010). The main reason why sequential pricing can arise is transactional and organizational costs that make the action of pricing subject to frictions and the prices slow and costly to change. The presence of these costs as well as their substantial magnitude are well known and has been documented empirically (Tirole 1988, Dixit 1991, Levy et al. 1997). Specifically, organizational frictions and inertia of processes on top of procedural costs prevent firms to make and implement pricing decisions instantaneously and costlessly. Further, as a firm gets bigger and more hierarchical, these costs increase and price changing inertia becomes larger. In particular, in our case the legal publisher firm is the price leader. Legal publishers, being large, often international companies, are much bigger and hierarchical than commercial pirates who are usually local, much smaller organizations or entities. Moreover, legal publishers’ price change opportunities are slowed down with multiple restrictions and contractual terms for pricing, distribution and revenue sharing agreements with multiple parties. As a result, their pricing costs and inertia are not only substantial but also significantly larger compared to those of the commercial pirates, and hence, commercial pirates are much nimbler and much more able to quickly respond to any price changes that the legal publisher will make.

In an ideal frictionless environment, one would expect pricing between two competing firms to evolve as a classic tatonnement process, where one firm sets its price followed by the other firm setting its price, followed by the response of the first firm to that adjustment by the second firm, and so on, until the firms reach to a stable point, where their respective prices converge to their Nash equilibrium values. However, when price changes are costly because of organizational frictions, depending on the cost of price changes and the firms’ expected differential returns from the outcome of the strategic cost changes, the firms do not keep on moving prices one after the other, and, in equilibrium, can stop after one or a few iterations. As a result, a sequential style pricing equilibrium can arise. In our current model, the legal publisher firm is the price leader because it is the producer and first releaser of the product to the market. The commercial pirate then follows by observing the legal publisher’s price, producing illegal copies of the product, and pricing it. As we mentioned above, legal publishers, are much bigger and hierarchical than commercial pirates and their price changes are much costlier and slower. Therefore, knowing that the commercial pirate is much nimbler, a legal publisher would recognize that price changes it would make after observing the commercial pirate’s prices would be too slow, too costly, and essentially ineffective because of commercial pirate’s readjustment, and hence would not engage in a fruitless price changing, settling for her original price, a single price setting equal to the corresponding Stackelberg leader’s price.

## 5. Concluding Remarks

In this paper we argued that in a market where both commercial and individual piracy are present, overchecking individual piracy can significantly hurt the profits of a legal producer of a copyrighted digital good. We showed that proliferation of a technology that enables individual piracy, such as P2P networks on the Internet, can in fact help increase a legal publisher’s profits by providing a strategic advantage over the commercial pirate. We also showed that increasing the detection and prosecution rate of individual piracy, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , intensifying legal action against consumers who illegally share copyrighted materials on P2P networks, does not necessarily increase the profits of the legal publisher. We demonstrated that such a phenomenon can happen whether a commercial pirate is actively present in the market or not. A future follow-up study to consider is an empirical investigation of the effects of increased online piracy on P2P networks on the prices of digital goods and commercial piracy activity. Market, technological environment, policy, and enforcement conditions vary significantly among different countries across the globe. Our model suggests that depending on these conditions, the effect of increased self piracy can differ on the prices of legal products and the market activity of commercial pirates. An empirical study that explores such effects among different countries and under potentially varying government and industry prosecution and policies can shed further insights on the interaction of the three channels to obtain digital goods for the customers, namely, legal purchases, self-piracy, and commercial piracy.

As indicated in the proposition statements, the condition $\mu _ { S } > \mu _ { C }$ is necessary for our results to hold. That is, for consumers, it is more likely to be prosecuted and penalized when using P2P (or equivalent) channels compared to purchasing from a commercial pirate. This is due to the significantly different nature of the roles the consumers play in the two different methods of piracy and the difference in prosecution methods pursued by legal publishers and entities. For individual digital piracy (of movies, music, software etc.) consumers are the direct and only perpetrators and in commercial piracy the hub of piracy activity is the commercial pirate. Because of better traceability of individual violators on P2P websites it is feasible in a much less costly way to target users of P2P piracy channels en masse compared to spreadout and much harder to trace and costly to track end users of a commercially pirated product. Accordingly, the prosecution of commercial piracy has been mostly focused on arresting commercial pirates who produce and sell the pirated products, usually redhanded with a large number of pirated copies, rather than chasing end users of commercially pirated products and the probability of being detected when purchasing from commercial pirates are usually very low. Because of these factors and the ramped up efforts of the legal publishers to prosecute P2P channels and their users, individual pirates on the P2P channels have been much more likely to be traced and hit with a law suit than purchasers of commercially pirated products (see, e.g., IFPI 2005, Bender 2006, USCC 2006, EFF 2007).

There are a number of other potential extensions of our model. One extension to consider is the legal publishers establishing an online presence or using an intermediary to offer the product online at a price (potentially different than the price on the traditional channel), primarily targeting the technologically savvy consumers. This extension would explore the effects of channel price differentiation for the legal publisher of a digital good in the presence of piracy. Note that the availability of the product online at a potentially lower price than the price on the traditional channel can move some technologically savvy consumers to the direction of legal online purchases and, as such, can also be a tool to fight commercial piracy as well. Another potential extension of our model could look into product differentiation for the legal publisher by providing an inferior version of the product and pricing it in a way to divert some consumers who would otherwise buy the commercial pirate’s product in order to gain strategic advantage in fighting commercial piracy. However, such strategies also come with downsides, as the inferior version would also cannibalize the full-quality version, which can yield lowered profits for the legal publisher. Exploration of these extensions can yield further insights into strategies to fight piracy for legal producers of digital goods.

In our results, in equilibrium, it endogenously emerges that when the users who originally pirated individually are discouraged to do so by prosecution, the diverted users mainly choose the commercial pirate as their alternative source. To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of any studies that directly examine what channel would be the main alternative for the users of P2P sites if they were diverted away from this form of piracy. This is, in fact, a difficult statistic to directly observe because especially collecting clean and reliable user-level data for commercial piracy is very difficult. However, there are some other indicators that can be used to assess the answer. In particular, there is strong and consistent empirical evidence that one of the strongest motivations for piracy is price and economic factors (see, e.g., Cheng et al. 1997, Marron and Steel 2000, Bhattacharjee et al. 2003). This implies that one of the primary, if not the most prominent, consideration of the individuals who are swayed away from individual piracy would be price, and given that prices for the commercially pirated products are usually significantly lower than those of legal copies, one can conclude that they would for the most part choose commercial piracy, as consistent with our results. However, empirical study of user migration behavior to alternative channels of distribution in case a certain piracy channel is made unavailable or less attractive could be another interesting future research avenue.

In our model, pricing follows a Stackelberg structure. As we explained in §3.2, with the asymmetry of the competing entities and costliness of pricing, this is a commonly employed pricing structure and fits well for the setting we are studying. In recent years, especially in fast developing emerging markets such as China, it has been observed that the legal producers are sharply reducing prices of their products in order to curb piracy (see, e.g., Kelly 2005). This is consistent with our model and findings in that our results indicate that in equilibrium, legal publishers can use price as a pressure tool to keep commercial pirates out of the market or segments of the market. The same could be true with other pricing structures, such as simultaneous pricing, as well, and studying the role of legal publisher’s strategic use of price under alternative pricing structures can be an interesting future research avenue.

Another trend, especially in emerging markets, is the bundling of pirated digital goods with hardware. An example for this is the rampant preinstalled pirated software such as operating systems with personal computers (China Sourcing News 2010). Such coupling is also possible for digital music and movies as they can come preinstalled with phones and digital media players. Exploring the effects of such hardware-software combinations on the strategic interaction between legal publishers and commercial pirates could also be a promising future research area.

In our analysis, we focused on a uniform consumer demand distribution reflected in a linear demand curve. Because our results technically require explicit calculations of the expected profit expressions for the commercial pirate, which are complex, and compare these expressions across a large number of potential market structures, the analysis is not tractable for general demand functions. However, we have explicitly shown that our results do not occur under “knifeedge” conditions, but are rather applicable on wide regions in the parameter space (Corollaries B.1–B.3 in the online supplement). Further, the central insights of our paper are robust to demand specification and are not driven by the specific simplifying assumptions (such as linearity of demand) and can also be applicable to model generalizations and further contextual applications.

Our results suggest a careful reconsideration of policies to fight individual piracy for information goods producers. The information goods industry, especially the music industry, has been spending significant resources globally trying to suppress new technologies that enable consumers to individually pirate, and to deter users from individual piracy in recent years. When fighting piracy however, the industry should consider the strategic role that Internet file sharing plays in the broader picture. Legal publishers of information goods can perhaps be even better off by employing Internet file sharing as a strategic ally in a controlled way to curb the deep losses that commercial piracy induces on the industry, economies, and communities all around the world.
