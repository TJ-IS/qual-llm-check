# The application and adaptation of a diffusion of innovation framework for information systems research in NHS general medical practice

## Abstract

This paper proposes an adapted diffusion of innovation (DOI) framework that may be considered relevant and useful to researchers undertaking studies of information systems innovations in healthcare organizations. A particular focus concerns problems and issues associated with professional cultures and powerful organizational control structures. A review of four empirical DOI studies are undertaken to identify candidate frameworks for the retrospective analysis of a pilot study conducted across General Medical Practices based within a Primary Care Trust in the north east of England. A research approach, based on phenomenology, semi-structured interviews and template analysis is adopted in order to conduct and provide a rich analysis of the data. The findings are discussed using the modified DOI framework. Discussion and conclusions relate to the extended use of the DOI framework, its further development and how it may be used to understand how ICT innovation is politically constrained, perceived and motivated within healthcare environments Journal of Information Technology (2007) 22, 44–58. doi:10.1057/palgrave.jit.2000093 Published online 2 January 2006

Keywords: diffusion of innovation; ICT; healthcare organizations; general medical practice; template analysis; information systems research

## Introduction

D <sup>iffusion</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>innovation</sup> <sup>(DOI)</sup> <sup>theory,</sup> <sup>inspired</sup> <sup>by</sup> <sup>the</sup>work of Everett Rogers (1983), has become a subject work of Everett Rogers (1983), has become a subject of widespread interest and importance. It has a long history of development, and has been used to research complex trends and patterns of innovation adoption, implementation and assimilation. The early research focused on agriculture and health, especially in developing economies. More recent research involves new technological innovations, especially their adoption and diffusion across industry sectors, organizations and individuals. DOI research, as applied to information technology (IT) and information systems (IS), has been the subject of an increasing level of interest since the 1980s, but has become a more formal area of study since the 1990s, particularly with the formation of special interest groups, such as DIGIT and IFIP TC8 WG 8.6 on Diffusion and Transfer of IT. Despite the relatively long history and growing number of studies and interest within this field, the theory is still controversial (McMaster and Wastell, 2005), has competing theories (Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 1998), an unclear joint common language and shared theoretical basis (Kautz et al., 2005), and is constantly producing an expanded list of IT innovation adoption and implementation factors and characteristics (Anand et al., 2006). A common theme arising from this research is a call for more in-depth, rigorous, relevant and interpretive case studies to assess DOI theory and apply it in practice.

This paper examines adaptations of DOI theory in four major studies in order to identify a pragmatic, relevant and rigorous DOI conceptual framework that may be used to better explain complex issues arising from the adoption and assimilation of IS within small healthcare organizations (NHS general practices). Such organizations are considered difficult to study due to their professional disciplines (medicine and nursing), the ethical requirements involved, their associated small business cultures (doctors may be self-employed and operate in partnership), their large populations of patients with diverse medical conditions (a complex social context) and their increasing dependence on advanced information, communication and decision support technologies. This complexity is even more conflated by a powerful authority dimension. There exists an unprecedented UK government IT investment (conservatively estimated at d6.5 billion in 2006), guided by an over-arching political agenda, driving a constantly changing government administrative, and regional managerial control bureaucracy.

The paper aims to contribute towards IS practice through the identification and development of a pragmatic and usable DOI framework that may be used in small organizations that exhibit complex professional cultures and political contexts. In terms of theoretical contribution, we aim to evaluate the potential of such DOI frameworks to understand complex human, social and political issues associated with a new ICT diffusion from the user/adopter perspective, but which is driven and controlled by politicians, managers and IT developers working within a national ICT strategy (NHS Connecting for Health).

The paper is organized into four sections. The first section provides an overview of DOI research together with a critical review of four empirical research studies that use retrospective case analysis. This section outlines the rationale for the DOI framework selected. The second and third sections explore the research methodology, and template analysis adopted in the study, and the findings that emerged from the large amount of rich data collected through semi-structured interview. The final section, comprising discussion and conclusions, provides a reflection on the approach chosen, its appropriateness to the study and its theoretical and practical value. Lastly, ideas for further development of the DOI framework are provided together with suggestions for future research.

## DOI research

DOI research and practice originates from many diverse fields of study. An innovation may be viewed as something that is new to an adopting organization but not necessarily new in its own right. Rogers (1983) over the course of several decades has developed and refined a DOI framework. He defines diffusion as the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system, and that an innovation is an idea, practice or object perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption. Also, that there are five attributes of innovation: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. Similarly five steps are involved in the innovation decision process: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation and confirmation. Five adopter categories based on innovativeness are identified: innovators, adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards, where the rate of adoption is the relative speed at which an innovation is adopted by members of a social system. The social and communication structure of a system is seen to either facilitate or impede diffusion where norms, opinion leadership, change agentry and aides are seen as key variables in the process. Finally, four types of innovation decisions are identified as: optional (independent choices), collective (consensus), authority (power enforced by a few members) and contingent (choices made after a prior decision).

The main corpus of work related to DOI studies provides useful generic theories, based on the innovation definitions and processes identified, to explain technology adoption in many diverse fields. Much of this work relates to studies of user adoption of IT in large organizations. There are fewer DOI-related studies of small- to medium-sized organizations, and these studies tend to adopt a case study approach, building competing theories of ICT adoption, but not specifically utilizing any recognized (based on Rogers, 1983) DOI framework (see, e.g. Poon and Swatman, 1999; Southern and Tilley, 2000; Cragg, 2002; Caldeira and Ward, 2003; Grandon and Pearson, 2004). Even fewer studies apply DOI theory to small healthcare organizations, even though such research could provide much richer accounts of ICT adoption than standard descriptive case study approaches. In order to make DOI research more understandable and accessible for small healthcare organizational contexts, a simple and pragmatic DOI framework is needed. This must still provide sufficient theoretical depth and rigour to undertake any meaningful analysis. There is uncertainty, however, concerning which framework or model is best suited to different contexts and also over competing claims regarding requisite levels of sophistication to explain complex human, social, political and organizational behaviours – as distinct from focusing purely on the technical artefact. The next section focuses on the identification and selection of a possible framework that fulfills these criteria.

## Selecting a DOI framework

It is beyond the scope and aims of this paper to provide a detailed review of innovation diffusion research and information systems. There are many other studies that do this; see for example, Prescott and Conger (1995); Anand et al. (2006) or for a more contentious and critical debate, Mcmaster and Wastell (2005). Instead, it was considered more useful to undertake a focused examination and critique of four empirical and conceptual studies from reputable publications that were considered interesting and pragmatic, but yet contained sufficient theoretical depth to be usable for our own research study. The studies by Kautz and Larsen (2000), Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2001), Gallivan (2001), and Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) attempt to develop innovation diffusion research through a synthesis of ‘traditional’ DOI research and organizational stage and process research. For the purposes of our research, the emerging candidate frameworks were considered to be theoretically rich, providing a greater degree of insight into complex IS adoption and diffusion problems, open to further development and adaptation, subject to the innovation problem context, proven useful for inductive and retrospective interpretive data analysis from an IS researchers’ perspective and potentially usable by IS practitioners from an innovation adopter’s perspective. Our aim was to identify either one, or produce a synthesis, of the candidate frameworks that would provide a richer insight into the complex adoption, diffusion and assimilation processes observed in our own field study of IS in small healthcare organizations.

## Theory development and retrospective DOI studies

The first selected study by Kautz and Larsen (2000) utilizes and adapts the original DOI framework of Rogers (1995). They use this to examine the diffusion of software process innovations by utilizing some key elements of the DOI framework to revisit and reanalyse the findings from the European Software Process Improvement Training Initiative (ESPITI) in Norway. A key aim of this research was to discuss the suitability and limitations of Roger’s (1995) framework as retrospectively applied to this project. The case analysis adopted categories of: innovation content and context, information needs and target audience, change agents and opinion leaders, and finally, innovation consequences and countermeasures. Kautz and Larsen (2000) conclude that the conscious use of Rogers’ DOI framework might have resulted in a better dissemination strategy and might have been beneficial for the project. A criticism, however, was that the model does not adequately address the dynamic relationship between all stakeholders involved in the ESPITI project: the ‘classic’ diffusion process often being perceived as too instrumental, driven by the needs of potential adopters and also as overly sequential in nature.

The second study by Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2001) utilizes three innovation diffusion models: the interactive model, the linked-chain model and the emergent model to produce distinctive but complementary knowledge (a retrospective analysis using competing theories) about three IT case settings. Importantly, they describe the DOI process in terms of an ‘ecological’ view (centralizes conflict and competition in the diffusion setting) and a ‘genealogical’ view (centralizes consensus and regulation). This is where the micro perspective is a genealogical view based on economics and innovation theory, and the meso and macro perspectives are ecological views that use power dependency analysis on networks of interacting agencies to understand how extra-organizational power dependencies shape the diffusion process (Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 2001: 182). The classic innovation literature is seen to typically adopt the genealogical view. Innovation is therefore seen as a linear process, either as technology push (market/adopter), need-pull or interactive (both technology linking and need linking). The linked chain model is also positioned within the genealogical view but incorporates a knowledge dimension as the base of the innovation and its diffusion. Emergent or ecological models of innovation are based on conflict theories where innovation may be stimulated by internal or external shocks to the organization, unpredictable setbacks and surprises are inevitable, and restructuring of the organization often occurs.

Interestingly, each DOI theory utilized proved equally meritorious and provided complementary and distinctive knowledge; there was no one best model. In terms of advice to other researchers, the ecological view is recommended in research focused on multiple entities involving evolution or confrontation and dialectic. This is certainly more characteristic of healthcare environments, which exhibit multiple organization structures, have a long history of power struggles between professional groupings and are shaped by a very intense political macro environment.

The third study by Gallivan (2001) highlights this debate concerning choice of DOI framework, subject to context, and the role of primary authority and secondary adoption decisions. He develops a new adapted framework to describe the organizational adoption and assimilation of IT, utilizing it to uncover ‘deep usage’ issues in a client server implementation in four selected organizations. In particular, this work focuses on situations where there is an organizational mandate to adopt the innovation; the framework thus attempts to explain the implementation of contingent authority innovation decisions where adoption may be considered to be non-voluntary. In these situations, adoption is seen as consisting of a primary innovation adoption process (e.g. a management mandate to adopt) followed by a secondary innovation adoption process (user adoption that is contingent on the primary process). Traditional innovation adoption models as derived from cumulative studies of DOI (Rogers, 1995) and studies in the domain related to Davis’s (1989) technology acceptance model are seen as problematic, when they are misapplied in situations where adoption usage is mandatory, adoption needs high levels of coordination and there are multiple dependencies across adopters, there are knowledge barriers requiring high levels of training and when adoption is examined through the eyes of a single stakeholder (Gallivan, 2001). Organizational level process and stage research models are advocated as a means of addressing some of the weaknesses of the traditional models. These may identify the context for events, and show causal linkages and temporal relationships among context, behavioural processes and outcomes. Assimilation is viewed in terms of how technology alters processes, structures and organizational culture and its overall impact within organizations, whereas technology infusion is seen more as the extent to which an innovation is used completely and effectively – perhaps more at the level of the individual. Gallivan (2001: 60) develops a new combined framework (Figure 1) that integrates constructs from traditional individual adoption frameworks with process and stage models, in particular, the six-stage assimilation model of Cooper and Zmud (1990) comprising: initiation, adoption, adaptation, acceptance, routiniza tion and infusion.

The centre of the theoretical framework is the primary focus of Gallivan’s study; the framework being initially used as a retrospective (where no framework was considered at the time of the first phase of field data collection) analytical tool to structure, and interpret case-based qualitative data and identifiable themes. These are used to explain the secondary adoption and assimilation process for client server systems in four large firms. Dialogic reasoning was used in later stages of their work to refine eight key themes, iterating between field data and the emergent framework, to connect the themes to three core constructs: managerial interventions, subjective norms and facilitating conditions. These were viewed as sets of factors that can mediate between primary authority and secondary stages of adoption and assimilation.

The fourth selected study undertaken by Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) attempts to develop a DOI framework in order to undertake a longitudinal analysis of process innovations. They adopt, expand and adapt Rogers’ DOI theory (Rogers, 1995), based on the original list of five factors that affect innovation adoption: innovation, individual, task, environmental and organizational. Based on these original categories, and from a comprehensive review of relevant IS diffusion studies literature, 28 attributes are identified, which represent independent variables; the dependent variable being the likelihood to adopt an innovation. These four empirical studies present important findings in relation to the application of DOI theoretical frameworks to explain complex, organizational scenarios for IS process innovations. Each approach has its merits and drawbacks however, and it was useful to critically reflect on the studies themselves, the degree of structure in the frameworks presented, and also their theoretical, potential and practical application with respect to our own study of ICT adoption in General Medical Practice.

## The four frameworks compared

Each of the four frameworks has been used by the authors, retrospectively, as an inductive lens for qualitative data analysis in a variety of organizational field studies. Their application and adaptation has provided novel insights into the process of IT adoption and assimilation at the level of the individual, group and organization. Each is a development from previous cumulative research within the DOI research domain and adapted to individual problem contexts. They have different strengths and weaknesses, however, regarding their use and application for providing theoretical insight and/or practical application.

Kautz and Larsen (2000) focus on developing their model to plan and perform large-scale diffusion actions to introduce software quality management process innovations. A loose interpretation of Rogers (1983) classic DOI theory utilizing standard constructs is applied to data compiled from survey, interview, participation and observation of training events. This provides the basis for a fairly descriptive account of the innovation adoption process, focusing mainly on the innovation content, context, information needs, dissemination mechanisms, change agentry, consequences and counter-measures. This liberal application of Roger’s theoretical constructs provides a useful and interesting account of the innovation process. Significant detail is lacking, however, regarding any deeper power, political and organizational issues concerning the adoption (or not) of software quality processes. Owing to the role of the researchers involved in the change process, acting as project and opinion leaders, it is difficult to defend against the claims for proinnovation bias. No well-defined, structured or detailed framework is presented as the basis for further study along with any advice for how the Roger’s constructs might be used in actual practice (by adopters themselves as opposed to the academic opinion leaders).

In contrast, Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2001) adopt a more theoretical stance with respect to their multipletheory analysis of an IT case. Three complementary innovation models (interactive and linked-chain are considered as representing consensus and regulation, and the emergent model is seen to represent conflict and competition) are utilized resulting in rich but orthogonal representations of the single case that is the object of study. The case was based around a small, web services development company, a strategic partner and a key customer with rich data collected by video-taped interviews

The use of genealogical models provides a very strong and useful business focused, and logical view of innovation adoption, but few relatively deep insights. Power and political issues are largely ignored. In this case, the analysis is constrained by the models used – perhaps providing too much of a business, economic and market ‘straight jacket’ to let the more human centred issues emerge. Simple process diagrams are used to map events and progress of innovations by taking an ecological view within the organization. In this case analysis, conflict and competition are brought to the surface and linked with factual events; this reveals that tension, false starts, political compromises and upheaval are essential aspects of innovation. The lack of structure and detail in the model makes it potentially difficult to apply however – apart from the use of simple process models to explain progressive and disruptive events. The power of the analysis is illustrated through the story, supported by relevant quotes and a further foray into action research, providing organizational learning. A very convincing argument for the use of complementary models is provided, but the approach may be considered rather too theoretical and academic, which might limit its practical application and value.

Gallivan (2001) has developed two theoretical frameworks from a process of inductive and dialogical interpretive research. This is where the framework has been retrospectively developed from a first phase of research and then, iteratively developed and refined over several more stages to produce both a conceptual framework and a more detailed causal model specifically linked to the problem context studied. A major strength of these frameworks are their well-explained development and synthesis from both traditional innovation research and organizational process models. The framework contains a high level of structure and detail that is further developed to explain secondary adoption innovation processes, leading to organizational assimilation of new client server systems. Culture, context and subjective norms feature as central to the model providing much scope for analyzing power, political and organizational issues. The interface between primary and secondary adoption stages is not specifically the focus of this study, but the issues relating to contingent authority are made overt, thus providing an important potential extension of the model. The frameworks are both theoretically rigorous and of demonstrable practical application and value.

Finally, Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) focus on adapting traditional innovation theory and develop an expanded framework encompassing 28 factors based primarily on Rogers (1983) and synthesized from a review of selected studies. Again, an inductive research approach is used to apply the framework retrospectively to a case study. The framework is simply structured with significant details provided with respect to the 28 factors considered important to the DOI. These factors are inclusive of both traditional innovation research in terms of user characteristics and adoption, together with organizational and process factors providing the possibility of analyzing power, political and organizational issues. The framework is both convincing in terms of its theoretical antecedents, and also considered to be simple and straightforward to apply in a practical sense.

From this brief review, all four frameworks can be seen to have complementary potential for use in the retrospective analysis of innovation diffusion and assimilation studies. For our study of small healthcare organizations, we were looking for a simple and highly adaptable framework that would provide sufficient analytical depth to explore complex power, political and organizational issues within the context of powerful bureaucratic authority and a political agenda. We decided to proceed using the expanded framework of Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) as a basis for analyzing the data, as this fulfilled our criteria for being sufficiently theoretically rigorous but also very structured and logical to apply. However, the other frameworks, and in particular, the model adapted by Gallivan (2001) were only temporarily put aside to be revisited later for their potential to add deeper insights into our data analysis. We reflect on the possible further development of this framework, through synthesis with the other frameworks examined, in our discussion and conclusions.

The next section deals with the primary research and the methodology used.

## The research methodology

The methodology that we adopted in this research was that of phenomenology based on template analysis (King, 2004). According to King (2004: 256):

The essence of template analysis is that the researcher produces a list of codes representing themes identified in their textual data. Some of these will usually be identified a priori, they will be modified and added to as the researcher reads and interprets the texts.

The approach may be considered sympathetic to Grounded Theory but is not as prescriptive in terms of rigorously following a purely inductive pathway. Its advantages include the ability to develop conceptual themes and the clustering into broader groupings with the eventual identification across cases of master themes. Template analysis works particularly well when the aim is to compare the perspectives of different groups of staff within a specific context (King, 2004: 257).

## NHS General Medical Practice: the context

The ICT strategy of the English NHS has a long and chequered history – not least because it changes its objectives, scope and emphasis at very frequent intervals. This mirrors the dominant government political ideologies (for a more detailed account see Wainwright and Waring, 2000). The strategy, throughout the 1990’s and up until the present, has evolved from a focus on resource management, integrated hospital information support systems (HISS), the Electronic Patient Record (EPR) to what is now a lifetime ‘cradle to grave’ record termed the Electronic Health Record (EHR). Laterly, after the millennium, a major ‘step change’ in ICT investment has taken place in parallel with government policies for modernization of the entire health service organization. There are official estimates of d6.5 billion and unofficial, but not really disputed, forecasts of up to d30 billion of investment in ICT hardware, software, consultancy services, training and infrastructure over an initial eight year period with a fully integrated ICT-enabled health service targeted for 2010. This is governed under the organizational umbrella of the National Programme for IT (NPfIT, www.npfit.nhs.uk), initiated in October 2002. In 2005 this became known as Connecting for Health (DOH, 2005), a government agency, which is tasked with developing and implementing a new care records service (CRS) to connect secondary care hospitals with primary care healthcare providers (General Medical Practices).

There is a paucity of research that examines ICT adoption within both the UK NHS generally (Wainwright and Waring, 2000) and primary care services specifically (Newton et al., 2003). The politics of diffusion of ICT, and the impact of managerial instrumentalism within a professional service culture, has been the focus of a small number of interpretive and critical theoretical studies over the past decade (e.g. Bloomfield et al., 1997; Packwood et al., 1998; Doherty et al., 2000, 2004; Pettigrew et al., 1994; and Waring and Wainwright, 2002). These studies have examined how political agendas have been the driving force for a process of ‘modernization’ or significant healthcare reforms; often viewed as the rise of managerialism, centralized control and an assault on, or an erosion of, power of the medical profession. The NHS itself has kept a very tight control of dissemination of information about the progress of the national ICT strategy. This has caused problems for many independent researchers who have requested access to the NHS over the last eight years.

The study: the adoption and assimilation of ICT in general medica practice

The project described here explores the impact of NHS Information and Communication Technology (ICT) initiatives at the level of General Practice organizations. There is little empirical research exploring current issues concerning ICT strategy adoption and usage within primary care from the ‘user’ as opposed to ‘policy maker’ perspective. We wanted to define key issues impacting upon the adoption of new ICT within primary care practice from clinician, management and administrative stakeholder perspectives. We then aimed to evaluate the progress of ICT adoption and its diffusion relative to targets defined within the current NHS information strategy. The research was designed and carried out by the authors. Initially, prior to our retrospective analysis, two main research questions were posed:

\- To what extent has the current NHS ICT strategy and diffusion of ICT impacted on working lives at the level of the General Medical Practice?

\- What are General Medical Practices’ experiences of engaging in ICT initiatives and how will this affect future adoption of ICT within primary care organizations?

Initially we approached Gateshead Primary Care Trust (PCT), in the north of England, to explore whether we could carry out the research with a number of their GP practices. The PCT was a relatively new organization created under the most recent wave of NHS and labour government organizational reforms. The staff in the PCT were only beginning to establish an understanding of the ICT issues within the Trust. The senior management of the PCT were very supportive of the research and we were asked to submit our research proposal to both the Gateshead PCT Ethics and Research and Development Committees. Eventually after almost twelve months, and further revisions, we were given clearance to carry out the research (this is not unusual in English health services research).

Initially a pilot site was selected for the first trial run of the data collection. This enabled the research design to be reviewed, assessed and refined prior to expanding it across a wider range of respondents and organizations. The study was conducted between January and September 2004 and aided by a small university research grant.

In discussion with the PCT we thought that we would like the study to include GP practices that fitted the following profile in order to get a representative cross-section across the PCT:

\- Opportunity, willingness and potential for good collaboration.

\- Mix of local General Medical Practices.

\- Levels of ICT usage and maturity (‘Paper-heavy’ to ‘Paper-lite’).

\- Involvement in specific ICT projects, Electronic health records, Prodigy (Decision support systems for Doctors and Nurses), Pathology and laboratory results reporting, Hospital booked patient admissions etc.

However after some discussion, we agreed with the PCT that GP practices should be sent a letter by the PCT inviting them to participate in the pilot research project. From the responses to these letters five practices were identified. Obviously these practices were self-selecting and this poses issues for the validity of the study and the finding that have emerged. However this was the best situation available and it may not reflect the PCT as a whole. The method of data collection for this study was that of the semi- structured interview. At each of the five research sites, tape recorded interviews were carried out with a core set of staff – a general practitioner (Doctor), practice manager and practice nurse. The interviews were based around a broad set of topic headings to guide the interviews but we tried as far as possible to allow the participants to lead the discussion. Research consent forms and information leaflets were provided in advance of the study.

Interviews took place at a time and place convenient to the respondent and ideally were limited to a maximum of one hour duration. All data has been anonymized and all confidences respected. Transcripts (verbatim) were presented back for verification by each respondent. Wherever possible the 2 researchers were present to enable consistency of the data collection approach and to enable peer reflexivity at a later date in terms of the data analysis and also the research process itself.

## Developing the template

In this section we will describe the development of the analytical template but it is essential to recognize that the development of the template is not a separate stage from its usage in the analysis of texts.

## Defining the codes and creating the initial template

Template analysis normally starts with some pre-defined codes intended to help guide analysis. The first issue we faced was how extensive the initial template should be. King (2004) suggests that if you start with too many pre-defined codes then the template might blinker analysis and prevent exploration of more pertinent issues. On the other hand too few codes may lead to an overwhelming mass of rich and complex data.

Our approach and starting point was the letter sent out to the GP practices introducing the research topic and some of the Government and Department of Health concerns. From these we constructed a template in a graphical format (See Appendix A). This took a period of time and reflection to ensure we agreed the initial codes. The template was used as a prompt for the respondent and adapted for use if necessary. The key words have been derived from the research literature, NHS documentation related to ICT strategy initiatives and from the personal research experience of the researchers. This approach has a proven record of successful use in qualitative research in healthcare settings specifically investigating ‘soft’ organizational issues (Clarke and Wilcockson, 2001, 2002).

## Revising the template

It is important to realize that the template must be revised in response to the concerns of the interviewees. Respondents were able to suggest other key words that they thought should be included and enlarged on them if they felt necessary. They were provided with the template at least one week prior to the interview. As part of the research we soon discovered that many of the key issues, words and phrases that we identified initially were not relevant to all of the interviewees and a number could be deleted. It was also apparent that GPs had different issues and perspectives to nurses and practice managers even when they came from the same organization Practice.

## Limitations of the research

The use of semi-structured interviews to gather data from three key perspectives within General Medical Practices provided a rich insight into the actual politics of ICT adoption and diffusion. The independence of the researchers was critical to the success of this exercise alongside the protection of anonymity for the respondents. The template methodology was also successful in determining key categories and codes around which to base both the interviews and also the initial data analysis. There are limitations to our study however. This is mainly due to it being on a small scale across five practices within one PCT organization, and also due to the self selecting bias as a result of having to solicit volunteers as opposed to selecting a more representative sample.

## Findings

In order to analyze our results we have used the DOI factors as developed by Rogers (1995) and have addressed, to a greater extent, the attributes of these factors as set out by Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003). Unfortunately the large amount of rich data collected in this study is difficult to encapsulate in a single paper and so we have developed a framework as shown in Table 2 that highlights the factors but also incorporates political issues that are intrinsically linked to the diffusion agenda within the study.

If we now consider each section in turn we will be able to highlight some of the key issues that have emerged and determine the extent to which policy and reality are mirrored at the level of the GP practice.

## Innovation

The term innovation has connotations with leading edge technology and new and better ways of doing tasks. Yet Rogers (1995) recognized that innovation can have many meanings in different stakeholder contexts. In the case of Gateshead PCT, and the pilot practices therein, innovation means optimizing the use of EMIS (this is the medical practice computer system used by all the practices sampled and also by 55% of doctors in England, emis-online.com). EMIS is constantly evolving and has many features, a number of which have not previously been used by the staff in the practices. However, the new environment of the GP contract (a new NHS government reform related to the adoption of new working practices for doctors allied to significantly enhanced financial incentives) means they have to engage more with the data to meet targets, demonstrate quality and enable financial probity. There is also the ambition of some practices, driven by PCT ‘erhetoric’, to implement full electronic patient records systems and become ‘paper-lite’. Nevertheless, when this was explored further it was difficult to establish to what extent staff in a practice understood these terms.

One interesting aspect of the PCT was the use of mobile technology. Community nurses based centrally at the PCT headquarters used notebook computers with wireless technology when visiting patients’ homes. Instead of supporting the work of the GPs this was a cause for some concern as practices were not consistent in their data entry requirements and community nurses appeared to have limited appreciation of their obligations. Staff at the practices often had to re-input data entries for consistency. Only one of the doctors we interviewed had used mobile technology (a type of Palm Pilot) while visiting patients and it had limited appeal:

I decided to review the medication of everybody over 75, a certain percentage of which were housebound. They gave me a Palm pilot, it was too cumbersomeywe’ve printed off some formsywe all know how to use thatyits a lot easier.

Taking hard copy out to patients was far more efficient in their eyes. This was also reinforced by new NHS structural reforms that enabled unsocial ‘out of hours’ services to be outsourced to a collective of locum GPs. This resulted in GPs doing much fewer home visits to patients.

The Internet is becoming a resource that is encroaching on GP practices in varying degrees. The GPs saw it as a useful resource for information but did not use it extensively in consultations. However, the nurse practitioners found it extremely helpful when dealing with patients and often used it to support the management of illness.

A final innovation in the pilot practices was the recent ability to receive results of patients’ tests (blood etc) directly into the surgery from the local hospital via email. This is not at the forefront of technological innovation but for these individuals it has been one of the major developments in supporting their work and when it malfunctions they are distraught. One nurse commented:

We didn’t get any results! We got no results for a long time, we couldn’t download itywe had to go back to paper copies.

The new GP contract has acted as a catalyst for engagement with the technology because payment for work done is by results and needs to be evidence-based. The PCT has online access into GPs systems and can download data at will. It is no longer sufficient to leave the practice administrative staff to do the data entry as clinical data must be collected at each consultation.

This has led to a number of issues impacting on the practices. Training of all staff in the use of the systems is essential. Doctors need to understand the finer points of clinical coding to ensure that the correct data is captured at source. This has been difficult for some. Nevertheless, if done well it can provide a great deal of data for evidencebased medicine and may contribute to the future health of the population; something which the Government insists on happening. The nurse practitioners (higher grade nurses who have the authority to perform delegated diagnostic work and prescribe certain drugs) have been instrumental in developing protocols for collection of clinical data on EMIS and the management of patient treatment for long term conditions such as diabetes. This has caused some growing tensions, in practices where they are based, as their new roles have yet to be clearly defined and the interface with GPs has the potential to be politically contested ground.

## Individual

From the perspective of the individual within the pilot practices there are some major issues if the diffusion of ICT is to progress and be used innovatively. First, the practice managers generally felt that the onus was on them to deliver much of the data and this meant they relied on being up to date with all aspects of the EMIS system pertaining to their area. They have limited budgets for staff training and therefore undertook on the job training with all staff, including doctors, in their practice. One practice manager said:

In the private sector there is a lot more training, development and coaching regarding change and another: I’ve never had any formal training on the computer system.

This lack of training and resource was putting them under a lot of pressure at work and leading to feelings of being undervalued. Although paid at a reasonable rate they were unsure how the new GP contract payment system would work and suggested that it would generally only financially benefit the GPs.

The practice managers were also in the front line in terms of patient contact and although not extensive, were increasingly using email to communicate with patients. They saw this as beneficial to both practice and patient. However, the GPs did not want email communication with patients and did not see any benefits to themselves. On the contrary, they felt this would add an unacceptable burden to workloads.

From the clinical practitioner perspective, ICT was impacting on them in different ways. One GP, who had been in practice for about 25 years, felt that the ICT was causing some difficulty and was becoming a barrier in the consultation room, leading to a poorer level of service to patients. She stated:

Consultation time is just 10 min, it’s barely enough time to speak to someone without turning around and start working on the computer.I’m doing a 4 h surgery in the morning and 4 h in the afternoon, and absolutely brain dead at the end of it.

There was also a feeling amongst the GPs that GP medicine was becoming much more mundane and they were seeing less acute cases due to the way out-of-hours medicine was now being managed and the intervention of NHS direct. Also with the Government’s initiative on targeted interventions and possibly the role of the nurse practitioners some GPs believed they could be deskilled. The nurse practitioners, however, were very upbeat about their emerging roles and saw themselves as advocates of ICT, one stating:

I think we are taking on a lot of new roles, taking over a lot of things that doctors would be doing.

Their nurse practitioner education programme was underpinned by ICT and its use to support the patient consultation. Many used the decision support system ‘Prodigy’ for advice on drug prescribing and drug conflicts when dealing with patients. The doctors interviewed did not use Prodigy. It was not on their horizon at all even though it had been initially developed in the north east of England with GPs.

## Environment

For the vast majority of those interviewed the NHS national strategy on ICT had passed them by. Their horizon was the PCT and only those who were part of some external network had any idea that there was a national strategy. The practice managers generally met regularly at the PCT headquarters and were exposed only to relevant issues from the strategy. However, these were generally contextualized for the PCT. They did not see ‘big picture’ or that they were part of a much larger initiative. An example of this was when we asked whether they were familiar with the current debate around ‘Local Service Providers’ (LSPs) (there are five clusters of LSPs in England, spread across geographical sectors and comprised of major consortia of consultancy companies and ICT vendors; Accenture heading the north east LSP), and that if the various contracts were signed they might lose EMIS, their current GP system, for a new one called Lorenzo (Accenture’s preferred system from iSoft). Only one interviewee was aware of this and the others were quite shocked that there had been no consultation on the decision particularly when they were perfectly happy with EMIS.

Another initiative that was looming large on the Government’s agenda was ‘Choose and Book’ where needy patients could, during a GP consultation, be referred through an ICT link to a hospital and consultant of their choice. None of the practices we visited had begun any form of discussion with local hospitals about how this would be implemented locally or even knew whether there was a system in place that could facilitate the process. This system was due to be implemented by December 2005 with current indications that the system may now not be ready for another year at the earliest.

Culture within organizations can often determine whether ICT diffusion will succeed. In the pilot practices studied it was apparent that a culture change was emerging through a number of factors. A major factor was the introduction of new staff into the practices. Three of the practice managers had come from the private sector and were used to working in different ways. This ‘can do’ approach was having a dramatic impact on how the surgery worked. Practices were working in more effective and efficient ways and tasks were getting done. The hierarchy within these practices is also changing and becoming much more team based with nurses working alongside the administrators to address patients’ needs. Some doctors are aligning with this approach but other still need to maintain the status quo. ICT and the need for common shared goals such as standardized patient data are also facilitating this culture change but the evidence for this is only emerging. The difficulty for the PCTs will be keeping these ‘champions of change and ICT diffusion’ and on limited budgets rewarding their efforts.

## Organization

It is important for all concerned that innovations or ICT diffusion experience is communicated to share good practice and discuss future developments. To some extent each of the three groups interviewed were part of networks but these networks were not ICT focused or even had ICT as agenda items when formally constituted. The doctors met formally at the PCT to discuss clinical issues and new medical developments. They also were part of an informal network as many went to medical school together around the same time. What is obvious from our study is that GP practices are not all embracing ICT is the same way and the PCT were unable to engage any of the ‘Luddites’ in our research. Even though the new GP contract expects GPs to work in certain ways a number are not using EMIS necessarily to deliver requirements. How this will evolve and how the PCT will deal with transgressors is still to be determined.

The nurses do not appear to be well networked and only those who are studying on the nurse practitioners programme have access to other similar individuals at a regional/national level. Thus they get to discuss more strategic issues but out of context to their local issues. In their practice they see themselves as part of the practice team and share information across the practice rather than across the PCT.

The practice managers are at the forefront of the ICT initiative and changes in the PCT. They meet regularly at the PCT but agendas are determined by the PCT management and generally relate to financial data and the new contract. The practice managers would like to network further to discuss their issues and share good practice and this may evolve through the efforts of new entrants into the arena. If practices are to further develop ICT and related systems then these individuals are the champions and the change agents. It will be through their efforts that diffusion will benefit the patients and bring about the evidence-based practice that the government believes is worthwhile.

## Discussion

Four areas are highlighted for discussion in this section. Firstly, the high level politics influencing the adoption and diffusion process at the level of the small healthcare organization. Secondly, the user level is examined in terms of the smaller scale power and political factors affecting individual adoption in the medical practices. Thirdly, we reflect on the use of the adapted DOI frameworks in terms of their use as a retrospective analytical tool for the case study. Further adaptations of the framework are then explored in terms of theoretical development and practical application.

## The macro politics of ICT adoption and diffusion

Our research has shown that in the PCT very few of the staff in the study were aware of either the political or technology driven agendas. They did not see themselves as part of a major ICT initiative or using innovative technology. They were generally unaware that their GP system, EMIS, was likely to be replaced by another due to contract negotiations at a national level. They were shocked that they had not been consulted. Another major Government innovation, the ‘Choose and Book’ scheme whereby patients could be referred electronically for consultation to a hospital had hardly been explored in the PCT. Yet this system was intended to be live across the UK by December 2005. Thus the government rhetoric and local reality of DOI are completely incongruent in this PCT. Patient choice is seen as a cornerstone of the strategy alongside a modern technologically driven health service. The government has used this message extensively to gain votes but at a cost, perhaps, of totally unrealistic expectations from the public.

## Micro-politics: the impact of the NHS ICT strategy on GPs and their practices

As has already been stated above the strategy per se has hardly entered the consciousness of the local GP practices. However, the new GP contract has had a much bigger impact and has brought ICT adoption and innovation, to a lesser extent, to the fore. The reason for this is the requirement to collect management information at source to ensure funding for primary care at the GP level. However some aspects of ICT innovations that have been assimilated into normal working life of many organizations such as email, electronic booking systems and mobile computing are being resisted by GPs in the pilot study.

It is evident that many individual and task-related factors are influencing the diffusion process. User resistance is not overt in many cases, but just manifested through lack of engagement with the new technologies and protectionism due to maintaining traditional professional boundaries. Much learning is taking place on an individual basis with the importance of peer to peer training underestimated. The national agenda for modernization is being pushed through the organization by the strict imposition of quality indices, targets and monitoring. The lure of financial rewards for doctors and increased esteem through enhanced professional status of nurse practitioners are significant factors. ICT plays a vital role in achieving targets and supporting the decision-making processes of less qualified health professionals.

## The contribution of adapted DOI frameworks for data analysis and interpretation

The literature on DOI research provided a different and alternative avenue upon which to examine and analyze the data. In particular the adapted framework extracted from Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) enabled a more sophisticated understanding of the data and provided a new structure from which to explain the politics of the diffusion process. This was more congruent with Baskerville and Pries-Heje’s (2001) view that complex political processes associated with DOI studies may be better understood by adopting an interactive and ecological model of diffusion. The framework used was largely based on an expanded list of Rogers (1995) DOI factors and could be seen to incorporate many of the elements examined by Kautz and Larsen (2000) except that change agentry and opinion leadership was a more central element in their research. The interactive and linked chain models utilized by Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2001) could potentially offer a much greater insight into the business market pull and ICT vendor technology push issues associated with high levels of ICT investment in healthcare. The ICT market for healthcare is extremely competitive where the major players have dominated and pushed out smaller providers. This could be seen with the battle for NHS certification and approval faced by the supplier of the EMIS system. The major and preferred government contractor for NHS GP systems was iSoft Ltd working in collaboration with Accenture as a local service provider for the north east of England. Their competing product, Lorenzo, was pushed as being more nationally compatible than EMIS. An interactive or link chained DOI analysis would provide a more detailed insight into the economic, market and technical forces driving these decisions that would eventually impact on the GP practices without their participative involvement. The ecological or emergent model would have provided a richer insight into the pattern of disruptive events over time, given that in this case government policy was subject to change, the power of the medical professions could not be underestimated, the technology vendors were subject to market forces and public awareness of impending IT failures was increasing. Note, since the research was undertaken, Accenture (in September 2006) has surrendered its role to CSC (the company which is LSP for the north west of England) due to sustained financial pressures along with reports of financial irregularities with its major subcontractor iSoft.

The primary authority and secondary adoption level model of Gallivan (2001) could also provide a richer insight into the power, political, cultural and organizational issues associated with adoption and assimilation of ICT in the case study. His theoretical framework does not contain the same number of DOI factors as the Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen (2003) model but does provide other relevant categories derived from cumulative DOI research studies. In particular, the primary authority adoption decisions could be adapted for an NHS and healthcare perspective. This would require breaking down each level into relevant factors mirroring the organizational structure. In this case it would be Department of Health, Connecting for Health (the national IT programme), Strategic Health Authorities and Primary Care Trusts. The interface between these authorities and any secondary adoption and assimilation processes is fundamental to any understanding of the complex power, political and organizational issues involved. The further categorization of factors into managerial interventions, subjective norms and facilitating conditions (as comprehensively illustrated by Gallivan) would significantly enhance the template analysis in terms of our research study. In our case it would provide richer interpretation concerning the nature, timing and impact of managerial interventions concerning the phased delivery of applications such as pathology results reporting and choose and book for on-line patient referrals to hospitals. In a similar vein, subjective norms could provide a deeper understanding of the shared perceptions of the modernization changes to work practices and the relative merits of the new technologies. Facilitating conditions would be particularly pertinent in exploring issues related to the availability of training, ICT support, and the importance of professional and social networks.

## Developing and contextualizing an adapted framework for NHS General Medical Practice

Through a reflective process of retrospective analysis of the medical practice interview and case data using the DOI factors framework of Mustonen-Ollila and Lyytinen it can be seen that this adds a level of insight into the data. This research is still in the early stages however, but results to date are promising. The framework is structured, logical and straightforward to apply. However, as Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2001) conclude, higher levels of interpretive richness can be obtained from utilizing complementary DOI framework adaptations. Our findings suggest that there is a need for deeper understanding of complex power, political and organizational issues concerning the adoption and assimilation of ICT innovations into general medical practice. Central to understanding these issues are questions related to primary adoption decisions. The interface between primary authority adoption decisions and secondary adoption, implementation and assimilation would seem critical to future progress. Understanding the role of positive barriers to diffusion (Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 1998) would also clarify the interface.

An adapted and contextualized DOI framework based on a synthesis of Gallivan’s (2001) theoretical framework and Mustonen Ollila and Lyytinen’s (2003) categories of DOI factors is proposed (Figure 2), as a useful device for analyzing and interpreting complex problems of ICT diffusion where there are strong contingent authority factors present, such as in NHS organizations. Relevant combinations of DOI factors identified would cut across all stages of the adapted framework. In addition, interactive or linked chain models would be relevant to understand the interface between primary authority and secondary adoption processes providing richer accounts of technology push, market forces and associated organizational networks.

## Conclusions

From our perspective this was the first detailed study that used a DOI research framework to analyze complex political processes within small organizations in healthcare. This contributes to our understanding of IT adoption within professionally dominated cultures that are constrained by authority adoption decisions. It also presents an alternative or complimentary approach to other organizational research in IS such as actor network theory (Latour, 1991; Munir and Jones, 2004), structuration theory (Jones, 1999; Walsham, 2002) and Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1981).

The adapted framework (Figure 2) is derived from a review of retrospective DOI research studies combined with our own exploration of the use of DOI factors (Mustonen Ollila and Lyytinen, 2003) to provide an interpretive account of IS innovation, adoption and assimilation into general medical practice. This approach consisting of template analysis and the derivation of codes and themes based around the DOI framework was found to be pragmatic, informative, useful and also theoretically robust. It is proposed that the adapted framework will offer more potential to research complex organizations with the characteristics previously described.
