Keywords: Open source project Longitudinal study OSS Software project success

## a b s t r a c t

In this paper, we investigate open source software (OSS) success using longitudinal data on OSS projects. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that restrictive OSS licenses have an adverse impact on OSS success. On further analysis, restrictive OSS license is found to be negatively associated with developer interest, but is positively associated with the interest of nondeveloper users and project administrators. We also show that developer and non-developer interest in the OSS project and the project activity levels in any time period signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the project success measures in subsequent time period. The implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for OSS research and practice are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

According to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition provided by Open Source Initiative (www.opensource.org, last accessed 5/30/2008), open source software (OSS) allows users to have access to the source code of the software, the freedom to use the software as they see <sup>fi</sup>t, modify the software to create derived works, and redistribute the derivative software for free or at a charge. The users of the derivative software could themselves modify and/or use the software according to their own needs. The open source approach is considered more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than traditional software development because OSS avoids the inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of a strong intellectual property regime [26] and it implements concurrent design and testing of software modules [22]. The widespread adoption of open source software (e.g. Apache, Sendmail, various <sup>fl</sup>avors of Linux) has generated immense interest among academics, who want to understand and explain various aspects of this phenomenon [6,11,28,39,40,41]. A key area of academic interest in OSS is the understanding of the predictors of OSS success. The results from studies on OSS project success also have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant managerial implications for OSS project administrators because successful OSS projects attract talented developers, attract more non-developer users, and attract more sponsors [16,25]. However, there are no known studies that have examined the OSS success from a longitudinal perspective. In this paper, we attempt to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by investigating empirically the relationship between OSS project success and OSS project characteristics, including time-dependent characteristics. In addition, we consider the success measures themselves as inter-related and include these inter-relationships in our model. One of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our analysis is that restrictive OSS licenses have an adverse impact on OSS success, which is in line with existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings [36]. However, in practice most OSS licenses are restrictive in nature (e.g., about 63% of OSS licenses are GPL, the most restrictive OSS license<sup>1</sup>). Hence, we analyze the data further and <sup>fi</sup>nd that restrictive OSS license negatively impacts developer interest, but positively impacts the interest of users and project administrators. We also show that interest in the OSS project and the project activity levels in any period is strongly associated with the interest and activity in subsequent periods. Our paper contributes to OSS research and practice by building on existing studies as follows:

a) We use longitudinal data on OSS projects, collected each month over a period of 5 years, to estimate the statistical models of the relationship between OSS project success and the determinants of success. Use of longitudinal data allows us to account for changes within OSS projects and differences among OSS projects.

b) Current literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three measures of OSS project success — developer interest in the project, project activity, and user interest in the project. However, the success measures are considered as independent of one another in current studies. We extend the existing research by including the potential relationships among these success measures in our model.

c) In our paper we include variables that capture network externalities, such as the operating system and programming language, which are not studied or are included only as control factors in current OSS success papers.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses related research and Section 3 presents the determinants of OSS project success. In Section 4, we present the data and statistical models for estimation, followed by the discussion of the results and the implications for OSS research and practice. We conclude by highlighting the contribution of our paper and future research opportunities in this area.

## 2. Related research

Scholars have been interested in the factors that contribute to the success of OSS projects since this understanding helps predict the effects of legal and policy decisions on the survival of this competing software development model. According to the DeLone and McLean's model for information systems project success, there are six interrelated success measures — system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact and organizational impact [10]. However, these conventional measures focus on the use and the use environment of the software. In the case of OSS, the development environment is more publicly visible while the use environment is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to observe [7]. Also, unlike commercial software projects, most OSS projects rely on voluntary participants and the success indicators applied to commercial software projects, such as being on time, being on budget, and meeting speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, may not be applicable to OSS projects [9,36]. Hence the current literature on OSS identi<sup>fi</sup>es other measures focused on participation and project activity to assess the success of an OSS project. For instance, project activity level, development team/community size (i.e. number of active contributors to the project), and time taken to <sup>fi</sup>x software bugs are some of the key measures for OSS project success and these success measures are strongly correlated [7]. [37] identify similar measures for OSS project success, i.e. the extent to which a project attracts input from the development community (e.g. number of developers), and the extent to which it produces observable outputs such as the addition of new features to the software or the <sup>fi</sup>xing of software bugs. [36] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed user interest over time (i.e. change in the number of subscribers to an OSS project) and amount of development activity (i.e. the number of <sup>fi</sup>les released) as measures of OSS project success.

On the topic of the factors critical for OSS success, literature has recognized the voluntary nature of developer participation, the freedom of users to modify the software, and the need to attract sponsors. The participation of developers in creating, debugging and maintaining the software resulting from the project is critical for its success. Hence, some of the OSS success factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the literature relate to the developers themselves, such as developer motivation and interest [4] and the presence of a critical mass of developers in the project [27]. [4] suggest that the technological characteristics of the projects are also important for success and show that server-based OSS projects, such as Apache web server, are more successful than client-based OSS projects, such as Linux. Other researchers <sup>fi</sup>nd that the degree and nature of network embeddedness of an OSS project impact its success and that greater embeddedness does not always result in project success [15]. The freedom to modify and redistribute OSS is determined by its license and some studies have shown that the restrictiveness of the OSS license plays a role in the success or failure of the OSS projects [24,37]. While existing OSS research has helped us understand the above factors as important for OSS project success, the studies have mostly used cross-sectional data for analysis of the models. There are no known studies that have incorporated both time-invariant and time-dependent characteristics of OSS projects in examining success. Most OSS projects are continually in development [9] and the interests of developers, nondeveloper users, and sponsors are likely to change throughout the life of the project as different versions of their software are released. Hence, it is important to understand the changing dynamics over time of the relationships between the project success measures and the project characteristics. [36] used two waves of data to analyze project success as the changes in user-interest and project activity levels between the two snap-shots , but the two waves can only help to capture the impact of incremental change and cannot highlight the longitudinal trends [33]. In contrast, our study uses OSS project data from multiple time periods to capture the longitudinal trends in the relationships between project success and project characteristics. In addition, unlike [36], our model considers the cumulative user-interest until the previous time period instead of the user-interest in the previous time period only, and hence allows us to capture the longterm effects of project success. On the practical side, there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant managerial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to understanding OSS project success since the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our study can help OSS administrators to develop long-term project management strategies.

## 3. Determinants of open source software project success

Success in OSS projects is better understood by examining the development environment which is more publicly visible [7]. Scholars have proposed measures such as project activity levels, release of new features, and the time taken to <sup>fi</sup>x software bugs [7,37]. Since OSS projects rely on voluntary input, the ability of a project to attract the interest of and contributions from the developers is a key success measure [36]. OSS is not just about developing or modifying software. Increasingly more non-developer users are relying on open source software for personal and business needs [24], and the interest shown by these users for open source software may be an important indicator of the project's success [36]. This interest can be measured by the traf<sup>fi</sup>c on the OSS project website and the extent of downloads of the software code [8]. The various success measures for OSS projects focus on the project activity and the interest of the main bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries — developers and non-developer users. Hence, we use developer interest, user interest, and project activity levels as the three measures of OSS success in our model. In addition, these three measures are more readily available from a single source (Sourceforge.net) for thousands of OSS projects for many months, which facilitate our longitudinal study. [36] show that the user-interest in a particular time period is correlated with the project activity in the subsequent time period and propose that project activity in a prior period might spur developer interest in the following period, though this speci<sup>fi</sup>c relationship was not tested. Hence, we believe that all three measures of project success are inter-related and include these interrelationships in our model.

There are two broad categories of factors which impact OSS project success — time-invariant factors related to the OSS project and timedependent project characteristics. The OSS project attributes that are incorporated in our model have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in literature as affecting the development and use of OSS. These project attributes are — (a) OSS license [8,24,28,36]; and (b) Interest shown by developers and non-developer users in an OSS [23,36]. We also include the operating system and programming language of the OSS in our model because these factors impact the participation of developers and users in the OSS [25], and hence its success. Each factor and its effects on project success are discussed in detail below.

## 3.1. Time-invariant OSS project characteristics

Three time-invariant project characteristics included in our model are the OSS license type, operating system, and programming language.

## 3.1.1. OSS license

OSS license has been found to play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the success or failure of an OSS project by affecting the interests of users and developers in the project [24,37]. One of the main characteristics that differentiate various OSS licenses is the degree of restrictions imposed on the user to re-distribute software derived or modified from OSS software [12]. While some papers classify OSS licenses into two broad categories, i.e. restricted and non-restricted [25], other papers propose three types of licenses, i.e., highly restrictive, restrictive, and unrestrictive [12,24]. In our paper, we use the three categories of OSS licenses re<sup>fl</sup>ected by their varying restrictiveness [13]. Strong-Copyleft licenses (e.g. GNU General Public License or GPL) provide that once software is licensed by a developer, the subsequent derivative programs based on the original must also be licensed similarly. Weak-Copyleft licenses (e.g. GNU Lesser General Public License or LGPL) require that once a program is licensed by a developer, the subsequent programs based on the original must also be licensed similarly. However the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed software can be released under a different license under certain conditions. Non-Copyleft licenses (e.g. Berkeley Software Distribution or BSD) provide that developers are not obligated to inherit the original license, when they redistribute any derivative work. Currently, there is a critical debate going on in the OSS developer community about the license best suited for promoting the philosophy of open source movement (e.g. [31]). While the proponents of open source software advise OSS developers to make their licenses GPL compatible (i.e. Strong-Copyleft) [38], some existing research has found that Strong-Copyleft license has an adverse impact on userinterest in an OSS [36]. This adverse impact could be attributed to resistance from OSS users who prefer to retain the rights for reuse of the software code in a way that best serves their objectives. For example, software that includes any amount of GPL licensed (a Strong-Copyleft license) code has to be released under GPL license. Since not all users might be interested in redistributing any derivative software under the parent license, they might prefer a less restrictive license, e.g. BSD (which is a Non-Copyleft license). Therefore, we propose that restrictive OSS licenses are negatively associated with OSS success measures.

## 3.1.2. Operating system

To understand the important role played by operating system in an OSS project's success, we need to consider the history of open source software (OSS). OSS can trace its roots to the GNU project and the Free Software Foundation launched by Richard Stallman [35], and the efforts of the Computer Science Research Group (CSRG) at the University of California at Berkeley in improving UNIX operating system in the 1970s and 1980s. The early work on UNIX was funded by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), and it resulted in a network of software programmers (popularly referred to as hackers in the open source community) who helped to debug, maintain, and improve the UNIX system. During the 1980s and early 1990s, open source software was developed in several relatively isolated groups. USENET and the Internet helped to coordinate their development efforts, and to build up strong user communities. Slowly, much of the software developed by these different groups was integrated. As a result of this integration, complete application environments could be built for UNIX operating system using open source software. In short, developers trained in UNIX, and later Linux (developed by Linus Torvalds in early 1990s), formed the initial core of the OSS community. The earlier members of this OSS community built various other applications, libraries, and utilities that complemented or supplemented the various <sup>fl</sup>avors of UNIX and Linux in existence. It is only recently, that we have started to see OSS applications and utilities being developed for other operating systems such as Windows. For these reasons, we expect that OSS projects developed to run on UNIX or Linux, rather than other operating systems, will attract more developers and users to the project and lead to greater project activity [8]. Hence, we propose that the use of UNIX/Linux operating system for an OSS project is positively associated with the project's success.

## 3.1.3. Programming language

The programming language is an important factor in determining OSS project success, because the participants have to be familiar with the programming language in order to understand the source code and make any changes. The C programming language has played a vital role in early OSS development for several reasons. First, it is the system implementation language for the nascent UNIX operating system [30] and we discussed earlier the dominance of UNIX/Linux operating system in OSS community. Second, despite the memorymanagement problems of the C language (e.g., several C functions are vulnerable to buffer over<sup>fl</sup>ow), it is one of the preferred language of OSS developers for codes that require portability, need faster processing, have real-time requirements, or are tightly coupled to the UNIX/Linux kernel. Furthermore, existing programs like parser generators or GUI builders that generate C code also add to its advantages, since these programs can reduce the effort required to code using C. Finally, high-quality C compilers are available as open-source software over the Internet. The best-known and most widely used open source C compiler is the Free Software Foundation's GNU C compiler. As a result, C and its later derivatives such as C++, Visual C++, and C# are still the preferred languages of open source software developers. A survey in 2005 by Computerworld found that C# and C++ <sup>fi</sup>gured in the top <sup>fi</sup>ve languages used by software developers.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, we expect that an open source software project written in C or its later versions are associated with more developers [36] and greater project activity [8].

## 3.2. Time-dependent OSS project characteristics

In addition to the time-invariant project characteristics, several time-dependent variables also impact OSS project success. The <sup>fi</sup>rst time-dependent variable we include in our model is the development status of the OSS project. The other three time-dependent variables in our model are the developer interest, user interest, and project activity until the previous time period, which are expected to impact the success measures in the current time period. The propositions regarding these variables are described below.

## 3.2.1. Project status

Software project's progress at any point in time can be identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed by its development status at that time. System development life cycle approach suggests that a software project can be in one of <sup>fi</sup>ve stages — Requirements Planning, Analysis, Design, Development, and Maintenance [18]. Another common way to describe project progress is by the software versions, such as Planning, Alpha, Beta, and Stable. Literature from software engineering suggests that software quality e.g. completeness, consistency, testability, usability, reliability, etc., improve as the software progresses to the later stages of development [3,14]. Therefore, OSS in later stages of development should be preferred by those interested in using, rather than modifying, the software. As a result of the increased usage, developers in an OSS project should also get more feedback, suggestions, and bug reports during the later stages, which in turn should result in more project activity. Furthermore, projects in advance stages signal a commitment on the part of the project administrators to support, guide, and advance the project. This commitment could attract new developers who are interested in working on active OSS projects, but wait to see the viability of the projects. Therefore, OSS projects in the later stages of development in any time period are expected to attract more developers, users, and activity in the subsequent time period. This reasoning leads us to the proposition that OSS project status is positively associated with its success measures.

3.2.2. Developer interest, user interest, and project activity until previous time period

Software belongs to the category of products/services that display network effects, both direct and indirect. A direct network effect is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the increasing utility that a user derives from consumption of a product as the number of other users who consume the same product increases [20,21,32]. Indirect network effects arise when the value of a product increases as the number of, or the variety of complementary goods or services increases [4]. In case of OSS projects, an increase in the potential number of users of the OSS results in an increase in the knowledgebase for that software. This increase happens because more users can now communicate with each other about their experiences with the software and seek help from each other when solving any problems related to the software. In addition, as the number of users increases, so does the amount of feedback on the software (e.g. software bugs, request for new features etc.), which provides opportunities to improve the OSS. OSS software can also exhibit indirect network effects as seen by the large variety of complementary software for OSS projects based on Linux operating system and Apache web server because of their large installation base. Due to these network effects, when users choose OSS, their knowledge about the existing installation base affects their expectations about its future installation base, which in turn plays a crucial role in their decision to download and possibly use the OSS. In fact, rival <sup>fi</sup>rms in the software industry often try to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' expectations about the installation base of their products, especially when consumers have only imperfect information about the size of the actual installed base. For example, the competition between OS/2 (by IBM) and Windows (by Microsoft) in 1992 demonstrates each <sup>fi</sup>rm's intentions to in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers' expectations. IBM and Microsoft both disputed the estimated numbers of sales by each other, and each company announced a wider adoption of its operating system [2]. [29] and [36] suggest that greater user interest in a project provides a wider audience for developers and increases the intellectual contributions over time. [36] hypothesized that user-interest has a positive impact on project activity levels in subsequent period and found support for the hypothesis. Surprisingly, the current studies on

OSS projects' success have not seriously examined the impact of these network effects on project success. Based on the network effects discussed above we propose that the interests of developers and users and the project activity in previous time periods are positively associated with the OSS project's success in the current time period.

## 3.3. Interrelationships among OSS project success measures

Existing studies suggest that the three key measures of OSS success are not independent of each other. For instance, [8] found strong correlation between developer interest and subsequent project activity level. Furthermore, OSS developers are experienced and regular users of the product they develop [27]. In fact, there is some evidence of relationship between the number of developers working on a project and the number of downloads for that OSS [23]. Therefore, at any point in time, we should expect developer-interest in an OSS to be positively correlated with user-interest in that OSS. We model the impact of project activity levels in any period on the user-interest in the same period because whenever newer versions of the software are released or new patches are developed and ready for use, we should expect to see an increase in the number of downloads. The reasons are that existing non-developer users download the latest versions of the software; new non-developer users download the software; and new developers who are attracted by the activity level of the software download the software. Finally, it has been suggested in the current literature that many developers participate in an open source project because they want to send out a signal about their advanced skills to potential employers [24] and to get peer-recognition for their programming skills [4]. Irrespective of whether the developers are interested in peer recognition or signaling their skills to potential employers, they would prefer to associate themselves with relatively more successful open source projects. Successful projects are more likely to provide the exposure to their peers and potential employers that developers want in order to signal their programming skills. Therefore, developer-interest in an OSS in any time period should be positively associated with the performance of the OSS project in the preceding time period, i.e. cumulative project activity and cumulative user-interest in the preceding periods, and developer-interest in the preceding period.

## 3.4. Control variables

In our study, we control for other factors that could be important to OSS project success but are not explored in this research. The age of a project may be a proxy for other factors affecting success, including the developers' group experience and the entrenchment of the software in the OSS user community [36] and we account for the effects of project age on success by using project age at time t as a control variable. OSS projects are targeted at different types of audiences. The non-developer users are average desktop users who download and use the software but do not actively participate in development activities. The developers participate in the OSS project by contributing code, writing documentation, and providing bug <sup>fi</sup>xes. The project administrators are software professionals such as quality engineers and systems administrators who are responsible for managing OSS project activities and developer communities. Since each type of audience has different expectations from OSS projects, the impact of the project characteristics on their participation are expected to be different, thus affecting the success measures differently [36]. However, we do not propose to study the effects of the audience type in our model and, hence, we use the target audience as a control variable.

## 3.5. Interaction effects between license choice and target audience

Even though we do not study the main effects of the target audience on project success, we believe that there are interesting interaction effects between the license choice and the type of target audience (i.e., developer or non-developer user) which may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the project success. For example, developers might prefer licenses that give them freedom to redistribute their derivative software and therefore, might not like to be constrained by Strong-Copyleft licenses. On the other hand, nondeveloper users are mostly passive users, since they are not interested in producing and distributing derivative works, and are less likely to be concerned about the type of OSS license. Surprisingly, none of the existing studies on OSS project success have investigated the impact of OSS licenses on OSS projects targeted at speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of audience. Therefore, we include the interaction effects between OSS license and the type of target audience in our OSS success model.

## 4. Measurement, data and statistical models for OSS success

In this section, we present the measurement of the model variables, the dataset used in our study and the statistical models chosen for the estimation of the relationships between OSS project success measures and their determinants.

## 4.1. Measurement

For our study, the measures are based on secondary data and we use “month” as the unit of the time period in our model. Developer interest in an OSS can be measured by the number of active contributors to the project or the number of developers participating in the project [27,37]. We operationalize developer interest in time period t (month t) by the number of developers working on the project in that month. Project activity can be measured by activities such as number of <sup>fi</sup>les released by the project, number of bugs <sup>fi</sup>xed, and time taken to <sup>fi</sup>x the bugs etc [9,37]. We operationalize project activity till time (t−1) by the total number of <sup>fi</sup>les released by a project till month (t−1) and the project activity for time t by the number of <sup>fi</sup>les released in month t. User interest is measured by factors such as the number of people accessing the project website, number of downloads of the project, and number of people accessing project related links [37]. We operationalize user interest till time (t−1) by the total number of downloads till month (t−1) of the project and the user interest in time t by the number of downloads in month t.

There are several measures to identify an OSS project's development status in order to assess its progress. Sourceforge.net uses a six stage model – Planning, Pre-Alpha, Alpha, Beta, Production/Stable, and Mature – and each project registered on Sourceforge.net records the development stage of its software on the website. The projects in stages 5 and 6 are considered stable projects and have the best chance to build a successful community around them [23]. Since we plan to use project data from Sourceforge.net for our study, we measure the project status for each OSS project by the development stage reported by the project administrator on Sourceforge.net. The project age is measured by the number of days from when it was registered at Sourceforge.net till month t.

The license choice, operating system, programming language, and target audience are all categorical and unordered variables, and hence captured by dummy indicators. For example, a value of 1 for the variable S\_Copyleft indicates the use of Strong-Copyleft license for the project and 0 indicates otherwise. A value of 1 for the variable W\_Copyleft indicates the use of Weak-Copyleft license for the project and 0 indicates otherwise. A project with Non\_Copyleft license is indicated by values of 0 for both S\_Copyleft and W\_Copyleft variables for the project. Similarly, we classify the target audience of any open source software into three categories — non-developer users, developers, and administrators. The three user categories are operationalized as three dichotomous variables User, Develop, and Admin respectively, since an OSS can be targeted at more than one type of user. ## 4.2. Data

The unbalanced panel data used in this study comes from the information on open source projects listed at Sourceforge.net. Sourceforge.net is a free service that maintains the largest database of software applications, which are preferably released under an open source license. For each project, the database provides a description of the software, links to download it and to obtain more information, and a history of the project's releases. A major concern about the validity of secondary data on open source projects relates to the accuracy of the data since it was self reported by project owners/administrators [9]. However, [24] provide a plausible reason for not suspecting the accuracy of this data. They explain “…..the project leaders are trying to recruit new developers, attract new users, and solicit donations for their project. Undertaking a “bait-and-switch” strategy to do so, e.g. making the project appear something other than what it really $i s ,$ is unlikely to be a positive signal for prospective developers, users and/or sponsors.” Hence, we believe that the use of project data from Sourceforge.net is valid for our study. In addition, to avoid other pitfalls of using a secondary data source such as Sourceforge.net [19], we did not use spiders or software to “screen-scrap” data from the Sourceforge.net website. Instead, we accessed data directly from a data warehouse, which is populated with Sourceforge.net data on a regular basis.

The Sourceforge.net database contains information on more than 100,000 software projects. For the purpose of this study we considered only those projects for which complete information was available, and which had been registered between January 1999 and December 2005. The number of such projects was 8627. Both the user-interest and developer-interest in an OSS project depend on their values in previous period and the error terms will be auto-correlated. Also, we have time-invariant variables as predictors for user-interest and developer-interest. ## 5. Model estimation results and discussion

The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates for the models for user-interest, developer-interest, and OSS project activity levels are presented in Table 4. In the following discussions, we present the major <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications for OSS research and practice.

## 5.1. Impact of time-dependent predictors on OSS project success

Our results show that project status has a positive impact on all three measures of OSS success, and as expected, the effects are greater on user interest and activity levels than on developer interest. Developers are less likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the OSS project status as they can contribute at any stage of the software development. On the other hand, users are more likely to download software in the later stage of development since it indicates greater stability and usability. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that project activity and developer-interest in any period is positively associated with user-interest in the same period. In addition, the project activity and developer interest till any time period positively impact the developer-interest and project activity in the following period. With respect to the user-interest, our study shows that user-interest till any period affects all the OSS performance measures in the subsequent period. Interestingly, the effects on developer-interest and user-interest are positive, showing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of network effects, while the effect on project activity is negative. Some plausible reasons for the negative effects of userinterest on project activity are: (a) Core developers believe that the software does not need any further improvements and therefore stop paying attention to user feedback, bug-reports, feature request, etc.; (b) The project does not have enough developers to address user feedback, bug-reports, feature requests; (c) The project has been abandoned because of <sup>fi</sup>nancial or other problems; and (d) The increased user-interest in the project implies increased participation and more bugs or requests for features, which leads to staggered <sup>fi</sup>le releases to accommodate the requests<sup>3</sup>. Our result is contrary to [36], who found that change in user-interest has a positive impact on project activity levels. Since [36] used only two waves of data separated by a few months, their result could re<sup>fl</sup>ect the incremental impact over a short-time period [33]. Our study uses monthly data over a period of <sup>fi</sup>ve years, and therefore, the results obtained in our study could illustrate the long-term effects of user-interest on project activity. In short, the combined results from [36] and from our research suggest that while change in user-interest has positive impact on project activity levels over short-term, user-interest adversely impacts project activity levels over longer periods.

The results from our study show the important role played by network effects of OSS, which should be taken into account when discussing the success or failure of any particular OSS. For example, Apache (with approximately 70% share of the web server market) is often cited as the example to illustrate the phenomenal success that an OSS can achieve. One of the factors that have contributed to its success is the large existing user base that provides several indirect network bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as technical support, and an array of supplementary and complementary software. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding the impact of current user base on success may provide a possible explanation for the limited success (in terms of user base) of most OSS. Many OSS projects have very limited number of users to start with (mostly consisting of core developers who work on the software), and this user base might not be large enough to encourage future downloads of the software.

## 5.2. Impact of time-invariant predictors on OSS project success

Our results show that OSS projects that develop software for UNIX Linux attract more developers and have higher activity levels. An interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is the positive impact of OSS projects built on Windows operating system on all project success measures. This <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates the increased acceptance of the Windows platform in OSS and has implications for OSS project managers in attracting developers and users to their projects. OSS projects that are written in

C programming language or one of its later derivatives also attract more developers and show higher activity levels. Interestingly, while projects using UNIX/Linux have a negative impact on user interest, projects written in C or derivative programming language have a positive impact on user interest. A possible explanation for these results is that the number of non-developer users who have UNIX/ Linux-based systems may be lower when compared to the number of users familiar with C or C-like programming languages. Also, OSS software developed in C or C-like programming languages are available for both Unix/Linux and Windows platforms and can reach more potential users.

With respect to the impact of OSS license on project success, our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that restrictive licenses (Strong-Copyleft and Weak-Copyleft licenses) negatively impact the activity levels of the OSS project. This result is similar to [36] where the authors found that restrictive OSS licenses have a negative impact on project success. However, about 63% of OSS projects have GPL license, which is a Strong\_Copyleft license (refer footnote 3, page 3). To explain this apparent contradiction, we included the interactions between the Strong\_Copyleft license type (the most restrictive OSS license type) and the target audience (i.e., developers, non-developer users, and project administrators) as additional predictors of project success. When the interaction effects are considered, Strong-Copyleft license has a negative impact only if the target audiences are software developers. This adverse impact of Strong-Copyleft license on the number of downloads of OSS targeted at developers could be attributed to the restrictive nature of these licenses. While Strong-Copyleft licenses permit free use and modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of OSS (factors most likely to be relevant to desktop users and administrators), they impose certain key restrictions on the redistribution of any derivative software, which may be important for developers who like to retain the right to modify the reuse terms of the derivative software to best serve their objectives. Since not all developers might be interested in redistributing derivative software under the parent license, they might prefer a less restrictive OSS license.

Our result on the interaction effects of OSS license and administrator users may provide an explanation for an earlier <sup>fi</sup>nding by [24], who found that OSS geared towards system administrators are more likely to have a restrictive license such as Strong-Copyleft license. However, it raises an interesting question — why is the positive impact of Strong-Copyleft license on user interest reduced when the target audience is non-developer user than when the target audience is administrator? One reason could be the fact that non-developer users generally use the software on an “as is” basis. They do not make modi<sup>fi</sup>cation to the software code or integrate it with other software, and therefore, are less concerned about the restrictions imposed by OSS license. However, project administrators usually decide on the OSS license and may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the project goals (for e.g., to protect the open source software from commercial exploitation [34]). OSS project leaders can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in choosing the license best suited to their target audience. For instance, they can choose a less restrictive license (e.g., BSD license) for OSS targeted at developers and a more restrictive license (e.g., GPL license) for OSS targeted at desktop users or other project administrators.

## 6. Conclusion

In this paper, we empirically investigate the longitudinal effects of OSS project-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics on the success of OSS projects. Our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms the importance of both time-invariant and timedependent characteristics of an OSS project for its success. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the three success measures – developer interest, user interest, and project activity – traditionally used in OSS literature are inter-related. In particular, the interest levels of OSS participants and the project activity in any given time period affect the project success measures in the subsequent time period. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant but adverse impact of Strong-Copyleft license (restrictive license) on the success of the OSS project is a re-con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings by [36]. However, our study explores this <sup>fi</sup>nding further and shows that the negative impact of restrictive license on user interest is present only if the OSS is targeted at software developers. When OSS projects are targeted at non-developer users and administrators, a restrictive license has a positive impact on the user-base. While [36] show that user-interest in any time period is positively associated with the project activity in the subsequent time period, we add to this understanding by showing that in the long-term, the user-interest is negatively associated with the project activity in subsequent time periods. Also, [36] consider the level of user interest on a project prior to the current period as a control variable and do not report its effect on project success. However, we include this variable in our model and show that user-interest until a given time period is positively correlated with user-interest in the subsequent time period. The key results and the incremental contributions to OSS research of this paper are summarized in Table 5.

There are several potential extensions possible from our research. The impact of the target audience on the project success measures can be explored in detail, since projects targeted at administrators seem to have a negative impact on user interest and project activity levels. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the effect of Windows-based OSS projects on the project success may indicate the increasing acceptance of Windows operating systems in the OSS community, and the competitive dynamics between Windows operating system and UNIX/Linux operating systems in OSS projects will be useful to explore. While the categories of operating systems and programming languages used in this study are based on the trends observed in OSS surveys, future research can incorporate a different categorization depending on the future trends in OSS. The OSS projects can also be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the purpose of the software, as reported in Sourceforge.net website (e.g., Multimedia, Networking, Storage, Security, etc.). The varying purposes of the OSS software may be a key determinant of developer-interest and user-interest and may be an interesting issue to investigate in future research<sup>4</sup>. Another issue for potential research is the impact of the presence of an active project sponsor on the project success.
