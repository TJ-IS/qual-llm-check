# Bridging social and technical interfaces in organizations: An interpretive analysis of time-space distanciation

Leigh Jin <sup>a,1</sup>, Daniel Robey <sup>b,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Systems, San Francisco State University, 1600 Holloway Avenue, San Francisco, CA 94132, United States

<sup>b</sup> Department of Computer Information Systems, Georgia State University, P.O. Box 4015, Atlanta, GA 30302-4015, United States

Received 27 September 2007; accepted 17 December 2007

## Abstract

Contemporary organizations increasingly rely upon information technologies as platforms for their core work processes. The Internet age has witnessed the creation of new business models based almost entirely on electronically-mediated business processes involving multiple organizations. Information systems link suppliers, manufacturers, logistics companies, and other partners, allowing orga nizations to add value using smaller investments in physical assets. The creation of these linkage establishes both technical and social interfaces between organizations and their business partners. We apply Giddens’ concept of time-space distanciation to analyze the interfaces in iTalk, an organi zation in Silicon Valley hosting Internet voicemail services. iTalk achieved initial success in bridging external social and technical interfaces with the major regional telephone companies in US, allowing their voicemail service to attract millions of subscribers. In efect, iTalk used information technologie to dis-embed social and technical elements from global systems (the telephone companies) and re-embed them as part of iTalk’s local organizational presence. However, iTalk was unable to provide a suficiently reliable service to customers as volume increased. Ironically, bridging external interfaces created internal interfaces within iTalk, which in turn produced technical problems and social conflicts that were not satisfactorily resolved by the time iTalk was acquired by a larger media company in 2001. The study provides theoretical understanding of the challenges associated with creating and sustaining social and technical interfaces in organizations that rely heavily upon electronically-med iated business processes that cross organizational boundaries. - 2008 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Many contemporary organizations rely upon information technologies as platforms for their core work processes. By creating information systems that link suppliers, manufacturers, logistics companies, financial services, marketing, and other functions, organizations seek to add value and to control costs, thereby making it possible to operate with smaller investments in physical assets. This approach to organizing is evident under various labels, including ‘‘e-business” (Jackson, Harris, & Eckersley, 2003; Weill & Vitale, 2001), ‘‘virtual organization” (Davidow & Malone, 1992; Mowshowitz, 2002), ‘‘T-form organization” (Lucas, 1996), and ‘‘extended enterprise” (Gries & Kasarda, 1997) among others.

The dot com boom in the 1990s fueled interest in leveraging Internet technologies to create economic value with a limited investment in physical assets (Keen & McDonald, 2000; Straub, 2004; Wheeler, 2002). The Internet was instrumental in loosening institutional structures and increasing the scope and speed of interorganizational relationships. Organizations continue to depend upon the Internet and other information and communication technologies to create new products and services, streamline business processes, and overcome the constraints of physical location. The dot com boom also infused new ventures with capital and human resources to develop new business capabilities. In many cases, financial resources provided by venture capitalists far outweighed revenue streams from customers, which were often non-existent. As a result of these developments, organizations became more dependent upon other organizations for resource flows and core business processes. They needed, in efect, to manage the ‘‘extended enterprise” essential to providing customer value (Borys & Jemison, 1989; Greis & Kasarda, 1997). Our purpose is to understand the processes by which organizations forge external partnerships and the challenges associated with bridging the resulting social and technical interfaces between organizations and their partners.

We analyze the case of iTalk (a pseudonym), an organization that was founded in 1998, raised \$53 million in venture capital, marketed a free nationwide Internet voicemail service in 2000, then foundered and was acquired in 2001 by a larger media company at a price less than half the amount invested in it. The acquiring company ofered positions to none of iTalk’s management, and most of iTalk’s employees left. Our analysis focuses on iTalk’s social and technical systems and the interfaces within the company and with its business partners. We draw upon Giddens’ concept of time-space distanciation (1984, 1990, 1991) to explain the dis-embedding and re-embedding of iTalk’s social and technical relationships.

## 2. Information technology and time-space distanciation

Organizations that are designed around electronically-mediated business processes can be seen as part of a broader change in social relationships enabled in part by the ability of information technologies to stretch the boundaries of time and space. Giddens’ theory of social structuration (1984) and his later analyses of modernity (1990, 1991) provide a comprehensive theoretical account of the processes by which social systems are broken apart and recombined across time and space. Although alternative theoretical lenses are available to analyze electronically-mediated organizations, Giddens’ analysis draws particular attention to the temporal and spatial character of social systems. Alternative theories include socio-technical systems theory, strategic alliances, and virtual teamwork. Sociotechnical systems theory (Davis, 1972; Trist & Bamforth, 1951) adopts the normative posi tion that social and technical systems should be jointly optimized without addressing the impact of time or space on either. Socio-technical systems studies also typically focus more narrowly on work design rather than organizational design. Although focused at the organizational level, studies of strategic alliances typically focus on governance issues, including coordinated contracts and revenue links (Child, 1987), which require high levels of trust among trading partners (Adler, 2001; Gallivan & Depledge, 2003). However, the influence of temporal and spatial diferences between partners is not addressed explicitly. The most relevant empirical research on work that is distributed across time and space has focused on teams rather than organizations (e.g. Nandhakumar, 2002; Watson-Mannheim, Chudoba & Crowston, 2002). For example, Watson-Mannheim et al.’s (2002) articulation of discontinuities in virtual teams bears similarities to the time-space edges in Giddens’ analyses. It seems inappropriate, however, to apply theories drawn from the study of teams to analyze organizations.

Focusing broadly on societal change, Giddens distinguishes between the concepts of ‘‘place”, which is conceptualized as geographically situated locales, and ‘‘space”, which refers to unbounded global activities:

In pre-modern societies, space and place largely coincide, since the spatial dimensions of social life are, for most of the population, and in most respects, dominated by ‘‘presence” – by localised activities. The advent of modernity increasingly tears space away from place by fostering relations between ‘‘absent” others, locationally distant from any given situation of face-to-face interaction (1990, p. 18).

In efect, modern social relations are ‘‘dis-placed” as distant events are ‘‘placed into” the local environment (Giddens, 1990, p. 140). Consequently, diferent areas of the globe become more closely interconnected through technology, allowing local activities to incorporate remote and absent elements.

Giddens’ proposes the term ‘‘time-space distanciation” to describe the process of disembedding social relations from distant contexts and re-embedding them into local con texts, thus connecting presence with absence. Giddens defines the dis-embedding of social relations as ‘‘the ‘lifting out’ of social relations from local contexts of interaction and their restructuring across indefinite spans of time-space” (1990, p. 21). Time-space distanciation is enabled by technologies for storing and retrieving information, including writing, printing and electronic communication. As Giddens notes, ‘‘the coming of electronic media has undoubtedly accentuated these aspects of displacement, since they override presence so instantaneously and at such distance” (1990, p. 141). Thus, dis-embedding allows organizations to break free from many local restraints.

However, as the elements of social systems extend beyond traditional temporal and spatial boundaries, they must eventually be inserted, or re-embedded, into local contexts. As illustrated by Giddens (1990), the destruction of older city neighborhoods and their replacement by towering skyscrapers triggers a re-embedding process that transforms social interfaces.<sup>2</sup> Just as the borders between urban neighborhoods are redefined when new buildings and highways are constructed, so too are organizational interfaces transformed as global elements are re-embedded next to local contexts. Giddens notes that changed contexts of action may either support or undermine the dis-embedded elements. Efective re-embedding requires attention to the interfaces between global and local elements, ensuring that social relations lifted out of remote contexts are successfully integrated with local conditions and activities. Giddens (1990) suggests that time-space distanciation is particularly risky because of persistent ‘‘design faults” and other ‘‘unintended consequences”, which arise unexpectedly during dis-embedding, and re-embedding.

Although Giddens focuses his analyses at the societal level, his insights are potentially applicable to any social system, including organizations that rely on information systems to establish partnerships with other organizations and to integrate interorganizational business processes. A few empirical studies use time-space analysis at the organizational level. For example, Schultze and Boland’s (2000) study of outsourced contractors examines the tensions inherent in work relationships that were dis-embedded from one context (a consulting firm) and re-embedded into a client’s organization. Although insightful in its use of time-space analysis, Schultze and Boland focus narrowly on contract work and shed little light on the management of interfaces across an entire organization. Sahay, Nicholson, and Krishna (2003) use Giddens’ time-space analysis, among other theories, to analyze global software work involving technology-mediated outsourcing relationships that stretch across geography and culture. Sahay et al. expose the tensions between the assumption that global work occurs independently of location and the reality of local, placedependent work practices.

As organizations create multiple interfaces with business partners, their success increasingly depends upon the re-embedding practices that tie distributed elements together along the interfaces of the social system. We focus on both social and technical interfaces because re-embedding processes typically rely upon information technologies. In the following sections, we describe the implications of time-space distanciation for both social and technical organizational interfaces. We deal first with external interfaces, then with internal interfaces.

## 2.1. External interfaces

An organization’s reliance upon other organizations creates interfaces between the organization and its business partners. Within the technical system, interfaces between partners require inter-operable technologies that are compatible in terms of requirements such as data and communication protocols. Activity at such interfaces would ideally be mediated by technologies that provide a seamless flow of information across the extended enterprise. However, the creation and maintenance of such technologies become problematic where business partners operate separate legacy systems. If such systems could be designed without regard to existing legacy systems, seams in interorganizational business processes might never occur. In most situations, however, technical interfaces between business partners may be dificult to create and navigate.

Within the social system, interfaces between organizations involve social relationships between trading partners. The ability to work efectively across external social interfaces is limited by the degree to which cooperating partners share common interests and agree on methods for resolving diferences. Where sharp diferences exist, partners may be more likely to pursue self interests rather than the collective interest of the alliance. Many social relationships among business partners reflect tensions between self and collective interests (Young-Ybarra & Wiersema, 1999), making the social interface between them more dificult to manage.

## 2.2. Internal interfaces

In addition to the external interfaces between business partners, organizations have internal interfaces among units performing diferent organizational functions. Within the technical system, diferent types of work may require diferent types of electroni cally-mediated systems. For example, organizations may create information products and services using specialized development tools and platforms. However, they must also operate large-scale production systems that serve numerous customers simultaneously. Although electronically-mediated, communication across technical interfaces may be difficult due to diferences in the purposes of development and production platforms. Development work seeks to introduce new features into products whereas production work seeks to have those features perform reliably at high volume. Thus, products created on development platforms may not be scalable or reliable at the levels required by production platforms.

Internal social interfaces also exist within organizations. As organizations grow and dif ferentiate their structures, new social boundaries are created. Such interfaces have historically been an important consideration in organizational design (Galbraith, 1977; Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967), guiding research on the diferentiation and integration of work practices across organizational units. More recent analyses focus on the problem of knowledge transfer across internal organizational boundaries (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2002, 2004). Because intraorganizational boundaries separate the work of diferent special ists, work processes must cross between specialist units. Sharing knowledge across group boundaries requires internal interfaces that overcome knowledge boundaries.

Our aim is to develop a theoretical interpretation of the processes by which organizations incorporate global elements into their core business functions, in efect stretching the boundaries of time and space through external partnerships. In addition, we seek an understanding of the consequences of dis-embedding global elements, specifically the chal lenges associated with re-embedding global elements into a local context. For these reasons, our empirical study focuses on the social and technical interfaces that connect organizations with their external partners and on the resulting internal interfaces within the organization. Our research methods are described in the following section.

## 3. Method

We employ an interpretive case study methodology (Walsham, 1993) to discover the subjective experience of life within the organization rather than its objective characteristics.

This approach is recommended for investigating socio-technical phenomena in organization studies (Barley, 1990) and has been applied increasingly in studies of information technologies in organizations (e.g. Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Klein & Myers, 1999). Participant observation was used to gather data in order to gain experience as a member of the organization. Observations were supplemented by interviews that allowed members to express their own interpretations of work, including retrospective accounts that a participant observer could not experience.

The first author was ofered an internship at iTalk, a pre-IPO startup company in Silicon Valley hosting online telephony services. From the middle of May to the end of August, 2000, she worked on iTalk’s web development team, performing duties that included prototyping new functionality for voicemail services and maintaining and enhancing iTalk’s Internet website. The company allowed her to conduct an on-site case study, including interviews with management and other employees. Before entering the field, the researcher investigated iTalk’s website, gaining an understanding of its services, customers, partnerships, and business model.

As a participant in the organization, the first author kept a detailed diary to record observations about daily activities and her personal experiences. She also took photographs showing the working environment and interactions during company social events. Participant observation helps to overcome the obtrusiveness of the research process and ensures more natural and less self-conscious responses from the participants (Jorgensen, 1989). Participation helped the researcher to establish rapport in interviews, given that she was familiar with the organization and its people through her work with them.

The first author conducted semi-structured interviews with 50 iTalk employees across all departments and levels of the organization. Table 1 summarizes the interviewees by department and role. Questions were asked about the service design process, design tools, dificulties encountered, system maintenance, and upgrades. Other questions focused on the history of the organization, its strategy, competitors, customers, and partners. All interviews were audio-taped and transcribed.

Whenever possible, the researcher also collected company documents, including the new employee handbook, marketing standards for the company’s logo, press releases, white papers, web page printouts, meeting memos, and internally broadcast e-mail announcements. Interviews were ordered according to the date and time conducted, and references to field notes and e-mails collected on the same day aided recollection of the context and personal impressions gained during the interview.

Data analysis proceeded inductively and iteratively. The initial analysis followed an approach similar to open coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). The text of each interview was broken into segments that were labeled to describe activities occurring in the organization. In this early analysis, the concepts of time and space were reflected in codes describing when and where events occurred and their significance to the respondent. For example, the text segment ‘‘when I started, I worked probably 16 hours a day, 7 days a week... I didn’t take a day of including weekends for more than 2 months” was labeled as ‘‘early days – engineers overworked”. By the end of initial coding, 1043 text segments were classified into 137 separate coding categories.

The next iteration in analysis resembled axial coding – making connections between categories of codes (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). To assist in the process of reducing 137 coding categories to a smaller number, theoretical guidance was sought. Giddens’ (1984, 1990, 1991) writings provided conceptual materials that were judged useful in interpreting the experiences related by iTalk’s employees and those experienced by the researcher as a participant observer. Giddens’ discussions of the interconnections, conflicts, and diferentials of power along time-space edges guided the creation of coding categories relevant to interfaces between diferent social groups. For example, text segments addressing the cultural diferences between iTalk and the telephone companies were grouped together in a category labeled ‘‘interface – iTalk and telephone companies”. As a result of this stage of anal ysis, 25 coding categories were developed. The complete set of axial codes and examples of each category are listed in Appendix A.

During the third iteration of analysis, which resembled selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990), we sought to refine our ‘‘theoretical account” by examining the 25 categories further, refining their meanings, and articulating relationships among them. Here, the analysis process produced theoretical categories reflecting both technical and social systems of the organization. In this phase, we also focused our analysis on the processes of dis-embedding and re-embedding social relationships. The outcome of this final round of analysis became the story line around which the results are written.

## 4. Results

We report the results in chronological order, beginning prior to the formal launch of iTalk’s voicemail service and continuing with experiences after the launch.

## 4.1. Before the service launch

## 4.1.1. From virtual voicemail to home answering

iTalk’s founder had previous experience in the shrink-wrap computer game industry. One of the founder’s friends had started Hotmail.com, which provided free e-mail, and in June 1998 iTalk’s founder got the idea to ofer a similar free voicemail service. According to him, an advertiser-supported free voicemail system was quite a novel idea at the time.

iTalk’s initial service featured only ‘‘virtual voicemail” in which iTalk did not directly answer the subscriber’s home phone. Instead, callers wishing to leave voicemail or check their mailboxes had to dial an assigned toll-free telephone number, which connected them with iTalk’s voicemail server. Virtual voicemail was relatively easy to create because it did not require cooperation from telephone companies. However, the ease of creating virtual voicemail allowed other Internet startup companies to compete with iTalk by ofering the same service. Moreover, the use of a unique number proved to be inconvenient for subscribers and their callers, who needed to know one number for the home and another for voicemail. These factors led iTalk to explore ways to allow its service to answer customers’ home telephones. This was challenging, however, because the deployment of the service relied on the support from local telephone companies.

## 4.1.2. Negotiations with telephone companies

In order to expand throughout the US, iTalk negotiated with each of the seven regional Bell operating companies (RBOCs) to provision services for iTalk customers. Only the RBOCs could configure the call-forwarding feature of iTalk’s customers’ phone lines, and the RBOCs were notorious for their bureaucracy and slow speed. Although required by law to cooperate, the RBOCs had little incentive to work with companies like iTalk because their voicemail services would compete with the RBOCs’ own services. iTalk’s founder described these negotiations as very dificult. To improve iTalk’s ability to negotiate with the RBOCs, iTalk hired people from the telephone industry. For example, iTalk’s vice president of Operations had over 20 years of prior experience in the telephone business and his intimate knowledge of industry regulations reduced the RBOCs’ ability to dissuade iTalk with misleading arguments. He explained:

One of the RBOCs told me that they had a whole bunch of people asking for the same kind of things that we’re asking for, but we are the only one that got it. And I said ‘‘why is that?” And he said ‘‘because the rest of them we went ‘boo’ to, and they ran away. Well, you guys, when we booed, you didn’t run.” Well, I didn’t run, because it was not scary to me, because I knew they were wrong, I knew they were blufing. I knew they could not do that, I knew the laws behind it, I knew what button to push, I knew what phrases to quote, I knew what people to call.

As a result of importing such expertise, iTalk established relationships with all seven RBOCs, allowing iTalk to deliver voicemail services nationwide. iTalk members viewed this accomplishment as instrumental to their competitive advantage because it raised barriers to entry for competitors unprepared to negotiate with the RBOCs:

It took about a year and a half to do that. Well, the next [competitor] that comes after us, it will still take them about a year to do it. So we have a year head start over our competitors for providing that.

## 4.1.3. Developing the software demos

Setting up provisioning services with RBOCs was only the first step. The bigger challenge for iTalk was to design the software to implement their voicemail services through both Internet and telephone technologies. To achieve this, iTalk had to receive audio messages, digitize them, store them in databases, and retrieve them when authorized users accessed their voicemail, either through telephone or through the Internet. These steps were executed by iTalk’s proprietary software installed on its phone servers and web servers. Phone servers were equipped with dialogic boards that connected directly with telephone lines, and the phone server software controlled incoming and outgoing voicemail messages.

Compared to computer and Internet technologies, telephone switches were standardized products designed by two companies that provided all of the switches in the US. Standardized switch design allowed each telephone switch to operate with every other, thereby forming the backbone of the telephone network. Telephone standards were established decades before the computer was invented, and the computer was characterized at iTalk as an ‘‘alien” intruding into the telephone universe. Consequently, iTalk’s software engineers needed to be knowledgeable about Computer Telephony Integration (CTI), a specialized field focused on creating computer applications to control telephony systems. However, the company’s software engineers were inexperienced in CTI, having joined iTalk from the web application development and shrink-wrap computer game industries. As one of them said:

When I first started, I had no idea what I was doing, I was still learning at the same time. Even though the code may be ugly or something, we don’t have time to think, we had to get it done as soon as possible.

Initially, this lack of experience was overcome by hard work. Stories about the early days were filled with details about heroic eforts and long working days. One engineer said that he had once worked 42 h straight to bring the service alive during the July 4th week end in 1999. Another reported working 16 h a day, seven days a week for more than 2 months. Sometimes due dates for projects were ‘‘insane”, calling for completion within two or three days. Under such tremendous time pressure, design and documentation were neglected at iTalk.

During the dot com boom, startup companies like iTalk needed only to ‘‘demo” prototypes of software to attract investor funding. Software prototypes served as showcases of main features of the software and were not fully functioning products. As indicated by iTalk’s founder, demos were designed to convince investors of product potential:

The prototype is really important; it’s the diference between explaining something, and people actually seeing how it might work. So it worked to some degree; it took voicemail and it played ads, but it could only handle one user.

In January 1999, the founder succeeded in raising one million dollars in private money to develop the voicemail system. By April, iTalk had a working prototype ready to show to venture capitalists, and in May iTalk closed \$11 million in its first round of venture funding. By December 1999, product development was complete, and a free voicemail trial was introduced in the San Francisco bay area. The trial convinced venture capitalists that iTalk’s technology worked, allowing the company to raise an additional \$38 million in its second round of funding.

## 4.1.4. Organization and management

In 1998, iTalk employed 10 people who occupied a small suite of ofices in the center of Silicon Valley. As the idea behind iTalk became clearer, more software engineers joined the company to develop the product, and the ofices became crowded. Employees developed close contact and friendly relationship with each other:

At one point, we had 50 people working for iTalk in a space that is supposed to hold maybe 12. So we had people lined up on tables, we had six people in an ofice that was intended for one, we had cubicles with more than one person sitting in them. We were crammed into this little tiny space. . . I used to know everyone in the building.

Most of the engineers who joined the company were relatively young, had never worked in a large corporate environment, and generally hated the bureaucratic environments. One stated:

For me, going to work for a giant corporation would just be like a slow death. I think the bureaucracies in those places would just drive me nuts. I wouldn’t feel like I was really contributing to anything. I’d feel like I’d be coming in at 9 leaving at 5. There’s no purpose to it.

Software developers were the most highly respected, powerful, and well-paid members of iTalk. They were regarded as ‘‘magicians” who could make something out of nothing. An admiring employee remarked:

I could never do it. I think they are fabulous, I mean, they take nothing, and they make something. They really do. They produce something, they create this machine. My god, when we first started these guys were working everyday, all day, all night, I mean, they just lived there. And I think they did a tremendous job.

In the early days, job functions were not clearly defined, and management structure was flat. There were no departmental boundaries, and everyone eagerly grabbed whatever task needed to be done next. For example, the vice president of Operations described his first day on the job:

I did many things when I walked in that day, I was ordering telephone circuits, doing project management, doing equipment engineering, I was buying equipment, ordering software. . .you name it.

Despite the lack of formal structure, tasks were coordinated and communication was never a problem. There was one daily meeting that everyone attended at 11:00 o’clock, so everyone was informed about what was happening.

## 4.2. After the service launch

Three months after launching the voicemail trial in the San Francisco bay area in December 1999, iTalk’s service went nationwide. The service was free to customers, but generated limited revenues from short advertisements played whenever voicemail customers retrieved their messages. During the sign-up process, iTalk collected demographic information about their customers. Although this information was not released to advertising companies, it enabled iTalk to target advertisements to customers. The service launch was an overwhelming success. According to iTalk’s press release, over one million subscribers signed up for free voicemail within a month after its oficial launch. Media Metrix reported that iTalk had over 2 million unique visitors to its website in April 2000, second only to AT&T among telecom providers.

## 4.2.1. Organizational growth and change

iTalk’s founders realized that in order to move the company quickly toward a successful IPO, they needed to import people with prior experience in the telecommunications industry. iTalk expanded at the rate of approximately 30 new hires per month, growing from 50 to 120 people. From the founder’s perspective, integrating people with both soft ware and telephone backgrounds produced a distinct advantage:

We have a lot of people among network, software, web, Internet business, but about half of the company are from Bell Atlantic and Pacific Bell. . . I think we are integrating them well.

As iTalk grew, its structure became more diferentiated. Three main departments were formed – Marketing and Business Development, Engineering, and Operations – along with a smaller quality assurance (QA) group. Engineering and Operations were the largest departments. Engineering was responsible for designing software that implemented the voicemail features requested by Marketing and Business Development. Operations was responsible for installing software, configuring the network, monitoring and maintaining the system, and responding to customers. Members of the Engineering and Operations Departments difered dramatically in their backgrounds. The engineers were primarily software developers who joined iTalk early, whereas the majority of Operations staf were more recent hires from the telephone industry.

## 4.2.2. The provisioning process

iTalk depended upon the RBOCs to provision services for customers. In order to obtain service, an iTalk customer needed to dial from the telephone that iTalk was to answer. That number was captured automatically from the phone line, stored in the customer database, and then exported electronically to the customer’s RBOC. Assuming that customers ordered service from the correct telephone, iTalk’s orders were 100% accurate. Unfortunately, when iTalk’s orders were received by the RBOCs, they encountered provisioning processes that included manual data entry into 30-year-old legacy systems. An iTalk employee responsible for provisioning speculated on the ineficiency of this crucial step in the process:

I am sure this is what they do: they print our spreadsheet order out, then they write the order, like type it into their machine, then they hand write down all of the order numbers and then they take the handwritten stuf, and then they fill in the spread sheet, then they e-mail it back to me.

This produced provisioning error rates as high as 26% for iTalk’s customers. Provisioning errors meant that service activation was delayed, calls were often forwarded to the wrong number, and the ring cycle was set incorrectly. Many of iTalk’s customers attributed these errors to iTalk and cancelled their service. Non-subscribers who were mistak enly enrolled in iTalk’s voicemail service were enraged. An iTalk employee explained how these errors jeopardized the company’s reputation:

So non-customers would call us and said ‘‘you put the service on without my permission, I am going to call an attorney on this.” We cannot find [the number] anywhere. We only find it’s one number of; it should have been somebody else’s [phone]. The order typist did it wrong. It’s their [RBOCs’] error; we aren’t making it.

To solve this problem, a team of iTalk’s customer service representatives worked with customer service departments of the diferent RBOCs to correct the errors. As one frustrated customer service representative at iTalk described this process:

They [RBOCs] are very dificult to work with. . .because we are this tiny little fly and they’re this bull right?... I think one of the problems is these companies are so big, so it’s hard to just have a particular group help us. And when we have problems with the customers, probably 99% of the time they don’t know who we are, what we do. . . They don’t want to give us any information. They don’t want to help us.

Despite the importance of these errors and iTalk’s attempts to correct them, iTalk was powerless to change the RBOCs’ provisioning processes. As stated by the manager of the Operations Department:

It cannot be avoided because it’s in the nature of the phone company. They are a big, bureaucratic, bloated organization that was designed to allow a high level of incompetence in any given job, and still be able to function.

## 4.2.3. Service reliability challenges

Although the RBOCs’ provisioning error rate was high, the operating reliability of the phone network was much higher than the Internet. In order to provide voicemail service that leveraged both Internet and telephone technologies, iTalk had to match the reliability of the telephony world. Standards for telephone reliability were spoken of in terms of the ‘‘five 9s”, that is, 99.999% reliable. By contrast, Internet platforms at that time were less reliable, frequently causing service interruptions. iTalk’s founder explained:

It has been very challenging for us to build our phone servers in the ways that are teleco level’s of reliability, not just web level of reliability. If power goes down, our phone servers still have to work. Anytime someone presses ‘‘1” to play a message, no matter how busy the Internet backbone is, it always starts to play the message.

Unfortunately, it was not until after iTalk had acquired over one million customers that the intrinsic diference between telephony and Internet reliability was understood. By then it was too late to redesign iTalk’s phone server software to match the quality standard required to deliver voicemail service reliably. Moreover, each RBOC had slightly diferent technical requirements, making it more dificult for iTalk to establish the connections needed to deploy iTalk’s voicemail service nationwide. When problems arose at the interface between telephony and Internet technologies, no one expected the telephone companies to modify their software to accommodate the requirements of iTalk’s phone servers. As the manager of Operations sarcastically commented:

We have situations when all of a sudden our dialogic board that is on the phone servers will knock everybody of the line. Everybody in the Engineering Department is insisting it’s got to be a problem with our telephone company. They have to figure out how to handle it, cause telephone companies are never going to change their switch software to go, ‘Oh, nice little iTalk voicemail machines, we don’t want to create a condition that causes you to faint’.

## 4.2.4. Software development process within iTalk

With growth, iTalk’s software development process expanded to include three versions of the voicemail application: a development system owned by the Engineering Department, a testing system owned by QA, and a production system owned by the Operations Department. In theory, these three versions were linked sequentially. A new version of the application was developed first by engineers, then tested and debugged by quality assurance, and then released as the production version after it proved to be stable. In practice, iTalk’s production version crashed frequently under increasing customer loads. As a result, Operations was bombarded with customer complaints and tried to repair the production version immediately. This was dificult because the production platform ran all the time, and error conditions could not be reproduced easily.

A support technician in Operations viewed the cause of service interruptions as a software design problem:

So when it comes to whether our outages are due to hardware or software, 95% of them are self-inflicted software problems. Self-inflicted meaning, our engineers gave us code, and the code doesn’t work.

This attribution was substantiated by the developers themselves. Given that many of the core engineers moved to iTalk from the computer game industry, they were not used to the higher reliability requirements of voicemail services. As explained by one developer:

When you worked on video games, there is a kind of accepted level of instability. It’s just a game. If it crashes every once in a while, it’s not a huge deal. . . So most our people are used to making things that didn’t have to run as reliably as this has to run. Some of us joke about if a voicemail was an airline passenger, how many people do we kill today? Because we do have reliability and stability problems. As a phone company type of reliable service, we are supposed to be a lot better than that.

As more customers adopted iTalk’s service, the weaknesses of iTalk’s demo-driven approach to software design became more apparent. As stated by a core developer in the Engineering Department:

You can do a demo of a feature in a fraction of the time, like one ten-thousandth of the time it takes to do the real thing. It’s like making a glider compared to making a jetliner. It flies, sure, and if you painted it right, it looks like a real thing, right? But the reality is, it doesn’t do anything. It doesn’t carry passengers, you cannot fuel it, it crashes, it cannot land. . . .And that causes a lot of scheduling problems because people don’t believe that things will take as long as they really do.

Although iTalk’s software had worked under lower load conditions, service reliability issues surfaced when higher load thresholds were passed. The QA group was not equipped to simulate the load conditions that a phone server would typically encounter in production:

Our code doesn’t have a lot of mileage and experiences done on it. Also, as we’re doing this, our customer base keeps growing. So our load keeps going up. Every time our load builds up a little, we uncover some problems that we never knew about, so we end up fixing that.

An even more fundamental issue was the pressure on QA to test the frequent updates and bug fixes coming from Engineering. Almost daily, the engineers would send patches and enhancements to QA for testing. As a QA specialist explained:

They were often told by the VP of engineering ‘‘you have an hour to test this build.” So you want to know why there is no relevance at the QA group here? Because you cannot test anything in an hour, in a day, in a week, thoroughly, accurately, precisely, and correctly. With that said, we now know why QA is a joke – time constraints.

Complicating matters further, the production platform also involved third-party software, such as operating systems and firewall monitoring tools. As a result, error conditions could originate from unusual combinations of software layers from multiple sources. Tracking the origin of failures in a networked environment, while operating at full scale, placed intense pressure on iTalk’s Operations Department. As one key employee in the department commented:

You are literally babysitting all the computers. You have problems every hour; you are rebooting a server every hour. It’s just like a bounce cycle that never stops. It’s like there are so many people at war with it that they constantly need to be up and down with the machine, their lives are tied with the machine. So it’s not 9 to 5, it’s not 8 to 10, it’s 7 by 24, 365 days a year.

## 4.2.5. Interdepartmental diferences

Following the service launch, diferences between the Engineering and Operations Departments became more evident. Most of the software engineers had joined iTalk before the funding streams materialized, and they saw themselves as more committed than employees who joined up later. As one founding employee remarked:

In the beginning, it’s risk. There is nothing. Money wasn’t an issue, it’s just the idea. So people we hired at that point were people who had a really strong belief in iTalk. But I think it’s very diferent now. We are moving to like more of a corporation. You get a diferent type of person coming in.

Although most engineers disliked bureaucratic environments, the majority of Operations Department employees with telephony backgrounds referred to large corporations as well structured – ‘‘mature” environments with ‘‘sophisticated procedures” – and they thought that their experience in large corporations could contribute to iTalk’s success. The two groups also had diferent views on internal procedures and documentation. On the one hand, most of the software engineers felt that documentation was an unnecessary impediment to creative work and irrelevant in an environment where requirements changed so rapidly. On the other hand, employees from large corporate backgrounds viewed lack of documentation as a serious problem that could waste resources. This view was expressed by an employee who had spent most of his career in telephone companies prior to joining iTalk:

I would never work for a startup again. It’s insane. It’s a waste of manpower and resources. . . .Either nothing is documented, or, if something is documented, it is not constantly updated. . . .When you document, when you work with other departments and groups, you are passing your knowledge and wisdom of to a large group of peo ple. By doing that, business wise, you are not relying on one person.

The diference between Engineering and Operations also reflected a more fundamental diference between the product orientation of the shrink-wrap software industry and the service orientation of the telephone industry. Operations employees thought that their contributions were overlooked by iTalk’s founding engineers and that they deserved more respect. One of them frankly remarked:

The only people in this company that really know the true flavor of the product is Operations. Engineers and everybody else, they don’t deal with it on the day to day basis. They are not bombarded with complaints from end users . . .I think 99% of the company is based on Operations just to make it work. I don’t think anybody is working as hard as Operations, to be honest with you.

## 4.2.6. Hierarchy

As iTalk grew, the company also became more hierarchically structured. New managers were hired from outside to oversee iTalk’s departments, in part to showcase a more professional management team to the investment community. A longer term employee questioned the authority and credibility of these new managers:

For people who have been around a long time, I think there is a feeling that we have more contribution, that we know the intricacy of the system better than everyone, especially since a lot is not written down. So if someone is relatively new, and maybe the manager makes a decision without talking to us, there is this feeling like ‘‘well, they don’t really know what they are talking about, how can they make this decision?” . . .It hurts morale by bringing somebody who has no experience in being your manager.

The engineers especially had problems with their new vice president of Engineering, who had 20 years of management experience in the telecommunication industry.

After the company grew to 170 people, it was more dificult to hold a single weekly meeting that everyone attended. More meetings were held that just included managers, prompting employee complaints that information was not being disseminated to the rest of the company:

Only managers get to take meetings. They are the people who are informed. But people who are actually working on something, they don’t have the information they need. There’s no feedback from those meetings to tell us what this meeting has accomplished.

## 4.2.7. The shifting business model

Over time, iTalk shifted its business model from deriving revenue through advertising to premium services that were billed to customers. As the strategic focus changed, many of the resources devoted to software development for the former business model appeared to have been wasted. An engineer’s comments reflected strong emotional attachments to the software he had developed earlier:

I looked at the code I wrote last summer, and they are not used right now, like the whole idea of what we were doing has been thrown out. This one piece of code that I wrote, they just decided to use a diferent technology. So look back. About a period of like 3 months is kind of wasted.

Simultaneously, engineers were expected to deliver rapidly new product features that could be marketed as premium services that customers would pay for. This pressure aggravated the tensions among Development, Operations and QA. One employee from Operations Department commented:

Marketing is going ‘‘Oh, get more features, get more features,” but then Operations are going ‘‘no, no, no, we are not that stable.” . . .From beginning till now, we have not really tried to stabilize the system; it is always the new features. Features, features, features. . . I don’t think that is the appropriate way to do it. Cause eventually, the more features you add on an unstabilized product, these will come back and bite you.

With less communication from management, employees became unsure about the mission of the company and interpreted the changes as lack of leadership from management:

There’s a lot of wishy-washiness that goes on. One week we’re this, next week we’ll be that. . . Without vision there’s just no direction. . . I mean we’ve got to say this is what it is and stick to it instead of just changing all the time.

In summary, despite its rapid growth and initial competitive advantage, iTalk experienced the limits of a business model based on advertising revenues. Although the company experimented with a new business model that drew revenues from subscriptions for premium services, it experienced internal conflicts exacerbated by the pressures to create premium services rapidly. In 2001, iTalk was acquired by a larger media company for \$23 million less liabilities, which yielded a net price of about \$15 million. According to anonymous comments made on an Internet weblog in 2001, iTalk burned through \$77 million in cash while only generating revenue of \$120,000.

## 5. Discussion

This study is motivated by an interest in the external and internal interfaces created in organizations that rely on electronically-mediated business processes. From a theoretical perspective, such organizations experience time-space distanciation, involving the disembedding of elements from global contexts and their re-embedding into local contexts. The study investigates the case of iTalk, a company that partnered with telephone companies to deliver a home-answering Internet voicemail service. Early in its brief history, before the service was launched, iTalk established service contracts with telephone companies and developed the software necessary to link computer and telephony networks together. These eforts enabled iTalk to grow more rapidly than its competitors and to contend for a leading position in the telecommunications industry. Although iTalk was successful initially, the company encountered problems following the nationwide launch of its service, leading quickly to its demise. Our purpose is to generate theoretical insights into the processes of dis-embedding and re-embedding elements in an organization’s social and technical systems.

Our analysis draws from Giddens’ (1984, 1990, 1991) concepts of social structuration and modernity, specifically the notion of time-space distanciation and its associated processes of dis-embedding and re-embedding. We treat dis-embedding and re-embedding as successive stages of development at iTalk, separated in time by the nationwide launch of its homeanswering voicemail service. Before the launch, iTalk had positioned itself to dis-embed social and technical elements from the telephone industry; after the launch, the challenges of re-embedding alien elements into the local context were never satisfactorily addressed. Our analysis focuses on iTalk’s external and internal interfaces within each stage.

## 5.1. Dis-embedding stage

Giddens’ analyses of modernity draw attention to the capability of social systems to stretch across time and space, in contrast to traditional social systems that are temporally and spatially confined to their local contexts. In the modern era, an organization’s ability to exercise time-space distanciation may bring competitive advantages. Thus, iTalk’s decision to pursue the home-answering feature of their voicemail service distinguished the company from both established telephone companies, which did not provide voicemail on the Internet, and Internet voicemail companies, which did not provide home answering. Time-space distanciation, therefore, creates new capabilities as global elements are combined with local elements. Indeed, many of the proclaimed advantages of so-called virtual organizations can be traced to their flexibility in combining components that are distributed in time and space (Lucas, 1996; Mowshowitz, 2002). Thus, the enterprise is extended by incorporating elements of global contexts, and both temporal and spatial contexts of dis-embedding become important.

Giddens’ observations about temporal context draw attention to the historical characteristics of both social and technical systems. The historical diferences between iTalk and the RBOCs are significant in both social systems and technical systems. The telephone industry’s 100-year history dwarfs the brief heyday of Internet startups. RBOCs were large, bureaucratic and legally protected while iTalk was small, informal, and vulnerable. Telephone networks and computer networks were also developed at diferent times and for diferent purposes. Telephone companies built a reliable service using standardized switching networks whereas development and commercialization of the Internet is a relatively recent phenomenon.

Giddens’ analysis of space draws attention to the more obvious geographical and cultural separation between global and local contexts. Geographically, iTalk was a small firm located in the midst of Silicon Valley, surrounded by scores of startup companies whose success depended on Internet technology. By contrast, the telephone companies were distributed throughout the US, serving local communities from regional headquarters in large cities. The enormity of such diferences suggests that the dis-embedding of remote, global elements could be problematic.

Although Giddens restricts his analysis to social systems, we extend our analysis to include both social and technical systems. In the following analysis, we examine the social and technical interfaces associated with dis-embedding. The discussion is summarized in the left-hand column of Table 2.

## 5.1.1. Social interfaces

iTalk’s success in delivering voicemail services that answered customers’ home telephones depended on establishing service agreements with the seven RBOCs. iTalk bridged this external social interface through human resource policies that brought new employees to iTalk from the telephone companies. This human resource strategy was efective because inserting teleco experience allowed iTalk to negotiate more efectively with the more powerful partners upon which it depended. Hiring former teleco employees reduced the RBOCs’ capacity to bluf iTalk with arguments about industry regulation, technology requirements and other issues. In efect, iTalk reduced the power diferential along its external interface by importing elements of the teleco social system. This was instrumental to iTalk’s ability to dis-embed its operations from a specific time and location and to reach across the entire US with home answering voicemail service.

The literature on resource dependency (Pfefer & Salancik, 1978) supports the notion that external alliances need to incorporate elements from the environment to reduce critical dependencies. Indeed, there is little diference between Giddens’ idea of power diferentials along time-space edges and resource dependence. Hiring experienced professionals from the firms on which one depends is a common strategy for dealing with resource dependence (de Rond & Bouchikhi, 2004). For example, Barley’s (1986) study of hospital radiology technology reveals that hospitals without technical expertise hire experts specialized in the new technology to manage it. From the perspective of time-space distanciation, importing human resources forges a bridge across time and space, bringing knowledge from distant contexts into the local.

Throughout the dis-embedding stage, iTalk remained relatively small and undiferentiated structurally. Employees were Silicon Valley software engineers and entrepreneurs experienced in pre-packaged, shrink-wrap software development. They valued small informal working relationships. Although the new hires from the telecommunications industry had diferent backgrounds, there were no clearly defined departmental boundaries to separate them from existing employees. This meant that there were no significant internal social interfaces within iTalk during the dis-embedding stage.

## 5.1.2. Technical interfaces

Much of the literature on new organizational forms recognizes the importance of using information technology to bridge the expanses of time and space to link global with local elements. The seamless integration of supply chains, for example, is viewed as a means to achieve global optimization of inventory levels across an extended enterprise (Gries & Kasarda, 1997). Clearly, the RBOCs and iTalk employed vastly diferent technologies in their fundamental services. To bridge the technical interface between iTalk and its partners, iTalk’s engineers designed innovative software that allowed iTalk’s computers to ‘‘talk” to telephone switches. Thus, the challenge of dis-embedding standardized technologies, which were designed decades earlier, and integrating them with modern Internet technologies appeared to have been met. The bridge across the technical interface was regarded as ‘‘magical” within iTalk, reinforcing the status of iTalk’s founding software engineers.

Prior to the service launch, iTalk’s internal technical systems were not diferentiated or assigned diferent roles in the service delivery process. Indeed, the software designed to interface with telephone switches was rudimentary in design and proven to operate only in demonstrations to potential investors.

In summary, iTalk’s dis-embedding stage extended bridges across the external social and technical interfaces between the local context of the company and the more global context of its most significant business partners. Social bridges were forged with human resource policies aimed at importing former teleco employees; technical bridges were forged with software designed by iTalk’s engineers. Thus, the distanciation of the enterprise across time and space positioned iTalk to ofer an innovative new service with economic potential and to establish a competitive head start over other companies.

## 5.2. Re-embedding stage

Giddens (1990) describes re-embedding as the dificult requirement to integrate displaced elements as functioning components in the local social context. In organizations, this requirement is often not apparent until operating under conditions of growth and changing environmental conditions. Although iTalk appeared to be successful during the dis-embedding stage, it encountered serious problems when the dis-embedded elements were required to work together to deliver reliable performance at rapidly increasing service loads. Our analysis traces iTalk’s problems to weaknesses in external interfaces that only became apparent following the service launch. Ironically, strengthening the external connections during dis-embedding led to the creation of new internal interfaces within iTalk during re-embedding. The corps of new hires from the teleco social system was established as iTalk’s Operations Department, which came into conflict with the Engineering Department. In addition, iTalk’s internal process for new product development experienced increasing dificulties due in part to flaws in design that were overlooked in demo versions of the software. This internal technical interface was dificult to bridge, especially under the pressure to produce new products rapidly to serve changing market demand. These social and technical interfaces are discussed below and summarized in the right-hand col umn of Table 2.

## 5.2.1. Social interfaces

Despite iTalk’s eforts to bridge the external interface with the RBOCs, its eforts proved to be incomplete. Although importing teleco employees strengthened iTalk’s abil ity to negotiate service agreements with the RBOCs, critical diferences in the business processes through which customers ordered service were not addressed. iTalk’s process for enrolling new customers was automated, capturing the customer’s telephone number in a field of the database when the customer called. By contrast, the RBOCs relied upon older provisioning processes involving manual data entry. Although it might have been possible to reengineer the provisioning process to reduce provisioning errors (reported to reach 26%), iTalk was powerless to efect such a change. Instead, iTalk dedicated a team of customer service representatives to work with the RBOCs to correct provisioning errors after they were detected. Thus, the external social interface required ongoing maintenance, consuming unanticipated resources and reducing the efectiveness of iTalk’s service to its customers.

This experience illustrates the critical nature of external social interfaces in organizations. There is little question that technologies are available to support seamless provisioning processes between two or more organizations. However, iTalk’s provisioning problems are more accurately attributed to diferences between social systems – the organizational decision processes that determine how interorganizational processes are constructed to bridge external interfaces. Clearly, interfaces across social systems involve power asymmetries, which Giddens identifies as a critical consideration. The power diferentials along external interfaces clearly favored the older firms and their more antiquated technologies. iTalk’s dependence on the RBOCs placed it in a weaker power position. Although legally required to cooperate with firms like iTalk, the RBOCs were not obligated to change their business processes to accommodate such newcomers to the industry. Inaccurate provisioning might be absorbed by the more powerful partners while proving fatal to a startup. As the literature on strategic alliances suggests, inter-firm relationships are beset with tensions between self interests and collective interests (de Rond & Bouchikhi, 2004; Gallivan & Depledge, 2003; Young-Ybarra & Wiersema, 1999). Although successful alliances depend on trust, it is unlikely that firms ever suspend self interest. Thus, the RBOCs were willing to cooperate with a potential competitor in voicemail services, but their commitment of resources to the partnership was limited.

The investment funding that iTalk secured through its successful software demos enabled the company to grow in size and diferentiate its structure. The Operations Department was formed, stafed with former teleco employees, and the founding software engineers organized themselves into the Engineering Department. iTalk’s new structure efectively created internal social interfaces between departments that were diferentiated in terms of task and the work experiences of department members. Structural diferentiation is a primary source of interdepartmental conflict, especially when work processes cut across functions as they did in iTalk (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Walton, Dutton, & Caferty, 1969). During the re-embedding stage, iTalk’s engineers worked increasingly with colleagues who had gained their experience in large, bureaucratic companies. As these formerly distant groups interacted, conflicts arose over issues such as the relative importance of documentation and testing. The social interfaces that had developed inside of iTalk thus became a new source of dificulties, characteristic of the problems of re-embedding elements displaced from one social system into another. This finding is consistent with Barley’s (1986) interpretation of the importance of expert knowledge in the power relations between radiologists and technicians, which afected the renegotiation of their institutionalized social roles.

More contemporary analyses shed light on the dificulties of knowledge transfer across internal organizational boundaries (Bechky, 2003; Carlile, 2002; Orlikowski, 2002). The knowledge needed to excel as a software engineer difers from the knowledge needed to deliver reliable services. Both types of knowledge are generated locally, embedded in practices, and vested in occupational and professional communities. Knowledge transfer across internal interfaces is only partially aided by technical systems, which are restricted to crossing syntactic knowledge boundaries (Carlile, 2002). Social systems involve semantic and pragmatic boundaries, which involve gaps in interpretation and diferences in work practices, respectively (Carlile, 2002). Our analysis suggests that such knowledge diferences originate in diferent periods of history, and that telephony experience predated software engineering knowledge by several decades. Thus, diferences in Engineering and Operations employees’ assessments of what was most important to iTalk’s success were especially deep.

It is also possible to regard iTalk’s internal conflicts as more deeply political. By with holding critical knowledge, iTalk’s founding software engineers protected their political position and resisted attempts by newly hired employees to influence the policies and organization of the company. Given the challenges of integrating telephone networks with Internet technologies, one might have expected a more aggressive hiring of software developers who were more experienced in CTI. The lack of program documentation also made the founding software developers indispensable because knowledge of the technical system was embedded in their heads and not shared. This made the software engineers the most powerful group within the company.

## 5.2.2. Technical interfaces

Like iTalk’s external social bridges, the technical bridges proved to be inadequate. In iTalk’s rush to secure venture capital to fuel further growth, the engineers focused on developing software demos that would impress investors. Although this strategy was successful in the short term, helping iTalk to raise \$53 million, the demo versions contained design flaws that became exposed under live operating conditions. Although such flaws were common in software developed for Internet applications and computer games, they posed fundamental problems in the external technical interface with telephone networks. Telephone networks operated at a reliability standard as high as 99.999%. Because the majority of iTalk’s customers accessed their voicemails from their telephones, they expected to receive the same reliable service that the telephone companies provided. As the number of customers increased to 1 million, iTalk’s servers crashed regularly, forcing suspensions in service.

The external technical interface was directly afected by weaknesses in the internal interfaces of iTalk’s new product development process. The development process was divided into three subsystems associated with diferent groups: development (associated with Engineering), testing (associated with quality assurance), and production (associated with Operations). The design flaws in the software caused ripple efects in all three subsystems. The new features were implemented on top of flawed designs in the development system; software errors could not be replicated or repaired in testing; and the production system was unstable. Indeed, testing typically followed rather than preceded production failures, practically ensuring that software problems could not be prevented.

Time-space analysis is instructive insofar as it traces the design flaws to the software engineers’ lack of CTI knowledge, which was not required in their previous endeavors to write packaged software for the computer game market. Ironically, the flawed demo software was instrumental in bridging iTalk’s social interface with the investment community, which was suficiently impressed by the potential value of iTalk’s proprietary software. However, flawed software was poorly matched with the demands placed upon it in iTalk’s rapidly expanding production environment in the re-embedding stage.

In summary, iTalk’s re-embedding stage required the company to integrate displaced elements, drawn from diferent times and spaces, into a local context. Under live operating conditions, weaknesses in the social and technical bridges across external interfaces became exposed. These external interfaces afected the corresponding internal interfaces. iTalk became more diferentiated structurally, and its process for developing and implementing new products became more elaborate. Unfortunately, diferentiation led to political strife, and elaborate processes failed to generate a reliable service. Thus, re-embedding provides a serious test for organizations depending on new elements dis-embedded from other times and spaces.

## 6. Conclusion

Although articulated at an abstract theoretical level, Giddens’ concept of time-space distanciation carries practical implications similar to older concepts of diferentiation and integration within organizations (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967) and the interdependencies between organizations (Pfefer & Salancik, 1978). In particular, organizations should be designed so that internal relationships are integrated and external dependencies are managed. The primary value added by Giddens’ analysis is the attention drawn to the temporal and spatial characteristics of organizations. Temporal and spatial characteristics are particularly relevant to the study of organizations that dis-embed elements from disparate social systems and re-embed them into local contexts. Our analysis illustrates the challenges facing organizations during the dis-embedding, and particularly, re-embedding stages of implementing their business strategies.

How might organizations be designed to manage their interfaces more efectively? Focusing on external interfaces, we can suggest limiting the number of asymmetrical relationships with business partners. iTalk’s management might be questioned for their desire to launch their voicemail service nationwide before it had proven reliable at a regional level. Jumping from a local, urban area trial to a national service did not allow the technical interfaces to mature into reliable networks. Defining a narrower target market by serving clients with specific needs, rather than everyone with a telephone, might also have helped to reduce the scale of operations and the associated risks. iTalk might also have searched for a partner that was more willing to change its provisioning processes to match iTalk’s needs. The fundamental lesson is that establishing relationships during dis-embedding needs to anticipate the challenges of re-embedding distant elements into the local operating context.

A second insight derived from time-space analysis pertains to the internal interfaces caused by dis-embedding. It seems ironic that iTalk’s initial success in bridging external interfaces replicated those interfaces internally. Importing alien elements into new departments placed great pressure on the relationships between departments. As innovative as iTalk was, it showed little innovation in internal organizational design. Interdepartmental conflicts might have been eliminated with more innovative approaches to designing the processes for new product innovation. For example, adequate software testing could have preceded the implementation of software products into production.

Other implications for organizational design can be drawn from a focus on time-space distanciation by considering alternative re-embedding mechanisms. Although software documentation may seem unglamorous, documented specifications serve as knowledge repositories for mission-critical production systems. It should be self-evident that docu mentation is necessary for all but the most temporary of technical infrastructures. Documentation facilitates knowledge transfer across semantic and pragmatic boundaries between departments, thus serving as a re-embedding mechanism.

Four aspects of our analysis limit the strength of our conclusions. First, our analysis focuses somewhat narrowly on the interface between iTalk and telephone companies. To a lesser degree, we have also discussed relationships with the venture capital community. We have not, however, described iTalk’s partnerships with the Internet service pro viders who operated the portals that connected iTalk with millions of customers. Because the degree of time-space distanciation needed to partner with other Internet companies was less than those involving the telephone companies, these interfaces were easier to bridge than those with the RBOCs. Unfortunately, length restrictions prohibit a compar ative analysis of iTalk’s external interfaces.

A second limitation is minimizing attention to the strategic shifts that afected the dis embedding and re-embedding processes over iTalk’s brief existence. iTalk pursued three dif ferent business models in sequence: providing free virtual voicemail, providing free home answering voicemail, and providing home answering voicemail to paid subscribers. Indeed, the third strategy, which was adopted to compensate for declining revenues from advertisers, precipitated the need to develop premium services that customers would actually pay for. Shifts in business strategy may trigger new issues related to dis-embedding and re-embedding and reshape the contours of external and internal interfaces. Each strategic shift, in turn, introduces new challenges involving new partners. A more complete analysis of these strategic shifts and their consequences would enrich our understanding of organizational interfaces.

Third, our analysis is circumscribed by the choice of Giddens’ concept of time-space dis tanciation to analyze the case. Although this choice brings powerful insights to bear on the social and technical interfaces of iTalk and firms like it, it potentially obscures insights available from other theories. For example, actor-network theorists might attribute iTalk’s failure to its inability to enroll significant actors such as the RBOCs to the same degree that it enrolled venture capitalists. Because the actor-network embracing iTalk and its partners involved disparate human and non-human actors, interests could not be translated easily and the actor-network never stabilized. As is common in most interpretive research, interpretations vary when diferent theoretical lenses are used. Thus, time-space distanciation should not be regarded as the only lens worthy of application to the case.

Finally, iTalk’s lessons are derived from a post-mortem analysis that speculates on the reasons for its demise. Although we believe that post-mortems provide valuable insights into the reasons for failure, it is potentially more constructive to analyze successful organizations. Learning from failure ofers insights into business models and organizationa practices that are not viable, but learning from success hold greater potential for revealing practices that might succeed.

In conclusion, our research applied Giddens’ concept of time-space distanciation to understand the interfaces crucial to the functioning of organizations that rely upon electronically-mediated business processes. Our research extends Giddens’ theory from social systems to technical systems, which must also be designed to enable dis-embedding and reembedding. Thus, the array of design issues facing managers of organizations is widened by an analysis incorporating temporal and spatial issues. The insights from Giddens’ analyses of time-space distanciation provide theoretical order and practical guidance for managing these issues.
