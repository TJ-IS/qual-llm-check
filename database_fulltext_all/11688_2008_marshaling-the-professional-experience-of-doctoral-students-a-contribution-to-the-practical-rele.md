## Abstract

In this paper, we propose a partial solution to the problem of the relevance of information systems research by adjusting doctoral programs to the specific needs and talents of doctoral students that have significant prior professional life experience. The purpose of this paper is first to recognize that the “professionally qualified doctoral student” (PQDS) has a different type of knowledge that may give her/him some advantages over other students, including greater symbolic capital. We examine the epistemic evidence for the claim that part of their practical experience constitutes a specific type of “applicative” knowledge that should be considered as different from but of equal value to theory, which has been the mainstay of academic education. Three independent lines of academic research contribute such evidence: the communities of practice literature, philosophical perspectives on applicative knowledge, and cognitive sciences. We argue that PQDSs may benefit from doctoral programs with specific features designed to leverage their practical knowledge. In turn, they may be able to “boundary span” and publish research results in forms that are appreciated by their professional communities. Finally we discuss some practical institutional issues that could be addressed if we are to sustain this profile of researchers.

Keywords: Professionally Qualified Doctoral Students (PQDS), applicative knowledge, technical knowledge, communities of practice, qualitative research, practice, relevance, symbolic capital

## Introduction

One of the major challenges facing the field of MIS today is to become more practically relevant so that it can better serve its business and public sector stakeholders and in turn can garner better political support (Khazanchi and Munkvold 2001). The failure of IS research to redeem its claims to practical relevance continues to undermine the legitimacy of IS research and has become known as the relevancy problem (Benbasat and Zmud 1999). During her address to the 1997 IFIP 8.2 conference in Philadelphia, Lynne Markus argued that one of the directions the field needs to take in the future is “the appreciation of practicality in IS research” (Markus 1997, p. 18). The intent of what she termed practical research is not to replace or overshadow research that builds or tests academic theory, but rather to complement theoretical research with “rigorous research that describes and evaluates what is going on in practice” (Markus 1997, p. 18). The purpose of this paper is to suggest some reasons why it is difficult to meet the call for obtaining a better understanding of “what is going on in practice” through IS research and what can be done to overcome the associated challenges of doing so.

This is not to say that we disagree with Straub and Ang's (2008) view, as expressed in the editorial in this issue, that the relevancy problem is predominantly an issue of how communication between the groups occurs. As Straub and Ang rightly put it, the assumption that writing, especially scholarly journal articles, is the only way we communicate with practitioners is both a bone of contention and also a “red herring.” Certainly, academic papers are not the only way academics and practitioners communicate. Moreover, most practitioners may not read MIS Quarterly or perhaps even MISQ Executive. And we agree that MISQ’s primary stakeholders are first and foremost researchers. Nevertheless, the relevancy problem is real and, in addition to being a communication problem is also a knowledge production problem (Van de Ven and Johnson 2006). That is, it is a function of both how we communicate and what we communicate about (see also Straub and Ang’s discussion of what they refer to as the first alleged gap). In this article, we address both problems by suggesting how our educational system should strive wherever possible to leverage the knowledge of practitioners to address these issues.

One of the obvious causes for the intractability of the relevancy problem is that IS researchers and professionals have evolved different communities of practice (CoP) with divergent mind sets, priorities, values, and criteria for what each considers relevant knowledge. From this perspective, we need to rethink the way we attract students, the content of their doctoral training, and potential institutional changes in both academic and professional communities of practice that could bring about the interactions necessary to achieve cross-fertilization between these two communities. We contend that the usual exhortations that practitioners and academics should attend each others' conferences and study each others' work are meaningless without fundamental changes in the education of future members of these two communities. In this paper, we propose a partial solution to this problem, one that is within the power of the IS research community to initiate.

The basic idea is to develop unique doctoral programs that are able to leverage the professional knowledge that student applicants have acquired prior to embarking on their doctoral education. We need these students more than they need us. They provide a powerful means for producing research that is relevant to practitioners. Marshaling this professional knowledge moves us one step further in the direction recommended by Benbasat and Zmud (1999, p. 8): “The best strategy for any IS researcher is to develop personal relationships with individual practitioners, both as a source of ideas for future projects and as a source of feedback on current projects.” If some doctoral programs were specifically designed to build on this intellectual capital, more doctorally qualified faculty would produce practically relevant research in the eyes of “expert” practitioners.

In many doctoral programs, doctoral student do enter with significant professional experience and contacts. However, in our combined experience with doctoral programs spanning more than 40 years, we observe that this potential symbolic (Bourdieu 1984) and intellectual capital (see Appendix A) is not effectively harnessed. Our field should not be wasting such valuable resources. In most cases, students' contacts and their practitioner perspectives are lost during their years of doctoral education and dissertation research. Therefore, we propose that we educate qualified boundary-spanning IS faculty by embarking on a two-pronged strategy. For the first prong of this strategy, we recommend that doctoral programs that are already receiving applications from professionally qualified students (as described below) adjust their program contents and student supervision to take advantage of their practical knowledge, while at the same time maintaining academic standards. After having gained some experience with such adaptive doctoral programs, the field as a whole should seek and admit more professionally experienced doctoral students into doctoral education adapted to capitalize on such candidates' prior experiences in external communities of practice. The second prong of this strategy is to embed methodological training in doctoral education to leverage practical professional knowledge and to recognize ways that professionally qualified doctoral students may be able to "boundary span" and publish research results in forms that are appreciated by the professional community.

Clearly these strategies cannot overcome the communication gap entirely, but they have the advantage of requiring few additional financial resources, necessitating primarily intellectual adjustments. An important first adjustment is to reconsider how we prepare students in qualitative, action research, and design science research methods. A second adjustment requires educating a new type of IS faculty, who are not only qualified to produce rigorous qualitative research, $^{3}$ but who may enable boundary spanning in order to bridge the knowledge production gap.

In light of these considerations, this paper seeks to characterize the Professionally Qualified Doctoral Student (PQDS) and explain why such students may have certain advantages over other students, particularly as they undertake qualitative, action research, or design science that leverages their working knowledge. The PQDS profile is described below. Please note that we are not privileging use of qualitative/design science methods over quantitative ones. Certainly the phenomenon should drive the method, along with the natural proclivities of the student. However, the field would benefit greatly if it harnessed the practical wisdom and modus operandi knowledge that these students have acquired before coming to their Ph.D. programs. Secondly, we distinguish such programs from the Executive Doctorate. $^{4}$ While such degrees clearly improve industry–academy interaction, we believe that the strategy we suggest is likely to convey greater credibility to the academic community.

In order to clarify the theoretical value of the PDQS profile and motivate a solution, we ground the justification of this profile in the recent communities of practice (CoP) discourse. This background is necessary to understand that PQDSs do not just have more business or IT knowledge in general, but also applicative knowledge that is tacit and embedded in practice. Moreover, their experience in these CoP helps them develop effective intuition and implicit learning processes. Following this, we discuss the second prong of this strategy: the notion that changes need to be made regarding methods education. We conclude our main thesis and refer the readers to an appendix outlining changes that our proposal implies for PQDSs, their host institutions, and the IS research community, in particular for the publication process, external relations, tenure and promotion, as has been suggested by Lyytinen (1999).

## The Profile of a PQDS (Professionally Qualified Doctoral Student)

Both educational and professional experience indicators are necessary to identify an acceptable level of professional expertise in a PQDS because the knowledge of a PQDS cannot be entirely observed or measured, and much of it is undocumented or tacit. Below we compare a PQDS to a non-PQDS and provide some examples of PQDS profiles (see Table 1). We then outline the domains of knowledge about which these students should have gained significant insights through their professional experience. Clearly substantial differences in PQDS profiles exist between different countries, but we focus on the situations in France and the United States.

## Description and Illustration of PQDS Profiles

Many students qualify for a Ph.D. program by performing well in their undergraduate and master' degree programs. Relevant work experience is an additional plus for admission (and may be a requirement in some programs), but typically work experience is not reflected in the Ph.D. program itself.

For a PQDS to be accepted as a doctoral student, he or she must have had a first real world work experience for at least two to three years $^{5}$ . Typically, such career paths start with an internship or a trainee program. Second, they must have advanced to a supervisory or other higher work role, and established a demonstrable record of accomplishments in their profession, which may add another two to three years before being considered for a Ph.D. program. They should have achieved a degree of intellectual and emotional maturity borne of advanced work experience wherein they have learned to interpret situations and broker solutions. ## Domains and Sources of Knowledge for PQDS

Obviously all students should acquire a thorough grasp of both research methods and theory through their doctoral studies. The faculty members of a Ph.D. program may presume that PQDSs, by virtue of their prior careers, enter their doctoral programs with a good background in the following three practical domains: $^{7}$

(A) Business knowledge and know how

(B) IT/IS knowledge and know how

(C) Social networking and communication skills $^{8}$

Type A results from participating in complex business practices such as contract negotiation, project management, promotion procedures, product development, etc. This builds the candidate's knowledge of social-cultural contexts in which professional work practices are situated and a tolerance for ambiguities, risks, and uncertainties. Type B is similar except that it is the result of technical work such as data modeling, requirements specification, technical documentation, systems maintenance, computer systems purchasing, RFP, etc. Type C is built primarily through company-paid on-the-job training for development of communication and leadership capabilities.

Students with several years of professional experience have been socialized into one or more communities of practice, which enables them to interact easily with seasoned practitioners. This allows them to grasp what practitioners really mean and translate this into academic terms, and they remember where their conversation partners “are coming from.” If properly trained in the Ph.D. program, they should be less likely to replace their professional background with an academic mind set, instead seeing it as a complement to their work experience. This experience is important to their future as researchers, since it can substantially improve their pre-understandings during data collection and analysis, saving time and effort, and improving the accuracy of their research.

## Communities of Practice and the Importance of the Applicative Knowledge of a PQDS

The CoP literature emerged from detailed empirical studies of professional communities. The concept is particularly well suited for capturing the organizational, work-related aspects of those who are integrated into the income producing, economic sectors of society and activities related to these. A short working definition is as follows (see also Wenger 1998):

The community of practice is that level of the social world at which practice is common, coordinated, and reproduced, at which significance is created, and consequently, in which the border is socially recognized and generic conventions are developed and shared. Thus, it is also the locus in which it is possible to explore and understand the social context of artifacts (Brown and Duguid 1994, pp. 19-20).

A substantial part of the knowledge that CoP members share cannot be clearly articulated, and therefore can only be acquired through apprenticeships, workshops, mentoring, etc. CoP members' shared frame of reference facilitates the transfer of intuitive knowledge but creates boundaries between different CoPs. Therefore, knowledge transfer between different CoPs is fraught with difficulties. Overcoming these barriers requires the special skills of boundary spanners. Understood in this sense, academics, too, also have their own CoP, their ways of being-in-the world, and these are quite different from those of nonacademics. This divergence accentuates communication barriers and is the root cause of one well-known gap between industry practitioners and academia (see Klein and Hirschheim 2006). Having been embedded in nonacademic CoPs, PQDSs come equipped with the capacity for such boundary spanning, a capacity that cannot be acquired from books and is important for effective field-based research.

Through participation in CoPs, PQDSs have acquired the following competencies:

\- Familiarity with specialized work languages (Holmqvist and Bøgh Andersen 1987, 1991; Holmqvist et al. 1996) that are at the core of the work practices and modes of thought of their previous organizations.

\- Command of work and social practices that are not fully documented and beyond the grasp of outsiders who lack the necessary background to interpret them properly.

\- More effective intuition related to their work through implicit learning processes (see Appendix C).

The problem we face is not merely a communication problem between the researcher and the practitioner community. That problem could be solved by new incentive and support systems, as suggested by Hoffman (2004), or may not even be that important, as suggested by Straub and Ang (2008). However, there is also a serious knowledge production problem, such that the knowledge we produce is not regarded as being sufficiently grounded in the problems encountered in practice. As a result, what we as scholars produce is simply not understood to be relevant to what practitioners actually do.

Applicative knowledge (see Appendix D) is important for increasing the relevance of research. While we agree with Van de Ven and Johnson (2006) that there is a knowledge production problem, we do not think their suggestion for enhancing engaged scholarship based on the concept of arbitrage—a strategy of exploiting differences in the kind of knowledge that scholars and practitioners possess—goes far enough. We do not agree that the problem of knowledge production is due to the lack of complementarity between researchers with theoretical knowledge and practitioners with practical applicative knowledge, which therefore can be solved through deeper engagement in field research. Rather, without the experience of dwelling in the CoP under study, researchers will be unable to grasp the tacit beliefs and meanings of their study participants, however deeply engaged they are in their field work. PQDSs are uniquely capable of translating applicative knowledge into theory, thus they pose an alternative solution to the knowledge production problem and the academia–industry communication gap that has not been discussed in the literature.

We now discuss issues regarding the doctoral education of these PQDSs, who come to their doctoral programs having already developed a deep understanding of a certain organizationally based life-world.

## Implications for Aligning Research Methods with Students' Qualifications

In order to leverage the applicative knowledge distinguishing PQD students from their non-PQDS counterparts, we suggest some changes to the standard model of doctoral education. This raises the question of which lens is most appropriate in course design for focusing on the types of knowledge and skills that best serve the needs of PQDSs. We believe that qualitative research methods, either interpretive or positivist (Morgan and Smircich 1980), action research, and design science are the research methods well suited to capitalize on applicative knowledge during the dissertation research. For this reason, the following overview of the key aspects of the doctoral program design is based on the principles of qualitative research according to recent descriptions of the qualitative research process by Denzin and Lincoln (2005, p.22-26).

## Contextual Experience and Subjectivity of the Researcher

Distinct from Denzin and Lincoln's emphasis on multi- and cross-cultural aspects of qualitative research, we emphasize the different knowledge profile of PQDSs. Indeed, we checked various textbooks on qualitative research methods and were surprised to find that none of these addressed the issue of the background knowledge of the researcher, on which different qualitative research methods depend, and how their application should be sensitive to the degree of prior researcher experience with the same or similar business contexts.

Perhaps this lack of sensitivity to experiential knowledge is another manifestation of the prevalent attitude in the academy of the value of theory over practical experience. Therefore, an additional key concern for PDQ student education is to convince them that the subjective knowledge and total life experiences they gained as members of a specific community of practice are a key resource to them and of equal importance to theory. However, in order to realize the competitive advantage thus afforded to them, it is mandatory that they also reflect on their past life-world experiences in terms of the theories they are now learning. This aspect might be taught most effectively when introducing PQDSs to the importance of hermeneutic pre-understandings and the various ways of dealing with subjectivity in qualitative and quantitative research. Another important theoretical vehicle for driving this message home would be to introduce the community of practice literature explicitly. Care must be taken that students do link this literature to their personal lives and experiences through serious reflection and exercises such as a phenomenological essay or autobiographic analyses.

## Reviewing Possible Ontological and Epistemological Beliefs: A Paradigmatic Orientation

It is especially important for PQDSs to develop the ability to formulate the problem in a theoretical framework and to reflect upon the (non)existence of cause-effect relationships, as is often done using Cook and Campbell (1979). Coming from the world of practice, many PDQSs accept the notion that managerial action and organizational outcomes are necessarily linked, without having to test causal linkages. So, emphasizing the issue of causality might also be helpful for overcoming this problem and at the same time raise awareness of ontological issues. Non-PQDSs tend to “imagine” the problem. They are not really conscious of how much they simplify it or at least of how their formulations artificially close the problem. On the other hand, their simpler formulations help them to quickly interpret the problem as pitted against rival theoretical explanations and “solve” it. Conversely PQDSs have a better appreciation for how much more complex and dynamic the problem is; they are less likely to oversimplify it. However, the way they tend to see the usual solution is not complex: it reflects their theories-in-use; it is not usually theory dependent, but just satisficing. There is often a huge gap between the complexity they see in the very often enduring and stressful practical problem that they are investigating, and the solution they live by. It is, therefore, very important to teach PQDSs more theory in order to develop their ability to more systematically compare theories. Thus, emphasizing the value of theory is critical if we are to change the status quo and alleviate problems encountered in practice through artifact construction and redesign.

## Research Strategies and Overall Methodology

The difficulties with formulating research strategy for qualitative dissertation projects arise from the complexity of concisely formulating the phenomenon of interest and the associated research goals. Both continue to emerge and, therefore, can only be addressed by an iterative process of strategy definition, redefinition, and refinement. These difficulties are best addressed through guidance and not through coursework. As part of this process, PQDS advisors should draw their attention to research strategies that are particularly well suited to problem situations and that are less suited or even unavailable to non-PQDSs. Action research and auto-ethnography (Behar 1996; Hayono 1979; for a recent application by a PQDS, see Barnes 2005, Ch. 4) are examples of research strategies that could be infeasible for non-PQDSs. Even if the advisor can provide a site for action research, the chances of conducting action research effectively will be much higher for a PQDS (with exceptions, naturally). For these reasons, PQDSs can focus on an obvious pool of research strategies from which they can choose, such as (auto-)ethnography, case study, grounded theory, action research, design science, and phenomenology. This will help them to focus earlier and give the advisor the opportunity to do more in-depth coaching than is often possible with non-PQDSs. In turn, this is likely to permit PQDSs to finalize their research strategy and design earlier than other students, in particular when the latter adopt a qualitative research design. Ph.D. advisors should be aware of this opportunity and encourage it, not so much because it could shorten the course of study, but in order to free up time for theory development and a more thorough study of the philosophical foundations of qualitative research.

However, in our experience, PQDSs have not fully exploited all research strategy opportunities. In most cases they have conducted case studies based on in-depth interviews or conventional ethnographies (see van Maanen 1988). This strategy permitted them to benefit from their easier access to the field, and boosted their confidence so that their dissertation project was both meaningful and doable. Such confidence is not just a matter of age, but also based on their pre-understanding of the kind of data they needed and could collect. This observation takes us to the central topic of data collection and analysis.

## Methods of Data Collection, Analysis, and Synthesis

For analysis, we will focus only on those approaches to inference making and synthesis, which relate to the advantages provided by the intuitive knowledge that PQDSs acquire from their prior professional lifeworld experiences.

## Data Collection Advantages for PQDS

With respect to interviews, our experiences with various PQDSs suggest that they are more capable of anticipating the time required, managing the duration of the interview, and bringing the interviewee back to the subject.

In the case of participant observation, there is less of a time management problem. In addition, PQDSs are better able to “blend into the scene” than non-PQDSs, who will, therefore, have more difficulties as participatory observers because they have not been socialized into the practices of the research site participants. The key challenge is to interpret and track the relevant observations of the work environment. Clearly the non-PQDSs are at a disadvantage here because they lack the prior understanding to succeed with this sense-making cognitive (over)load. The jargon used by participants is known to the PQDS and therefore is not a hindrance to understanding. To some extent this also applies to interviewing, which deserves a few more comments (see Appendix E).

## Data Analysis and Synthesis

PQDSs should be encouraged to practice the four types of generalizations described by Lee and Baskerville (2003), and interpret them as major modes of inference. Advisors should be aware that PQDSs have both major advantages and disadvantages in learning all of them and should coach accordingly. Two modes of inference need special attention—abduction (a type of theory generation that combines induction and deduction) and deduction (a type of theory testing)—because we have found that these are the ones in which PQDSs are most likely to excel due to their prior experiences. In the other two modes, which we call induction and synthesis, we reason that PQDSs have no advantage or are even at a disadvantage (see Table 2).

## Finding a Publication Strategy Matching Research and Practitioner Community Interests

The fundamental issue at stake in this phase of the education of PQDSs is how to make sense of the “mountains of empirical materials” (Denzin and Lincoln 2000, p. 23) with which the researcher emerges from the work at his or her research site and to “sell” these ideas to the larger research community.

This tends to include consideration of the politics of the research community, but at the core of this phase is the issue of making good sense of students' research findings.

For PQDSs, a particular concern lies in the publication process. This consists of finding a form of representation and communication of their research results that can speak to the professional CoP that was studied, so that their former peers can see the practical implications of the research results. This goes far beyond the usual confirmation of the internal validity of the research results with the study participants. Without finding at least a partial match between research and practitioner community interests and publicizing results through the preferred channels of the CoP involved, which are quite different from academically preferred publication outlets, the special boundary spanning contributions that PQDS should make would be lost. After clearing the initial hurdles of an academic career, the workload for such double involvement is not much different from that of traditional academics who often publish in more than one area of specialization. PQDS would have the additional advantage that their continued involvement with practitioners should have a stimulating effect on their academic publication projects as well. We agree with Hoffman (2004, p. 217) that “all our communities provide inspiration, emotional support and intellectual challenge” and that practitioner communities in particular “help us to ground our ideas in the real world, a place where our ‘data’ originated and where our future phenomenological questions will emerge."

## Conclusions

Ph.D. education should take into account the professional experience of students. When we do not, our field misses the opportunity to tap into a wellspring of relevant experience and to address the issue of practical irrelevance leveled at the field. We have argued that we need the experience and problem-framing connection to practice that PDQSs can bring to IS research, and that our insistence that we train them like any other Ph.D. student is shortsighted. By training PQDSs only in quantitative methods, we deny them a large part of their potential competitive advantage, which would enable them to make substantial contributions to solving the knowledge production problem that has led to a disturbing gap in IS research relevance. We need their unique capacity to serve as future boundary spanners between academia and practice and the contributions to knowledge that they can make if properly trained in qualitative research methods. This need might become even more important in the future because new research on qualitative methods is working on overcoming the gap between the descriptive nature of qualitative research and realizing its potential for addressing practical problems of action, with grounded theory methods, interpretive field studies, and case studies.

Beyond institutional limitations (see Appendix F), which were not the core idea of this article, we recognize other important avenues for developing these ideas further. First, the value that the PQDSs can bring to the academic community through doctoral programs is likely to be heavily dependent on the specific practical work experience of the student and to its fit with their Ph.D. dissertation. In that respect, a study of which work experiences seem to help or hinder the progress of the PQDSs might be very helpful. For this purpose, future research should systematically examine the frequency, demographics, and background of PQDSs. This type of data is also needed for planning the organizational arrangements best suited for welcoming this new type of student into adjusted doctoral program arrangements.

Our proposal includes a motivational component: by encouraging the student to maintain their relationships and turn to their CoP for their research, they may continue to engage in relevant problems. However, this proposal is a necessary but insufficient condition for the field. We strongly recommend that the academic community should critically evaluate its high preference for theoretical knowledge in the form that dates back to the Age of Enlightenment.

## About the Authors

Heinz K. Klein was most recently Invited Chair at Salford University in Manchester, United Kingdom, and Adjunct Professor at the School of Management of the State University of New York at Binghamton. He held teaching and research positions at Temple University in Philadelphia as well as at universities in Germany, Canada, Finland, Denmark, New Zealand, and South Africa. His research on the philosophy of IS research, foundations of IS theory, and methodologies of information systems development have appeared in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Information and Organization, Information Systems Journal, Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, and other journals, as well as in research monographs and international conference proceedings. He served on the editorial boards of several scholarly journals including Systèmes d'Information et Management and the Wiley Series in Information Systems. He held a Ph.D. from the Faculty of Business Administration of the University of Munich, and received honorary doctorates by the University of Oulu, Finland, and University of Pretoria, South Africa. Professor Klein passed away in June 2008; he is sadly missed by the IS community.

Frantz Rowe has been serving as a professor, and as a CIO and an advisor to the president, at the University of Nantes, France, since 1995. Previously, he was a professor at Telecom ParisTech, and an assistant professor at Ecole Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées, Paris.

He recently visited Bentley College (now Bentley University) and taught at Harvard. His research on individual and organizational transformations related to information systems projects and use (ERP dynamics, call centers—including shared or virtual—and electronic marketplaces) have appeared in Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, Data Base, Accounting, Management and Information Technologies, Journal of Global Information Management, Journal of Decision Systems, Transportation Research, Technological Forecasting and Social Change, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, ISDN

Networks and Computer Systems, and proceedings of the International Conference on Information Systems. He has been on the editorial boards of several scholarly journals including European Journal of Information Systems and Systèmes d'Information et Management, which he has served as editor-in-chief for 13 years. He holds an M.A. in Economics from Lyon University, an M.E. from Ecole Nationale des Travaux Publics de l'Etat (ENTPE), an M.S. from the University of California, Berkeley, and a Ph.D. from the University of Paris-Nanterre. With Dov Te'eni, he is the co-chair of ICIS 2008 in Paris.
