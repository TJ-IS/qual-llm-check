# The use of domination and legitimation in information systems implementation

Zahid I. Hussain\* & Nelarine Cornelius<sup>†</sup>

School of Management, University of Bradford, Emm Lane, Heaton, Bradford, BD9 4JL, UK, \*email: z.i.hussain@bradford.ac.uk, and <sup>†</sup>email: n.cornelius@bradford.ac.uk

Abstract. In this paper, we present the results of a longitudinal case study on information systems (IS) implementation conducted in a community healthcare organization. Using structuration theory as a sensitizing framework, we highlight how the information technology (IT) Management improved their influence through gaining legitimation from other organizational stakeholders, and how the nature of this evolved over time. Our results highlight how an appropriate, sophisticated use of what Giddens refers to as the duality of structure contributed to the consolidation of the IT Manager’s credibility and authority. We also report on how the IT Management had most of their actions legitimated as an integral element of their actions. The results also highlight the distributed nature of power, such that even those at the lower end of organizational hierarchy were able to influence the success or failure of IS implementation.

Keywords: information systems implementation, case study, structuration theory, domination and legitimation, systems leadership

## INTRODUCTION

The success of information systems (IS) implementation depends on many factors. In order to achieve success, there is a need to consider the organizational context, implementation process and system content aspects. First, the social and organizational contextual factors are important, including organizational ideologies (Orlikowski, 1996), organizational complexity (Keen, 1981a,b; Xia & Lee, 2005), organizational pathology (Goulielmos, 2003), management support (Sharma & Yetton, 2003), IS developer assumptions, leadership and commitment (Salaway, 1987; Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Tractinsky & Jarvenpaa, 1995; Newman & Sabherwal, 1996) and end-user beliefs and expectations (Gerlach & Kuo, 1991; Vaughan & Schwartz, 1999; DeLone & McLean, 2003).

Second, IS implementation-related factors are important (Drori, 1997), such as choice of IS implementation methods (Davenport et al., 1992; Orlikowski, 1993; Avison & Fitzgerald, 1999; Iivari & Lyytinen, 1999; Maguire, 2000; Russo & Stolterman, 2000; Iivari et al., 2001; Rose, 2002; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003).

Third, there is a need to consider system content-related factors, such as fit between technology and the organization (Flowers, 1996; Porra et al., 2005), whether the user requirements change during IS development and implementation (Cotterman & Senn, 1992; McDermid, 1994; Holtzblatt & Beyer, 1995; Drori, 1997) or whether the system itself changes during implementation (Sabherwal & Newman, 2003).

Furthermore, effective leadership and careful planning of IS implementation-related activities of the information technology (IT) Management and line management can result in IS success (Lawrence & Low, 1993; Nadan, 1997; Ravichandran & Rai, 2000; Sharma & Yetton, 2003; Hussain et al., 2004). For example, as Bjorn-Andersen et al. (1986) noted, IS developers could resolve conflicts and differences by paying an equal amount of attention to both technology and organizations. Organizational characteristics can influence the implementation of IS. IS developers and line management need to understand the types of uncertainties involved, for example, social issues concerning stakeholders’ views, requirements and informal ways of working (Vidgen, 1997; Mitev & Kerkham, 1998).

## Exercise of power and domination

IS developers’ assumptions and attitudes play a crucial role during IS development and implementation (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Hirschheim et al., 1995; Porra et al., 2005; Hussain & Taylor, 2007). Their preferred role will depend in part on the degree and type of power and influence they can exercise (Myers & Young, 1997; Jasperson et al., 2002), as IS development and implementation require appropriate management and control (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992; Introna, 1997; Doolin, 2004). The deployment and use of this power is risky and can have many negative implications, such as user resistance and lack of system ownership (Flowers, 1996; Liebowitz, 1999; Sauer, 1999) and loss of image or credibility of the developer. Thus, IS developers need to exercise power responsibly and democratically while sanctioning their actions by obtaining approval of others (Nadan, 1997; Scheepers & Damsgaard, 1997; Hussain et al., 2004).

For example, Nadan (1997) found in his study of the lending processes of a development bank that all the stakeholders possessed and were able to exercise some sort of power, irrespective of their status. He concerns himself with the dialectic of control and looks at actions taken by managers to maximize their own decision-making authority in order to influence the perception of their performance, so that they could secure a good performance appraisal, better bonuses, promotion and other incentives. He believes that Giddens’ (1984) dialectics o control concept is ideal for looking at deliberate actions such as manipulating performance indicators, introducing budgetary measures and self-beneficial choices.

These findings are consistent with Giddens’ theoretical view of power: that it is ‘the capacity to achieve outcomes’ (Giddens, 1984, p. 257) and further: Whether or not these [outcomes] are connected to purely sectional interests is not germane to its definition. Power is not, as such, an obstacle to freedom or emancipation but is their very medium – although it would be foolish, of course, to ignore its constraining properties. (257). He also makes a clear connection between domination, structure and power, thus: The existence of power presumes structures of domination whereby power ‘flows smoothly’ in processes of social reproduction (and is, as it were, ‘unseen’). The development of force or its threat is thus not the typical case of the use of power. Blood and fury, the heat of battle, direct confrontation of rival camps – these are not necessarily the historical conjunctures in which the most far reaching effects of power are either felt or established (257).

So, Giddens argues, his view of power differs from that of Marx’s implicit focus on schism and division of interest: from Parson's normative consensus and his diminution of contestatior of norms; and from Foucault’s positioning of power as ‘prior to truth’ (1984: 257) and for which, in Giddens view, the Foucauldian account of agency and knowledgeability are less than satisfactory. For Giddens, power is a more ubiquitous and subtle phenomenon. Domination involves controlling and using allocative and authoritative resources, along with power, over other people or resources. The ‘rightful’ exercise of power and other social actions requires legitimation by human beings as knowledgeable agents. Therefore, individuals are seen as knowledgeable agents who have power to intervene and influence their surroundings and events. Thus, human acts are continuous flows of power. Indeed, in Nadan’s (1997) bank study, he found that even those strategically placed in the organization and who had access to allocative and authoritative resources were not completely autonomous. Mostly, the outcomes were achieved through acts of their subordinates who had access to these resources. Overall, he found that power was possessed by those at the top and those at the bottom, where super-ordinates and subordinates are related in a dialectical sense, and they depend on each other.

The aim of our research is to investigate the use of domination and legitimation by the IT Management during IS implementation. Here, attention is paid to the use of existing and production of new forms of power and domination by developers. The implementation stage within IS development is particularly informative, as the decisions have been made about the nature of the system, and how to proceed. It is therefore how the decision is interpreted and acted on, and the socio-psychological dynamics that unfold, which comprise the primary focus of our investigation.

Structuration theory (Giddens, 1979; 1984) is a useful framework for understanding situations like IS implementation in terms of social structure and human interaction. It has been found to be a useful and powerful framework for looking at the development and use of IS in organizations (Macintosh & Scapens, 1990; Nandhakumar, 1996; Brooks, 1997; Huang, 1997; Gregor & Johnston, 2000). More specifically, it is expected that structuration theory would assist in unearthing new insights into the nature of domination and its use by stakeholders at different levels of the organization, how stakeholders gain approval from others and use this to become more dominant, and how these interactions might change at the implementation stage.

In the remainder of the paper, we provide: (1) an overview of structuration theory and its use by IS researchers; (2) research strategy, explaining our research context, data collection and analyses; (3) results, comprising episodes, subdivided into three implementation phases: pre-implementation, implementation and postimplementation; (4) discussion, summarizing the main findings on the use of legitimation and domination; and finally (5) the conclusions.

## THEORETICAL APPROACH: STRUCTURATION THEORY

The combined focus within structuration theory on structure and action is its powerful feature, as it does not isolate individual behaviour from social interactions or mental assumptions. Structuration theory is concerned with two main issues; first, the influence on human interaction of institutional (structural) aspects of social life such as rules, communication and power structures and, second, the production and reproduction of these structural aspects through human interaction. The term ‘duality of structure’ represents the central element of the theory expressing the notion that structure not only influences interaction but is also produced by it, and cannot be conceived without it. Some of the core elements of structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) are shown in Figure 1, where social structure and human interaction are subdivided into three interlinked dimensions. In their communication, human beings draw on interpretative schemes to make sense of their own and others’ actions; in so doing, they produce and reproduce structures of signification. This sense making implies cognition, often regarded as a psychological construct, but one which Giddens incorporates into structuration theory as interpretive schemes, and which is an important and integral aspect of how organizational actors engage with structuration.

Structure within structuration theory consists of memory traces which are continuously re/produced by individuals’ actions. The production and reproduction of structures, referred to as duality of structure, is a powerful aspect of structuration theory (Walsham, 1993; Jones, 1999; Pozzebon & Pinsonneault, 2005). Legitimation structures are comprised of ‘normative regulation’ (for example, a policy), with social agents ‘reflexively monitoring the flow of interaction with one another’, and where normative elements are ‘contingent claims’ (Giddens, 1984, pp. 30–31). Crucially, the capacity to exercise normative sanctions if regulation is violated evidences asymmetries in domination, within the specific social setting. Legitimation structures such as policies, strategies, methodologies and objectives can be produced and reproduced to serve as legitimation frameworks. In seeking legitimacy, individuals constantly refer to organizational ways of working, such as organizational guidelines and IT strategy to ensure that their ideas fit into relevant frameworks. At other times, organizational guidelines may be discussed and customized to suit a project, which means new legitimation structures are produced.

Domination structures can be produced and reproduced. This can be achieved, for example, through use of the management hierarchy by key stakeholders to control resources, such as finance, IT and equipment. Another group of stakeholders may have dominating personalities and characteristics allowing them to dominate situations within a project context. Their ideas may then become codes of practice in departmental or organizational operational strategy and thereby part of new domination structures. Domination can also be achieved by those with expertise and skills, although their influence depends on their competence in political or leadership skills, their value to others and their link with those individuals who already have a high degree of domination.

Domination happens when social agents use their personal influence, skills and expertise in a given situation, using the facilities and resources of power at their disposal while at the same time conforming to existing codes of practice and achieving recognition and approval of othe actors. Giddens (1979; 1984) highlights two types of power used in achieving domination: the transformative capacity of human action and relational power. All social relations utilize both types of power. Furthermore, there are two types of resources of power: command ove allocative resources (economic capacity) and command over authoritative resources (ability to organize and coordinate). Some actors have more power and can also use it well in achieving domination. Additionally, once the domination is produced, it can be exercised to reinforce o increase it. This happens when those with domination and power influence individuals and gain their cooperation. Finally, according to Giddens, all social actors have some sort of power (dialectic of control), as they have at least some sort of resource under their control; otherwise, they cease to be social agents. There is an interlacing of meaning, normative elements and power . . . Awareness of such contestation, of divergent and overlapping characteristics, is an essential part of the knowing of life. (pp. 28–29). It is these central notions, of the ubiquitous presence of power as a social phenomenon, and its contestation, which differentiate the structuration theory approach to domination and power from others. He does not believe that power is instrinsically connected to the achievement of sectional interests.

The signification aspect of structuration creates meaning and codes of discourse (communication) in the structure. Structure as signification involves semantic rules. In a signification structure, for example, standard codes can be produced and reproduced, such as operational codes for the daily running and coordination of an organizational department and defining the breakdown of its functions. Many other codes are based on organizational guidelines. For example, administrative codes define quality levels for individual tasks and IT use.

Many researchers have used structuration theory in IS research. First, a number of IS researchers have used structuration theory to look at the role of specific systems within an organizational setting. For example, Barley (1986) considered the application of compute tomography scanners in radiology departments, and used structuration theory to focus on the implementation of IS for radiologists and technicians involved in scanning as part of their jobs. Lyytinen & Ngwenyama (1992) used structuration theory to look at Computer Supported

Co-operative Work, while Nandhakumar (1996) investigated the development of an Executive Information System in a large multinational organization. Through structuration theory, Brooks (1997) showed the importance of social aspects in the implementation of computer-aided design/drafting, while Scheepers & Damsgaard (1997) used the theory to look at the implementation of intranet solutions in four organizations. Chu & Smithson (2007) used it to look at the implementation of an e-business initiative in a traditional automotive manufacturer.

Second, many researchers have used structuration theory to specify IS implications for organizations. Macintosh & Scapens (1990) showed the importance of accounting systems in organizations in characterizing social and organizational life. Barrett (1995) investigated management issues which influence the IT implementation process. Huang (1997) applied structuration theory using a longitudinal case study to focus on the issues of participation in IS implementation. Walsham (2002) used the theory to examine cross-cultural software production and use; Lamb & Kling (2003) used it to examine theoretical constructs which shape user concepts.

Structuration theory was considered a suitable sensitizing framework, as it focuses on the actions and mental structures of stakeholders (Riley, 1983; Orlikowski, 1992; Scapens & Macintosh, 1996; Scheepers & Damsgaard, 1997; Gregor & Johnston, 2000). The domination dimension of structuration theory was chosen because it shows how stakeholders became dominant and powerful, allowing them to steer the IS implementation process, make important decisions and even negotiate better terms of service (Knights & Murray, 1994; Introna, 1997; Jasperson et al., 2002; Doolin, 2004). Our goal is to investigate how IS developers produced and reproduced structures of domination by performing certain activities during IS implementation. In particular, we aim to show if and how domination is achieved through gaining the approval of others, or by legitimating their actions and decisions.

## RESEARCH STRATEGY

## Research context

Many articles have been written about IT adoption in conventional general hospital settings (Westrup, 1996; Salauroo & Burnes, 1998; Wainwright & Waring, 2000; Massey & Williams, 2006). One example is Kohli & Kettinger’s (2004) study of the exercise of power and influence by physicians, who were part of the elite ‘clan’ of the clinical organizational structure. They found that this group had the freedom to stall or sanction the adoption of an information system (driven by the IT Department which resided in the less powerful functional, non-clinica structure). Indeed, the UK National Health Service (NHS) is widely regarded as being inherently highly static and bureaucratic, and the introduction of an IS into such an organization is regarded as difficult (Bloomfield & Coombs, 1992; Keen, 1994; Mitev & Kerkham, 1998). The research organization (referred to as ‘MedService’ for anonymity) is a community healthcare organization operating from 99 geographically dispersed locations. The majority of those working at MedService are nursing and healthcare professionals, with doctors representing roughly 2% of the workforce. The purpose of the new IS project in MedService (the Project) was to implement an intranet system and its supporting technologies. The need for this sort of system was highlighted by the Information Needs Survey conducted by the IT and Strategy Managers about 2 years prior to the Project. The IT Manager initiated and managed the Project involving the acquisition of the intranet. The data collected are drawn from a large study of change in the NHS, and in this article, we focus only on the domination and legitimation aspects of structuration theory emerging from this context.

The MedService is a community healthcare organization and not a hospital. Thus, the nature and types of control held and practised by functional and clinical professionals differ from those of general hospitals. Relative to general hospitals, the degree of control possessed by functional groups, such as the IT Department, is higher. There are a number of reasons for this. First, there were fewer medical and clinical professionals in this organization and they were dispersed across 99 sites. The control was diffused as opposed to concentrated in one or several smaller sites. The reason for a lower number of clinical/medical professionals was that MedService did not offer complex medical treatments. Such work was passed to two large local general hospitals and other specialist organizations. Furthermore, the chief executive of the organization was a former nurse, which served as a token for a more neutral use of power between medical, nursing and functional professionals.

Second, the budgets were decided centrally at the headquarters, as opposed to by ward clinic consultants. Their involvement was relatively small compared with conventional hospi tals. The IT department happened to be located at the headquarters and benefited from an image of being associated with strategic management. Furthermore, the strategic direction for provision of services was also decided by strategic management at the headguarters. So while the power and authority of functional professionals may be weak in conventional hospitals, their control and power are relatively high in community health organizations like MedService.

As far as the process of IS implementation is concerned, MedService as part of the NHS used Procurement of Information Systems Effectively (POISE) methodology for IS develop ment and implementation which is structured and linear in nature. Although according to Beynon-Davies & Williams (2003), there are different categories of IS development and implementation approaches: structured and linear, such as Structured Systems Development Analysis and Design; iterative, such as Rapid Applications Development; and object oriented, such as Unified Modelling Language. POISE emphasizes procurement of IT and is cost centred. It is rational and functional in orientation (Hirschheim et al., 1995) and is concerned with functional and process aspects of users’ work rather than being user-centred like other methodologies (Wynekoop & Russo, 1997; Beynon-Davies & Williams, 2003), such as the agile approach (Beck, 1999) or dynamic approach (Stapleton, 1997). POISE requires the system specification to be written following a structured format so that customers and vendors alike can check and agree on the work and finalize the costs accordingly. This specification then becomes a legal contract between two parties.

Despite following this structured methodology, the IT Manager took a measured approach and involved the line management and user representatives in the IS implementation in orde to achieve their legitimation, thus increasing system ownership. It was important to increase the sense of ownership as the intranet was going to have an impact on the organization in a major way. As it was to be implemented across entire MedService, it was designed to completely eliminate some of the departmental policies and procedures. Thus, many stakeholders were affected by this change. In line with Poole et al. (1989), we selected stakeholders from different sites representing different levels for this study. Table 1 illustrates the stakeholders involved in this project, highlighting their respective aims/concerns related to it.

These aims/concerns were both obtained directly, and inferred from, discussions with the stakeholders during the research period. However, the main stakeholders involved in the Project’s initial stages (requirement construction stage) were the IT Manager, Communications Manager, Contracts Director and Information Services Director (Communications Director). These stakeholders were the driving force in implementing the new system. They sough top-down agreement on the introduction of technologies and attempted to gain initial suppor from the middle and operational managers before developing the intranet facilities. The main hindrance to their task was the resistance from the line management that were, relatively, ‘IT illiterate’ and very traditional. Interestingly, the Project received more support from workers. As implementation commenced, a number of line managers also came on board, although a majority of them were still relatively new to IT.

## Data collection

The data collection was undertaken using a qualitative research approach (Hirschheim, 1992; Walsham, 1993; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Myers, 1995). The research design enabled us to learn about the types and sources of domination exercised by key stakeholders and their desire to maintain it. We considered socio-psychological issues, such as the personal interests of key stakeholders, and focused on how their personalities, characters and aspirations influenced IS implementation. The data collection involved:

1 Observations of project-related meetings, seminars, events and discussions. These were documented systematically, on paper or electronically, and then subsequently placed into a database. Some meetings and interviews were also recorded on audiotapes. Approximately 35 visits were made and over 70 meetings were held over a 2-year period, and records were kept, using a system similar to that developed by Dhillon (1995).

2 A combination of questionnaires and face-to-face interviews was also used to collect data. Initially, we used a simple stakeholder profile and stakeholder record form to collect information about stakeholders. A first questionnaire was completed by us to collect more ‘personal information about each stakeholder, such as their political interests. A second questionnaire was used to collect information during interviews on the ‘demographic’ background of each stakeholder, such as the positions they held and their length of service. Both served as initial guides enabling us to learn more about each of the stakeholders, although we learned much more about the stakeholders as the research progressed. At the end of the Project, an IS evaluation interview was held to evaluate the usefulness of the system for each stakeholder. In these semi-structured interviews, respondents were also asked to describe certain action(s) or situation(s) associated with the Project, in particular those related to IS implementation. The respondents chosen for this exercise were usually those we viewed as most influential and/or dominant during the Project. This interview was used as a guide to the discussion. Interview responses were continually checked and where necessary, refined by the interviewer. This helped to verify them. The categories of questions asked and the reasons for asking them are shown in Table 2.

There were core dimensions to the interview schedule: reflections on thoughts, feelings and actions against selected situations using the two dimensions of structuration theory – legiti mation and domination, which are central to our current research interest; and additionally, signification dimension, in order to further elaborate legitimation and domination outcomes. Although these guided the interview, the use of technical jargon was kept to a minimum and any technical terms used were fully explained. The data collected through observations and different types of questionnaires and interviews were used to enable a more informed and balanced use of structuration theory. The reasons for using observations and questionnaires were to cross-reference the stakeholder responses, and to gain a better understanding of the situation and stakeholder viewpoints.

## Data structure and analysis

## Project phases and episodes

We primarily focus on power-facility-domination and sanction-norm-legitimation elements, as shown in Figure 2, as our basis for the application of structuration theory for the analysis of IS implementation and related changes.

The activities taking place within the Project phases of pre-implementation, implementation and postimplementation were recorded as episodes. An episode is a short description of an IS implementation activity or an event. We provide narrative explanations of several chosen episodes from IS implementation phases.

Figure 2 shows that there were two types of episode: those involving reproduction of existing structures or shaping of situations and those involving production of new structures. These types of episodes reflect the duality of structure where structure is used to produce action and vice versa. To determine whether a particular structure was produced or reproduced and whether it was the legitimation or the domination structure, we attempted to:

1 Gain knowledge of the organizational context through observations, interviews and meetings.

2 Investigate the motives, beliefs and values of stakeholders through interviews, participation in project meetings and questionnaires.

3 Observe stakeholder behaviour and actions, and the resulting outcomes.

A comprehensive data classification regime was followed, where data classification initially required a clear definition of structuration theory principles. The data collected using observations (of meetings, seminars, events and discussion) and questionnaires (stakeholder profiles, stakeholders’ records and project evaluation) were codified and mapped against the theory. A key criterion for this classification was to make sense of IS implementation-related activities using structuration theory. A single researcher carried out the data collection and the data were then checked and classified with the help of another researcher. Here, the researchers would jointly ascribe theoretical meaning to actions, behaviour and stakeholder responses. Furthermore, we carried out data quality checks, using respondent validation, which was conducted with 33% of the research subjects.

We endeavoured to establish the validity and reliability of our findings. Golden-Biddle & Locke (1993) argue that research validity is the ‘best approximation to truth’ and researchers have developed a variety of frameworks to make sure that data are reliable and the findings are valid (Hammersley, 1992; Kaplan & Maxwell, 1994; Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999; Bryman, 2001). The validity in this research was determined using the quality criteria of Lincoln & Guba (1985) and Klein & Myers (1999) for completeness.

The analysis of the data collected will be shown in the form of episodes which will be grouped into the following phases to provide a more systematic and logically presented narrative:

1 Phase 1 Pre-implementation aspects involving planning and setting aims.

2 Phase 2 Implementation, involving system transfer.

3 Phase 3 Postimplementation, involving system use.

The IS implementation phases were defined in order to report the Project progress in a logical and chronological fashion. The episodes were designed to report a ‘snap shot’ account of chosen IS implementation-related activities. We limited the number of episodes to meet word-length limitations of this article. To restate, the intranet was an off-the-shelf system, so the actual technical design required within the organization was very small, although the system did require customization.

## RESULTS

Overall, data analysis concerning the improvement of domination, through the use of legitimation is presented. This is done by first presenting a synopsis of episodes for the three main phases of IS implementation (Table 3), and then by providing a more detailed structuration theory-based analysis of the episodes and details of the context within which they were enacted, for each phase.

## Phase 1 – pre-implementation

The episodes in Phase 1 are discussed next.

## Episode 1: reproduction of domination structures – the IT Manager

This episode shows the reproduction of domination structures concerning the IT Manager. He undertook this project to enhance his image and achieve greater credibility with strategid management, line management and workers in the IT department. He justified and legitimated his actions in order to enhance this domination. This new domination would take the form of projection of the image of an ‘expert’ as well as more formal authority.

The IT Manager was recruited shortly before conducting the Information Needs Survey and his major strength was his commercial experience. It was hoped that he would use this to help the organization to make cost savings and improve efficiency. He was aware of such expectations and knew that his credibility in his Department and his relationship with Strategic Management depended on his making IS-related changes throughout the organization. He jointly conducted the Information Needs Survey (the first of its type ever conducted in the organization) and then proposed a few large organization-wide systems to replace smaller departmental systems situated at various sites. These were old and incompatible with each other and, therefore, expensive to maintain.

The IT Manager’s newly proposed systems were going to be expensive initially, but, if successful, they would assure a better return on investment. The criteria for success were to help to quicken the work process, save costs, increase vertical and horizontal communication, increase and improve methods of worker control and monitoring, and change worker attitudes. The success of the Project was to be monitored by running an evaluation survey (functional in its orientation) and by subjective evaluation of the IT Department and reports from the Finance Department on expenditure and wastage.

One of the first steps that the IT Manager took was to implement an organization-wide computer network as an infrastructure development across all 99 sites on which other system could run. The next step was to propose an organization-wide intranet system and justify its ability to enhance organizational communication and increase worker awareness of IT on using this more user-friendly technology. Two functional applications were to be implemented: email and a shared patient database in addition to the departmental homepages for all 99 sites

The IT Manager began to gain a lot of support from his subordinates for his efforts in moving the IT Department forward. In part, this was because the IT Manager ‘demonstrated’ that his aim was to benefit the organization more than seeking to enhance his own personal support, although he was aware that this Project would increase his personal credibility. We inferred from our observations and conversations with various senior IT workers that the IT Manager’s ultimate aim was to increase the importance of the IT Department and to increase his own domination.

A major challenge for the IT Manager was the need to address initial feelings of alienation of ordinary, non-IT workers about the IT Department. For example, one interviewee (CP) in one of remote clinics said, ‘They sit in the Ivory tower’. The Ward Manager at one site said, They don’t seem to care about us. In contrast, the information management and technology (IM&T) Manager, who is subordinate to the IT Manager, from the IT Department, confirmed that: The development of information systems is still generated by the IT Department who recognize the benefits to the organization. This balance [between technology awareness and provision] can only be re-addressed when we have a more knowledgeable workforce, including at Management level.

Thus, the IT Manager sought to use legitimation tactics to overcome worker dissent and reservations, as well as to protect his image and maintain his domination. He practised this with a high degree of care, as he was aware of the existence of many other powerful groups with a high degree of domination, such as line managers, some of whom were consultants. He was aware that his domination could increase over a long period of time, and he had to make use of many types of resources. He also had to legitimate his actions and achieve approva from stakeholders, and hence was very liberal in his approach. Thus, he involved a number of stakeholders in the Project to help him to communicate to workers and involved line managers.

## Episode 2: production of legitimation structures – organizational workers

This episode shows the production of legitimation structures, involving organizational workers, where the use of the intranet would result in change to worker attitudes towards IS use. Thus, the initial worker approval for its implementation was important. On the other hand, these workers also possessed different forms of power and authority by virtue of their formal position, expertise in a particular clinical area, informal contacts and relationships, ability to influence the views of others and choosing to use or not to use technology as intended by the IT Management. The IT Manager had to practise his existing and new emerging domination liberally and gain approval and legitimation of workers. In order that the IS implementation would be legitimated by them, it was important that workers saw this change as important, so the IT Department highlighted the potential of the intranet in facilitating a more open flow of communication and replacement of various laborious tasks.

In order to encourage a more positive attitude towards IS implementation, the IT Manager aimed to demonstrate the intranet to line managers and workers as a medium for communi cation. The intranet was promoted as a way to achieve more horizontal and vertical communication through, first, use of email to send messages; second, the use of homepages to publish or broadcast information related to the department or speciality; third, sharing of computer applications to carry out tasks, such as inputting patient records, work scheduling and diary; and, finally, collaboration between different departments and workers when enhancing the intranet use in tasks such as creating data entry forms or checking patient profiles. This communication and dialogue would take place between workers in different departments o similar status and also between senior and junior staff.

The introduction of the intranet would not be just a new advancement, such as communication, but would also serve as a beginning to a series of technological changes. The IT Manager confirmed during an interview that in his view, the intranet would bring about a sense of change and generate new beliefs and values to encourage workers to embrace change more openly. The scale of change necessary in worker attitudes would need to be high, almost a transformation or the start of a new era of working. For example, in a stakeholder profile survey made during the middle of the Project, an interviewee (CP) commented that The system will enable medical and nursing staff to access and use relevant information for education and updating [development]. Furthermore, the possibility of workers having access to organizational and external information, having an email address and not requiring much technological training was seen as a positive factor.

An important factor in the intranet’s acceptability for the workers was that it would give them an opportunity to communicate with the outside world more freely. In the past, this had been almost impossible; it was therefore a totally new phenomenon, which expanded their horizons, gave administrative workers new IT skills and reduced laborious administrative tasks. In an interview conducted a few months after the implementation of the intranet, in order to obtain her reflective account, the IM&T Manager was asked about the changes which had occurred to worker values since the IT Department faced the initial hurdles when planning the implementation of the intranet, as far as worker values were concerned. She argued that: One of the changes is that they can [now] see the power of technology. [For example] It can bring the world to your desk. The idea of being able to communicate with each other changed people’s work and ways of working! They’re beginning to set the pace, where in the past we’ve done it. Which is what I’d like it to be.

## Episode 3: production and reproduction of domination structures – the IT manager, general workers and IT workers

This episode shows the production and reproduction of domination structures involving the IT Manager, general workers and IT workers. The IT Manager’s management of this Project and controlling of resources created his image as a new authority and body of expertise in the organization. His level of domination increased, mainly in the form of controlling IT resources and making IT-related decisions. He took a measured approach in deploying the resources, such as using his subordinates to raise IT awareness through appropriate communication and dialogue, as well as purchasing relevant computing infrastructure.

The IT Manager proposed a number of IT projects upon initial approval of the intranet by Strategic Management. He was then allowed by Strategic Management to control all project resources as he was responsible for resource allocation, including the budget. In an interview, the IT Manager said: IT services management and [the] IM&T Manager saw it as a rich time for gaining support and money for IT ventures. He possessed the strategic knowledge of organizational working and project management both in this organization and other organizations, plus expertise in budgeting and management strategy, as well as project management ‘know-how’. Most IT workers accepted his formal role and assumed it was logical, or perhaps natural, to defer to him as a figure of authority and expertise, especially because he was libera in his use of authority and was a ‘democratic’ decision-maker who involved them where possible. His standing increased even more after his involvement in obtaining management permission, support and funding for the intranet project. The intranet (including email) and network infrastructure projects were in a series of developments proposed by him. IT workers gave credit to him for receiving such high recognition and felt comfortable under his leadership.

Furthermore, the IT Department was seen by general workers and line management to have a close working relationship with Strategic Management. Most functional departments and workers saw the IT Department as a strategic support department which worked closely with Strategic Management. For example, one of the line managers (SB) was asked about her view of the IT Department, and she replied, From a distance [i.e. not part of us]. Instead, she associated the IT Department with Strategic Management. Similarly, another line manager (CP) was asked about her impression of the IT Department. She replied: They are important and are becoming more important as the time passes. But they need to interact more with us to gain further recognition.

So by this point, the IT Department could afford not to interact with line management and had to be liberal in the use of domination, as the line management was also powerful in its own righ and possessed professional and positional domination. A lower-level worker also confirmed that, in her view, the IT Management as close to Strategic Management and made join IS-related decisions. The image of the IT Department as being close to Strategic Management worked to the IT Department’s advantage, helped it to assert its authority and to have the intranet accepted by functional departments.

The main reasons why this image existed in the minds of general stakeholders was, first, that the IT Department and its workers portrayed themselves in this way by constantly referring to ‘strategic will’ to improve efficiency through the use of IS. Second, there was generally a lack of communication between the IT Department and functional departments, other than for the purpose of implementing or supporting the existing systems. Third, there was continuous mention of the IT Department in organizational newsletters signed by the Strategic Management. This was one of the reasons why most functional departments did not directly decline the IT Department’s requests and maintained a good basic relationship with it. They possibly believed that if they declined, then they would be reported, or the IT Department would take some kind of reprisal against them, possibly through the Strategic Management.

The improved image and the perceived authority of the IT Department had relatively less influence over the clinical specialists and consultants than workers, partly because they had better access to strategic organizational information. The clinical specialists and consultants had considerable amounts of power and authority and were aware that Strategic Management would not upset them, in which case, their positions would remain unchanged irrespective of their cooperation with the IT Department, although most did try to help on requests from the IT Department. However, although the positions of the consultants and clinical specialists remained the same, their credibility and value to their subordinates, as sources of knowledge, would depreciate due to the availability of other and sometimes superior sources of information online. Some felt threatened by this and others were a little wary about it. On the other hand, eventually, consultants were going to lose some of their positional authority as their subordinates were going to be monitored by Strategic Management and were to perform standard work routines (organization-wide) using the intranet applications and not those designed with reference to the preference of clinical consultants. Workers may have simply begun to see the consultants primarily as their local specialists but with their power and authority weakened.

## Episode 4: reproduction of domination structures – organizational workers

This episode shows the reproduction of domination structures involving workers. The existing domination structures were reproduced when senior stakeholders involved in the Project withheld more sensitive and strategic information from workers, mainly to prevent the latter from resisting by using their collective power rather than by using excessive authority to compel them. Furthermore, the existing structures of domination were used, where workers were put under an implicit rather than explicit obligation by Strategic Management to try out the intranet as a new tool in a bid to be more liberal.

A careful and liberal approach was taken to release different amounts of project-related information available at different levels of the organizational hierarchy during the IS implementation process. These can basically be divided into strategic and operational levels. Lowerlevel workers were given access only to operational information, the management and supervisory staff were given access to more strategic level information, which was often seen as sensitive and was associated with power. A lower-level worker (SC) noted that, We were only told about it when they came to install the program on our machines that we could use this software that we have to get used to.

In contrast, although they had access to information about the Project, some line managers expressed little interest in accessing it. When a line manager (WV) was asked about the amount of information available to them initially, he replied, Did anyone know, or care, that Intranet tools were being purchased? In part, this may have reflected the ‘trickle down’ of what was considered to be important information about the usefulness of the system. The same line manager also noted that, Little information cascaded] about why/what/how. The potential use of the system needs to be more explicit. Another line manager (CP) was asked about the level of information given to her and her Department during IS implementation, and she replied, I was told about it but wasn’t sure what it’ll [intranet will] do. Indeed, the IM&T Manager’s (CC) view was that, PM [IT Manager] was very much the ideas man. He convinced RH [Contracts Director] that we needed to go forward. Together they convinced KT [the Director of the Directorate]. This group of people initiated the Project.

Withholding strategic information concerned with future plans and IS-related change was a measured approach for ensuring that the IT Management retained, and gained, authority. Organizational management at all levels used its skills and ability to prevent sensitive information from reaching workers and causing unnecessary reprisals. Lower-level workers were entitled to receive only basic operational or functional information. Workers in most departments were conscious of the fact that their superiors were withholding more specialized information, such as the IS-led change, where they may be monitored more closely in the future. Increasingly, although they were not sure how the intranet would benefit management, they remained suspicious and cooperated only when they really had to.

## Episode 5: reproduction of domination structures – IT management

This episode shows the reproduction of domination structures, involving IT Management. It shows how IT Management, with the help of Strategic Management and other individuals, deployed legitimating tactics together with appropriate authority to overcome resistance from line management and workers. The achievement of legitimation required deployment of many resources and a high degree of communication. Seeking legitimation was a more democratic alternative to repression and compulsion.

Furthermore, although most workers eventually welcomed the changes, some remained highly resistant to the implementation of the intranet. This manifested itself in denial of the usefulness of the intranet, stories of potential intranet misuse by employees and a threat of collective action coordinated by trade union representatives. Importantly, a closer reading of the reasons behind the resistance of these workers pointed to a group of line managers who denied subordinates access to the new intranet and generated stories of difficulties in using the new system and the extra workload that it would create. The subtext was that the status quo in which these line managers remained gatekeepers to organizational information was best. Moreover, although not stated explicitly, implicitly, the freedom and power bases created by these line managers could remain intact, and their influence over employees also could remain unchallenged.

However, line management’s ‘passive resistance’ was minimized by a direct appeal by Strategic Management to organizational workers at large, to be open to the intranet. Furthermore, line management was required to encourage its subordinates to accept the intranet while justifying its benefits through the coercion of the more powerful Strategic Management. The Human Resources (HR) Department also helped to facilitate a number of discussion groups on making the best use of technology in organizations and justifying its benefits. A lower-level IT worker (PE) said during the IS implementation process, Staff/management discussion groups are in place to discuss and resolve potential conflict situations. On the other hand, a line manager (AB) during the IS implementation process said, Discussions involving stakeholders are ideal, but personally I would have reservations regarding outcomes, for example, emancipation from constraints.

The IM&T Manager visited different departments to convince workers to use the intranet. For example, the IM&T Manager said, Change has been difficult due to the resistance of both clinical staff [who often eschewed involvement in tasks which they saw as not within their remit] and general IT ignorance of line management. There were small ‘pockets’ of enlightenment from the younger end of the age spectrum. Another way to keep the resistance to the minimum was to restrict the information available to general organizational workers. For example. we asked a line manager (CP) about her reflections a few months after the implementation of the Project about resistance to the intranet implementation. She denied resisting and said, We didn’t know enough about it to resist. Now that I’ve seen it, I think it was a good idea to implement it. I try to encourage my staff to use it. But another line manager (MH) said, Everybody really wanted to know about it. But they were a bit frightened.

## Episode 6: production of legitimation and domination structures – IM&T manager

This episode shows the production of legitimation and domination structures, involving the IM&T Manager, who after many attempts succeeded in obtaining approval of workers to accept the intranet as a new tool and use it in their daily work. It shows that in achieving this, some help was sought from the HR Director and several pro-technology line managers. This approval, by using a careful and measured approach, improved the domination of IT Department workers, especially the IT Manager. The IM&T Manager was asked after the implemen tation about what she did to encourage the workers to accept the system. She replied that, A lot of talking, showing by example. It takes a lot of time and ‘patience’. Having email was a status symbol. Having a laptop with remote access was the ultimate. [For example] The Trust Board all got laptops. When the IM&T Manager was also asked about how she approached line management and what their initial reaction was, her response was, A lot of it was the ignorance. In the beginning, I used the OD [Organizational Development] approach to get people to identify projects. She negotiated with line managers to allow the IT Department to implement solutions, such as the intranet and to encourage workers to make a fuller use of IS She was further asked about the part played by line management in getting workers to accept the system. She suggested that, It varied, but was not very good. But they did open the doors for you. If they saw the benefits, they’d open the door for you. Furthermore, an important element of her strategy was to win the support of employees, who could in turn also influence line management.

Success in this Project would have meant that the IM&T Manager would gain better recognition and appreciation. She acted to safeguard her domination and that of the IT Manager and other IT workers, as securing their domination, at least on the face of it, was important for maintaining her own credibility. Success in getting line management to support the intranet project at earlier stages would have meant that she could also get a chance to participate in the city-wide IS strategy formation group. Participation in this group would result in higher internal and external recognition. She faced various challenges in relation to line management such as their fear of IS, making assumptions that the intranet would involve too much time and effort, or simply reluctance because they had had unpleasant previous experiences of using systems. It was difficult for her to persuade those who were not willing to change. But she continued to use a carefully measured approach of convincing workers and explaining the importance of the intranet in meeting the needs of the workers as elicited in the Information Needs Survey and the fact that Strategic Management had already approved the Project. Her main tactics were to highlight the benefits of the intranet to line management, such as increasing their divisional efficiency as well as showing how it could enhance their direct and indirect control over their divisions. Furthermore, successful implementation of the internet would result in better strategic recognition for the IT Department; hence, the chances of its receiving more funding would increase.

## DISCUSSION

In MedService, the IT Manager initiated and managed the Project involving the acquisition of the intranet. His aim was to increase his domination and credibility along with that of his Department within the organization. In the past, many line managers and general workers had resisted IS initiatives, resulting in a minimal use of technology throughout the organization. His ultimate aim was to acquire more responsibility and freedom to initiate and manage IS solutions in the organization. This increased his importance in the organization and gave him greater say strategically, which is a form of power and domination (Jasperson et al., 2002).

The IT Manager, with the help of the IM&T Manager and the Contracts Director, identified various groups of line management and organizational workers over whom he wished to gain more domination. In order to achieve this, he had to obtain more resources from Strategic Management as well as the freedom to implement IS solutions. Gaining more resources for implementing IS and being in charge of IS provision throughout the organization also gave him even more enhanced recognition and domination over his departmental IT workers. However, he realized that line management and workers possessed different types of powers and domination, such as medical or clinical expertise. This is similar to the findings of Nadan (1997) who found that different types of power are possessed by different types of workers.

The IT Manager noted that the worker agreement and collaboration required a measured approach to domination. Workers were the bulk of the organization, and it meant that they could resist changes to their job with relative ease, given the past events in the organization, and in a manner that would be difficult to control given the array of resistance strategies which might be deployed. The IT Manager was aware that the intranet could easily have been rejected as it was not a necessity in functional tasks of the majority of workers. So the IT Manager considered his activities very carefully and sought the advice of his colleagues in achieving the approval of these stakeholders. So despite being powerful and having even been granted new authority by Strategic Management, he had to justify and legitimate his actions before making IS-related decisions which would affect others.

## CONCLUSION

In this article, we aimed to explore the usefulness of the domination and legitimation dimensions of structuration theory for the evaluation of the dynamics of IS implementation. In order to undertake this research, we investigated the increase of domination of key stakeholders during IS implementation of the intranet in a UK NHS organization. To this end, we evaluated the actions and thoughts of key stakeholders.

First, we argued that the results validate the use of structuration theory as a framework for the evaluation of IS implementation and, additionally, show how domination structures are produced and reproduced during IS implementation and, thus, authority is pursued and secured by the IT Management. Furthermore, the results of our longitudinal case study suggest that the success of IS implementation depends on the actions of the IT Management. More specifically, in order to improve, the senior stakeholders have to legitimate their actions, as legitimation is linked to domination. For example, there was a need for the IT and IM&T Managers to assess when and how to produce new structures and when to reproduce old ones.

As the IS implementation project unfolded, our research findings highlighted the thinking and action of IT workers. The IT Management refined its strategy, responded to contextual factors and, in particular, worked in a politically astute manner. For example, the legitimation imparted to the IT Management by lower-level employees through their acceptance of the intranet and the benefits associated with it reduced the likelihood of their challenge to the IT Department’s domination. These actions were necessary not only because potential sources of resistance changed as the Project progressed but also because of the distributed nature, and different forms, of power and political action across the organization. Moreover, the focus on the domination and legitimation aspects of structuration theory has allowed us to evaluate the distributed leadership and influence of specific stakeholders. To restate, the work o scholars such as Lawrence & Low (1993) suggests that effective leadership and careful planning of IS implementation-related activities by senior developers and management can result in IS success. Therefore, within an IS implementation context, our findings suggest that these and other socio-psychological contextual factors appear to be at least as important as the technical processes, given the distributed nature of power within the organization and, thus, the distributed potential to resist the new system. Our use of structuration theory in looking at ways to undertake successful IS implementation will be of value to IS practitioners along with IS researchers who wish to develop people-centred strategies in support of IS implementation.

Furthermore, our use of structuration theory along with longitudinal interpretivist research methodology can be categorized in terms of what Lee & Baskerville (2003) refer to as ‘theory to empirical’ (TE) generalizability. They define this as: . . . the generalizability of a theory to a description of the results that the practitioner would observe if he was to use the theory in a new setting – i.e. a setting other than the one(s) where the theory was empirically tested and confirmed – is arguably the most important form of generalizability in business school research (p. 237).

Therefore, we have made several academic contributions in this research by: (1) exploring the use of domination and legitimation in IS implementation; (2) operationalizing struturation theory in a practical setting over a longitudinal period; and (3) revealing the complexity involved in social interactions and the dynamics of power and domination within it.

However, there remain many possibilities for future research in this area. We were struck by the difficulties and opportunities presented by unintended consequences. By the latter, we are not concerned with those areas often linked to contingency planning but are more concerned with outcomes of a ‘changed’ system, or in structuration theory terms, a new structure, which yields unanticipated benefits or difficulties. For example, in this research, the intranet representatives were appointed to help create intranet homepages and provide lower-level skills training. However, their frequent involvement with employees within their departments mean that they became regarded as local experts, whose power and influence increased because o their perceived expertise.

Although these intranet representatives were supportive of the IT Department’s strategy, and were able to help those unfamiliar with the new system, potentially, they could have, or could in the future, decline to provide any assistance to the employees or the IT Department, given that many of the requests for help fell outside their job description. We suggest that the nature of IS implementation is likely to yield many unintended consequences such as this, which could reveal much about the dynamics of IS implementation and its socio-psychologica context, such as new sources and configuration of resistance or the gatekeeping emerging from new system-related change. Such emergence of uncertainty resulting from unintended consequences warrants further in-depth study.

## Biographies

Zahid Hussain has a BA in Management, an MSc in HRM, an MSc in IS, an MA in Learning and Teaching and a PhD in IS. He has a research background in information systems development in the UK National Health Service and has worked for several private organizations as a technical consultant. Currently, he teaches Information Systems at the University of Bradford where his research interests include the application and development of IS, IS planning and relevance of social behaviour approaches in looking at IS use in organizations, Some of his recent publications/accepted paper are in: Information & Manage ment, Journal of Applied Behavioral Science, Journal of Engineering and Technology Management; Journal of Organizational and End User Computing, Behaviour & Information Technology and Journal of Information Science.
