## a b s t r a c t

The prevalence of cyber crimes has threatened the business model enabled by email. Users have to evaluate email related risks before forming their attitude and read intention toward commercial emails. Drawing on a seminal theoretical framework in risky decision making, we propose a research model that incorporates computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception as in<sup>fl</sup>uential in cultivating commercial email attitude and read intention. The research model is empirically validated using survey data and the results provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant support. This study contributes to the literature on email use by exploring the process of risky decision making and in<sup>fl</sup>uence sources.

## 1. Introduction

Since the inception of Internet, email has served as a cost-ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism that supports and enables interactions between online business and consumers [26]. Firms rely heavily on emails for online marketing campaigns, business services, and commercial transactions. Jupiter Research has estimated that email marketing spending will hit \$1.1 billion by 2010 [48]. Email however is subject to an increasing level of threats introduced by cyber crimes [28]. Cyber crime is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “illegal computer mediated activities that often take place in global electronic networks” and it includes Internet fraud, computer hacking, network intrusions, and virus attacks [15]. The limitations in technical design, security provisions, and legal solutions render email an ideal attack vector for cyber crimes of all sorts. Attackers abuse email systems to propagate fraudulent information, steal individual privacy and <sup>fi</sup>nancial data, and spread malicious codes. End users may be discouraged from participating in the email-based information sharing and communication, endangering the business models such as email marketing that emails enable. Despite the active engagement of online vendors in email marketing, the end users may choose to discard these emails. Statistical data from Bronto indicates that the opening rate of commercial emails for reading is only 28.9% [48]. The majority of emails are deleted by recipients before they are processed, in part due to the concerns on email threats [7]. Therefore, in order to retain email as a cost effective channel for marketing and advertisement even with the threats of cybercrime, it is important to understand how email recipients form a positive perception of commercial emails in a risky environment.

Our study attempts to address the research question: “given a risky Internet environment what shapes users' attitudes toward commercial emails and their intention to read them?” Drawing upon Sitkin and Pablo's theoretical framework in risky decision making [63], we collect empirical data to uncover the antecedents of commercial email attitude and read intention. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that user intention to read commercial emails is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affected by users' attitude toward commercial emails, and that computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception are the key determinants of commercial email attitude. The effect of computer risk taking propensity is strengthened when the user has a higher email risk perception. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the antecedents of computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception are sensation seeking, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

User perception and interaction with commercial emails is an underdeveloped research topic in the information systems discipline [77]. Considering the threats imposed by cybercrime in recent years, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to apply theoretical frameworks from risky decision making literature to understand commercial email attitude and read intention. This research makes a twofold contribution. First we validate and extend Sitkin and Pablo's theoretical framework for decision making under risks within the context of commercial email processing. Second, we identify the important antecedents (e.g., computer risk taking propensity, email risk perception, and sensation seeking) to risky decision making in the context of emails. The study also sheds light on the development of managerial strategies that help retain email as a cost effective business communication channel.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the theoretical background of this research. Section 3 develops the research model and a set of hypotheses. Section 4 describes the research methodology including survey instruments and data collection procedure. Section 5 presents the results based on partial least squares analysis (PLS). Section 6 discusses our contributions, implications, limitations, and directions of future study. We conclude the paper in Section 7.

## 2. Conceptual background

Nowadays, users' decision regarding the use of emails for commercial information sharing and communication is characterized as being risky. The fact that criminals utilize emails as attack vectors exposes email users to threats such as computer viruses, Internet fraud, and identify theft. In this context we de<sup>fi</sup>ne decision risk in email use as the extent to which there is an uncertainty of potentially negative outcomes of decisions in using emails.

Recent risk literature has proposed theoretical referent models to understand risky decision making behavior. Prospect theory [36] suggests that positively labeled situations (domain of gain) result in risk averse behavior whereas negatively labeled situations (domain of loss) lead to risk-seeking behavior. March and Shapira [46] suggest that when conditions are perceived positively, decision makers will focus on the opportunities and tend to be more risk seeking. Staw et al.'s[66] threat-rigidity hypotheses suggest that the presence of threats results in risk-averse behavior.

Sitkin and Pablo [63] postulate that these <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be synthesized by accounting for the risk propensity of decision makers. Based on an integrative analysis of these existing theories and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, Sitkin and Pablo [63] develop one prominent model to understand decision making under risk. It posits that risk taking propensity and risk perception are the two determinants of risky decision making behavior. Risk taking propensity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual's tendency to take or avoid risks [63]. Individuals with high risk taking propensity are likely to favor risky decisions and engage in risky activities. Risk taking propensity is considered as an individual trait that may change over time and thus is an emergent property of the decision maker. Meanwhile, risk perception is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an individual's assessment of how risky a situation is in terms of probabilistic estimates of the degree of situational uncertainty, how controllable that uncertainty is, and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in those estimates [63]. Risk taking propensity has a main effect on risk behavior, which is moderated by risk perception.

Sitkin and Pablo's model has had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the literature of decision making under risk [19,51,61,81]. Its empirical validation is however scanty with few exceptions such as [37,38,64]. Much of the research model has yet to be tested and new research is warranted to fully examine its potential and validity as a useful theoretical foundation. Risky decision making model of Sitkin and Pablo has been earlier proposed in the context of organizational behavior. In this study, we extend the model to a new context — email processing under risk. We utilize the model as the theoretical underpinning to explore the formation of user attitude toward commercial emails and their intention to read commercial emails.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 1 illustrates the research model for the examination of email processing under risk. Drawing on risky decision making literature, we propose that computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception are the imperative determinants of an individual's attitude towards commercial emails, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences his or her intention to open and process them. Further, email risk perception positively moderates the effect of computer risk taking propensity on email attitude. We also propose that personal traits such as sensation seeking impacts computer risk taking propensity and that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and individual email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy jointly shape email risk perception.

## 3.1. Commercial email attitude and intention

Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [21] and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [2] suggest that an individual's attitude predicts his or her behavioral intention and that behavioral intention determines whether the individual will carry out a given behavior. In this study, attitude refers to an individual's positive evaluative effect about commercial emails and intention refers to his or her willingness to open and read the emails received. In line with TRA and TPB, we hypothesize:

H1. Attitude towards commercial emails positively impacts intention to read commercial emails.

## 3.2. Computer risk taking propensity

We anticipate that computer risk taking propensity has a positive relationship with the attitude towards commercial emails. Prior studies have found that risk taking propensity varies across situations and various risk taking propensities have been de<sup>fi</sup>ned in domains such as business, physical, and health [9,60,64]. However, there exist no prior studies that focus on user risk taking propensity in the use of computer systems. In the current research, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne computer risk taking propensity as an individual's tendency to take or avoid risks in the use of computer systems. Computer risk taking propensity may be assessed as one's tendency to engage in computer related activities that are likely to incur risks. The core principles of information security suggest that engagement in computer systems may leave individuals vulnerable to threats to information con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, integrity, and availability [20]. Con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality is the property of preventing disclosure of information to unauthorized individuals; using of computer, individuals may be attacked by malicious codes or hackers and scammed by Internet fraud, leading to the leakage of personal data and business <sup>fi</sup>les. Integrity refers to the fact that data cannot be modi<sup>fi</sup>ed without authorization; computer system intrusions however frequently leave sensitive data <sup>fi</sup>les altered and deleted. High availability systems aim to remain available at all times; virus attacks, among others, challenge the computer systems in providing uninterrupted service. Individuals with high computer risk taking propensity are likely to favor risky decisions and engage in risky activities pertaining to email use [63]. Therefore, they are likely to hold a more favorable attitude towards commercial emails when compared with users with low computer risk taking propensity. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. Computer risk taking propensity positively impacts attitude towards commercial emails.

## 3.3. Sensation seeking

Prior risk literature suggests that risk taking propensity may be linked to factors that are trans-situational such as personality. Prior studies have developed a repository of personality related constructs in relation to risk propensity. Foremost is the sensation seeking construct that has been found to be particularly important [67]. Sensation seeking is a personality trait of a need for physiological arousal, novel experience, and a willingness to take risks to obtain such arousal [83,84]. Sensation seeking has been found to be associated with risky decision making in the scenarios of illicit drugs [50], reckless driving [29], and alcohol use [65]. Individuals exhibit stable differences in whether they prefer or disdain risks. And thus, sensation seeking is a personal preference for risks and this risk oriented disposition may act as a good predictor of risk taking propensity in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of hazard context. Users who enjoy the challenge that risks entail will develop a high tendency in taking computer risks whereas those who do not will become more declined to take computer risks. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. The extent of sensation seeking positively impacts computer risk taking propensity.

## 3.4. Email risk perception

Email introduces considerable risks as they are frequently employed by malicious parties as attack vectors to spread malicious codes such as virus, worm, mal-ware, spy-ware, and Trojan horses. Further, emails may be forged to deceive message recipients for fraud and consequently lead to identify theft in the forms of phishing and pharming. We expect email risk perception has a negative relationship with attitude towards emails. Risk perception may lead decision makers to deny uncertainties in email use, to overestimate or underestimate risks from email threats, and to exhibit unwarranted con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their judgments of email processing. It is common for a user who is using email systems to be reluctant to open incoming emails because the sense of risk may be overwhelming. This perception of uncertainty in the email usage makes users hesitant to rely on email for commercial information sharing and communication. Recent studies have provided some support to this relationship [52,54]. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4. Email risk perception negatively impacts attitude towards commercial emails.

We also argue that computer risk taking propensity in<sup>fl</sup>uences the relative salience of cybercrime threats and thus leads to biased perception of email risks. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, computer risk taking propensity may not only limit the information an email user attends to but also his or her ability to notice and respond to the risky attributes in email messages. This is consistent with previous work that has de<sup>fi</sup>ned risk perception in terms of situational labeling and estimates of the extent and controllability of risks [4,34]. As a consequence, users with low computer risk taking propensity are likely to attend to and weight potentially negative outcomes more heavily than positive outcomes, thus overestimating the probability of loss relative to the probability of gain in using emails. Conversely, users with high computer risk taking propensity may attend to and weight positive opportunities more heavily, thus overestimating the probability of gain relative to the probability of loss in email usage [46]. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5. Computer risk propensity negatively impacts email risk perception.

We expect that email risk perception reinforces the association between computer risk taking propensity and email attitude. By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, risk propensity in<sup>fl</sup>uences decision making only when risk is present. When a user does not perceive much email risks, the prediction power of his or her computer risk taking propensity on email attitude may not be salient. When the user is challenged with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant perceived risks in using emails, however, his or her computer risk propensity may largely explain his or her email attitude. That is, users with a low computer risk propensity tend to demonstrate more risk aversion behavior as perceived email risk rises whereas those who enjoy computer risks may show riskier behavior as their perceptions of email risk increases. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6. Email risk perception reinforces (positively moderates) the relationship between computer risk taking propensity and attitude towards commercial emails.

## 3.5. Social influence

We posit that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence plays an important role in forming email risk perception. Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which users believe that “important others” would approve or disapprove of their behavior [2,33]. Prior literature has presented various conceptualizations and associated operationalization of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. A dominant conceptualization is subiective norm: social influence is driven by social norms that de<sup>fi</sup>ne acceptable behavior for the group or society to which an individual belongs. That is, strong social norms will result in high level of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Another conceptualization draws upon Social Information Processing Theory (SIPT) to suggest that information conveyed via individuals' social networks in<sup>fl</sup>uences their cognition and behavior [23,62]. Lewis et al. suggest that the effect of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence manifests through the psychological pathways of internalization and identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [44].

We expect that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence will affect an individual's belief about the risks embedded in emails. Compelling messages received from important others such as family members are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence one's cognition about the expected risky outcomes in email use. When social in<sup>fl</sup>uence is entrenched with the beliefs that email risks are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, users are likely to hold a similar perception. On the other hand, users may consider email risk low when his or her social network believes so. Gardner and Steinberg found that individuals are susceptible to peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence in risky situations [24]. We hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7. The level of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on email risks positively impacts email risk perception.

## 3.6. Email screening self efficacy

Social cognitive theory (SCT) underlines the important role of cognitive factors in individual attitude and behavior [6]. Self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is the belief about one's ability to perform a particular task. It in<sup>fl</sup>uences behavior choices, the effort and persistence exerted, and the mastery of the behavior [17]. Email recipients are very often confronted with decision making on whether or not to open and read incoming emails. The focal decision problem in this scenario is the assessment of potential risks introduced by email threats. Extending the ideas of self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in SCT, we propose that the self conviction that one can accurately assess email risks will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the risk assessment in decision making. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne such self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy tailored to email processing as email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy.

Users rely on a variety of cues to assess email risks. Prior email security research <sup>fi</sup>nds that users may employ simple cues such as email sender ID and message title in risk assessment [75]. Users however differ in their perceived ability to exercise these decision strategies. They may vary along the dimensions of magnitude, strength, and generalizability in email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Bandura pointed out that “the outcomes one expects derive largely from judgments as to how well one can execute the requisite behavior” [5]. Users' perception in their own ability to detect malicious emails is therefore pertinent to their perceived email risk. Those who perceive high email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy tend to believe reduced probabilities of being attacked and hence decreased risk perception. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 8. Email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy negatively impacts email risk perception.

## 3.7. Control variables

We consider several control variables in our analysis. Prior literature has found that gender may in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' risky decision making [35,69]. Thus gender is controlled for its impacts on computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception. Studies have shown that age may affect individuals' risky decision making [24,47]. Age is also controlled for its impact on computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception. Existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings in email research <sup>fi</sup>nd positive impact of perceived email bene<sup>fi</sup>t on email attitude [76]. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>nally email bene<sup>fi</sup>t is controlled for its impact on email attitude.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measurement development

Most measurement items for the principal constructs in this study are adopted directly from existing measures in order to enhance validity [68]. They are adapted into the current context and are shown in Appendix A. Among the adaptions, we measure sensation seeking through Likert scale questions instead of “True/False” questions developed by Zuckerman [85]. To maximize the variance, recent studies have transformed the original measurements of Zuckerman (i.e., SSS-V) from “True/False” into Likert scales. Studies have found that Likert scaled measurements are reliable to capture the sensation seeking disposition of individuals. For example, Hoyle et al. tested a subset of the SSS-V in Likert scale and found that the associations of Likert-based measures with variables such as drug and alcohol use and various risk and protective factors were at levels consistent with those based on the original measurement of sensation seeking. In his recent publication [3], Zuckerman also changed the measurements of sensation seeking, included as part of the Zuckerman–Kuhlman–Aluja Personality Questionnaire, from “True/False” to Likert scales. As Zuckerman put, “a change in response format from true–false to a Likert scale might improve them and increase effect sizes” [3; pp.147]. And therefore we believe that the use of Likert scale measures will not bring in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bias. The preliminary instruments were pilot tested for comprehensiveness and clearness following Churchill [16]. Content validity of the instrument was assessed by an expert panel of eight experienced IS researchers. The survey instruments were pretested with 30 undergraduate students to check the psychometric properties of the measurement scales. Following these pretests, the measurement instrument was shortened, re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and validated for its statistical properties. Since the current study explores users' decision making in commercial email processing, we provide instructions de<sup>fi</sup>ning commercial emails as “commercial emails refer to emails coming from sources other than personal acquaintances (friends, family, peers)” in the survey.

## 4.2. Survey administration

The study was carried out with undergraduate students taking the course of “Introduction to MIS” at a major university in the northeastern U.S. The subjects were considered appropriate for the study due to their regular use of e-mail for business communication. Since this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst required MIS course for all undergraduates majoring in business administration, the undergraduate students in this class came from a variety of business majors such as marketing, <sup>fi</sup>nance, accounting, human resource, operations, and MIS. The fact that the respondents came from diverse background creates an inbuilt heterogeneity. Potential respondents were mailed a copy of the research description with a cover letter from the class instructor encouraging their participation. Among the 400 invitations distributed, 134 usable responses were obtained. Respondents were guaranteed both con<sup>fi</sup>- dentiality and access to the aggregated survey results. All participants received extra grade points and \$10 gift cards redeemable at the university coffee shop.

The average age of the respondents was 21.3 years, ranging from 19 to 50 years. On average the participants had used computers for about 10 years. 60% respondents subscribed to listservs from which they received e-mails on a regular basis. When asked about online commerce activities for which records of transactions were sent through e-mail, 11.4% respondents indicated that they participated very often, 67.4% participated sometimes, while 21.2% never participated. When asked about giving out their e-mail addresses to shopping or advertising avenues from which information is sent on regular basis, 8.27% respondents had never done so, while 91.7% had occasionally or often done so. Regarding their level of skills on Internet and e-mail related activities, respondents indicated an average of 5.2 (standard deviation: 1.15) for e-mail and 5.5 (standard deviation: 1.04) for Internet on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from none to extensive.

## 5. Analysis results

We test the research model using partial least squares (PLS) analysis which employs a component-based approach for estimation and places minimal restrictions on sample size and residual distributions. PLS is best suited for testing complex relationships by avoiding inadmissible solutions and factor indeterminacy [13]. PLS also supports exploratory research. Our paper studies an important yet understudied research perspective that is email processing under risk; PLS is therefore appropriate.

Since the data was collected through surveys, we check for the extent of common method bias [56]. First, we use Harman's one-factor test in which all of the variables in our study are simultaneously entered into an exploratory factor analysis and we examine the unrotated factor solution to determine the number of factors that are necessary to account for the variance in the variables. Common method bias exists when one single factor emerges or when one factor accounts for the majority of the covariance among the variables [57]. The results show that all constructs explain roughly equal variance and not a single factor accounted for the majority of the covariance, inferring no common method bias. Second, a partial correlation method is used in which the variable that accounted for the most variance from the factor analysis is entered as an additional independent variable [57]. This variable does not create a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change in the variance explained in the dependent variables. Finally, the correlation matrix (Table 1) does not indicate any highly correlated factors, whereas common method bias will result in extremely high correlations (rN .90) [55].

## 6. Discussion

Email exempli<sup>fi</sup>es one technology that is endangered by the prevalence of cyber security threats [82]. In our examination of email systems use, we argue that users form beliefs about emails within a broad milieu of in<sup>fl</sup>uences emanating from the individual and social contexts. Drawing upon the literature in decision making under risk, we argue that two sources are likely to create a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence: computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception. These two sources are postulated to not only have direct impacts but also interact with each other in developing attitude toward commercial emails.

Further, we argue that sensation seeking, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and email screening self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are the important antecedents to computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception.

Through empirical validation we obtain signi<sup>fi</sup>cant support for the proposed research model. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that computer risk taking propensity encourages email attitude while email risk perception depresses it. In addition, the impact of computer risk taking propensity is partially mediated through email risk perception in that risk propensity reduces an individual's email risk perception. Further, email risk perception is found to positively moderate the relationship between computer risk taking propensity and email attitude. Besides, we <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that users with higher sensation seeking are more likely to form high tendency in accepting computer related risks. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence one receives from important others as well as his or her personal believes in own ability to detect potential hazards in email messages will in<sup>fl</sup>uence email risk perception.

This paper contributes to information systems and risky decision making literature. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings evidence the important role of risk taking propensity and risk perception on risky decision making. When risk taking propensity is concerned, prior literature has generated mixed results with respect to its impact on risky decision making behavior. Sitkin and Weingart [64] and Keil et al. [37,38] found no empirical support, while other recent studies <sup>fi</sup>nd some support [41,70,79]. The mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be due to research designs and data collection processes. Sitkin and Weingart [64] and Keil et al. [37,38] followed scenario-based experimental design where subjects assumed decision making roles and addressed hypothetical decision problems. Such design may be prone to (1) oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>cation of the decision options and (2) introduction or segregation of extraneous in<sup>fl</sup>uences from the environment, resulting in limited validity and generalizability [37]. Our study employs survey methodology within the context of email use (which is a relevant and daily activity of all participants). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer con<sup>fi</sup>rming results that highlight the theoretical importance of risk propensity in decision making. The development of computer risk taking propensity in the study is of high relevance and interest to IS researchers as well [37]. Prior literature also offers limited understanding of the interplay between risk taking propensity and risk perception. It is argued that the potential interaction between the two theoretical construct will be the key to reconcile the divergent viewpoints of prior risk decision research [63]. Empirical evidence that is for or against it is however not in existence. In the context of email use, we collect empirical evidence that reveals a significant positive interaction effect. thus adding value to the decision making literature and allowing the development of a more comprehensive model that predicts decision making under risk. Second, the current study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the important antecedents to computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception. They capture the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individual, problem related characteristics, and environment in forming an integrated and accurate model that explains risky decision making. These antecedents may be applicable to studies that examine consumer decision making when risks are present in online products and services [27,42].

The study also has a number of practical implications. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and social in<sup>fl</sup>uences impact the perception of email risk signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. To encourage the continuous adoption and increasing use of emails for commercial information, public awareness and education campaigns may be exercised [30]. Campaigns as such are likely to enhance users with their skills to detect and mitigate cyber security threats, contributing to increasing email screening self ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Meanwhile, campaigns may also help remove the cognitive bias pertaining to email and facilitate the cultivation of fair and accurate social in<sup>fl</sup>uence on email risk. Further, the results suggest that online user attitude towards email is affected by individual computer risk taking propensity which in turn impacted by personal trait such as sensation seeking. Users differ in their levels of risk tendency [40]. Managers of email marketing services may therefore consider the attributes of the target population when they develop customized marketing strategies [45]. Email marketing may be an option for customers who are likely to possess high computer risk propensity but may not prove effective to population who are the opposite. Email marketing providers are therefore encouraged to analyze the customer information such as personality through business intelligence tools when conceiving marketing campaigns [11].

This paper is subject to a number of limitations that create opportunities for future research. This study is a survey-based research using college students as research subjects. Compared with the general consumers, college students may have less motivation to process commercial emails. Most respondents of this study have online purchase experience and have opted in for commercial communication, suggesting satisfactory motivation. Nevertheless, future research can employ more representative samples and examine populations other than university students (e.g., general online consumer) so that the external validity of the results can be improved. Field-observed data and behavioral logs may also be utilized to overcome the limitation of survey-based method. Next, the current research <sup>fi</sup>nd that sensation seeking explains a limited portion of the variance of computer risk taking propensity. Future studies may consider other theoretical predictors such as personal inertia and outcome history that pertains to computer attacks [63,64]. However predictor such as outcome history may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure and the responses could be highly skewed due to the lack of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency in detecting computer attacks. Further studies should carefully address challenges as such. In addition, we did not study a number of other reasons which may explain the users' reluctance to open and read commercial emails. A 2008 Mediamark Research and Intelligence report found that 32% of U.S. adults are adverse to advertisements on any media and thus ad-aversion may affect individuals' intention to read commercial emails [39]. Prior studies also suggested that information overload may discourage the users in email processing [76]. Future studies may expand the current research model by considering these issues.

## 7. Conclusion

The prevalence of cyber crimes has challenged information assurance on end user computing [8]. Online users have grown cautious in their judgment and participation into Internet related activities [31,32]. Email and its enabled e-commerce models such as email-marketing are hence at stake. Research efforts that shed light on online consumer email decision making is therefore important. To this end, the paper develops and validates a framework that models email processing under risk. The framework pinpoints the major source of in<sup>fl</sup>uences as computer risk taking propensity and email risk perception, and distinguishes their individual contributions to the formation of consumer risky decisions. The framework further con<sup>fi</sup>rms a set of antecedents to these sources including sensation seeking, email screening self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. In doing so, this paper generates new knowledge to explain how online users perceive and evaluate emails that keep losing credibility when they are abused by the malicious attackers.
