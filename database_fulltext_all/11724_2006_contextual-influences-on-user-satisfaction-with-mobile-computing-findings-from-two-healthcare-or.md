# Contextual influences on user satisfaction with mobile computing: findings from two healthcare organizations

Rens Scheepers<sup>1</sup>, Helana Scheepers<sup>2</sup> and Ojelanki K. Ngwenyama<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Victoria, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Caulfield School of Information Technology, Monash University, Victoria, Australia and <sup>3</sup>School of Information Technology Management, Ryerson University, Toronto, Canada

## Abstract

Mobile information technologies (IT) are transforming individual work practices and organizations. These devices are extending not only the boundaries of the ‘office’ in space and time, but also the social context within which use occurs. In this paper, we investigate how extra-organizational influences can impact user satisfaction with mobile systems. The findings from our longitudinal study highlight the interrelatedness of different use contexts and their importance in perceptions of user satisfaction. The data indicate that varying social contexts of individual use (individual as employee, as professional, as private user, and as member of society) result in different social influences that affect the individual’s perceptions of user satisfaction with the mobile technology. While existing theories explain user satisfaction with IT within the organizational context, our findings suggest that future studies of mobile IT in organizations should accommodate such extra-organizational contextual influences. ## Introduction

From the earliest studies following the introduction of computing in organizations to the more recent studies on user satisfaction, information system (IS) researchers have examined how new computing devices can affect users’ behaviours and how such behaviours affect the organization. A contemporary challenge is to understand and explain users’ satisfaction with mobile computing technologies, nomadic work practices enabled by these devices (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002) and their impact on organizations. Mobile technologies challenge our current theories of user satisfaction due to their usability within multiple contexts. Historically, we have based our understanding of user satisfaction on fixed computing devices situated within a single context: the organization. The mobility of these devices and their multiple contexts of use add several dimensions to the user satisfaction and organizational adoption problem. In this research, we question how individuals’ satisfaction with mobile computing is influenced by the multiple contexts of use afforded by these devices.

In a longitudinal study of the implementation of mobile technologies in two healthcare organizations, we found that private, professional, organizational, and societal contexts all influenced individual user satisfaction with mobile technology. Based on these findings, we argue, the conceptualization of user satisfaction should be extended in the case of mobile technology use in an organizational environment.

The following sections of the paper provide a review of the literature on user satisfaction, an outline of our research methodology and a description of the two case studies. We then present the results and discuss the implications of our findings for the conceptualization of user satisfaction.

## User satisfaction in organizational contexts

The last few decades have seen the emergence of two dominant streams of research in the area of individual adoption of IS within the organization: (a) user satisfaction (e.g., Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Ives et al., 1983; Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988; Doll et $a l . ,$ 2004); and (b) technology acceptance (e.g., Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1992; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Initially, user satisfaction was defined as, ‘the extent to which users believe the IS available to them meets their information requirements’ (Ives et al., 1983). However, we adopt the more encompassing definition of user satisfaction as based on the users’ evaluation of their overall experience with the system (cf. Lucas et al., 1990; Chin & Lee, 2000). User satisfaction has been operationalized by a set of measures to assess broader aspects of organizational IS effectiveness (e.g., quality of content and systems design, IS success and effectiveness) (Melone, 1990; DeLone & McLean, 1992; Seddon, 1997; Khalifa & Lui, 2004; Wixom & Todd, 2005). On the other hand, the technology acceptance stream seeks to explain and predict individual users’ acceptance of technology, linking behaviours to attitudes and beliefs (such as ease of use and usefulness). The acceptance stream has largely focused on voluntary use scenarios (i.e., situations where the user has some degree of discretion in terms of the adoption decision) (Brown et al., 2002). In mandatory use situations, users often have no choice but to use the system; hence, actual use behaviour may not be a good reflection of their perceptions of the system. In such circumstances, it has been argued that user satisfaction is a more appropriate reflection of users’ overall contentment with the system (Lucas et al., 1990; Melone, 1990; Brown et $a l . ,$ 2002). Recently, efforts have focused on integrating user satisfaction and technology acceptance models to harness the advantages of both these streams of research (e.g., Wixom & Todd, 2005).

In this paper, we investigate influences on user satisfaction with mobile devices that emerge from use contexts outside of the organization. A number of studies have introduced aspects related to the use of technology by mobile users in different contexts (cf. Yoo & Lyytinen, 2005, special issue of Information & Organization). Such studies have described the use of mobile technology in environments such as the office, car, and home (e.g., Cousins & Robey, 2005) for both work and personal purposes (see also, Davis, 2002; Jarvenpaa et al., 2005; Lu et $a l .$ , 2005). These multiple contexts in which users engage with mobile technology (within and beyond the organization) generate new research questions. Given current conceptualizations of user satisfaction, we ask, how do extra-organizational contextual influences (cf. Pettigrew, 1985) contribute to users’ satisfaction with mobile computing? Chin & Lee (2000) argue that users’ prior expectations and desires (which could be attributable to extra-organizational contexts) influence satisfaction. At present there is, however, no explicit approach for studying these within the current conceptualization of user satisfaction. Some technology acceptance researchers suggest that social influences beyond the organizational context (professional peers, family and friends, computer use at home) can impact individual acceptance of technology (Compeau et al., 1999; Karahanna et $a l . ,$ , 1999). Many researchers attribute these influences to volitional use of technology; but, are such extra-organizational influences manifested in mandatory use situations? The importance of addressing attributes of the focal technology in theorizing has been established (e.g., Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). Mobile computing technology use (mandatory or voluntary) transcends the organizational context and calls upon researchers to revisit underlying assumptions about how individuals evaluate such technologies.

## Research methodology

Our longitudinal case study research method included interviews, participant observation and standard case study techniques. This research method is especially appropriate to new topic areas as it allows for an indepth description of the relationships in a particular situation (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 2003). Further, this intensive inquiry allowed us to give close attention to the individual use contexts of the mobile devices and the underlying logic of the users’ assertions (Abbott, 1990). The data were collected between 2000 and 2003. We conducted an initial pilot study in July 2000 followed by a formal baseline study and subsequent rounds of intensive interviews.

We adhered to the basic criteria of (a) triangulation; (b) systematic gathering; and (c) reliable recording and transcription of data to ensure the validity of the empirical observations (Klein & Myers, 1999). To enable triangulation, we selected the interviewees based on the ‘sampling for heterogeneity’ criteria (Johnson, 1990; Miles & Huberman, 1994) as illustrated in Table 1. We use the pseudonyms SlimCo and NurseCo to protect the identities of the companies and participants. The first round of formal interviews was held in September– October 2000 at SlimCo and April–May 2001 at NurseCo, before the implementation of the mobile technology. Follow-up interviews (mostly with the same interviewees) were held between December 2000 and February 2001 at SlimCo and between September and December 2001 at NurseCo, after the mobile technology had been implemented and used for some months. In addition to formal interviews, we used participant observation to obtain anecdotal data about the adoption process. Mintzberg (1979) underlines the value of such anecdotal data: ‘While systematic data create the foundation for our theories, it is the anecdotal data that enable us to do the building. Theory building seems to require rich description, the richness that comes from anecdote.’ (p. 587). One of the researchers interacted with members of both organizations on an informal basis by means of regular e-mail and phone contact and through participation in discussions and implementation meetings at the organizations. Brief informal follow-up interviews were also conducted in 2003. From these informal modes of data collection, a diary of field notes was maintained in which the various interactions with the case organizations were detailed.

## Interview procedures

The interviews were semi-structured and comprised of open-ended questions derived from the implementation literature, user satisfaction literature, prior research studies, and the unique technological aspects related to mobile technology (such as the wireless infrastructure and organizational support for mobile computing applications). For this purpose, we developed pilot pre- and post-implementation interview scripts.

The initial pilot interview scripts were tested in a number of interviews in one of the case organizations and were subsequently refined for clarity. The preimplementation script was used for the initial formal baseline study and served as a basis for reference in the post-implementation interviews. In most instances, interviews were transcribed and shared with the interviewees to correct possible errors and omissions (Yin, 2003). The duration of a typical pre-implementation interview was between 15 and 30 min. The post-implementation interviews each lasted between 45 min and 1 h.

## Data analysis

The data analyses consisted of a thematic analysis (Miles & Huberman, 1994) followed by a detailed content analysis (Weber, 1985) of the interview data. During the thematic analysis we classified individual responses according to specific themes. Initial themes were developed from the extant literature (examples included: use experiences with the mobile technology, experiences of the implementation, and satisfaction statements). Further themes emerged about the users’ experiences with the mobile technology; in particular, themes attributable to contexts of use. In the content analysis, we further analysed individual responses from the postimplementation interviews.

The coding scheme for the content analysis was based on the eight steps prescribed by Weber (1985). We defined the recording unit as an individual response on a particular issue, ranging from a few words to a number of sentences (as long as these dealt with the same, single issue). We used qualitative data analysis software (Atlas.ti) for the content analysis. Various features of the analysis software, including the so-called ‘word cruncher’ (which ranks the frequency of words in the text), ‘search swarms’ (to locate text patterns) and queries (to analyse code combinations in the data set) assisted our systematic analysis of the data. Two of the authors coded the interviews separately and then compared the coding results to verify reproducibility (Weber, 1985, p. 16). During the content analysis, each statement was assessed within the context in which it was said and then coded accordingly by the authors.

## Two cases of mobile IT implementation

The two case organizations are similar in many respects. Both are Australian service-based organizations that operate in the healthcare sector, rely on personal relationships in their service delivery, and both seek to harness mobile computing to enhance the delivery of these services. Although the organizations each applied different mobile computing devices, the software applications on these devices provide essentially the same functionality: both support users’ personal productivity and communication with the office while they are off-site. In both cases, the management mandated the use of the mobile technology.

## NurseCo

NurseCo is a non-profit organization located in Melbourne, Australia. The organization has 1200 employees and provides home health services to the community. A pilot project was run in 1999 to replace paper-based processes. A formal requirements specification and systems development process followed. In late 2000, the first NurseCo centre launched the implementation.

The wireless handheld system allows patient appointment schedules and clinical data to be downloaded to the handheld computer. The system also allows updates to a central patient IS. The information provided to the nursing staff on the handheld is more comprehensive than the previous paper-based system. In addition, the turnaround time for changes to the patient information and the scheduling time of consultations were reduced.

NurseCo focused mainly on organizational use of the technology. All software and hardware that would enable the downloading of private information were removed from the mobile device.

## SlimCo

SlimCo is a small organization based in Melbourne, Australia that specializes in weight loss. The organization’s aim is to provide doctors with a weight loss program that allows them to retain a supervisory role over each patient’s progress. SlimCo consultants visit clients at their home for consultation. In early 2000, SlimCo decided to implement mobile devices to support their consultants during consultations, for their weekly and monthly reporting, and for invoicing. In October 2000, they embarked on a training program where the device was introduced to the consultants. The mobile system could record all transactions made by consultants. On a daily basis, these transactions were synchronized with the central database at head office. Information about marketing activities during the week was synchronized weekly through e-mail. The managing director of SlimCo actively encouraged private use of the mobile device.

## Results

In this section we present the results of the thematic and content analyses.

## Results of the thematic analysis

During the thematic analysis, we found themes (content, accuracy, format, ease of use, and timeliness) consistent with user satisfaction constructs pertaining to use of mobile technology in the organizational context. Additionally, we also detected certain themes in the interview data that called for a refined interpretation. In both case studies, the influence of extra-organizational contextual influences on user satisfaction became evident. In SlimCo, the influence of personal use contributed to consultants’ perceptions of overall satisfaction with the technology. In NurseCo, the context of the nursing profession (and nurses’ loyalty to their profession) influenced individual expectations about the inevitability of the technology in transforming ways of working. Some nurses reported that the implementation of mobile technology was ‘part of being professional,’ while others were concerned that the technology would distract them from their main duty of caring for patients. For both cases, we also identified broad societal influences on the adoption and assimilation processes in order to ‘keep up with the technological age’ and to follow suit with other organizations that use technology to improve service delivery.

## Results of the content analysis

In the following section, we outline results from the content analysis that illustrate how extra-organizational contextual influences (private, professional and societyat-large), identified in the thematic analysis, combine to shape users’ overall satisfaction with the mobile technology. Examples of statements in the interview data that are attributable to these influences are given in Table 2.

The analytical software was used to generate the summary results from the coded post-implementation interview data as shown in Table 3. Interviewees were asked to rate their overall satisfaction level with the mobile system on a scale of 1 (very low) to 10 (very high). We arranged these responses into the following groups: mostly satisfied (6–10), indifferent (5), and mostly dissatisfied (1–4). Table 3 summarizes their response in this regard and provides the number of statements relating to the organizational and extra-organizational contexts (for the post-implementation interviews conducted in both case organizations).

Overall, we analysed the content of 39 post-implementation interviews. Of the NurseCo interviewees, 21 (68%) indicated that they were overall mostly satisfied with the mobile system. In the SlimCo case, seven out of the eight respondents stated they were mostly satisfied with the system. On average, the SlimCo interviewees made more positive than negative statements about the mobile system, whereas the opposite was true of the NurseCo interviewees. An interesting difference can be noticed in the number of statements made by the interviewees relating to extra-organizational contexts. SlimCo interviewees made 19 statements about private use, whereas NurseCo interviewees only made four such references. This can be attributed to the emphasis placed on private use in the SlimCo case. NurseCo interviewees made 51 statements within the professional context, whereas there were only three made within this context in the SlimCo interviews. On average, the number of statements relating to society-at-large was similar in the two cases (approximately one statement per interviewee).

Using the content analysis software, we drew comparisons between respondents’ expressed overall satisfaction with the mobile system and the number of positive or negative statements made in the post-implementation interviews. A total of 17 interviewees (12 in NurseCo, 5 in SlimCo) indicated they were overall mostly satisfied with the mobile system and made more positive than negative statements about the system. Eleven interviewees (10 in NurseCo; 1 in SlimCo) indicated an overall dissatisfaction or indifference to the system; this was also reflected in the occurrence of more negative than positive statements about the systems.

Using the content analysis software, we analysed why, despite the large number of negative statements about the system (especially in the NurseCo case – see Table 3), the majority of users stated they were mostly satisfied with the system. In particular, nine interviewees in NurseCo and two in SlimCo made more negative than positive statements about the system within the organizational context, yet still expressed their overall satisfaction with the system. Noticeable among the eleven interviewees in this category was that they all made one or more statements pertaining to extra-organizational contexts during their interviews. This indicates that perceptions about their satisfaction with the technology within the organizational context were conflated with those pertaining to extra-organizational contextual influences. Hence, even if some were critical of their organizational experience with the technology, their expectations and desires were influenced by extraorganizational contexts. For example, the nine NurseCo interviewees all associated their organizational use of the technology with professionalism in nursing care.

## Discussion and conclusion

Our findings show that in the case of mobile computing (even in mandatory use scenarios), user satisfaction is judged by users on more than the use of the technology within the organizational context. Seemingly counterintuitive results (such as when users reported an overall satisfaction with the technology despite expressing numerous negative sentiments) may be explained by taking into account extra-organizational contextual influences.

While our findings do not suggest a wholesale rejection of user satisfaction constructs, we argue that the influence of extra-organizational contexts should be considered in studies of mobile technology. Our results show that the varying social contexts of individual use (individual as employee, professional, private user, and as member of society) result in different social influences that can affect the individual’s perceptions of user satisfaction with the technology. A richer conceptualization of the interrelated layers of contexts that influence an individual’s overall evaluation of mobile computing is depicted in Figure 1.

As mobile technologies continue to pervade the organizational computing landscape, it will become necessary to conceptualize user satisfaction more broadly. Compared to traditional business computing, specific consideration should be given to all the relevant individual use contexts that are pertinent to mobile technology. This is because the physical mobility of the technology and the new contexts of individual use, by way of mobility, demand a more dynamic conceptualization. These additional contexts of use shape users’ conceptualization of the technology itself (and also of their use experiences).

In this regard, Chin & Lee (2000) propose that satisfaction pertains to users’ experience of a system, but also their expectations and desires. We argue that such expectations and desires emanate from multiple contexts. In combination, these have an effect on users’ overall perception of the technology. It should, however, be stressed that distinctions between these contexts are only of analytical relevance. From the viewpoint of the individual, the different contextual influences are typically conflated, even within a single interview.

In this study, the physical mobility of the devices (specifically their small size and wireless connectivity) enabled usage behaviours not commonly associated with more traditional forms of computing. Some of the interviewees mentioned that they would take the mobile devices with them everywhere, discovering new uses in a range of situations. Such evidence support the argument that the specifics of an information technology (IT) are necessary considerations in research studies (cf. Monteiro & Hanseth, 1995; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001).

As depicted in Figure 1, our findings suggest that rigid analytical delineations between mandatory and voluntary use of mobile computing should be considered carefully (i.e. consistent with Karahanna et al., 1999). Even in a mandatory use scenario in the organizational context, nuances could exist in terms of concurrent voluntary use pertaining to other contexts. In this respect, the influence of extra-organizational contexts can explain seemingly contradictory individual responses within the organizational context when individuals refer interchangeably to these contexts.

When comparing the individual responses to user satisfaction, the two case organizations drew on different use contexts. SlimCo management actively encouraged private use of the mobile device to leverage the overall satisfaction of the technology within the organization. This was reflected in the SlimCo user responses that related to a broader scope of use (including voluntary private use) than the mandated use reported by the NurseCo interviewees. The incorporation of private use seems to have strongly influenced the user satisfaction at SlimCo. This contrasts NurseCo’s more traditional approach to user satisfaction that focuses only on the organizational use of the technology. Despite NurseCo’s focus on the organizational context, individuals still related the technology to their professional and societal contexts. The negative statements in the organizational context were attenuated in many interviews by the perceived professional and societal relevance of mobile technology. Thus, both cases illustrate the potential merits of harnessing these extra-organizational influences. The fact that most of the SlimCo respondents made mention of private use during their interviews underpins the merits of explicit communication about extra-organizational implications of the technology during implementation.

The study has two main limitations. First, the mobile computing devices in this study hold the potential for extended use. This limits the practical implications of this research in terms of application context because some mobile technologies do not typically offer the option of use outside the organizational context. Second, the two cases incorporate influences that pertain to healthcare professionals. Further research is needed to assess extra-organizational influences on the perceptions of individuals toward mobile technologies in other sectors. We suspect that our findings will be applicable in other professional contexts, especially where loyalty to the profession is prominent.
