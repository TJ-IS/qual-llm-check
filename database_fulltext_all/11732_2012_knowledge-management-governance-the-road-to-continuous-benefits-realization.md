## Abstract

Investment in knowledge management (KM) programmes is often contentious due to the challenge of meeting the need for continuous and sustainable benefits realization. In KM, the word ‘sustainable’ describes how programmes of strategies to leverage organizational knowledge remain productive over time: that they deliver strategic value to the organization. The focus of this paper falls on the constructs of the governance of KM and on its leadership, in KM strategy development and implementation, including risk management, financial controls and transparent evaluation mechanisms for continuous benefits realization. This paper presents a KM governance model and explores its recent validation through six case studies of large, distributed, multinational organizations, and through these introduces the granular constructs of KM governance. Exploration of the impact of KM governance on developing and implementing KM programmes in each case study organization demonstrates that there is a clear nexus between strategic KM governance and benefits realized from those programmes.

## Introduction

n information-based economies, knowledge is a key organizational resource and competitive differentiator (Grant, 1996; Teece et al., 1997). Thus the management of organizational knowledge is an imperative for forwardlooking strategic leadership.

Following Alavi and Leidner (2001) and Alavi et al. (2006), we define KM as the basic processes of creation, storage/retrieval, transferring/sharing, and applying knowledge to fulfill organizational aims and objectives. Benefits gained through such strategies are diverse and can include: improved decision making, support for knowledge retrieval, improved communication between staff, improved information about and for customers; benefits in workflow, quality and productivity; improvements in innovation and business opportunities; expansion in organizational and personal knowledge bases and finally financial outcomes in volume of sales, profit margin, competitive advantage and improvements in cash flow (Seeley and Dietrich, 2000; Davenport, 2005; Alavi et al., 2006). However, many KM programme implementations are troubled by obstacles to ongoing benefits realization (DeLong and Fahey, 2000; Chua and Lam, 2005; Kulkarni et al., 2006). Taking a knowledge-based view of organizations (Grant, 1996; Teece, 1998; Eisenhardt and Santos, 2002; Reus et al., 2009), it follows that the capacity to effectively utilize the relevant knowledge of employees to achieve organizational aims and objectives depends on deliberate ongoing investment in leveraging organizational knowledge. While funding such initiatives may continue, their value is frequently questioned as they fail to realize the expected benefits – the return on investment (ROI) from KM sought by organizations (Swart and Kinnie, 2003; Desouza and Raider, 2006; Agrawal and Zyngier, 2009). Organizations will only continue to invest in programmes to manage organizational knowledge where certain long-term strategic ROI is delivered (Murphy and Simon, 2002; Kelleher and Courtney, 2003; O’Dell and Leavitt, 2004; Desouza and Raider, 2006; Braganza et al., 2007).

Sustainability is the capacity to endure. In KM, the word describes how systems, tools and techniques to leverage organizational knowledge remain productive over time:

that they deliver strategic value to the organization (Zack and Street, 2007); ensure that knowledge resources are managed effectively and used appropriately (Holsapple and Joshi, 2000; Husted and Michailova, 2002; Smits and deMoor, 2004); and control and minimize risks to the KM strategy so that it can be revised in response to perceived flaws in its capacity to transfer knowledge effectively (DeTienne et al., 2004; Scott et al., 2004; Chua and Lam, 2005). However, there is rarely an established and systematic mechanism to monitor and demonstrate such benefits from KM programmes.

Governance is the implementation of authority through a model that ensures delivery of anticipated or predicted benefits of a service or process in an authorized and regulated manner (Karake, 1995; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999, 2000; Weill, 2004). Therefore, the governance of KM can be a transparent instrument for the authorized, strategic alignment of KM strategy, and for rendering the predicted benefits from implementing KM tools and techniques. Transparent governance is also linked to organizational mechanisms, which enable the delivery of KM value to organizational stakeholders (Braganza and Lambert, 2000).

Earlier research has examined the fit between KM governance and generic governance structures (Dilnutt, 2002; Smith and McKeen, 2003a; O’Dell and Leavitt, 2004; Wiig, 2004a; Schroeder and Pauleen, 2005; Onions and de-Langen, 2006; Peltokorpi and Tsuyuki, 2006; Strong et al., 2008). However, that work does not provide an empirical analysis to demonstrate a granular analysis of KM governance structures or of its practical implications. The earlier research neither identified the constructs of KM governance that were applied to the roles and tasks required, nor described the flow of authority and communication. Further, no research has been conducted on either the overall application patterns of KM governance as an applied management tool to achieve long-term benefits from implementing KM strategy, or on the constructs of supporting KM governance structures. These matters, therefore, are an underdeveloped theoretical area with the potential for significant implications for practice.

This paper derives from a case study phase of research to gather in-depth field data subsequent to survey research on uptake and understandings of KM. This research has studied how the relationship between KM authority and organizational mechanisms supported the realization of continuous and sustainable benefits from KM. This paper investigates how the constructs of KM governance can operate at the highest level to overcome obstacles in developing and implementing KM strategy, and how these constructs are relevant to the success or failure of KM programmes in a long term. While other research has focused on KM processes, tools and techniques (Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Tiwana, 2002), this paper focuses on the nature of leadership in a KM strategy. In particular, it considers how governance intersects with leadership. This research capitalizes on theoretical contributions from both corporate and IT governance literature and reflects on their relevance to KM strategy development and implementation. The concept of ‘KM governance’ is explicated and defined as the collection of explicit and systematic functions and constructs to ensure the delivery of anticipated or predicted benefits of KM strategy development and implementation in an authorized and regulated manner (Zyngier, 2005). The outcome of KM governance is to transparently ensure the development of policies to align KM with the strategic aims of the organization. KM governance controls how, where and by whom KM policies or principles are developed and implementation decisions are made. KM governance also ensures the identification and management of risks in KM strategy implementation. Therefore, KM governance defines the tasks and authority for the mode of delegation of responsibilities, as along with reporting and measurement structures, which ensure benefits are realized from investment in KM.

In order to study KM governance as a tool for sustainable KM strategies, the following research proposition was investigated:

Preposition 1: The formal governance of KM strategy development and implementation contributes to the sustainability of strategies to leverage and transfer knowledge to achieve organizational goals.

This paper presents the constructs that constitute the KM governance model and relates it to the literature analysis in other approaches to KM strategy implementation. We also describe the empirical case study research through which these constructs were validated.

This paper is structured as follows. In the next section, the major issues in managing organizational knowledge – issues of leadership, obstacles to ongoing and sustainable implementation of strategies – are discussed as part of a review of the relevant literature. The review supports our argument for governance as a structural mechanism and a process for ensuring KM benefits realization and sustainability. As a result, the constructs of KM governance are derived in relation to the organizational governance, and the internal roles, tasks and relationships, which lead to achieving its purpose. The paper provides case study evidence demonstrating that organizations that fit the proposed KM governance model have realized effective and continuous benefits from KM strategies. Whereas those that fail to apply principles of KM governance may not have achieved the same results. Finally, the paper summarizes the research contributions and limitations, and draws out proposals for future research.

## KM strategy

A knowledge-based perspective on the organization clarifies an understanding of the role and value of employees tacit knowledge in organizational value creation (Eisenhardt and Santos, 2002). It supports the view that individuals and groups of individuals contribute to the knowledge of the organization in its capacity to create new knowledge: employees become central to the operations and strategic decision making of the organization rather than external or alienated forces (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Hendriks, 2001; Turvani, 2001; Eisenhardt and Santos, 2002; Zack and Street, 2007). Others argue that effective management of explicit and tacit knowledge increases the effectiveness and profitability of an organization (Davenport and Prusak, 2000; R. Davenport, 2005; Anantatmula and Kanungo, 2006; Sharma et al., 2007).

Such management requires developing, implementing and maintaining the appropriate organizational structures, human resource management and technical infrastructures, to enable access to the required knowledge where it is needed for effective organizational operation.

There is a broad range of tools and techniques that can be used in KM (Butler and Murphy, 2007), each meeting a perceived organizational need (Earl and Scott, 1999; Amidon and Macnamara, 2003). Organizations put forward a KM strategy that aims to achieve benefits from better leveraging its knowledge resources. Regardless of the specific tools and techniques utilized, KM implementations continue to be troubled by obstacles and fail to realize the benefits sought, especially in a long term (Argyris and Scho¨n, 1996). Failure of KM strategies implementation can lead to a loss of confidence and reputation, and, as a result, to a permanent loss of trust in KM practices in an organization. Strategic planning processes fail when they are not constructed to understand, internalize and synthesize the successes or failures of the strategic process as it is implemented (Mintzberg et al., 1998). Thus, deliberate KM strategies must be articulated as processes, structures, policies and mechanisms that are transparent and coherent, and which allow systematic review and revision in the light of obstacles and successes encountered in their implementation. Recent research confirms these as internal obstacles to the effective implementation of KM (Barth, 2000; Braganza and Mo¨llenkramer, 2002; Chua and Lam, 2005; Riege, 2005; Zack et al., 2006).

## KM benefits realization

Strategic initiatives aim to bring advantage to the organization in a long term. These benefits are usually defined in a business case and translated into a business plan. Benefits realization requires that the outputs and outcomes of a strategy are explicitly and strategically aligned with organizational aims and objectives, and those benefits should be defined at the planning stage.

Reported benefits of KM range widely and reflect the varying needs of organizations across industry sectors. McElroy (2003), Jennex and Olfman (2004), Ryu et al. (2005), and Tainio et al. (2001) suggest that KM increases organizational capacity for enhanced staff learning and development. Others see KM as a means of stimulating and supporting process and product innovation, and innovative client service (Seeley and Dietrich, 2000; Georgopoulos, 2005; Davenport, 2006). Best practice replication, access to corporate memory, and the reuse of knowledge resources is a motivation for many organizations (Walsh and Ungson, 1991; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Spender, 1996; DeTienne et al., 2004; Oshri et al., 2008). Some see benefits derived from breaking down or straddling departmental silos (McDermott and O’Dell, 2000; Wenger et al., 2002; Swart and Kinnie, 2003; Spender, 2006). The deficit in this recital of organizational KM benefits is that these are usually described as desirable outcomes rather than as an explicit intention in the planning process. Critical success in systematic and regular realization of these benefits remains elusive.

KM strategy leadership and its role in organizational strategy A KM leader develops and implements KM strategy, manages knowledge resources and is equipped with skills that enable and empower knowledge sharing activities (Amidon and Macnamara, 2003; DeTienne et al., 2004; Lakshman, 2007; Zhang and Faerman, 2007). A KM leader investigates need, and strategically aligns planning and execution of activities to realize the organization’s value proposition and mission. The need for KM leadership has been identified as one of the critical success factors of KM programmes (Zyngier and Burstein, 2004; Heisig, 2009). It has been suggested that, as part of the business strategy, KM leaders should propose and lead the KM strategy implementation which aims to deliver a planned process of identifying, acquiring, developing, sharing, distributing and using knowledge (Holsapple and Joshi, 2000, 2002; Amidon and Macnamara, 2003; Lakshman, 2007; Burstein et al., 2010). The strategy development process is informed by the nature of the work, organizational knowledge needs, available knowledge resources in people and artefacts, available KM tools and techniques, the environment, and the organizational culture (Kemp et al., 2002; Burstein et al., 2010).

Practitioner evidence suggests that knowledge managers sometimes find themselves isolated, are the sole champions of a KM strategy, and are victims of competing forces between IT/IS departments, HR departments and Information Management departments, each of which claim the KM mantle (Zyngier, 2002; Chua and Lam, 2005). At the same time, leadership is an essential element of an organizational model implementing a KM strategy. In most KM initiatives, employment of organizational leaders is time-bound. Individual successes may be linked to the energy and charismatic personality of a leader (Lakshman, 2007). Evidence suggests that if a KM initiative is implemented as a ‘one-off’ project, then employees may revert to prior knowledge transfer behaviours that do not necessarily support organizational knowledge needs (Schroeder and Pauleen, 2005, 2007).

Common KM leadership responsibilities reflect Mintzberg’s (1994) classical view of the manager as a figurehead, disseminator of information, spokesperson and decision maker (Peterson, 2004). However, management techniques alone fail to provide appropriate mechanisms to ensure systematic benefits realization from KM. The problem does not lie with the roles of the individuals who lead the KM strategy. We therefore propose that:

Preposition 2: There may be a deficit in those organizations that lack executive level KM governance mechanism to create policies, exercise authority over KM, manage KM risks, and evaluate and review KM strategy implementation outcomes to ensure their long term sustainability

## KM strategy and its governance

Governance is an ongoing mechanism to authorize, moderate and improve practice in order to realize strategic benefits (Weill, 2004). Use of the term ‘governance’ varies. In this paper we refer to governance of business processes with IT as the most visible form. Comparing IT governance and IT management, Peterson (2004) argues that there is clear distinction between the two in that IT management relates to the ‘efficient and effective supply of IT services and products, and the management of IT operations, [whereas] IT governance faces the dual demand of both contributing to present business operations and performance, and transforming and positioning IT for meeting future business challenges’ (p. 37). IT governance is the centre of decision-making authority, an executive model to deliver the expected benefits of an IT or IS strategy (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999; Hirschheim and Schwarz, 2003; VanGrembergen et al., 2004; Weill, 2004; Weill and Ross, 2004). However, IT governance is differentiated from KM in that the management of knowledge requires the analysis of people, processes and technology.

In applying the same principles to the management of knowledge, this research seeks to define decision-making rights and responsibilities, align strategy, risk management, performance evaluation and measurement in accord with business needs to deliver financial value or similar expected benefits.

Linked to the strategic goals, the mission and the values of the organization, then the KM strategy must support the work and transfer of knowledge in the organization. This should be reflected in the policies that underpin KM strategy development and implementation. It is the role of governance to establish policies for this purpose. Following what is learned from corporate and IT governance, it can be suggested that authority, financial accountability, risk management, measurement and evaluation, and benefits realization need to be the aspects of KM governance.

## Prior research into KM governance

Wiig (1997) appears to be the first to use the term ‘governance’ in relation to KM, suggesting governance functions as a mechanism to monitor and facilitate knowledge-related activities in organizations. However, Wiig (1997) did not specify the constructs for KM governance implementation, or provide any supporting empirical evidence. There is some evidence of research into KM governance that looks at the configuration of those governance structures (Dilnutt, 2002; Smith and McKeen, 2003b; Frost and Morner, 2004; Mahnke and Pedersen, 2004; O’Dell and Leavitt, 2004; Wiig, 2004b; Schroeder, 2005; Peltokorpi and Tsuyuki, 2006). Ciborra and Andreu (2001) refer to knowledge governance; however, this terminology is interchangeably used with KM to describe the construction, development and management of the strategy. However, none of these authors include detailed research linking any theoretical attributes of governance of KM in the roles and tasks required of it, nor of the flow of authority and communication within it.

Much research mention the importance of strategic issues of measurement (Bose, 2004; Smits and deMoor, 2004; Lavergne and Earl, 2006; Mehta et al., 2007; Yu et al., 2009) and others analyse the appropriateness of organizational governance and its relationship to knowledge (Foss, 2007; Foss et al., 2010). In those studies the underlying theme has been leadership and the importance of knowledge to the organization as a whole, and how corporate governance mechanisms influences knowledge processes.

A recent study links KM governance to success (Kannabiran and Pandyan, 2010), although there is little indication of detailed research furthering theoretical attributes and constructs of KM governance as described and defined by Zyngier (2005). Therefore we find that no existing theory provides the answer as to whether governance is a means of achieving sustainability of a strategy to leverage organizational knowledge. As such, theory testing is not possible and we find that the constructs and the theory must be developed. This paper links the constructs of KM governance to the roles and tasks involved, and to the flow of authority and communication within KM governance to the realization of benefits from KM.

KM governance is compatible with a knowledge-based view of the organization within a knowledge-centred, valuebased, network economy (Menkhoff et al., 2005; Braganza et al., 2007; Foss, 2007; Heisig, 2009). KM governance confirms, through development of KM policies, the alignment of KM with the value proposition and strategy of the organization. KM governance assumes setting up explicit and transparent access conditions to organizational knowledge: quality and maintenance procedures; decisionmaking processes; and means for resolving KM obstacles. KM governance holds authority and is the mechanism that delegates authority and consequential responsibility. It is an executive model for delivering, in a controlled manner, the expected benefits of strategy implementation. Authority is the power or right to give orders, to make decisions, and to enforce these; whereas responsibility is the obligation to do to produce expected outcomes as part of one’s job or role and be answerable for those outcomes (Little et al., 1973). Hence KM governance is also concerned with authority and its delegation.

In the next sections the constructs and responsibilities of KM governance are defined and described in detail, together with the processes and tasks involved to operationalize KM.

## KM governance: an examination of constructs

The development of an understanding of the constructs of KM governance draws on prior research that identifies authority, policy development, risk management, measurement and evaluation, and financial accountability as issues to be addressed (Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999; Hirschheim and Schwarz, 2003; Peterson, 2004; VanGrembergen et al., 2004; Weill, 2004; Weill and Ross, 2004).

KM governance assumes an effective understanding of the role and the potential of KM within the organization, and the alignment of KM with the value proposition and organizational strategy. KM governance establishes the decision-making authority, and an executive model to realize benefits of the strategy (Zyngier, 2005). It enables the delegation of authority for KM planning, KM strategy development, and KM strategy implementation. Through establishing checks and balances, this authority is responsible for knowledge-based benefits realization in the mode of service delivery. Moreover, the associated executive model ensures that evaluation feeds back into the KM strategy implementation review and that all stakeholders’ knowledge needs and expectations are sufficiently met. This authority can then establish risk management processes to be conducted in a controlled manner through service delivery.

This view of governance falls within an organization or user-centred (problem-finding) view where the problem area is organizationally and therefore contextually specific. Policy development is a construct of KM governance are statement of principles and guidelines that set KM objectives the organization wants to achieve. Policy guides decision making, direction and specifies requires outcomes (Weill, 2004; Weill and Ross, 2004). Therefore, KM policy strategically aligns KM processes and business processes and can be a basis for assigning responsibilities for KM strategy development and implementation. Measurement of success or otherwise in achieving those objectives can therefore be measured. Policy can be reviewed and revised in accordance with measurement and evaluation, as internal and external environments change, and for the management of risk.

Management of the risk and obstacles to effective KM have been well described and the need for risk management in KM is generally acknowledged (Probst et al., 2000; O’Dell and Leavitt, 2004; Chua and Lam, 2005; Lakshman, 2007). Proper consideration of knowledge issues associated with these risks and their management or resolution acts to strengthen strategies to manage knowledge within the organization. KM governance ensures management of both cultural and structural risks associated with KM as they arise during both initiation and ongoing implementation of the KM strategy (Jennex and Zyngier, 2007; Zyngier, 2008).

A major risk to the development and implementation of KM strategies continues to be sustainable fiscal viability. This relates the level of financial support for developing and implementing the KM strategy to the value realized by the organization as a result of strategy implementation. Based on prior empirical research, financial accountability is recognized as a challenge to KM (Chua and Lam, 2005; Zyngier, 2007). Financial accountability forms part of managing KM risk, and is a construct of KM governance.

KM governance implies and demands explicit measurement criteria. Thus, KM governance processes incorporate evaluation and measurement mechanisms in order to assess the incremental value or otherwise from KM strategy implementation, and to facilitate review existing practices in the light of outcomes achieved. Anantatmula and Kanungo (2006) surveyed and confirm a number of criteria that have been used to establish the value and ROI for KM strategies: These include human capital growth (Liebowitz and Suen, 2000; DiVanna and Rogers, 2005); Spender (Spender and Tsoukas, 2006), Sveiby (Sveiby, 1998), the Edvinnson and Malone (Edvinsson and Malone, 1997) measure intellectual assets; Kaplan and Norton (Kaplan and Norton, 2001) use the Balanced Scorecard with a number of measures including financial, growth, customers and internal business processes. Probst et al. (2000) suggest looking at the strategy’s normative, operational and strategic goals to see if they are being met. Other common techniques include simple staff retention measures and improved quantity and quality of ‘product to market’ delivery (Vanthournout, 2006). All such measures should be carefully assessed and selected for their appropriateness to the KM policy, and based on specific organizational needs and context. In all cases, such measures are expected to form a part of KM governance.

Evaluation looks at both successes of, and obstacles to, implementing a strategy (DeLong and Fahey, 2000; Chua and Lam, 2005; Zyngier, 2007). Evaluation of obstacles to the KM strategy implies the capacity to question why the risk may not have been foreseen, and therefore managed using an alternative method. KM governance presents transparent and consistent measures of KM outcomes and outputs against criteria established as part of strategy implementation. Evaluation and measurement facilitates the regular review, approval and monitoring of ROI from KM, each of which is central to ensuring systematic benefits realization.

## The KM governance model: synthesis

From a meta-review of methodologies and frameworks intended to aid the sustainable implementation of a KM strategy in an organization, representative research was selected to gain a general overview of the field. Among others, we analysed the seminal work of Holsapple and Joshi (2002), the European Committee for Standardization of the European Union (2004) who synthesized 10 such frameworks and the investigations of 113 individual KM researchers and Heisig (2009) who examined 160 frameworks. We found that none of these frameworks explicitly describes KM governance as a structural mechanism for authorizing activity to achieve a strategic alignment through policy, for delegating authority, or for managing risks to a sustainable KM programme. In addition, none of these approaches explicitly spells out the constructs of the concept of ‘management’ in relation to knowledge. Nor do they define the roles for the leaders as part of a governance mechanism. In particular, they omit the need for systematic policy development, risk management, and revision and evaluation of strategy implementation. However, as argued earlier, based on comprehensive literature analysis, this is essential for long-term benefits realization.

The earlier empirical research, and recent analytical examination, has led to the development of a KM governance model for KM strategy implementation that fills the aforementioned gaps (Zyngier et al., 2006; Jennex and Zyngier, 2007). This research has developed constructs that form the KM governance model depicted in Figure 1. This figure indicates how a KM governance environment can support the development and implementation of a sustainable KM strategy in all its levels to realize strategically aligned benefits. Governance impacts at the levels of documentation, systems and processes; however, this paper develops the theoretical premise of KM governance rather than looking only at specific systems levels. The shaded area indicates main area of this research focus.

The model reflects three fundamental objectives for KM sustainability:

\- strategic value to the organization (Zack and Street, 2007);

\- knowledge resources are managed effectively and used appropriately (Holsapple and Joshi, 2000; Husted and Michailova, 2002; Smits and deMoor, 2004); and

The KM governance model synthesizes these objectives. These are shown on the model and in the key to Figure 1 as follows:

1. The KM governance body or function establishes KM policies which align with the aims and objectives of the organization. These policies define the delegation of authority and accountability for KM activities, manage the risks, and evaluate the implementation of the strategy, according to the outputs or other value-added benefits as measured against criteria established as part of the policies’ objectives.

2. The interaction between KM strategy development and KM governance is twofold. Firstly, the governance process develops the principles and rationale for the impetus of the strategy, the management of risks, financial duty of care and control, and accountability for stakeholder response. Internal stakeholders includes several areas including IT, organizational behaviour, economics and organizational strategy, Human Resources and Information Management departments, and other user groups that are engaged in knowledge-based practice (Arnott and Pervan, 2005). Secondly, the governance process provides mechanisms to evaluate KM activity according to defined and articulated performance measures as directed by internal and external stakeholder expectations.

3. KM strategy implementation follows a course of action that is determined by the plan in order to support the aims and objectives of the organization as a whole. The KM strategy is normally implemented by specialized KM staff, or those performing the roles as required and defined by KM governance. KM governance should be supported and promoted by ‘champions’ in the organization. Evaluation data are generated by the staff responsible for KM strategy development and implementation. The data are supplied to the governance body that then ensures its respective decisions are considered in the subsequent review and refinement of the strategy.

Therefore, a KM governance model provides for the management of risk, review mechanisms, and fiscal accountability in leveraging tacit knowledge and sharing explicit knowledge within an organization.

Specifically this research validates KM governance as the means to continuous and sustainable benefits realization of KM to leverage organizational knowledge by delivering strategic value to the organization over time. The focus therefore falls on the constructs of KM governance and its relationships indicated by the shaded section of the KM governance model in Figure 1. For completeness, the model also includes KM programme implementation.

## Model validation: constructs of KM governance

In order to validate the KM governance model the authors conducted empirical field work and looked for the following constructs of KM governance in the case study organizations:

Construct 1. The exercise of authority at the appropriate level of seniority to ensure strategic alignment. This implies a relationship with organizational governance.

Construct 2. Development of dynamic organizational policies to support appropriate KM process.

Construct 3. Management of the risks and obstacles to KM.

Construct 4. Evaluation according to outputs or value added as measured against criteria established in policy.

Construct 5. Delegation of authority and accountability for KM activities in an explicit and transparent way.

Construct 6. Financial accountability for KM activities

These constructs provide the basis for empirical validation of the proposed KM governance model as described in the following sections.

## Research design

A case study research method was used to test the validity of the KM governance model (Cavaye, 1996; Darke et al., 1998). Six organizations were selected from the lists of winners in the Most Admired Knowledge Enterprises Study (MAKE: see http://www.knowledgebusiness.com/), by referral from other KM professionals, and through the examination of those organizations’ websites. By purposefully locating organizations that were considered successful with KM it was speculated that they might have had governance structures in place to support their success.

The criteria for case site selection were that the sites: be large, multi-sited organizations with over 2000 employees;

be without independent subsidiaries; hold a reputation as knowledge intensive; and hold a reputation for excellence in KM. Table 1 presents an overview of the six case study sites.

In each organization the study sample sought to include those actively involved in the leadership of KM strategy development, users of the KM strategy and those implementing the strategy as stakeholders. The case study used the constructs presented above to guide data collection and analysis – a technique often used in an environment where theoretical constructs are unclear or not well developed (Eisenhardt, 1989; Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007). The focus of investigation was: the organizational philosophy of KM; the history and implementation of the KM strategy; the process and allocation of authority for KM; and the organizational structures that supported and governed the KM initiative.

## Data collection and analysis

Collection and analysis of interview data took place between 2003 and 2007 in the UK, the USA and Australia (see Appendix A for details of interview subjects, locations and schedule). Data collection included semi-structured interviews, observation, and collection of secondary data in the form of available documentary evidence from the organizations themselves plus additional publicly available materials (Stake, 1995; Myers, 1997; Altheide and Johnson, 1998). In each case, the informants gave semi-structured, in-depth interviews, each lasting approximately one hour. These interviews were tape-recorded, supplementary notes were taken, and additional organizational documentary material was collected for analysis. The recorded interviews were transcribed and submitted to the interviewees for their comment, thus establishing the defensibility of that data and that of comparison cases through ‘replication logic (Benbasat et al., 1987; Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007).

The researchers analysed interview transcripts and organizational documents including annual reports, minutes of the KM strategy meetings, and organizational charts. Transcribed interviews were imported into QSR software, a thematic analysis software tool. This provided a systematic way to store and analyse data obtained through interviews, develop nodes, compare nodes, provided an avenue for independent inspection of data to increase validity, while providing the researcher with the ability to store extraneous material obtained through the literature or analysis of organizational documents (Creswell, 2009). The analysis first looked for broad themes within derived KM governance constructs, then developed axial analysis of those themes looking for granularity that would further develop or illuminate organizational KM activity along the specified constructs (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Myers, 1997; Punch, 2005). The data were coded for meaning using descriptors from extant KM literature and from the constructs of KM governance model. Descriptors were clustered to develop themes (Punch, 2005).

Cross-case analysis established evidence of the KM governance constructs and indicators of associated benefits realization (Eisenhardt, 1989; Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007). The informants explicitly stated what activities were introduced as part of the KM strategy implementation that led them to achieve benefits realization. ## Discussion

Table 2 summarizes the empirical results in relation to the constructs of KM governance as identified in the section KM governance: an examination of constructs above of this paper. In five of the six cases analysed, there was a KM governance body that effectively represented the interests of all stakeholders and which appeared to generate organizational engagement with those KM programmes, thus responding to the knowledge needs of the organization, staff and management. Most have a federal or centralized form of governance that is often linked to corporate governance. It was found that these bodies were responsible for authorizing activities that supported KM strategy development and implementation. The KM strategies were formally recognized and accepted across each organization. The representation of stakeholders on governance bodies empowered KM activity across geographic boundaries in multi-sited organizations. Risks were explicitly addressed, as was financial accountability.

As predicted by the proposed model, KM governance body actions were responsible for evaluating and revising strategies, and for ensuring adjustment and realignment of strategies and tools according to the successes and failures of their initiatives. Specifically, KM governance body activity was driven by the aims and objectives of the organization, based on clearly defined evaluation criteria.

As an example, the KM governance process was described in Case 2 as follows: These guys then approve y they’re the authorities, so we say this is what we want to do, this is why and this is the value that we believe that we will get out of it. Once these guys [the Steering Group] have approved it, the implementation people actually physically go and do it. I mean obviously there’s involvement from [the Strategic Design Team] but the leg work is actually happening here [at the implementation level] (Informant 1, Case2).

It was further found that strategic alignment was transparent through the KM governance process, an example was described in Case 1 as follows: basically [we] had financial, client and people plans but in order to actually deliver on our mission we needed to get the IMKM policy out (Informant 1, Case 1). In contrast the informant in Case 4 was highly specific in that We identified some core issues, we identified a number of goals that KM should contribute or help to provide some value ... a policy for KM. It was improving risks ... we are working in the oil and gas industry has an inherent risk and KM should improve the management of these risks. We would create those business processes and we would create communities of practice to collaborate and share that business process and that in turn will improve the management of risks (Informant 1, Case 4). These two respondent statements above clearly underline the impact of strategic alignment and integration of specific but very different aims and objectives.

We refer to the model and in the key to Figure 1, and to the six constructs of KM governance model. Table 2 summarizes the KM governance elements in each case.

In contrast there is no formal or informal governance of the KM strategy in Case 6, it was acknowledged that the KM team or community have no authority (Informant 1, Case 6). Some of the members of the KM community are at director level but have no authority for KM.

The informant suggested that this voluntary character of KM was one reason the strategy had been successful. The informant also suggested that not having governance from the top is a benefit because I have spoken to people from a number of companies. They’ve had a top-down corporate KM program and they’ve died a death after a year or so (Informant 1, Case 6). This statement is at odds with case study evidence of corporate KM programmes that stress the importance of high level leadership, and the findings from other researchers about the KM programmes initiated by CEOs and appointed CKOs (Davenport et al., 1998; Collison and Parcell, 2003; Chua and Lam, 2005).

An ironic post script to Case 6 is that shortly after that data were collected the KM leader left to set up a KM consultancy. A second informant from the organization subsequently advised that two years later a governance mechanism had been set up and that the KM emphasis had changed from attempt at knowledge capture to knowledge sharing for best practice and innovation. In particular, we’ve evolved into a more focused group which is smaller, ywith representatives from global areas, different parts of the global company based on a formal basis to review progress and interpretation issues and knowledge management ythe objectives and goals are set at a very high level (Informant 1, Case 6). Thus governance was developed in an effort to set meaningful direction for KM at a strategic level.

## Operational benefits realized through KM governance

In each case the informants explicitly linked the exercise of governance and authority over the KM strategy to the realization of operational benefits from that strategy development and implementation. The benefits realization varied across all cases. However, informants reported that governance supported the benefits realized by their organization through the effective implementation of KM (see Table 4 for details).

The knowledge needs of those organizations were clearly expressed as principles by which KM strategy was developed and implemented. The benefits realized were strategically aligned with the aims and objectives of the organization. Benefits across all cases included those found in the KM literature as described in Section ‘Knowledge management benefits realization’. Some others not represented in the literature were also evident – for example: KM encourages process and product innovation rather than merely supporting it; KM is viewed as contributing to the sustainability of the firm; there is increased speed of attaining competence for new and current employees; and perceived greater unification of the organization is attributed to KM. An additional claim within the cases examined was the more active use of technology (databases) as explicit knowledge resources. These were described in Case 5 as areas for potential growth – growth in [CoP] membership, the growth in [bulletin board] postings, the growth in [the number of intranet] views, that sort of thing (Case 5, Informant 1).

As can be seen from such measures, the outcomes are not always linked to precise measures of the effectiveness of knowledge sharing and knowledge reuse. However, the informants clearly indicated that these measures constituted evidence of KM impact on improving ROI from knowledge transfer, and ultimately, its impact on improving business outcomes. Informants noted the following specific measures:

\- Implementation of a Balanced Scorecard approach (Informant 2, Case 1);

\- Things like speed to market, reduction of inventory (Informant, Case 2);

\- Cost savings (Informant 1, Case 3); and

\- Uniform business processes (Informant, Case 4).

The claim on business process from Case 5 was ‘the highlight of the value add that we did. It’s actually getting people to collaborate to agree on a common business process. It’s a great achievement and it’s felt throughout the whole organization’ (Informant 1, Case 5). In Case 5, KM contributed to the sustainability of the organization in a competitive economic environment where the requirement was ‘rapid access to our best thinking available to the people who need it, when they need it’ (Informant 1, Case 5).

This provides clear evidence of the impact of KM governance on improving ROI on business processes. Case 6 was the exception. At the time of data collection the realization of benefits was limited. Further, the funding and staffing continuity of the programme was annually reviewed at a local level. It is of particular interest that the subsequent maturation of the KM programme in this organization included the establishment of KM governance in a form similar to the other case descriptions.

The nature of the benefits varied between the cases. However, all these organizations have developed and used KM governance to authorize KM activity. KM governance principles and framework are constant. Possible differences in the development of policy and selection of tools and techniques depend on the aims and objectives of the KM strategy as aligned with organizational aims and objectives. The results of KM strategy implementation are expressed as improved knowledge transfer across divisions and functions, and ownership of KM at an organizational level.

## Conclusions and implications

In this paper we have described the theoretical premises supporting the KM governance model and the case studies used to empirically investigate this concept. We have identified constructs for KM governance as a mechanism for achieving strategic KM benefits in a sustainable way. We have provided evidence that organizations successfully implementing KM governance, within the constructs of the model, effectively and systematically realize benefits of strategies to leverage and transfer knowledge to achieve organizational goals.

We find that the research proposition is consistent with most of the case studies described in the section Knowledge management governance structures above, the particular attributes of Cases 4 and 6 differentiate them from the other four cases. These attributes may be particular to large-scale manufacturing and is worthy of further investigation.

From a theoretical perspective, this study advances understanding of KM and transfer in distributed contexts and offers a detailed analysis of governance of these processes, outlining the functions and responsibilities of the governance mechanism. This case study research contributes to the research literature by identifying and explicitly describing the processes that govern KM. It strongly suggests that KM governance maximizes strategic benefits where it is transparently implemented in large multi-sited organizations.

As the analysis of the cases demonstrates, benefits realization is best achieved when senior staff take the time to design, implement and communicate through authorizing KM governance processes. While this should be a reasonable assumption, this has not been evidenced in earlier research.

It has been demonstrated that the KM governance model provides constructs as the structural mechanism for implementing KM. Moreover, the case study organizations reported that such governance resulted in ongoing KM benefits realization. Conclusions can be drawn that:

\- Sustainable KM is supported by high level organizational commitment through KM governance that: establishes policy to guide the development and implementation of KM strategy; manages risks associated with implementing KM strategies; takes financial accountability and authority for the strategy; and seeks to monitor through measurement how best to realize the benefits of that investment. Additionally it is strongly suggested that sustainability is further evidenced by continuing investment in KM.

\- KM governance defines the transparent distribution of decision-making rights that better allows the realization of value from investments in implementing successful and sustainable KM strategy in large, distributed organizations. It achieves this through transparent alignment of organizational knowledge to meet the aims and objectives of the organization.

This research demonstrates the nexus between a strategic approach to KM and sustainable benefits realization through governance of KM programmes in large distributed organizations, which fills the gap in KM research identified by Zack et al. (2006) in their exploratory survey. This study supports the argument that, in addition to KM systems quality, firms must pay careful attention to authority and goal setting as well as managing risks to their KM programme to realize continuous and sustainable benefits.

This research breaks new ground in understanding the practice of KM in organizations. It has a practical value, showing how effective and systematic realization of benefits from KM strategy implementation can be facilitated by providing a recommended governance model that organizations can use as a guide to ensure that authority; risk management; financial control and measurement are operationalized at the right level of authority. To this end, we have defined: the attributes of KM governance; the constructs, roles and tasks associated with the layers of governance; the planning and implementation of an organizational KM strategy that permits the realization of value from investments in the KM strategy.

## Limitations and future research

The research findings are drawn from a small number of cases; the claims made on the basis of these findings must be read with this limited context in mind. This research has taken a case study approach to gather rich information about: how KM governance is represented; whether KM governance activities exist in organizations; and how the key KM governance roles are defined in organizations which claim systematic benefits realization. This research has considered authority and its delegation at the highest level. This research did not seek to address issues relating to industry sector, organizational structure or organizational business models on which KM governance practices were articulated.

Based on the experiences of the organizations studied, the authors recommend that further consideration be given to the forms and execution mechanisms of the constructs of KM governance proposed in this paper including problemsolving methods and processes. We plan future empirical research in two directions. We will address the level of authority in the granularity of the components at the levels of strategy planning and development, at implementation. We will survey to further test this model across a large number and variety of organizations, and will seek to determine any causal variation in KM governance patterns impacting sustainability.

Of related interest for future research is an investigation of KM in corporate risk management processes for Enterprise Resource Management and the value of KM strategies and technologies in the corporate decision-making process and in the definition of aims and objectives.

## About the authors

Suzanne Zyngier, PhD, is currently a senior lecturer at the Faculty of Business, Economics and Law, La Trobe University, Australia. Her research centres on the governance of knowledge management strategies and innovation. She has conducted qualitative and quantitative research on knowledge management since 2001 and initiated an Australia-wide survey on the status of understanding and adoption of knowledge management in top 1000 public and private organizations, and more recently investigated this phenomenon at the global level, and within the healthcare supply chain. Dr. Zyngier publishes widely and regularly presents her research at international conferences and to industry. Prior to her appointment at La Trobe University, Suzanne worked at both Monash and Swinburne Universities, and she has consulted to the professional, corporate and not-for profit sectors.

Frada Burstein is currently a professor in the Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia. Professor Burstein initiated and continues to lead Knowledge Management Research Group. She has been a chief investigator for a number of research projects supported by grants and scholarships from the Australian Research Council and industry. Professor Burstein’s current research interests include knowledge management technologies, intelligent decision support, cognitive aspects of information systems development and use, organizational knowledge and memory, and systems development. Professor Burstein is a secretary of IFIP WG8.3, Decision Support Systems and is a past Chair of SIG DSS of the Association of Information Systems.
