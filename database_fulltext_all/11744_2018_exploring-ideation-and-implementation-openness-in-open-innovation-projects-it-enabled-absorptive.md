# Exploring ideation and implementation openness in open innovation projects: IT-enabled absorptive capacity perspective

Tingru Cui<sup>a</sup>, Yi Wu<sup>b,⁎</sup>, Yu Tong<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Computing and Information Technology, University of Wollongong, Australia

<sup>b</sup> College of Management and Economics, Tianjin University, China

<sup>c</sup> School of Management, Zhejiang University, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Open innovation project IT-enabled absorptive capacity Ideation openness Implementation openness Product innovativeness Speed to market

## A B S T R A C T

Advancements in information technology (IT) have made organizational boundaries so porous, thereby resulting in a trend toward leveraging external knowledge for innovation. However, <sup>fi</sup>rms experience signi<sup>fi</sup>cant obstacles in drawing external knowledge. This study aims to disentangle the role of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in new product development performance. The research model was tested with survey data of open innovation projects from 152 <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that IT-enabled absorptive capability improves open innovation project performance in terms of new product innovativeness and product speed to market, but we do not detect a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of openness on performance. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also show that the interaction of IT-en abled absorptive capacity and ideation openness signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly ampli<sup>fi</sup>es new product innovativeness while the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness accelerates the product speed to market. This study theoretically contributes by building an IT-enabled absorptive capacity theory in the open innovation context and uncovering the e<sup>f</sup>ects of two dimensions of openness. In practice, it o<sup>f</sup>ers managers strategies to successfully conduct open innovation projects in deploying e<sup>f</sup>ective ITs and leveraging variou types of openness during the two phases.

## 1. Introduction

The mobility of knowledge workers, velocity of technological changes, and globalization of markets have shifted the way in which innovations are created in organizations. While organizations are used to developing new technologies internally and transferring these to their own products and services in a practice called “closed innovation,” an increasing trend of leveraging external knowledge for innovation exists. “The use of purposive in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows of knowledge to accelerate internal innovation and expand the markets for external use of innovation, respectively” is termed as “open innovation” [1], p. 1). With the proliferation of interconnectivity and interactivity through information technology (IT), organizations now frequently engage in open innovation with customers, suppliers, universities, research institutions, and other sources of knowledge [2]. Such emerging innovations can bring signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to organizations. Primarily, organizations can obtain complementary knowledge from collaborative partners, thereby encouraging creativity and novel solutions and resulting in the rise of new technologies or new market possibilities. Several pioneering companies, such as Procter & Gamble, General

Electric, IBM, and Siemens, have obtained bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from pursuing open innovation strategies. For instance, Procter & Gamble collaborates with external innovators on more than 35% of its new products through its “Connect and Development” program, and its research and develop ment (R&D) productivity has increased by nearly 60% [3].

By using external knowledge, organizations expect to introduce more innovative products and accelerate their innovation process [4,5]. Despite the promising bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, many other adopting organizations experience signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>fi</sup>culties in pro<sup>fi</sup>ting from external knowledge [6,3]. Open innovation brings complexity and di<sup>fi</sup>culty into the innovation process, which leads to increased risks to the project performance [7]. Firms encounter challenges in knowledge acquisition, exchange, integration, and coordination with their partners [1,8,9].

The open innovation phenomenon has been drawing attention from academics. Identifying a theoretically driven way to address the challenges of open innovation is important not only to help <sup>fi</sup>rms seize the potential of open innovation but also to advance the extant literature. Evidence shows that IT could enable the exploration, creation, dissemination, and use of knowledge [10] and can facilitate the open innovation strategies of <sup>fi</sup>rms. For instance, organizations largely depend on social media tools or business analytics to search for useful external technology [11]. Collaborative innovation IT systems are used to support knowledge exchange between <sup>fi</sup>rms [12]. We propose that IT-enabled absorptive capacity, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to employ IT-based resources with other resources in building organizational capability in knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation [13,14], is a useful theoretical lens for understanding how IT can help overcome the challenges in open innovation. By engaging in interlinked processes that enable high-quality knowledge sharing and processing, IT-enabled absorptive capacity enhances knowledge transfer at the inter-organizational level, thereby resulting in improved knowledge recombination and creation [15].

Openness bene<sup>fi</sup>ts <sup>fi</sup>rms through “technological fusion,” which is the combination of existing technologies from several resources, and the powerful market e<sup>f</sup>ects these combinations can create [16]. Innovation openness has substantial potential to contribute to innovation outcome [7]. Prior literature on open innovation suggests that organizations may engage in di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of openness for innovation purposes [8,17], thereby resulting in varying performance of open innovation projects. Moreover, the development of innovations is com prised of two di<sup>f</sup>erent phases: 1) idea generation and 2) idea implementation [18,19]. The study of value constellations shows that the open innovation openness is also important for the innovation idea implementation [20]; however, innovation openness during the idea implementation phase has been overlooked in prior literature and is not yet integrated into the broader context of open innovation. The idea generation phase is characterized as the initial conceptualization and development of new product idea and prototype. During the idea implementation phase, the focus is to transform the <sup>fi</sup>nalized product idea and prototype into an actual product. These two phases entail their own di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics; consequently, being open in these two phases may yield di<sup>f</sup>erent innovation outcomes. To provide executives with actionable guidance for managing open innovation projects, a deeper exploration is needed to untangle the impacts of openness. Thus, it is important to distinguish implementation and ideation openness and explore their distinct e<sup>f</sup>ects on open innovation performance.

Furthermore, several previous studies pointed out the important interaction e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT and other <sup>fi</sup>rm resources in achieving organizational performance [21,22]. Integrating IT with other complementary organizational resources obtains a highly comprehensive understanding of its e<sup>f</sup>ects on open innovation performance. As innovation openness enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to access external knowledge resources, we expect that the extent of how knowledge is assimilated and exploited by IT-enabled absorptive capacity can bene<sup>fi</sup>t open innovation outcomes subject to such di<sup>f</sup>erent nature. Considering that two important measures of open innovation outcomes are product innovativeness and speed to market (e.g., [23,24], this study aims to answer the following research questions:

(1) What is the role of IT-enabled absorptive capacity on open innovation project performance in terms of new product innovativeness and speed to market?

(2) How do ideation openness and implementation openness afect innova tion performance (i.e., product innovativeness and speed to market)?

(3) How can the two dimensions of openness diferently influence the efects of IT-enabled absorptive capacity on new product innovativeness and speed to market?

To answer these questions, we draw on the notion of organizational absorptive capacity and organizational learning literature. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the current study di<sup>f</sup>erentiates two dimensions of open innovation openness, namely, ideation openness and implementation openness, and examines their interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects with IT-enabled absorptive ca pacity on the product innovativeness and speed to market. These two phases entail their own di<sup>f</sup>erent characteristics, objectives and focuses; consequently, being open in these two phases may yield di<sup>f</sup>erent innovation outcomes. In the idea generation phase, the idea has not been <sup>fi</sup>nalized and the inputs from external knowledge sources can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the conceptual innovativeness of the idea and solution. High ideation openness increases the exposure of the innovation project team to complementary and diverse knowledge [8]. Ideation openness provides a substantial amount of inputs for IT to extract value from. As ITenabled absorptive capacity facilitates the innovation team to identify and absorb the right knowledge, high ideation openness provides innovating <sup>fi</sup>rms with the opportunity to fully realize the potential of IT. Thus, ideation openness strengthens the positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IT-en abled absorptive capacity on project idea development. In the idea implementation phase, the new product idea has been <sup>fi</sup>nalized and the prototype has been developed. The knowledge recombination occurs at an incremental pace and may not lead to very creative ideas. Consequently, it will not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the innovativeness of the new product. Instead, tasks in the implementation phase are more standardized and modular. This process can be sped up by dividing tasks into smaller components and relying on specialists to work on each diverse technological area [25]. IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness together provide complementary capabilities and generate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through economies of specialization from diverse external knowledge sources [26]. This approach hence enables the innovation team to save a considerable amount of time in project development. Therefore, we expect that ideation openness can improve product innovativeness while implementation openness strengthens the speed to market. They also have di<sup>f</sup>erent interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects with ITenabled absorptive capacity on the two performance measures. Hy potheses are empirically tested through survey data collected from 152 <sup>fi</sup>rms regarding their identi<sup>fi</sup>ed open innovation projects. Our study <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to e<sup>f</sup>orts in open innovation and to the literature on IT and innovation. From a practical perspective, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings specify managerial guidelines on how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively improve new product in novativeness and speed to market during each phase of an open innovation project. An appreciation of the roles of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in open innovation projects can help organizations mindfully invest in IT and deploy it in future endeavors.

The rest of this study is organized as follows. First, a theoretical framework is developed to explore IT-enabled absorptive capacity and its e<sup>f</sup>ect on new product innovativeness and speed to market. The effects of ideation openness and implementation openness and their interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects with IT-enabled absorptive capacity are also addressed. Second, the empirical context and data collection procedures are described, followed by data analysis and presentation of results. Finally, the theoretical and empirical implications are discussed, as well as limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and hypotheses

## 2.1. Open innovation project openness

The past decades witnessed a transition from closed innovation to an open innovation model, where <sup>fi</sup>rms frequently engage in collaborative innovations with customers, suppliers, universities, research institutions, and other sources of knowledge [2]. Compared with tra ditional closed innovation, open innovation models highlight the in teractive and distributed nature of innovation [27]. The central premise of open innovation is to open up the innovation process and leverage external knowledge for innovation success [1].

The concept of openness has drawn extensive attention from researchers and has been studied in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. For example, by investigating online open-innovation platforms, [28] conceptualized and operationalized platform openness from the perspective of application developers. [29] focuses on openness as revealing ideas that are previously hidden inside organizations. Innovation openness has sub stantial potential to contribute to innovation outcome [7]. Prior open innovation literature (e.g., [30] emphasizes why organizations acquire resources from the external environment for innovations. Some experts argue that organizations may engage in di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of openness for innovation purposes [8,17]. This condition may result in variance in open innovation project performance. Moreover, the study of value constellations shows that open innovation openness is also important for implementation of innovative ideas [20]; however, such openness has been overlooked in previous literature and is not yet integrated into the broader context of open innovation. Openness is one innovation strategy in <sup>fi</sup>rm and it is an essential organizational knowledge-sourcing strategy for acquiring new knowledge and technologies needed from external sources outside organizational boundary [8,31]. The mobility of knowledge workers, the velocity of technological change and the globalization of markets have shifted the way that innovations are created in organizations. While organizations used to internally developing new technologies through closed innovation, there is an increasing trend of leveraging on external knowledge for innovation creation through open innovation [1]. Open innovation literature has emphasized that the entrepreneurial behavior associated with forming connection with external valuable complementary resources and knowhow is strategic in nature [32]. The innovation openness re<sup>fl</sup>ects a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for participating in open initiative and is <sup>fi</sup>rm’s strategic choice and decision of sourcing externally for knowledge or technology inputs for its innovation [32,33].

Bearing the aforementioned <sup>fi</sup>ndings in mind, we developed two concepts that are in accordance with the work of [8] that represents the openness of an organization’s open-innovation project. An essential aspect of the transition toward an open innovation model is a change in organizational knowledge-sourcing strategies for new knowledge and technologies needed [8]; therefore, openness is determined by the ex tent of drawing from various external knowledge sources for company innovation [34,8,31,35]. The development of innovations can be viewed as a process comprising two phases: (1) idea generation and (2) idea implementation [18,19]. As <sup>fi</sup>rms constantly conduct external knowledge sourcing along these two phases, open innovation project openness involves two dimensions: ideation openness and implementation openness. Ideation openness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent of drawing from external knowledge sources during the idea generation phase. Implementation openness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of the extent of drawing from external knowledge sources during the idea im plementation phase. Together, these two dimensions re<sup>fl</sup>ect a compre hensive picture of open innovation project openness. In addition, given the important supporting roles of IT in open innovation project, our research model considers the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT-enabled absorptive capacity. Considering the nature of open innovation with the use of purposive in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows of knowledge during the two innovation phases [1], each phase is characterized by various objectives of knowledge sourcing and may lead to di<sup>f</sup>erent aspects of open-innovation project performance. Therefore, our research model (illustrated in Fig. 1) posits that the two dimensions of openness induce interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects and may contribute to various innovation project outcomes.

## 2.1.1. Ideation openness

Learning and information processing perspective, drawing from diverse external knowledge sources, likely augments the variety of knowledge used for new product idea generation [6]. For instance, by collecting feedback from product users, the innovation team will be able to have a comprehensive understanding of user preferences and expectations of new products [36]. Interactions with external service providers or suppliers of technology or equipment can also help o<sup>f</sup>er useful knowledge to address customer demands [37]. Furthermore, pooling information and knowledge from di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives creates an environment with substantial opportunities in knowledge recombination. Thus, such an environment encourages creativity and novel solutions, and consequently results in the creation of new technologies or new market possibilities [38]. By drawing from diverse external knowledge sources, the innovation team will be able to access a broad range of technological knowledge. This condition consequently creates a broad range of resources that contribute to the solutions of non-routine problems [39]. Therefore, the potential for innovative solutions increases. Great open innovation openness at the ideation stage of new product development projects can bring substantial new knowledge into new product idea and solution generation, thereby signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhancing the new product innovativeness.

However, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of ideation openness on the speed to market is inconclusive. Drawing from external knowledge sources during the ideation phase o<sup>f</sup>ers more and diverse technological inputs to generate of product idea and innovation solutions. This approach may accelerate innovation speed as the infusion of new knowledge into the innovation process can help the innovation team to understand unknown areas and solve challenges e<sup>fi</sup>ciently [27]. However, innovation project teams may have to dedicate more time to process and absorb external knowledge [40]. The time cost introduced by ideation openness may hinder product speed to market in the entire innovation process. Therefore, we expect that ideation openness only has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on product innovativeness but not on the speed to market, and we hy pothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. Ideation openness is positively associated with product innovativeness.

## 2.1.2. Implementation openness

We posit that the new product speed to market can be accelerated when the innovation team is open to external knowledge sources during the idea implementation phase. First, drawing knowledge from diverse external sources helps gain and leverage ready-to-use knowledge and technology for implementation. Constrained by resource limitation of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, the innovation team may face di<sup>fi</sup>culty in obtaining all required resources internally Baczark, 2009. The innovation team can actively build on external knowledge from suppliers, research institutes, or even competitors and focus only on those technologies or components that are not yet developed [27,41]. Furthermore, as suggested, a large percentage of innovation project delays are due to mistakes and rework Baczark, 2009. Pooling knowledge from diverse external sources also helps reduce the possibilities of rework and potential mistakes. If working alone, the innovation team may focus all e<sup>f</sup>ort on its own existing working style and path, and may face di<sup>fi</sup>- culty in detecting mistakes that they have made or potential risks during the implementation process. Contrarily, timely feedbacks can be obtained e<sup>fi</sup>ciently through exposure to external examination and distinct perspectives. This, in turn, may shorten the project development time.

Second, compared to the idea generation phase, tasks in the im plementation phase are more standardized and modular. The innovation team can divide project tasks internally across units or externally among partners and leverage external expertise in particular technology <sup>fi</sup>elds to address a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task simultaneously [25]. In accordance with the “division of labor” concept, the implementation process can be sped up by dividing tasks into smaller components and relying on specialists to work on each diverse technological area. This approach enables the innovation team to save a considerable amount of time in project development.

However, during the idea implementation phase, the new product idea has been <sup>fi</sup>nalized and the prototype has been developed. The focus of this phase is to transform the <sup>fi</sup>nalized product idea and prototype into an actual product. The knowledge recombination occurs at an incremental pace and may not lead to very creative ideas. Consequently, it will not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the innovativeness of the new product. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2. Implementation openness is positively associated with product speed to market.

2.2. Organizational open innovation and IT-enabled absorptive capacity

In the age of information and with the growing open innovation trend, absorptive capacity is considered essential for organizational success [42]. Organizational absorptive capacity refers to the ability to assimilate, acquire valuable external knowledge, transform this knowledge into the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s knowledge base, and exploit the new knowledge through innovation [40,43]. As an important lens to understand how acquired external knowledge can be transferred to de sirable organizational outcomes, absorptive capacity has been applied to the research on organization innovation performance with other organizations (e.g., [15].

IT contributes to the assimilation, creation, and application of knowledge [44]; thus, it signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly builds the knowledge absorption capabilities of organizations. In the information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld, the role of IT in enabling organization absorptive capacity has been drawing attention from academics (e.g., [14,15,10]. IT facilities external knowledge assimilation and accumulation by building organi zational electronic repositories [13,45]. Standard IT interfaces or applications also facilitate knowledge sharing between an innovation <sup>fi</sup>rm and its external collaborators [15,46]. In the context of new product development, the ability of an innovation project team to e<sup>f</sup>ectively use IT applications enhances its absorptive capacity, which ultimately in-<sup>fl</sup>uences the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitive advantage in innovation [47]. The literature on absorptive capacity in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld also advocates in-depth investigation of the concept of IT-enabled absorptive capacity [10].

Therefore, we posit that IT that supports knowledge assimilation, acquisition, transformation, and exploitation provides IT-enabled absorptive capacity. IT capability refers to “the ability to mobilize and deploy IT-based resources in combination or copresent with other resources and capabilities” [48], p. 160). Simply put, IT-enabled ab sorptive capacity is the ability of a <sup>fi</sup>rm to implement IT-based resources with other resources to execute knowledge acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation processes. Through thi theoretical lens, we examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of IT-enabled absorptive capacity on the performance of collaborative innovation projects. While discussing collaborative or open innovation, various previous studies have demonstrated the indispensable role of IT (e.g., [49,50]. Collaborative or open innovation projects require greater IT capability to enhance organizational learning and reduce coordination costs, conse quently resulting in positive outcomes. Thus, we propose that IT-enabled absorptive capacity is positively associated with open-innovation project performance in terms of product innovativeness and speed to market. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, product innovativeness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the new product o<sup>f</sup>ers meaningful features to customers and is di<sup>f</sup>erent from alternative products on the market, thereby re<sup>fl</sup>ecting meaningful uniqueness [51]. Speed to market refers to the period from the initial idea development until the introduction of the <sup>fi</sup>nal product to the market [52].

On the one hand, IT-enabled absorptive capacity enables collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms to acquire and assimilate knowledge from a large number of areas. For example, data/web mining, data analytics, and business intelligence facilitate the collection of new knowledge assets from diverse technological <sup>fi</sup>elds through improved search capabilities. Prior creative literature has recognized the contribution of knowledge heterogeneity to the innovation outcomes [53–55]. Searching across a variety of external sources may stimulate further creative recombinations of knowledge [56]. It also o<sup>f</sup>ers di<sup>f</sup>erent ways of reasoning and distinct problem-solving techniques [57]. Thus, supported by IT-enabled absorptive capacity, heterogeneous external knowledge can be recombined to generate more innovative outcomes [43].

In addition, IT-enabled absorptive capacity can also encourage the transformation and exploitation of external knowledge by enhancing knowledge exchange across collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [10]. The broader range of knowledge exchange can lead to better organizational learning and innovation [47] because it enables the technological knowledge from collaborative partners to be exchanged and combined, thereby yielding better quality of innovation [57]. For example, the use of knowledge repositories, CAD/CAM, visualization, and simulation tools a<sup>f</sup>ords a standardized architecture with extensive knowledge-exchange capability among collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms [58]. Such tools enable collaborators to leverage complementary assets and combine knowledge to solve challenges [59]. Thus, the discovery of new knowledge-recombination opportunities is expected to be enhanced and the outcomes of the collaborative innovation project are likely to be innovative. Based on this idea, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 3. IT-enabled absorptive capacity is positively associated with product innovativeness.

On the other hand, IT-enabled absorptive capacity enables the acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation process of knowledge to occur in a smooth and e<sup>fi</sup>cient way. IT-enabled absorptive capacity provides collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms with quick and e<sup>fi</sup>cient ac cess to the right knowledge Roberts et al. [10], thereby improving the overall e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the innovation process. By accelerating the speed at which knowledge is acquired and disseminated among collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms, IT helps ensure that each collaborator can easily share interpretations of the knowledge. For example, CAD/CAM systems can di gitize a new product design and make it available among partners in the innovation development process. Shared electronic knowledge repositories, such as collaboration technology and groupware systems, help achieve mutual understanding and cultivate shared frames of reference, thereby leading to e<sup>fi</sup>ciency in consensus development [60]. Furthermore, as computer simulation and rapid prototyping reduce the cost of experimentation, they also allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to exchange and integrate knowledge rapidly [59]. This condition could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the time needed for knowledge processing, making the innovation process e<sup>fi</sup>cient. Therefore, we further propose that greater IT-enabled absorptive capacity is likely to promote e<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the open-innovation project process. Based on this idea, the following hypothesis is pro posed:

Hypothesis 4. IT-enabled absorptive capacity is positively associated with product speed to market.

2.2.1. Interaction efect of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness

We propose that the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness will have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the innovativeness of the developed new product. As stated in Hypothesis 3, ITenabled absorptive capacity enables acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation of external knowledge by supporting and facilitating knowledge processing and exchanges [10]. Drawing from external knowledge sources during the ideation phase (i.e., high idea tion openness) increases the exposure of the innovation project team to complementary and diverse knowledge [8]. Ideation openness provides a substantial amount of inputs for IT to extract value from. As IT-enabled absorptive capacity facilitates the innovation team to identify and absorb the right knowledge, high ideation openness provides in novating <sup>fi</sup>rms with the opportunity to fully realize the potential of IT. Thus, ideation openness strengthens the positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of IT-en abled absorptive capacity on project idea development.

Through IT-enabled absorptive capacity and pooling of distinct knowledge, further opportunities are presented for the recombination of previously unconnected ideas from di<sup>f</sup>erent sources [38]. This triggers higher creativity and truly novel solutions, consequently resulting in the creation of more innovative products [6]. For instance, when ideation openness is high, business analytics help process a large amount of information e<sup>f</sup>ectively from di<sup>f</sup>erent external sources. Knowledge repositories can store potentially useful external knowledge and facilitate capability to recombine knowledge across <sup>fi</sup>elds of tech nologies, leading to improved quality of knowledge creation [60]. Conversely, low ideation openness may lock <sup>fi</sup>rms in an existing unpro<sup>fi</sup>table learning path [8], thereby inhibiting IT to leverage a wide range of knowledge as ideation inputs. The value of IT-enabled ab sorptive capacity cannot be fully utilized into the innovation idea generation. Myopic knowledge search within the local context of the open innovation project will yield less creative recombinations [61]. Evidently, the association of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and idea tion openness will strengthen the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of possessing knowledge assimilation and exploitation capability when <sup>fi</sup>rms are exposed to a large number of external sources. The alignment between IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness enhances the innovativeness of open innovation project outcome.

As discussed, ideation openness o<sup>f</sup>ers new knowledge inputs for IT to extract valuable information; consequently, it facilitates e<sup>fi</sup>cient comprehension of unknown areas. Meanwhile, it requires longer time to process and absorb external knowledge even with IT-enabled absorp tive capacity. Therefore, the interaction e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness on the speed to market is inconclusive. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 5. IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness have a positive interaction e<sup>f</sup>ect on product innovativeness.

2.2.2. Interaction efect of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness

We propose that for open innovation projects with high implementation openness, the positive e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT-enabled absorptive capacity on new product speed to market will be further strengthened. Entry into the implementation phase signals the completion of a new product idea generation and starts further development of the product prototype, which requires complementary competence (e.g., legal and regulatory related capabilities, manufacturing competence of product parts, and assembling competence). As stated in Hypothesis 4, IT-en abled absorptive capacity provides collaborating <sup>fi</sup>rms with quick and e<sup>fi</sup>cient access to the right knowledge and accelerates the speed at which information is acquired and disseminated Roberts et al. [10]. By opening up the implementation process, <sup>fi</sup>rms will be able to collaborate with external partners for various complementary capabilities. ITenabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness together provide complementary capabilities and generate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts through economies of specialization from diverse external knowledge sources [26].

The alignment of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase <sup>fi</sup>rms’ access to technology developed elsewhere and enable the <sup>fi</sup>rm to collaborate with the right partner in developing the required product parts [62]. Thus, innovating <sup>fi</sup>rms do not need to develop these externally available technologies from scratch, and can yield accelerated innovation speed [63]. Furthermore, as tasks in the implementation phase are more standardized and modular, they can be split into small pieces to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of “division of labor”. Each task can be targeted at and addressed by speci<sup>fi</sup>c pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency or capability from diverse external knowledge sources. IT enables the innovation team to appropriately divide the project tasks into di<sup>f</sup>erent parts as well as to smoothly integrate different parts of the innovation in a timely fashion [50]. When implementation openness is high, the project team with strong IT-enabled absorptive capability can utilize visualization IT (e.g., CAD and CAM) and prototyping or modeling tools to e<sup>f</sup>ectively build an underlying “knowledge architecture” of the product and use the external implementation knowledge in a more e<sup>fi</sup>cient manner. The value of ITenabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness has been enhanced due to the co-existence of both. Thus, IT-enabled absorptive capacity and openness in the implementation phase may in<sup>fl</sup>uence innovation e<sup>fi</sup>ciency in a positive way.

However, as discussed, the new product idea and prototype has been <sup>fi</sup>nalized in the implementation phase. The focus of this phase is to transform the <sup>fi</sup>nalized product idea and prototype into an actual product rather than generating innovative ideas or solutions. Therefore, we do not expect the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness to in<sup>fl</sup>uence product innovativeness. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 6. IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness have a positive interaction e<sup>f</sup>ect on product speed to market.

## 3. Research methodology

We adopted a survey method to test our model, as it provides a basis for establishing generalizability to the population, facilitates the replication of results, and exhibits statistical power. In our study, the unit of analysis is an open innovation project. As part of a large-scale project, we collected high-quality nationwide data through the sampling frame of China’s high-technology company directory. The targeted respondents of the survey were the open innovation project directors.

## 3.1. Scale development

The survey instruments were adapted from prior literature (see Table 1).

## 3.1.1. Dependent variables

Measures adapted from the work of [23] were used to measure new product innovativeness. A six-item, seven-point Likert scale was used to ask respondents to evaluate the extent of the new product creativity $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } }$ , it is very novel for the industry or it is challenging to existing ideas in our industry). New product speed to market, the second dependent variable in the model, was measured using <sup>fi</sup>ve items with a seven point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). These were also adapted from [23] scale and ask respondents the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the new product development process $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , it is faste than time goals or industry norm).

## 3.1.2. Independent variable

The eight measures of IT-enabled absorptive capacity were adapted on the basis of the literature on absorptive capacity [42,64] and on IT leveraging competence in NPD [47]. These measures were used to ask the open innovation project directors about their absorptive capacity by e<sup>f</sup>ectively using IT tools during the projects. The scale was further validated by following [66] procedures and the detailed validation will be illustrated later. The instrument originally developed as part of the European Community Innovation Survey was used to measure ideation openness and implementation openness. This instrument has been widely adopted in prior open innovation research (e.g., [34,8,35]. The adapted complete list of 13 knowledge sources from [35] work is reported in Table 1. The respondents, who were open innovation project directors, were asked to rate the importance of each knowledge source at the idea generation and idea implementation phases of an open innovation project. A 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very unimportant) to 7 (very important) was used. Following previous studies, the level of openness was captured as a simple count of the number of knowledge sources that were considered as being “slightly important,” “important,” or “very important” at the idea generation phase or idea implementation phase [8,35]. The formative indicators of ideation openness and implementation openness range from 0 to 13, with 0 indicating a relatively closed innovation phase and 13 suggesting a highly open innovation phase.

## 3.1.3. Control variables

Previous studies indicate that <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics and R&D related characteristics have impacts on innovation project performance. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rm age (i.e., the number of years of <sup>fi</sup>rm establishment), size (i.e., the number of employees), and R&D investment (i.e., the annual R&D expenditure) were included as control variables [35]. As organization innovation project performance can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced partially by its industry related characteristics, industry sector (using industry dummies) was also controlled [14]. Moreover, given that the project-related characteristics may also a<sup>f</sup>ect its outcomes [67], therefore, project size (i.e., the number of employees participating in the open innovation project), project cost $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the monetary cost of the open innovation project), and task complexity were also included as control variables [35,65].

## 3.2. Conceptual validation

We <sup>fi</sup>rst consulted several colleagues to identify and rectify some potential issues rooted in the framing and phrasing of the instrument. A two-stage item sorting was conducted to enhance the conceptual validity. Ten postgraduate IS students were recruited for each session. Minor modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were made on some items to address the concerns raised by them. Subsequently, to ensure comparability and equivalence in meaning, the method of back-translation was adopted Brislin, 1970.

Two independent postgraduate students translated the questionnaire into Chinese and then retranslated it into English. Thereafter, we compared the translated version with the original questionnaire and made necessary changes. To check face validity, the open innovation project directors of two <sup>fi</sup>rms were invited to comment on the survey questionnaire and indicate confusing questions. With their suggestions, we made some further modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in the phrasing and framing of the questions.

## 3.3. Survey administration

To ensure the generalizability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings, <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample should represent a wide variety of industries. Therefore, following the procedure of Rai et al. [74] work, we chose high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rms from four industry sectors that have a relatively wide-ranging presence in China $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { i } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . ,$ , chemical and pharmaceutical, electronics and other electrical equipment, industrial and commercial machinery equipment, and fabricated metal and other material industries). A cover letter stating the study’s objective and the survey questionnaire were included in the survey package. The questionnaire was <sup>fi</sup>rst sent to the R&D department manager in each <sup>fi</sup>rm and then forwarded to the open innovation project director. Data collection was conducted in May 2015. Respondents were requested to identify a speci<sup>fi</sup>c completed open innovation project and use this project and their <sup>fi</sup>rm condition of year 2014 to complete the questionnaire. The survey was distributed to 1000 <sup>fi</sup>rms randomly selected from the high-tech <sup>fi</sup>rm directory. Altogether, 152 surveys were returned and usable for subsequent analysis. Hence, an e<sup>f</sup>ective response rate of 15.2% was achieved. The demographic pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the sample <sup>fi</sup>rm is presented in Table 2.

To further check the representativeness of the responding <sup>fi</sup>rms, we conducted two statistical analyses to check the problem of nonresponse bias Phillips and Bagozzi, 1986. First, we veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that early and late respondents did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>er in their demographic characteristics and responses on principal constructs. Early respondents were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by selecting those that responded in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two weeks. We then conducted t-tests of the two groups’ means and it did not demonstrate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences (at $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5$ level). Second, the dif ference of the expected and observed number of responses was compared across the four industry sectors included in our data collection. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ -test results did not detect signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erence (at $p \ < \ 0 . 0 5$ level). Collectively, it is suggested by the t-test and $\chi ^ { 2 } \cdot$ -test that our data do not have the issue of response bias.

## 4. Data analysis and results

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was adopted in this study as it can simultaneously analyze all paths with latent variables in a single analysis Gefen et al. [77]. Within SEM, partial least square (PLS) was chosen over co-variance-based SEM for two reasons: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the dependent variables may not conform to the proportionality constraints and uncorrelated measurement errors of co-variance-based SEM Gefen et al. [77]. Second, PLS is a suitable choice for models with interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects such as ours Wetzels et al. [78]. To computer interaction terms, stan dardized items of the relevant constructs were used as the cross-mul tipliers. Therefore, SmartPLS 2.0 software was adopted to analyze the data.

## 4.1. Measurement modeling

We followed prior literature to value our measurements by examining the convergent and discriminant validities Hair et al. [79]. The convergent validity was evaluated through Cronbach’s α (CA) (at > 0.7 level), composite reliability (CR) (at > 0.7 level), average variance extracted (AVE) (at > 0.5 level), and the factor analysis results [75].

Table 3 shows that the item-factor loading of each item was above 0.7 on its intended construct. Moreover, values of CR, ranged from 0.86 to 0.97, and values of CA, ranged from 0.87 to 0.97, all exceed suggested 0.7. In addition, the values for AVEs were greater than the threshold of $0 . 5 ,$ according to Straub et al. [75]. Hence, su<sup>fi</sup>cient convergent validity for all constructs was demonstrated. Discriminant validity was assessed by examining the indicator factor loadings and comparing AVEs with inter-construct correlations Gefen and Straub [76]. The results in Table 3 show that all items load more strongly on their intended constructs than on other constructs and that the square root of AVE was larger than the inter-construct correlations in Table 4, demonstrating su<sup>fi</sup>cient discriminant validity of all constructs.

## 4.3. Post-hoc analysis

While our hypotheses were drawn from the literature on organiza tional learning and open innovation, an additional post-hoc analysis was conducted to obtain a more comprehensive view of the phenomenon and enrich our understanding. We examine the non-hypothesized relationships of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and openness, which are the interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects of (1) IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness on product innovativeness, and (2) IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness on product speed to market. First, the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness does not have any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on speed to market. Second, the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness does not have any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on new product innovativeness. The results are displayed as Model 4 and Model 8 in Table 5.

Therefore, the post-hoc analysis results indicate that the non-hypothesized relationships of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and open ness, indeed, are unable to provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant innovation outcomes. Rather, the interactions of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness on product innovativeness, as well as IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness on product speed to market will serve as the desired model for open innovation project as we hypothesized.

## 4.4. Common method bias

We adopted a single-informant approach to collect survey data and therefore the possibility of common method bias should be assessed [68]. To address this bias procedurally, we allowed respondents to answer questions anonymously to reduce their evaluation apprehension and to minimize social desirability bias. Next, to evaluate the presence of common method bias, we performed two tests. First, we conducted Harmon’s one-factor test on the re<sup>fl</sup>ective construct variables to check common method bias [69]. We entered all the re<sup>fl</sup>ective principal constructs into a principal components factor analysis. Factors with eigenvalues > 1 were extracted from all the measures in this study and in total accounted for 56% of the variance. The <sup>fi</sup> > 1 factor accounted for 34% of the variance.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

Our work, as an initial and original e<sup>f</sup>ort, examines the intersection of IT capability and openness in the open innovation context. As in novation becomes increasingly open, <sup>fi</sup>rms need to identify e<sup>f</sup>ective mechanisms to improve both the product innovativeness and speed to market. To achieve this goal, we conceptually and empirically distinguish between ideation openness and implementation openness. An appreciation of the roles of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in open innovation projects can help organizations mindfully invest and deploy IT in future endeavors.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, IT-enabled capability does improve the performance of open innovation projects in terms of new product innovativeness and product speed to market. On the one hand, IT-enabled absorptive capacity provides collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms with a wide range of knowledge that may stimulate more creative ideas [56]. Empowered by IT-enabled absorptive capacity, <sup>fi</sup>rms are able to draw on and recombine heterogeneous external knowledge for innovative outcome [43]. Furthermore, ideation openness widens the ranges of external knowledge sources for the innovation project teams, thereby increasing the exposure of the innovation project team to complementary and diverse knowledge [8]. As IT-enabled absorptive capacity facilitates identifying and absorbing the right knowledge, high ideation openness provides innovating <sup>fi</sup>rms with further opportunities and capabilities to fully realize the potential of IT. Therefore, the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and ideation openness signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the new product innovativeness in the idea generation phase.

On the other hand, IT-enabled absorptive capacity provides collaborative <sup>fi</sup>rms with quick and e<sup>fi</sup>cient access to the right knowledge Roberts et al. [10], thereby improving the overall e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the innovation process. Thus, IT-enabled absorptive capacity improves the product speed to market of open innovation projects. Additionally, implementation openness broadens the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ external collaborative partners to further develop the generated innovative ideas for product prototypes. IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness together provide complementary assets and generate rents through economies of specialization from diverse external knowledge sources [26]. In the implementation phase, IT-enabled absorptive capacity and openness may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase <sup>fi</sup>rms’ access to technology devel oped elsewhere and collaborate with the right partners to co-develop the required product parts [62]. Therefore, the interaction of IT-enabled absorptive capacity and implementation openness accelerates the product speed to market in the idea implementation phase.

However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not indicate any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of ideation openness or implementation openness on open innovation performance. The possible reason is that our samples are high-tech nology <sup>fi</sup>rms from four industry sectors (i.e., chemical and pharma ceutical, electronics and other electrical equipment, industrial and commercial machinery equipment, and fabricated metal and other material industries). Their identi<sup>fi</sup>ed open innovation projects tend to be information-intensive and technologically complex. Being open to diverse external knowledge sources only does not necessarily lead to improved performance if the access to these external sources is not supported by other organizational resources (i.e., IT resources). Moreover, as prior studies have not empirically investigated the role of IT in inter-organizational innovation together with other organizational or project-level characteristics in open innovation, our research high lights the importance of employing IT and drawing knowledge from external sources together to achieve the success of open innovation projects.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

Before discussing the implications of this study, we need to specify the limitations and potential future directions for research. First, this study surveyed only open innovation project directors as the primary target of this study. A few quantitative studies on open innovation are solely based on project directors, thereby rendering this study interesting and valuable [35]. However, we realize that the results may be triangulated using other data sources. Future research could seek to complement this study by surveying other open-innovation project participants. Moreover, further research can triangulate the self-reported perception measurement with real performance of the new product, such as revenues and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from its introduction. Second, the sample population for our data consisted of high-technology <sup>fi</sup>rms in China. However, this should not be an issue because prior research with data collected in China (e.g., [71] does not suggest systematic di<sup>f</sup>erences from the research with a sample of Western <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., [31] in terms of innovation collaboration. Thus, a cross-country study could be conducted to further replicate this research model, as di<sup>f</sup>erences in culture, economy, and institutional pressures could a<sup>f</sup>ect an individual’s usage of a system. Third, the control variables included in our study (e.g., project size, project cost, and project complexity) are crucial and may have interaction relationships with other factors to in<sup>fl</sup>uence open innovation project performance. Future research could study the project- or task-related control variables in depth, which may provide further implications.

## 5.3. Theoretical implications

This study is expected to theoretically contribute to the areas of IT and innovation, open innovation, and absorptive capacity. First, our research contributes to the IT and innovation literature by <sup>fi</sup>rst providing an in-depth theoretical understanding of open innovation and its associated challenges. This study attempts to theoretically establish the link between IT-enabled absorptive capacity and open innovation performance. While engaging in open innovation may be a growing trend, a stronger theoretical foundation is needed to extract the potential it o<sup>f</sup>ers. IS research has much to contribute to the development of this strategy due to the pivotal role of digital technologies in enabling open innovation initiatives. Our study contributes to the open innovation literature by exposing the roles played by IT-enabled absorptive capacity. With the increasing trend of open and collaborative innovation, our e<sup>f</sup>orts help establish the foundation for subsequent research.

Second, this study serves as one of the earliest works in comprehensively de<sup>fi</sup>ning openness in open innovation project and in vestigating its di<sup>f</sup>erent in<sup>fl</sup>uential roles. Along with the existing literature on developing a comprehensive and rich conceptualization of openness (e.g., [28,72,29], this study examines ideation openness and implementation openness in an open innovation context. This study contributes to the existing research on openness by conceptually and empirically distinguishing openness into two dimensions (i.e., ideation openness and implementation openness). Furthermore, previous literature on innovation openness has contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Although some studies $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . , } }$ [73,61] emphasize the positive impact of openness, others warn of its downside. Considering that innovation openness during the idea implementation phase has been overlooked in prior literature and its e<sup>f</sup>ect is not yet integrated into the broader context of open innovation, this study is placed in a di<sup>f</sup>erent manner. We classi <sup>fi</sup>ed two dimensions of openness and proposed its focus on di<sup>f</sup>erent performance outcomes. This study contributes to the open innovation literature by investigating the di<sup>f</sup>erent impacts of ideation openness and implementation openness on open innovation project performance. Furthermore, these results point to the unexpected conclusion that for projects with higher ideation openness, IT-enabled absorptive capacity will support the development of more innovative products, whereas for projects with higher implementation openness, introducing the new product to the market is faster with IT-enabled absorptive capacity.

Finally, this study expands the reconceptualization of the absorptive capacity theory of [43] by invoking it in the IT-enabled open innovation context. The study systematically establishes that the absorptive capa city in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the open innovation performance can be partly created and augmented through IT. This study enables us to theoretically and empirically demonstrate the roles and importance of IT-enabled ab sorptive capacity in driving open innovation among <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 5.4. Practical implications

This study also provides important practical implications for open innovation management. Notwithstanding the growing trend of organizational open innovation and the promised bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of exploiting external knowledge, limited managerial guidance exists for practitioners to e<sup>f</sup>ectively implement the open innovation model in organizations to achieve desirable outcomes. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings enable open innovation practitioners to mindfully consider openness as well as IT facilitators to enhance innovation performance. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings emphasize the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value of IT in open innovation and practitioners should understand the fundamental roles played by IT. The central contention is that being open to external knowledge sources by itself will not enhance innovation outcomes directly. The implementation of IT in open innovation strategies is imperative and quite impactful. The position of the Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer should be given substantial attention in the open innovation context and can be elevated from a secondary role to the champion of open innovation implementation. An appreciation of the roles of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in open innovation projects will help organizations mindfully invest and deploy IT in future endeavors. For instance, <sup>fi</sup>rms can use environmental scanning IT (e.g., business intelligence) to explore external knowledge that can bene<sup>fi</sup>t with more creative new products. Some visualization IT (e.g., CAD and CAM) should be employed to facilitate inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm learning for an e<sup>fi</sup>cient process.

Second, managers should note that the success of open innovation lays in the e<sup>f</sup>ective control of open innovation project openness during the idea generation and idea implementation phases. Various openness strategies should be employed to achieve di<sup>f</sup>erent innovation out comes. If the new product speed to market is critical, managers should consider deploying IT to build absorptive capacity and being more open during the implementation phase. However, if the new product innovativeness is the objective, managers should pay attention to both ITenabled absorptive capacity and be more open during the ideation phase.

## 6. Conclusion

This study serves as an important attempt to investigate the interactive relationship between IT-enabled absorptive capacity and open ness in open innovation project performance. This research system atically examines the supporting role of IT-enabled absorptive capacity in open innovation projects. We also conceptually and empirically distinguished between ideation openness and implementation open ness. This distinction will enable us to develop nuanced and in-depth arguments on how <sup>fi</sup>rms can maximize the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts provided by drawing knowledge from external sources. Therefore, practitioners should be mindful in deploying e<sup>f</sup>ective ITs and leveraging di<sup>f</sup>erent extents of openness during the two phases to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the desired outcomes.
