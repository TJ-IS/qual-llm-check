# Theoretical approaches to gender and IT: examining some Australian evidence

Gail Ridley\* & Judy Young<sup>†</sup>

\*School of Accounting and Corporate Governance, University of Tasmania, Hobart, Tasmania, Australia, email: gail.ridley@utas.edu.au, and <sup>†</sup>School of Computing and Information Systems, University of Tasmania, Hobart, Tasmania, Australia, email: judy.young@utas.edu.au

Abstract. Researchers have sought a theoretical perspective to explain the under-representation of women in the information technology (IT) workforce of many nations. Gender imbalance limits the size and skill sets of IT human resources. The essentialist theory, social construction theory and individual differences theory of gender and IT have been proposed to account for the IT gender gap. This study examined evidence for these theories to explain this gap through content analysis of articles published in the national newspaper, The Australian, over three time periods. Newspaper articles report implicit theoretical perspectives on IT and gender and influence the views of the Australian public, including women. While evidence to support all theories was found over the three periods, the essentialist theory was dominant. Increased utility of the individual differences theory to account for the IT gender gap was seen in 2007–2008. The primary contribution of this study is to provide evidence that suggests that theoretical approaches, whether implicit or explicit, shape how people understand the underrepresentation of women in the IT workforce. Media interventions are proposed to help redress the imbalance through increased awareness.

Keywords: theoretical approaches, gender and IT, Australia, IT gender gap, gender theory

## INTRODUCTION

The under-representation of women in the information technology (IT) workforce is perennial (Gallivan, 2003). The problem affects both the recruitment and retention of women in the industry (Trauth et al., 2009). Its consequences are numerous, far-reaching and significant. Not only are women an underutilised resource with abilities and skills needed by the IT industry, under-representation also limits their career choices (Adya & Kaiser, 2005).

Although the issue has attracted considerable research attention over the years (Trauth et al., 2008a), it has been complex to understand and address (Robertson et al., 2001). Trauth & Quesenberry (2006) attributed the complexity to difficulty in identifying one root cause.

Developing theoretical perspectives will facilitate data interpretation and lead to effective interventions (Trauth et al., 2009). However, many early studies into the disproportional underrepresentation of women in the IT workforce either lacked any theoretical basis (as identified by Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006), or the theory was implicit (as confirmed by Trauth et al., 2009), i.e. not directly expressed.

Support for the limited theory proposed in this area has usually been derived from sources close to women, and relates to IT work or training. Examples of this kind of research include Riemenschneider et al. (2006) and Trauth & Howcroft (2006). Data to support theory in the area appear not to have been gathered more widely. However, the societal messages in which women are immersed influence their involvement in the IT workforce (Quesenberry et al., 2006) and others.

The aim of this paper is to investigate implicit support in an Australian context for three theories to explain women’s under-representation in the IT workforce. The term ‘theory’ is used in this paper to refer to ‘Theory for Explaining’ (Gregor, 2006), which aids understanding by showing ‘how the world may be viewed’ (p. 624). The study sought to contribute to the literature by examining evidence on whether theoretical perspectives (either explicit or implicit) influence the ways that gender differences in the IT workforce can be understood by Australian society.

This paper will first discuss the gender gap in the IT industry before reviewing popular theories put forward to account for the problem. Then the methodology used for evaluating support for the theories, results and proposed interventions will be presented and discussed.

## GENDER GAP IN THE IT INDUSTRY

Redressing the gender imbalance in the IT industry is motivated by social inclusion reasons and to achieve better business outcomes (Pless & Maak, 2004). Creative problem solving and design, as well as an understanding of human behaviour, are critical for IT professionals (Adya & Kaiser, 2005). A diverse IT workforce increases the likelihood that these skills will be available to meet the varied needs of IT end-users (Florida & Gates, 2002).

Although the problem is most marked in Western nations, it has been described as a global one (Trauth et al., 2009). Women constitute just 14% of the Indian IT workforce (Pande, 2006), while only 20% of German and UK computing professionals are women (Downie et al., 2004). In many countries, women’s participation in the IT industry has declined from low levels. For example, in 2008, women held 26% of US professional IT positions, a decline from 36% in 1991 (National Center for Women & IT, 2010). This decline happened while the IT industry diversified (Adya, 2008), although broadening the IT skills in demand should assist women in finding employment. Even after joining the US IT industry, women left three times faster than men (Wardell et al., 2005).

Australia’s liberal market economy (Waring & Edwards, 2008) has a high labour turnover (Parker, 2004). The World Economic Forum ranked Australia as one of 10 leading nations in reducing gender inequality (Theage.com.au, 2005). Approximately 3.6% of the Australian workforce worked in information and communications technology (ICT) positions in 2006 compared with 2.5% for Europe and 2.8% for the USA (Department of Communication, Information Technology and the Arts (DCITA), 2006). Management, interpersonal and com munication skills became more important for ICT workers in Australia, while 52% had an ICT-related degree and 13% had a technical and further education qualification as reported by DCITA (2006).

Despite Australia’s achievements, it still has a gender imbalance in its IT workforce. A study of IT and multimedia companies found segregation by gender for IT positions, even where mixed skills were required (Roan & Whitehouse, 2007). In 2005, a smaller proportion of females was employed as ICT professionals in every industry category, while women filled 18% to 26% of the three ICT industry workforce categories (DCITA. 2006). The Egua Opportunity in the Workplace Agency found that 31% of employees working in Australian IT sector organisations with over 100 staff were women, while 24% of the same organisations had female managers (DCITA, 2006). Drawing on Australian Bureau of Statistics figures, the Australian Information Industry Association announced that around 18% of Australian ICT industry employees in 2007 were women, while the figures were 23.6% and 16%, respectively, in 2001 and 2004 (Moon, 2007).

The disproportional representation of women in IT has attracted attention from both aca demia and industry. Academics have developed theory to better understand the issue (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2006) and to guide interventions.

## THEORIES OF GENDER AND IT

Three theories used to account for the gender imbalance in the IT workforce, the essentialist and social construction theories and the individual differences theory of gender and IT (IDTGIT), are now reviewed. Although other theories have been proposed to explain the perplexing relationship between technology and gender (Trauth et al., 2009), these were chosen for consistency with current influential research.

## Essentialist theory

Essentialist theory, which stems from psychology, posits that men and women have fixed and different natures (see critique in Wajcman, 1991). Adya (2008) reviewed claims made regard ing how essentialist theory affects women’s participation in the IT workforce. These are through biological and psychological differences, and in bringing different perspectives to the workplace.

Gender and IT research that is situated in an essentialist theoretical perspective includes Venkatesh & Morris (2000). These authors found that differences in women’s IT response and use differed to men’s even after controlling for confounding variables from organisational behaviour research. Fetter (1985) explained differences in computer literacy from an implicit essentialist position. Venkatesh et al. (2000) accounted for computer aptitude and anxiety through inherent gender differences, implicitly drawing upon essentialism. Gender and IT research that is underpinned by essentialist theory has usually taken a positivist approach, including these three studies.

An example that illustrates the flaw in applying the essentialist theory to gender and IT work is the contribution of Grace Hopper. Although computing was seen exclusively as a male domain, Hopper had sole responsibility for programming one of the first electro-mechanical computers. Hopper later developed a compiler that remains a key component in modern computer programming (San Diego SuperComputer Center, 1997). Essentialist theory is not able to explain Hopper’s contributions to the computing industry.

Because of its categorisation of work role according to gender, essentialist theory has been criticised as being simplistic (Adya, 2008), one-dimensional (Trauth, 2002) and deterministic (Trauth, 2002; Adam et al., 2004; Trauth et al., 2004; Howcroft & Trauth, 2008).

## Social construction theory

Drawn from sociology, social construction theory proposes that human actions are the product of the culture in which people are born and raised (Berger & Luckmann, 1966).

Most studies into gender and IT with a theoretical underpinning have used social construction theory (Trauth, 2002; Trauth et al., 2004). In their examination of learning organisations, Nielsen et al. (2000) concluded that male values negatively impacted on the IT educational attainment of female students. Alvesson & Billing (1997) demonstrated how the structure and practice of modern organisations were shaped by gender, while Adya & Kaiser (2005) found that the primary influence on girls choosing to enter the IT workforce was their fathers. Empirical evidence for the social construction theory in gender and IT research has usually utilised interpretivist methods (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2007), such as the Australian study by Nielsen et al. (2000).

Adya (2008) examined the influence of the same theory by comparing South Asian and native-born US women working in US IT positions. The results indicated the influence of social constructs on the career choice and experiences of the two groups. Male domination had less impact on the Asian women, while the native-born women reported discomfort in their work situations. Adya (2008) argued that acceptance of the workplace situation by the Asian IT employees provided evidence of social influences on female participation in the IT workforce.

Trauth & Quesenberry (2007) summarised the findings of social construction theory research on ways to assist female participation in the IT workforce. The first was to offer support structures through mentoring, support groups and role models, and second, to communicate positive messages about women working in IT to counteract stereotypical images through societal messages. A third approach was adapting the IT work domain for women, while the fourth was enabling women and girls to develop as a social group in cyberspace.

As societal messages are not received and addressed identically by all women (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2007), social construction theory has been challenged for not accommodating individual differences (Trauth, 2002). Consequently, research that draws upon social construction theory has resulted in contradictory findings (Trauth et al., 2009), including in different national contexts (Weil & Rosen, 1995).

## Individual Differences Theory of Information Technology and Gender (IDTGIT)

Trauth developed the IDTGIT (Trauth, 2002; 2006), which places attention on the differences within genders rather than between them (Trauth & Quesenberry, 2007). The IDTGIT extends on the cultural and environmental forces of social construction theory by recognising that individual differences, in combination with socio-cultural influences, shape the involvement of individuals with IT (Trauth, 2006; Trauth & Quesenberry, 2007). The IDTGIT conceptualises gender and IT:

. . . at two different levels: (1) women as members of a group who encounter group-level (ie gender) biases; and at the same time (2) women as individuals having distinct personalities, and experiencing different individual and socio-cultural influences. [Quesenberry & Trauth, 2008, p. 5]

The IDTGIT is better able to account for the contradictory evidence sometimes found in gender and culture studies that use essentialist or social construction theories (Trauth et al., 2009). The IDTGIT includes three constructs: individual identity (including personal demographics and career items sub-constructs); individual influences (including personal characteristics and personal influences); and environmental influences (including cultural influences, economic influences, policy influences and infrastructure influences) (Trauth et al., 2009).

As an example of an IDTGIT study, although Ash et al. (2009) found differences in occupational personalities between genders, they also found within-gender differences. Both types affected IT career preferences. Morgan et al. (2004) examined women’s reactions to informal social networks in the IT workplace to find their strategies varied. In a third study, Trauth et al. (2008a) used data from four nations to demonstrate that the themes of parenting, family and economics, which impacted IT career decisions, were experienced differently by women in different countries.

Trauth et al. (2008b) analysed data on female IT professionals in three US regions to find that two environmental factors, economy and culture, influenced female participation in the IT workforce. The findings demonstrated how individual women experience different regional environmental influences and have individual responses to them.

Several studies that collected Australian data have been influenced by the IDTGIT, including Trauth et al. (2003), Trauth et al. (2008a) and Mackrell et al. (2009). Empirical studies underpinned by the IDTGIT are typically interpretivist and collect data through: interview, participant observation and/or analysis of documents associated with the participants (e.g. Trauth et al., 2004; 2008a; Adya, 2008).

This section has examined three prominent theories that have been used to explain the more limited involvement of women in the IT workforce. The first theory, essentialist theory, points to the biological and psychological differences between the genders. Social construction theory, the second theory examined, posits that environmental factors and life experiences influence behaviour and career choices. In proposing the third theory, the IDTGIT, Trauth (2002) extended on the second theory, enhancing its explanatory power for women’s involvement in IT careers. The IDTGIT acknowledges that individual differences, as well as sociocultural factors, influence women’s participation in the IT workforce. The next section will detai the method used to address the objective of this research.

## METHODOLOGY

This study investigated the following research question: do theoretical perspectives shape how Australian society understands gender influences on the IT workforce?

A subsidiary aim is to consider whether theoretical perspectives in the same context varied by the demand for IT workers.

## Epistemology

Typical research in the area draws upon, and reflects, perspectives of individuals working or training in the IT industry. Sometimes, studies collated individual perspectives, synthesising results across studies or nations (as in Trauth et al., 2008a). It is challenging for such studies to examine the wider societal viewpoint. Yet as socio-cultural forces are acknowledged in two of the theories discussed earlier, there is value in examining these forces from a broad rather than an individual perspective. Evidence from society that corroborates collated individual views through triangulation, using a non-interpretivist epistemology, will strengthen claims for the theories. But more than this, awareness of the broader societa view will increase understanding of the socio-cultural context that acts upon women and their relationship with IT.

This study sought public evidence of the utility of three theories to account for female IT employment in Australia as reflected through analysis of a mass communication medium. Journalists reflect the ‘norms and values of the cultural context . . . in which they work, and . . . draw on . . . the hegemonic ideology when constructing news’ (Noakes & Wilkins, 2002, p. 651). Analysis of media discourse reveals both the pervading beliefs “of the context in which it is revealed” (Isakhan, 2005) and how events and people are constructed (Pietikainen & Hujanen, 2003). Therefore, newspaper articles are artefacts that make visible society’s perspectives and behaviours. The articles also influence the public, including women in their relationship with IT, in a feedback mechanism. As the three theories are proposed to account for the IT gender gap, it was expected that evidence of them would be found in an analysis of artefacts from newspaper discourse. Therefore, a positivist epistemology was used to investigate the extent to which the three theoretical positions shape how Australian society understands gender influences on the IT workforce as reflected in the articles.

Although feminist research is usually (but not always) non-positivist (Taylor, 1998), this gender study is not feminist research as it meets only three of the five requirements that underlie the core of feminist methodology (Taylor, 1998). That is, while this study had a gender focus, examined women’s everyday experiences and included an action component, it did not emphasise reflexivity or participatory methods.

## Data collection procedures

To limit regional bias, Australia’s only ‘nation-wide’ newspaper, The Australian, which is owned by a subsidiary of the international News Corporation, was chosen for data collection.

Analysis for 2001, 2004 and 2007–2008 allowed 3-year snapshots. The periods were chosen to examine whether there was a link between the highest (2001), lowest (2004) and variable demand (2007–2008) for IT staff recently, and theoretical perspectives on IT and gender in articles published over the same periods. Data collection for 2007 was extended into 2008 as few relevant articles were found. Full-year data were unavailable prior to 2001 or afte 2008 from the source used at the time of data collection.

The database, Australia/New Zealand Reference Centre, was searched for newspaper articles that included ‘information technology’ and ‘woman’ or ‘girl’ or ‘female’ and derivatives, using wild cards. The term ‘information technology’ was chosen instead of alternatives for consistency with Trauth’s publications. One of the researchers reviewed the articles for relevance. Relevant articles were saved as a text file to a folder for each period, stripped of unrelated header information and then compressed into zip files for analysis.

## Data analysis procedures

Data analysis utilised some of the capabilities of Leximancer Pty. Ltd., Brisbane, Australia, a sophisticated automated content analysis tool (Smith & Humphreys, 2006). Leximancer enables tracking back to original text passages, as in an audit trail. Experiments have shown that concepts derived through Leximancer are similar to those obtained through manual coding (Martin & Rice, 2007).

A limitation of Leximancer is that its graphical display output from analysis of heterogeneous data such as is found in newspaper articles may be unstable. Therefore, this study used Leximancer for analysis of articles up to, but excluding, the clustering of concepts into themes and the mapping phase. Manual examination of Leximancer’s output using content analysis was undertaken, seeking evidence of the theoretical constructs. A study of ICT graduate attributes in the USA and in Australia also combined manual and Leximancer analysis (Debuse & Lawley, 2009).

Two changes were made to Leximancer’s default settings, which meant that the unit of analysis varied from a phrase to paragraphs. The number of sentences per block was changed to one as many articles featured one-sentence paragraphs. The option to break analysis at paragraphs was disabled as discourse often carried across paragraphs

The concepts generated by Leximancer and the passages they derived from were examined individually in relation to the remainder of the article, using an a priori coding scheme developed from the literature (see Appendix). All these passages were classified as illustrating a theory (and possibly IDTGIT constructs and sub-constructs) or as unclassifiable. In places, reference to more than one theory (and IDTGIT construct or sub-construct) was found in one article. Passages were considered unclassifiable if they were duplicates or if the passage was not relevant to gender and IT. Totals were calculated for each category in each period and across periods.

One limitation of artefact analysis is that it is impossible to seek additional information through questioning. Consequently, the record provided in the articles of actions and behaviours may be incomplete, which may affect reported frequencies. However, under-reporting o theories and constructs/sub-constructs will not threaten a finding of their existence.

Although use of Leximancer does not require reliability analysis (Smith & Humphreys, 2006), evaluation was needed of the researcher coding for the theories. A second person trained in research methods independently categorised a large sample of the passages into the categories.

The Leximancer concepts for each period were scrutinised by drilling down into the sup porting passages to determine which had potential for theory classification. Only the concepts women and girls (for 2001 and 2004) and women and females (for 2007–2008) were found to be useful.

The primary researcher read all instances of the concepts in the passages from the identified articles before categorising them using the coding scheme. Some articles were coded into more than one category, i.e. two theories or different IDTGIT constructs/sub-constructs.

After considerable discussion about the meaning of the theories, constructs and sub constructs, and undertaking a pilot, a second person classified a large random sample of the same passages independently, using the same coding categories. After comparing the codes of both individuals, all except one of the codes in the 2001 data set and two of the codes in the 2004 data set were the same. These differences were resolved after discussion. As the coding outcomes from the two researchers were so similar, the reliability of the coding was regarded to be within acceptable limits without formal testing.

The social construction theory had the highest proportion of textual passages that illustrated a theoretical approach, with 53 (60%) of the total. Another 20 (23%) of the passages supported the IDTGIT, while 15 (17%) illustrated the essentialist theory.

Examples of the extracted passages are now presented to illustrate their categorisation and connection to the theory. Each passage is identified by publication year, concept and passage number.

## Essentialist theory

Support for the essentialist theory was identified in 15 passages. These passages were categorised by how essentialist theory impacts on participation in IT work: biological differ ences, psychological differences and bringing perspectives to the workplace that are different to those of the other gender. Examples to illustrate each are discussed next.

## Biological differences

Essentialist theory suggests that women’s biological characteristics impact on their capacity and willingness to participate in the IT workforce. In the following passage, biological characteristics are seen as affecting women’s intellectual capacity for work:

As Steven Pinker argues in his book The Blank Slate: The Modern Denial of Human Nature, the high proportion of men among engineers, physicists and similar occupations reflects their greater average ability and interest in maths. [2004: women: passage 15]

## Psychological differences

Proponents of the essentialist theory point to psychological differences that impinge on women’s ability and preparedness to participate in IT employment. This difference is exemplified by the next passage:

It’s not malicious – if girls find something broken, they ask how do I get help to fix it, whereas boys won’t, they will try to fix it themselves. [2004: girls: passage 8]

## Bringing different perspectives to the workplace

In a third sub-category of essentialist theory, women contribute to the IT workforce by introducing different perspectives to those of men. Several passages were found that illustrated this idea, including the following:

Human relations manager Gayle Adams says ‘we want more women [in IT] because they bring different perspectives and fresh ideas’. [2001: women: passage 14]

Support was found for essentialist theory and its three sub-categories in articles over the three periods. However, passages linked to the essentialist theory accounted for the smalles proportion of the three theories.

## Social construction theory

Support for social construction theory was found in 53 of the 88 identified passages regarding gender and IT. Examples of the four approaches to social construction theory identified as facilitating women to participate in the IT workforce are presented later.

## Support structures

Passages that illustrated each of the three different forms of support structures were identified, namely, mentors, support groups and role modelling. For brevity, only an example to suppor mentoring is provided.

Evidence of mentoring approaches for female students, and in the IT workplace, was found in several passages. The aim of the mentoring was to help assimilate females in participating in IT. A passage that refers to mentoring in an educational environment is provided later:

The IGNITE program [an accelerated pathway for gifted students in South Australia], set up to encourage more women into . . . information technology, is worth \$10 000 across her degree and includes a mentoring program. [2007\_8: women: passage 50]

Two additional subcategories of support structures were noted when analysing the passages. These were support programmes including positive discrimination, and education and training Many references to support programmes and strategies appeared in the passages, an example of which appears in the next paragraph:

The report says women in engineering and information technology should be retained as an equity group with targets of 40 percent participation . . . (2004: women: passage 27)

Supporting evidence for the second additional subcategory, female education and training, appears next:

Australian Information Industry Association chief executive Sheryle Moon said gender specific training on negotiation and self-marketing skills would also be valuable to women. [2007\_8: women: passage 29]

Many examples were found to support the existence of five subcategories of support structures from the social construction theory. The second approach, societal messages, is discussed next.

## Societal messages

Two subcategories illustrating societal messages as forms of social construction theory were identified. The first was most common and concerned negative attitudes acquired by females about IT work, while another passage pointed to industry’s negative images about women working in IT. A sample passage of negative images held by girls is presented in the next paragraph:

‘There is this myth of the technological geek, and young girls are hardly going to choose to go into IT when it is perceived that technology is the private domain of anti-social, boring male geeks,’ she says. [2004: girls: passage 2]

## Adapting the IT work (or education) domain

Other passages that illustrated social construction theory referred to the need to adapt the IT work domain or educational environment to suit females, which exemplified the third of the four categories for this theory. An example from industry is provided:

Organisations need to be aware of the different, intrinsic needs that women desire in an idea job particularly job flexibility, family friendly policies and work-life balance and make them available accordingly. [2007\_8: women: passage 6]

Enabling women as a social group in cyberspace

Many passages enabling women and girls to develop as a social group in cyberspace were found, from industry and school, providing support for the fourth and final category of socia construction theory. An industry example follows:

. . . the IT for women website [is] to encourage more women into the industry. [2001: women: passage 25]

Many passages were identified from The Australian articles to suggest that social construction theory enhanced understanding of women’s participation in the IT workforce. Although these passages could be grouped into the four categories, two additional subcategories of the first, support structures, were identified from analysis. These were support programmes including positive discrimination and education and training. The next section discusses the third theory used to explain women’s participation in the workforce, the IDTGIT.

## IDTGIT

The 20 passages that supported the IDTGIT were classified using the three IDTGIT constructs. While some IDTGIT components are common to the social construction theory, an important distinction is that the differences within a gender are emphasised in the former theory rather than the differences between males and females, as in the latter theory. Although the IDTGIT is not characterised by individual differences alone, this criterion is necessary (as implied by the IDTGIT’s title). Therefore, a passage was not considered to illustrate the IDTGIT unless this criterion was evident.

Each of the three IDTGIT constructs and their subcategories were examined, in turn, for supporting evidence from the newspaper passages. No passages were found that illustrated the first construct, Individual Identity, from the IDTGIT, or its two subcategories of persona demographics and career items. The remaining two constructs are discussed next:

## Individual influences

Many passages were found to support the second IDTGIT construct, Individual Influences, and its two subcategories, personal characteristics and personal influences. The identification of multiple passages suggests that personal preference was an important variable to help understand women's participation in the IT workforce. The passages were grouped into the personal characteristics subcategory of this construct:

Personal characteristics. The following passage provides one illustration of many that showed how personal choice influenced girls’ relationship with IT:

The real problem with girls is that they are just not choosing to come to IT. [2004: girls: passage 15]

Personal influences (mentors, role models, experiences and significant life experiences). Sup port for the way in which role models were effective for some women is seen in the following passage, which illustrates the personal influences subcategory of the two proposed for individual influences:

We found that having female students talk to girls makes them realise that there are women out there doing interesting things in IT. [2004: girls: passage 13]

This example was one of many that referred to the contribution of mentors and role models, which supported the IDTGIT to explain the gender gap in the Australian IT workforce.

Support for the utility of the third IDTGIT construct, environmental influences, is examined next.

## Environmental influences

Four subcategories of the IDTGIT environmental influences construct have been proposed. The following passage illustrating the sub-construct, cultural influences, pointed to national culture as influencing women’s attitudes to IT employment. It was one of several examples identified.

## Cultural influences.

It reported the results of yet more research that found that personality differences between men and women were smaller in traditional cultures such as India or Zimbabwe compared with The Netherlands and the US. [2007\_8: women: passage 9]

Economic influences. The following example illustrates both the economic influences and policy influences (of the region where the work is located) categories from the environmental influences construct. The example illustrates how different permutations of the IDTGIT’s constructs and sub-constructs act on different women in an individualistic way. The passage was regarded as a manifestation of the IDTGIT rather than the social construction theory as it implied that individuals had choices.

You’d think the more family-friendly policies and richer the economy, the more women should behave like men, but it’s the opposite. [2007\_8: women: passage 32]

No passage was found to illustrate the subcategory of infrastructure influences from the environmental influences construct. Overall, few passages were found that illustrated the economic, policy and infrastructure influences subcategories from this third construct.

In summary, it can be seen that passages were found in the newspaper articles to suppor two of the three ITDGIT constructs, including evidence of their integration.

## DISCUSSION

Evidence was found from analysis that supported all three theories, with greatest support for the social construction theory and least for the essentialist theory. This finding suggests that implicit credence was given to all three theories over all periods by different groups and individuals in Australian society. Moreover, the Australian public, employers, women and girls have been subject to, and influenced by, messages communicated in the media that reflect all three theories.

The social construction theory was the most popular for explaining the relationship between gender and the IT workforce over the periods examined. While support for it and the essentialist theory changed little over the periods, with marginal dominance of the social construction theory most recently, support for the IDTGIT increased considerably during 2007–2008. This finding suggests a recent change in the way that Australian society understands the gender gap in the IT workforce. In broad terms, although Australian society appears to remain uncertain of the best explanation for the gender gap in IT work, recently, two alternative but related explanations influenced women, girls, employers and the broad Australian community. It will be interesting to extend the investigation to future periods to determine whether increased relevance of the IDTGIT continues.

A limitation of the method used is the risk of under-reporting theory and construct occurrence. As the data analysed were artefacts, incomplete detail may have been reported. To illustrate, it is possible that actions and behaviours were influenced by the individual identity construct of the ITDGIT but not reported or reported incompletely, leading to no evidence of this construct being found during analysis. For the same reason, although an example of the integration of two sub-constructs from the ITDGIT was discussed earlier, it is possible that additional integration of constructs/sub-constructs from the ITDGIT has also been under-reported.

However, as support for all three theories was found in each period, under-reporting will not threaten the study aim to investigate evidence of the theories in newspaper discourse. Moreover, as the authors assumed that under-reporting occurred to a similar extent for all theories, it is argued that the contribution of each theory as a proportion of the total will be similar.

Another limitation of the study is that analysis was restricted to one newspaper from one nation. However, as The Australian is allied with a major international newspaper publisher, i is possible that the Australian perspective is not unique in the Western world

Both industry and educational institutions have experienced cyclic demand for IT in past decades. Managers have most motivation to understand and address the gender disparity in the IT workforce at times of strong demand for staff. Therefore, the analysis periods were chosen to allow assessment of whether application of the theory in the newspaper articles examined was linked to demand for IT employees. The frequency of the theories changed little between 2001 and 2004, while demand for IT staff was high in 2001, lower in 2004 and variable during 2007–2008. This research suggests that support for the three theories to account for the gender gap in IT is independent of the demand for IT employees.

## Proposed interventions

Although this study has focused on theoretical issues, much of its value lies in the potential of the findings to redress the under-representation of women in the IT industry. Analysis suggests that from 2001, each of the theories has underpinned some understanding of gender and IT in Australian society. Media messages are likely to influence women, organisations and Australian society. Awareness of how assumptions impact behaviours and actions, and the implications of some of these actions and behaviours for women and the Australian IT industry, is necessary before effective change can take place.

One suggested intervention from the study will utilise the same setting used for data gathering to influence Australian society, as newspaper articles have the capacity to both report on, and influence, behaviours and actions. The proposed intervention is to distribute a media release that reports the findings (or those of a future extended study) in layman’s language to explain the three different ‘approaches’ to gender and IT reflected in the newspaper articles, presenting implications of each for individuals, organisations, the IT workforce and the Australian economy. An engaging way of interpreting the findings for the public would be to use mini case studies on three women who have each experienced actions or behaviours consistent with one of the three theories and report how these experiences affected each woman and their organisation. When interacting with the media, encouraging the reporting of diverse cases would reveal both the complexity of the influential factors, and the dynamics of the relationships, between gender and IT.

The IDTGIT acknowledges the influence of national characteristics on women’s participation in the IT workforce. Australia has different national characteristics even when compared with other Western democracies. Interventions that are tailored to Australia’s characteristics are likely to be more successful. As Australia is characterised by a high staff turnover, the awareness-raising media releases referred to earlier should refer to the relationship between the ‘approaches’ identified in Australian society as revealed in the media and the implication of each on IT staff turnover for organisations.

Another related intervention is to target the media’s awareness of its capacity to influence the views of the public by distributing a media release which evaluates the impact of its reporting on IT and gender.

The perceived credibility of those releasing the media releases should be considered carefully as it will impact both on how the message is received and the interest generated. Media releases that have the endorsement of relevant high-profile organisations or peak industry bodies, such as Skills Australia or the Australian Computer Society, are recom mended. Obtaining funding for a future related study from an organisation of this kind is likely to maximise intervention effectiveness through greater reach in the dissemination of the findings through the media.

## CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated implicit support for three theories to explain the under-representation of women in the Australian IT workforce: the essentialist theory, social construction theory and IDTGIT. The study analysed data published in The Australian newspaper over three periods. Analysis aimed to provide a reflection on the views and behaviours of the broad Australian society, seeking to uncover the perspectives disseminated to, and reported by it, and to investigate the fit to the theories.

The findings offer the potential to confirm and refine some of the theories. Support was found for the three categories adopted for the essentialist theory, without refinement. Clear suppor was found for all four categories adopted for the social construction theory with evidence of an additional two categories: support programmes including positive discrimination and education and training. Further research may examine the scope to extend this latter theory.

While many of the articles found supported the IDTGIT, no data were found for the individua identity construct, or the infrastructure influences sub-construct of the environmental influences construct. These omissions may have arisen from a limitation of the artefact analysis method, the under-reporting of details. Previous research studies in Western settings have found that the individual identity construct, including the demographic characteristic of ethnicity, does affect women’s participation in the IT workforce (e.g. Adya, 2008).

It is recommended that future work extend analysis to a longer and more complete data set. Future investigation may also compare Australian results with those of other nations.

This study sought to make a contribution to theory that accounts for the under-representation of women in the IT workforce. Its primary contribution has been to demonstrate that theoretica perspectives, whether explicit or implicit, direct how individuals understand gender differences in the IT workforce. Unusually, the findings have enabled analysis of theoretical evidence from society as the unit of analysis, while most related prior research has used individuals or organisations as the unit of analysis. The different method used has allowed triangulation through comparison of results to confirm, or suggest a need to refine, existing theory.

Media messages influence and disseminate messages, both negative and positive, about gender and IT. Awareness must precede long-term behavioural change. Interventions incorporated into mainstream media are likely to be more effective than those which aim to change behaviours in particular settings. This study has proposed interventions that have potential to reach a broad cross-section of Australian society to raise awareness of the issue and its impact on women, the IT industry and Australia.
