## 1. Introduction

Although the share of e-commerce in total U.S. retail sales has steadily increased from 0.7% to 4.6% between 2000 and 2012 (U.S. Census Bureau 2012), online retailers have yet to fully reap the benefits of online commerce. Some of the most frequently cited reasons why people hesitate to shop online relate to the lack of credible signals of quality and trustworthiness. Unlike brick-and-mortar stores, which have a physical presence and are usually located near their customers, online retailers may expose potential customers to a host of unknowns. Uncertainty about the quality of the products listed on the website, the security of payment process, the reliability of shipping, the protection of a customer’s privacy, and the credibility of service terms, among other concerns, are some of the substantial risks perceived by online shoppers.

Among the various mechanisms online retailers use to convince customers of their quality and trustworthiness, third-party assurance seals are among the longest and most pervasively used. To obtain an assurance seal, an online retailer typically goes through a certification process administered by the seal provider. Upon successful completion of this process, the retailer is permitted to display the assurance seal on its website. For instance, the TRUSTe<sup>1</sup> seal certifies that the online retailer has a privacy policy that outlines confidential treatment of shoppers’ private data. Likewise, VeriSign certifies that the payment process uses digital certificates and Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) connections.

Despite the popularity of assurance seals and a number of studies investigating their effectiveness, no consensus has been reached regarding their impact on online transaction outcomes. Kimery and McCord (2002) find that some online seals denoting privacy protection can enhance customer trust, whereas

McKnight et al. (2004) do not find any significant impact. The findings of Head and Hassanein (2002) and Hu et al. (2003) are similarly mixed, finding that only certain seals influence e-consumers’ behavior. McKnight et al. (2004) suggest that the ineffectiveness of assurance seals could be because many consumers simply do not understand their function. Mauldin and Arunachalam (2002) further suggest that presenting such quality assurance symbols to consumers who did not ask for them may actually generate suspicion.

One possible reason for these inconclusive and contradictory findings is that the studies above use either controlled experiments in artificial lab settings or surveys of consumer perceptions. The scientific community still lacks, however, the kind of empirical evidence necessary to understand the effects of assurance seals on transactional outcomes. This may be due to the lack of data and the difficulty of inferring causal effects based on secondary data.

In this study, we exploit a data set comprising realworld online purchase records in order to examine the impact of assurance seals on online transactions. To evaluate transaction outcome measures, we focus on the purchase conversion rate (i.e., the proportion of visits that end with a purchase), which is a crucial performance factor for online retailers (Moe 2003). The data set, generated by a major provider of assurance seals, includes 9,098 sessions from an online retailer’s website, conducted over five days in early December 2008. One key contribution to the empirical value of our data set is that it is generated from a randomized test: the seal provider, in agreement with the online retailer, conducted “A/B tests” during a specified period to measure the impact of its assurance seal on key site metrics. In this test, the retailer allowed the seal provider to display the seal to 50% of website visitors (referred to as As) and not the other 50% (the Bs). The visitors were randomly designated as either an A or a B, ensuring that the only differing variable between the two sets of visitors is the presence or absence of the seal. Thus, data from these A/B tests give us a valuable tool for examining the causal impact of the assurance seal on visitors’ shopping behavior.

This study makes two important contributions to the existing literature. First, previous research efforts attempting to quantify the value of online assurance seals were methodologically limited to surveys and lab experiments with student subjects and simulated purchase decisions. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to evaluate the effectiveness of assurance seals using real-world transactional data. Second, our data set comes from a randomized natural field experiment, which allows us to infer the seal’s causal impact.

## 2. Research Context

Online shoppers often face unfamiliar online retailers. By comparison, it is far easier for them to verify the identity and judge the trustworthiness of a brickand-mortar merchant since they can rely on common factors that often signal quality in traditional settings, such as premier location, investment in the store’s appearance, and personal communication with customers. However, these features are hard to replicate online (Ba 2001). Customers are also unable to inspect and verify merchandise prior to purchase as they can during an in-person transaction. Although a few large online retailers (such as Amazon.com) have managed to establish a strong identity and reputation through substantial investment in branding and advertising, this option is not viable or economical for most online retailers. As a result, most online retailers lack the branding and recognition that help mitigate consumers’ concerns about online shopping. Shoppers worry, for example, that the products will not be delivered or that the quality is not consistent with the description on the retailers’ web pages.

Many of these consumer concerns have legitimate causes. According to the Internet Crime Report (2010), of the 121,710 U.S.-based complaints referred to law enforcement agencies in 2010, the “nondelivery of merchandise and/or payment” category ranked the highest with 21.1% of all complaints. Online commerce has low barriers to entry, often allowing “fly-by-night” operators and low-quality merchants to masquerade as high-quality sellers. Government regulation of online commerce is currently inadequate to combat such abuses, and the lack of a central regulatory authority exacerbates information asymmetry problems between consumers and online retailers (Tang et al. 2008).

Taken together, all of these consumer concerns and vulnerabilities highlight the significant information asymmetries between buyers and sellers in online markets. Traditionally, markets with significant information asymmetries have resorted to qualitysignaling mechanisms (cues provided by a seller to reveal that seller’s true quality) to help reduce friction and the likelihood of market collapse (Akerlof 1970). A signaling mechanism refers to cues provided by a seller that reveal her true quality. Researchers have identified various external cues, including advertising, warranty, brand name, product price, and certification, among others, that can act as quality signals (Milgrom and Roberts 1986). Online retailers are aware of the importance of such quality signals, which online shoppers typically receive from three types of sources (Daignault et al. 2002), as summarized below.

The primary source of quality information is firstparty information, which is provided directly by the online retailer. The quality of the website’s design (Schlosser et al. 2005), presence of contact information, clearly displayed privacy policy (Tsai et al. 2011), performance reports, and trust-assuring arguments in the website (Kim and Benbasat 2006) may all be useful in addressing consumers’ concerns. Two operational variables, the technological capabilities of the website (Ba and Johansson 2008) and quality of online service (Field et al. 2004) have also been shown to correlate with customer satisfaction. First-party information, however, is limited in its capacity to influence consumers, who are aware that self-generated statements may be biased and that good web design does not always equal a good product.

Second-party information, another source of quality information, originates in the experience of previous shoppers and can be obtained through reputation systems and rating mechanisms (Dewan and Hsu 2004). The most commonly seen second-party mechanisms are consumer review websites that host customers’ comments on and ratings of retailers. Although such sources can provide detailed and timely information, they also have limitations: influenced by individual experience, they may be highly subjective, and they can also be noisy. Furthermore, the information posted is generally not verifiable.

Finally, third-party mechanisms—the focus of our study—endeavor to provide independent verification of a retailer’s quality. Such independent verification is usually provided by specialized certification companies such as buySAFE, TRUSTe, and VeriSign. These companies investigate online retailers regarding their privacy, security, and service quality, then approve those online retailers who meet the company’s quality standards to display the assurance seal on their websites. Alternatively or in addition, some agencies like the Better Business Bureau (BBB) may publish directories of trusted online retailers.

Certification companies can be classified into three major categories, according to consumer advisory site Trust Seal Review (http://trustsealreview.com). The first category consists of Privacy Seal Providers, such as TRUSTe and Trust Guard. These providers ensure that the online retailer has a privacy policy and treats shoppers’ private data confidentially. The second category includes Security Seal Providers, such as VeriSign and GeoTrust, that provide digital certificates and SSL connections. The third category comprises Business Verification Seal Providers, which include BBB and buySAFE. BBB investigates the identity of the online retailer and ensures that the retailer represents itself truthfully, whereas buySAFE verifies businesses identity and also enables bonded purchases. Although most seal providers focus on providing a single functionality, a few, like VeriSign and NexTag, provide multiple services. Figure 1 provides a sample of assurance seal logos.

## Related Studies

Although information asymmetry issues and the resulting lack of trust hinder the growth and functioning of online markets, such unregulated markets with significant information asymmetries also provide opportunities for businesses (Clemons 2007). Extensive studies have examined the role of secondparty feedback mechanisms in e-commerce; when the impact of reputation on price-related outcomes was examined, a generally positive relationship was found between the reputation of the seller and the resulting price of the transaction (Dewan and Hsu 2004). Dellarocas (2003, p. 1407) suggests that online reputation mechanisms “can be used as a technology for building trust in electronic markets.” Other studies also concur on the value of a strong online reputation: Pavlou and Gefen (2004) conclude that online feedback mechanisms reduce information asymmetry, and Walden (2000) provides evidence for the value of second-party feedback mechanisms in building trust. Ba and Pavlou (2002), using online field data, propose that buyers and sellers who are interested in long term profits are better off by being honest because cheating now will hurt their reputation in the future.

Compared to second-party feedback mechanisms, there are very few studies analyzing the role of third-party mechanisms in helping reduce information asymmetries and engendering trust in online settings. Most of the studies of third-party mechanisms such as certification and licensing examine their role in traditional markets. Within this body of research, one well-established stream examines certification (Lizzeri 1999) and its efficacy as a quality signaling mechanism, finding that third-party mechanisms such as certification can help reduce information asymmetries for buyers, generate new information for all market participants (Jin and Leslie 2003), and increase demand from buyers who otherwise may not have entered the market (Tirole 1988).

However, there exist few empirical studies of the role of third-party mechanisms, and the evidence of the effects of certification on market outcomes is mixed. Some studies document a positive relationship between certification and price and/or quality in thoroughbred racehorses (Wimmer and Chezum 2003) and in ISO9000 certified manufacturing programs (Terlaak and King 2006). Yet others (e.g., Kleiner and Kudrle 2000) find that third-party mechanisms do not necessarily improve outcomes for consumers.

Third-party quality information has also been studied in the specific context of the accounting field, where it strongly overlaps with the concepts of auditing and financial attestation. As defined by the American Accounting Association (1973, p. 18), auditing is “a systematic process of objectively obtaining and evaluating evidence regarding assertions about economic actions and events to ascertain the degree of correspondence between those assertions and established criteria and communication of the results to interested users.” Elliott (1997) suggests that auditing satisfies the vital and otherwise unmet information needs of decision makers in the financial marketplace. The auditor, as an independent third party, verifies and attests to the firm’s published data, providing assurance, mainly to the investors and creditors, of the reliability of that data. Despite the corporate accounting scandals of 2002 (Forbes 2002), Oliverio and Newman (2005) surveyed the CEOs of the largest publicly owned Fortune 500 firms in the United States and found that 92.5% still believe in the value of external auditing. Thus, the idea of third-party assurance as a mechanism for bridging the information gap in the traditional marketplace is well established in the accounting literature.

Regarding online markets, there is an emerging body of research examining the role of third-party mechanisms in addressing issues of information asymmetry, trust, and risk aversion. Dewan and Hsu (2004) compare the value of third-party certification mechanisms for stamps with second-party reputation mechanisms on eBay, and Jin and Kato (2007) examine the role of online certification of sports trading cards. These studies find that third-party certification mechanisms have a positive impact: they can reduce adverse selection, attract risk-averse customers, decrease transaction costs for buyers, and help sellers charge a premium for their products. However, both of these studies examine the effect of certification on the products rather than on the retailer itself.

Among the few studies on the impact of quality assurance seals on retailers, the findings are mixed. In one of the earliest studies on the role of thirdparty assurance seals, Kimery and McCord (2002) use controlled lab experiments, simulated retail websites, and online questionnaires to examine the value of various online assurance mechanisms. Based on 622 subject responses, they find that among the four quality assurance mechanisms studied (TRUSTe, BBB, VeriSign, and a privacy policy statement), only TRUSTe has a positive effect on consumers’ shopping behaviors. McKnight et al. (2004) use a similar controlled setting but observe that no privacy seal or industry seal studied significantly affects consumer outcomes. Head and Hassanein (2002) conduct a survey with 223 Canadian subjects; contrary to their expectations, they also find no significant relationship between the presence of assurance seals and consumers’ purchasing behavior. A related study by Hu et al. (2003) yields different results. Surveying 120 undergraduate business students to investigate the impact of five different assurance seals (TRUSTe, VeriSign, BizRate, BBB, AOL) on purchasing behavior, they find empirical evidence that some seals did increase the conversion rates of online shoppers; however, the effectiveness of the seal did not depend on the product category. Clemons (2007) conducts a case study on the operations of a quality assurance seal provider and observes that the seal benefits both buyers and sellers.

Although these studies make important contributions to the knowledge of third-party assurance seals, most of them rely on surveys, self-reported measurements, or simulated lab experiments. The conflicting findings suggest that the effect of assurance seals is likely influenced by the research design and the survey instruments used. Until now there has been a dearth of evidence based on real-world data, with the exception of the study by Nikitkov (2006) examining 847 auctions of consumer electronics and computers on eBay. Although the study finds a significant and positive association between the presence of assurance seals and shoppers’ purchasing behavior, merchants self-selected whether they would display an assurance seal, making it difficult to infer causal impact.

The emergence and growing prominence of online retail, inconclusive findings of existing studies, and lack of real-world data up to this point all highlight the need for a larger-scale field study to examine the impact of assurance seals. Our work seeks to fill this gap by using a randomized field experiment to investigate the following research question: what is the causal impact of assurance seals on online shopping outcomes?

## 3. Data and Measures

The data set was obtained from a well-known assurance seal provider. The company performs a stringent review of online retailers seeking a trust certificate/seal by confirming their financial stability, verifying their identity, and assessing whether they have a successful business track record. Merchants seeking certification also agree to the seal provider’s terms of service, which include monitoring of the merchant’s website operations and performance in every transaction following certification. Once applicants are vetted, the credentials verified, and a certification fee paid by the merchant, the seal provider places a piece of JavaScript code in the approved retailers’ websites. The code serves three purposes: to display the assurance seal as an image; to provide some necessary services like security encryption; and to transfer transaction details from the merchant’s website into the seal provider’s own database, from which our data set was extracted. The online retailer then continues to display the focal assurance seal logo as an endorsement of its quality claims by an independent verification authority. Shoppers can click on the seal to verify the membership status of the online retailer.

As noted by Kovar et al. (2000), assurance seals are only valuable if the consumer notices them. Our focal seal is of a persistent type, as preferred by the online retailer. It remains in a prominent location, at the right bottom corner of the consumer’s screen, as a floating image during every stage of the shopping cycle. No matter what page the visitor browses and regardless of how far he/she scrolls down, the seal is always visible.

Our data set originates from a randomized field experiment conducted by the seal provider on the website of an online healthcare products retailer. The online retailer’s website includes a variety of overthe-counter healthcare products, and daily sales average approximately \$7,600. The retailer has been in business since 2002 and offers its products online only, with no physical stores. Buying healthcare products online, as compared to other product categories such as books, DVDs, and tools, carries unique risks due to the potentially serious consequences of low quality on consumers’ well-being. Therefore, consumers may be seeking additional assurance that the products they purchase will be of high quality. Hence, the online healthcare products marketplace provides a good setting to test the impact of an assurance seal on consumer purchase behaviors.

One unique aspect of the data is that they are based on a randomized A/B seal test conducted by the focal assurance seal provider. The background check on the retailer started in September 2008. After the retailer passed the certification screening, the seal provider worked with the retailer to design an A/B test in order to measure the seal’s impact on consumers’ shopping behavior. The test was carried out during the first five days in December 2008, when the seal was turned on and off randomly. The seal was not shown on the retailer’s website before the A/B test. The nature of this A/B test enables us to infer the causal relationship between the presence of the assurance seal and visitor purchase conversion rates (the percentage of website visitors that complete a purchase). We obtained detailed session data for the transactions that took place during these five days. A session is defined as browsing behavior within a 24-hour period. If there was a 30-minute gap in browsing, the old session expired and a new session was created. The seal provider, in agreement with the online retailer, installed Flash cookies on visitors’ computers so that first-time visitors would be recognized in subsequent visits.

We should note that the online retailer’s website displayed two other assurance seals, which were both of a nonpersistent type and located at the bottom of the home page. These two seals are quite different from our focal seal in that they are about the retailer’s website being virus-free and consumers’ privacy protection rather than the quality of the online retailer. Since there is no variation in the presence of these seals to consumers, the A/B test allows us to identify the effect of the focal seal on purchase. Still, cautions should be taken in interpreting our findings. First, our estimates are clearly conditional on the presence of the other two seals. Second, to the extent that these seals have substituting effects in improving consumer confidence, our findings are likely conservative compared to the case where only the focal seal is present on the website.<sup>2</sup>

Over the course of the A/B test, consumers generated a total of 9,098 online shopping sessions. Each observation in our data set contains a set of metrics about the shopping session, including online retailer ID, status of the session (abandoned or created a cart), start and end date stamps and session duration time, email provided (if any) and IP address of the visitor, value of the cart (if a cart is created), number of items in the cart, status of the cart (abandoned or purchased), and the ID of the cookie placed on the consumer’s computer. The data set also includes the seal’s status (ON or OFF) in each session and information about the session’s referral source (the URL of the website that referred the visitor to the online retailer). As previously discussed, the JavaScript code transfers all these metrics from the online retailer’s website to the seal provider. The overall conversion rate among sessions in our data set is 4.10%.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

## 4.1. The Impact of the Seal

First, we measure the impact of only the seal (in the absence of any control variables; see Table 4). Out of 4,507 sessions displaying the seal, 453 sessions (10.05%) produced a shopping cart, of which 240 (52.98%) were eventually paid, resulting in a complete purchase. This implies a 5.33% purchase conversion rate when the seal in on. On the other hand, of the 4,591 sessions not displaying the seal, 295 sessions (6.43%) produced a shopping cart, of which 133 (45.08%) completed the payment process, generating a purchase conversion rate of only 2.90%. Thus, comparing the conversion rates with (5.33%) and without (2.90%) the seal, the rate increases by 83.79% when the seal is on. In fact, this increase in conversion may be a rather conservative estimate, given that other seals (see §2) are present simultaneously.

In order to test the statistical significance of this result, we conduct a Pearson’s chi-squared test, which is widely used to analyze experimental data with binary outcomes. The Pearson chi-squared statistic equals 34.10 with p-value less than 0.0001. Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis that the likelihood of conversion is independent of seal status.<sup>5</sup> This provides strong evidence for the seal’s impact on session conversion.

In addition to calculating the conversion rate, we also calculate the seal’s impact on the expected revenue per visit by conducting a t-test. The average revenue generated per visit is \$2.66 with the seal off and \$5.66 with the seal on, suggesting a 112.78% increase. The difference is statistically significant (t-value = 6.33).

## 4.3. Robustness Checks

Since our goal is to estimate the causal impact of the seal on conversion rate, we perform a series of robustness tests. First, we examine empirical models that are alternatives to the logistic regression formulation. Second, we examine the impact of consumers’ previous experience as a visitor to the online retailer’s website.

Alternative empirical models: Since the majority of visitors do not turn into customers—which is typical in online retailing—our dependent variable (conversions versus nonconversions) is imbalanced (i.e., the occurrence of conversions is rather rare). Logistic regression is known to underestimate the probability of such rare events when applied to small samples (King and Zeng 2001). In our data, there are 373 conversions in 9,098 sessions, which might be insufficient to overcome the small-sample bias. Therefore, in order to verify the robustness of the estimate of the seal’s impact on the conversion rate, we apply the method suggested by King and Zeng (2001) to correct this potential bias. The estimates are reported in the third column of Table 5. We find that the logistic regression with rare event correction produces estimates very similar to those generated in the ordinary logistic regression in column 2, which further improves our confidence in the finding that the seal has a positive effect on purchase conversion.

Although logistic regression is a popular tool because of its simplicity and ease of interpretation, the model relies on a logistic probability distribution for the error term; an alternative assumption for the error distribution leads to the probit model. The regression result of the probit model is reported in the fourth column of Table 5. We find that the coefficient of Seal\_ON is 0.33 and is significant at the p < 0001 level. In order to compare the effect of the Seal\_ON coefficient in the probit model with its effects in the logit model, we calculate the marginal effects. We find that the magnitude of the seal’s impact on conversion in the probit model is 80.46%, which is quite close to the effect previously found in the logit model (87.76%). We also find that the model fits of the probit and logit models are very similar.

Repeat sessions: The next robustness check concerns repeat sessions. Some visitors had multiple visits to the online retailer’s website during the A/B test. We identified 418 such repeat sessions in our sample (4.59%). Although they constitute only a small portion of the total observations, it is possible that repeat visitors react to the seal differently and might skew our findings related to the impact of the seal. We removed all 418 sessions generated by repeat visitors and repeated the same analysis we did for all sessions. Comparing the conversion rates with (5.12%) and without (2.49%) the seal, the rate increases by 105.62% when the seal is on for the first session created by a visitor (Table 6). Moreover, fitting the four different empirical models of Table 5 on the subset of first sessions, we observe that the coefficient of Seal\_ON variable gets slightly stronger, and the direction and significance level remain the same—further reinforcing the robustness of our findings (Table 7).

## 5. Discussion

In this study, we provide empirical evidence pertaining to the impact of assurance seals on purchase conversion rate in online markets. Based on detailed session data from a retail website, we find that the presence of the seal increases the conversion rate from 2.90% to 5.33% at the aggregate level, an increase of 83.79%. The finding is consistent across various empirical specifications. We conclude that the risk perceived by consumers while shopping online is partially mitigated when a trusted third party provides an assurance seal that endorses the quality claims of the online retailer. The seal acts as an additional signal of the online retailer’s quality, reducing the amount of information asymmetry perceived by consumers and thereby facilitating their purchasing decisions.

This research offers implications for several different stakeholders in e-commerce: seal providers, the online retailers displaying those seals, and the broader online marketplace. First of all, our findings can help lead to better consensus regarding the efficacy of assurance seals. Industry groups and publications place high value on online assurance seals, which are a longstanding phenomenon in e-commerce. For example, VeriSign claims that use of its seal and SSL solutions result in a 20% average increase in transactions, whereas TRUSTe states on its website that the presence of its privacy seal results in up to 29% increased sales for online merchants. Despite these claims, academic studies produce mixed findings, as discussed in §2. Our study, based on actual shopping data rather than surveys of consumer perceptions, provides valuable evidence supporting the causal effect of assurance seals in increasing online purchase conversion rates.

The second implication of our findings pertains to policymakers and regulatory authorities. Although the lack of institutionalized regulation has arguably contributed to the rapid growth of online commerce, it has also led to the emergence of fly-by-night operators, making it difficult for consumers to credibly gauge an online retailer’s quality. Our study highlights the importance of quality assurance and certificates in e-commerce. Consumers, retailers, and the market as a whole would benefit from regulatory oversight that holds third-party certifiers to high standards.

Methodologically, this study contributes to the literature examining the use of third-party assurance seals in e-commerce in several respects. First, compared to most previous studies, our analysis is based on real transaction data, therefore helping us to uncover the true impact of third-party quality seals on sales. Second, the randomized nature of the field experiment’s design allows us to infer causal impact with greater confidence. Third, the large sample size (compared to previous studies) enables us to estimate the magnitude of the impact with much precision, and various robustness tests suggest that our findings are statistically significant and meaningful.

## Limitations

The first limitation of our study is that our findings are based on experimental data collected from a single online retailer in one specific product category. Replication of our study across different retailers should increase the generalizability of our results. Our second limitation is that the data lacks detailed consumer-level measures. Bart et al. (2005) provide empirical evidence that the drivers of online trust vary across different segments of consumers; therefore, such measures may be desirable. The randomized nature of the field experiment does significantly alleviate concern about omitted variables regarding consumer characteristics. Overall, however, the major goal of this paper is to examine the impact of assurance seals at an aggregate level, and future studies with more customer-specific measures can advance the understanding of how assurance seals work at the individual customer level.

One particular area for future exploration is the differential effect of the seal with respect to consumer experience. Although the online retailer in our study only began displaying the assurance seal at the start of the experiment (so the seal was new to all the site’s visitors), some of them may have had prior shopping experience with the website. Such customers could be relying more on personal experience than the thirdparty assurance seal, making the seal less relevant. Unfortunately, our data set does not allow us to identify the subjects that used the online retailer’s website prior to the study period. However, including those subjects that are immune to our treatment should only make our findings more conservative. Because our findings show a significant average treatment effect across a sample of both first time and repeat visitors, it is reasonable to infer that the magnitude of the assurance seal’s impact would be even stronger with first-time visitors. This conjecture, however, would need to be tested in future studies.

Thirdly, our experiment focuses on a single assurance seal, which belongs to the Business Verification Seal category. There could be inherent differences between individual seals or types of seals. For example, in terms of inducing trust, checkout seals such as PayPal and Google Check-Out may yield different results than do price comparison seals such as BizRate and NexTag. Even within the same category, seals can vary in quality. It will be interesting, then, to compare multiple seals’ efficacy in influencing consumer behavior.

Finally, this study focuses on the empirical estimation of the quality assurance seal’s impact, leaving much room for investigation into the mechanisms by which it makes this impact. Although we refer to the information asymmetry literature for the theoretical explanation, readers should be aware that alternative explanations exist. For example, in addition to signaling the retailer’s quality, the assurance seal could also increase the conversion rate because it reduces the risks perceived by consumers.<sup>8</sup> Furthermore, it has been suggested that online markets are characterized by a lack of trust compared to offline settings (Lee and Turban 2001), and trust can be one key differentiator among Internet retailers (Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000). If a seal works on the psychological level to promote trust in a retailer’s website, the conversion rate should be higher. A seal might help to reduce concerns over privacy (Hoffman et al. 1999, p. 82) or the security of consumers’ sensitive information (Udo 2001). Further research is needed to better understand the mechanism behind the effectiveness of third-party seals in online retailing.
