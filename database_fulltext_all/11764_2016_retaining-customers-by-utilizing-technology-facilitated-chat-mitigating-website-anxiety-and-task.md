# Retaining customers by utilizing technology-facilitated chat: Mitigating website anxiety and task complexity

Jingjun (David) Xu

Department of Finance, Real Estate, & Decision Sciences, W. Frank Barton School of Business, Wichita State University, Wichita, KS 67260, USA

A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 4 October 2014 Received in revised form 5 December 2015 Accepted 29 December 2015 Available online xxx

Keywords: Performance Enjoyment Social presence Intention to return Website anxiety Perceived task complexity Technology-facilitated chat

## A B S T R A C T

We examine how technology-facilitated chat moderates the effect of website anxiety on intention to return to a website, and alleviates the negative impact of task complexity on the three factors (performance, enjoyment, and social presence). We also investigate how website anxiety moderates the relationship between user perceptions of the three factors and intention to return, and how the three factors could be negatively influenced by task complexity. Among others, the results reveals that technology-facilitated chat moderates the effect of website anxiety on intention to return, and attenuates the negative effect of task complexity on user perceptions of the three factors.

## 1. Introduction

Customer retention is important to business success [80] and profitability [81]. Users’ website anxiety [5,10] and shopping task complexity [64] are two of the barriers that might decrease customer retention. This study examines the role of technologyfacilitated chat in mitigating the possible negative effects of task complexity and website anxiety on customer retention. The chat function leverages available communication media, such as instant messaging, to allow a firm’s customer service employees to interact with customers ‘‘virtually.’’ Some companies such as Dell.com and LandsEnd.com have provided technology-facilitated chat through their websites. Marketing research surveying e-commerce directors and online customer service decision makers shows that only 26% of respondents had already implemented live chat [56]. Among those implementing live chat, 74% of participants said that live chat had improved the quality of service and 73% claimed it had improved the conversion rate of products. Given that it is a unique service technology that can add a human element to the online service environment, it is important to investigate how the possible negative effects of task complexity and website anxiety can be lessened by the implementation of technology-facilitated chat. The results of this study will be relevant to e-commerce companies or companies that provide online customer service and strive hard to retain customers, particularly those that have not yet implemented technology-facilitated chat.

In order to demonstrate the positive effects of technologyfacilitated chat in retaining online customers (the key objective of the study), it is necessary to examine how customers’ intention to return to a website is formed, and how it is influenced, directly or indirectly, by website anxiety and task complexity. Online customers’ intention to return to a website is widely recognized to be influenced by their perceptions of performance, enjoyment, and social presence (hereafter referred to as the ‘‘three factors’’) in using a website (e.g., [67,23,51,52]). However, whether website anxiety changes the effectiveness of these three factors (or some of them) in influencing intention to return remains unknown. Although Internet users constitute 42.3% of the global population,<sup>1</sup> less than half have made online purchases.<sup>2</sup> This is because users are anxious with regard to using websites, as website anxiety is a major factor influencing people’s use of websites [62]. Presumably, a user with a high level of website anxiety will be less inclined to return to a website. However, it is less obvious whether the relative impact of each factor (perceptions of performance, enjoyment, or social presence) on users’ intention to return to a website will be different for users with high versus low website anxiety.

Thus, we examine how website anxiety moderates the association between the three factors and intention to return. Its importance is three-fold. First, the moderating effect of website anxiety in this context has not been examined in the literature. Studies have examined the effect of anxiety in general [142] and specifically how computer anxiety directly influences usage intention [127], usage [20], and overall satisfaction [83]. Few studies have investigated the moderating role of website anxiety in influencing intention to return. Second, it will deepen our understanding of the phenomenon and demonstrate that the impact of these three factors on intention to return is not universal for all individuals. This will allow us to further understand the types of users (i.e., with low or high website anxiety) for which performance, enjoyment, or social presence best predict intention to return to a website. By incorporating this user characteristic, we can describe the dynamic nature of individual perceptions about websites [127]. Third, in terms of practical significance, this research can help online merchants develop strategies for customizing their website features to best fit the unique characteristics of their customers (i.e., with low or high website anxiety). With the prevalence of online business, it is important to explore the individual differences in customer website use [70]. All vendors deal with a degree of customer heterogeneity, and the degree of website anxiety is one of the key differences among customers that may then hinder website adoption [62]. Understanding how website anxiety moderates the effect of the three factors on intention to return helps website designers minimize the possible negative impact of that anxiety through the selection of improved remedial or even preventive treatments.

In addition, we examine the effects of perceived task complexity on the three factors. Previous studies indicate mixed results regarding the effect of task complexity. For example, in some studies, task complexity (manifested as the number or types of problems) was not found to have a negative effect on decision performance [63] and attitude [86]. Other studies, however, have shown that task complexity (manifested as the number of product alternatives) had a positive effect on users’ decision confidence [125]. However, others demonstrated the negative impact of task complexity (manifested as task difficulty) on information technology usage habits [69].

In this study, we examine the effect of task complexity from the perspective of the number of product attributes, rather than the number of product alternatives, the perspective examined in most previous studies on Information Systems (IS) (e.g., [64]). This focus on the number of product attributes is more relevant to website designers. On one hand, online products have different levels of complexity, ranging from products with a small number of product attributes (e.g., pencil, watch) to those with a great number of product features (e.g., laptop, camera). The empirical examination of the effect of task complexity will enable website designers to consider whether or not to take steps to mitigate the negative effect of task complexity (if any) arising from products with highly complex features. On the other hand, the number of product alternatives in an online environment tends to be very large for each product, which makes this variable (i.e., product alternatives) constant and less relevant in influencing website designer’s intervention.

Based on the above discussion, the following are the four research questions of interest:

(1) How does technology-facilitated chat moderate the effect of website anxiety on intention to return?

(2) How does technology-facilitated chat mitigate the negative effect of perceived task complexity on the three factors?

(3) How does website anxiety moderate the effects of perceived performance, enjoyment, and social presence on intention to return to a website?

(4) Does perceived task complexity negatively influence each of the three factors?

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows: the next section reviews the literature. Section 3 develops the hypotheses. Sections 4 and 5 describe the research method and the data analysis, respectively. The final section discusses the theoretical and practical contributions of the study’s findings and presents avenues for future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and literature review

## 2.1. Intention to return

We define intention to return as the idea that a user is inclined to revisit a website in future. Thus, it is similar to the concept of customer loyalty, which occurs after a consumer visits a website. However, it differs from usage intention, which refers to the degree to which a customer thinks he or she will use a website, which occurs when a consumer uses the website for the first time.

This study focuses on intention to return for four reasons. First, the behavior of returning to a website is important to the e-commerce firms, and intentions are immediate and strong predictors of actual behavior [3,4]. Second, customer retention is one of the most important aims for all online merchants [67] as it is a key driver of their financial performance [39]. It is regarded as an even more prominent factor in the survival of online businesses, as recommendations and support from loyal customers can be spread more quickly online as compared to offline [102]. Third, an increasing body of IS research has recognized the significance of intention to return [64,67,42,43,118]. The current study builds on this stream of research and focuses on intention to return. By examining the key factors and moderating factors influencing intention to return, this study provides researchers with a better understanding of the formation of intention to return for people with different levels of website anxiety. Practitioners can better manage their online presence to enhance these key factors appropriately, in order to achieve a higher level of customer retention. Finally, as the study participants will be first exposed to the experimental website, an examination of the intention to return to a website will be more appropriate in this study.

## 2.2. Utilitarian, hedonic, and relational factors

Motivations to engage in retail shopping include both utilitarian and hedonic dimensions [11,92]. ‘‘Utilitarian benefits’’ refer to the functional, instrumental, and practical benefits of consumption offerings such as website performance, while ‘‘hedonic benefits’’ refer to their enjoyment-related benefits [15,16,31]. Given the hedonic aspects of the Internet and webbased systems (e.g., [67,24,105,85,124]), and as online shoppers are motivated by both utilitarian and hedonic values, a shopping experience should account for more than just functional values [9] such as performance.

Perceived enjoyment has been considered an appropriate construct for capturing the hedonic perspective of service [11,65,72,139]. The construct of perceived enjoyment can also be derived from the flow literature [22], which describes ‘‘flow’’ as an affective state when individuals are involved in certain activities. In the context of computer-mediated environments, flow is used to represent a subjective psychological experience that

Hoffman and Novak [57] have portrayed online flow experience as a state that consists of a seamless sequence of responses facilitated by human–computer interactivity. A common measure of flow is the level of intrinsic enjoyment of an activity [67].

Social presence theory focuses on the degree to which communicating parties in a mediated environment perceive one another in terms of being a ‘‘real person’’ [108,132]. Social presence is the extent to which a medium allows users to experience others as being psychologically present [37]. Social presence implies a psychological connection with the user, who perceives the referent other (or technology) as, for instance, warm, personal, or sociable, thereby creating a feeling of human contact [143]. According to the social presence theory, face-to-face communication is a medium with a high degree of social presence [140], whereas e-mail (without multimedia cues) and paper-based mail correspond to a low degree.

In the online shopping context, social presence has been employed to evaluate the relational aspects between users and websites [51,40,55,100]. Creating a virtual shopping experience that will entice the masses must engage the cognitive and social sides of the customers [68]. When a website conveys a low social presence, it is transmitting a ‘‘cold-shoulder’’ message [41]. Thus, achievement of social presence in the online service environment is a very important step toward building social relationships with online shoppers and enhancing their overall shopping experience.

## 2.3. Computer anxiety and website anxiety

The existence of computer anxiety is commonly recognized by researchers (e.g., [122,117]). Computer anxiety represents the affective responses of individuals toward using computers [20,54], and specifically, the negative side of the affect, that is, the feelings of apprehension or anxiety that one experiences when using computers [6]. It refers to fear about the implications of computer use such as the loss of important data or fear of other possible mistakes [109,120]. Computer anxiety is the product of combinations of various psychological variables such as neuroticism and locus of control [79,93]. Researchers have found that computer anxiety influences how individuals perceive and use information technology (IT) [50]. A significant body of research in IS and psychology has brought attention to the importance of computer anxiety by demonstrating its impact on key variables such as attitude, behavior, and performance [126].

Website anxiety is the fear or apprehension that individuals experience when using a website [99,121]. This particular anxiety does share some common ground with computer anxiety. For example, both computer anxiety and website anxiety may include an individual’s anxiety toward dealing with a technical difficulty [113]. In addition, both are stable personality traits rather than mutable and temporary states [113].

However, computer anxiety and website anxiety are distinctly different, and the two are not necessarily linked. Computer anxiety includes one’s competence with computers and handling computer equipment [93,82], while website anxiety stems from the use of web-based applications that are more software oriented. As shown in Table A1 (Appendix), website anxiety has a variety of properties not observed in terms of computer anxiety. Having low computer anxiety is not necessarily associated with low website anxiety. For instance, a person with low computer anxiety might use computers to process a large amount of data, but may still not use websites to purchase products. Instead, he or she might prefer to visit a local store due to high website anxiety. The reason for their anxiety could be varied, ranging from fear of suffering loss through credit card fraud to loss of privacy due to the security issues of a company’s Internet infrastructure.

However, a person with a high degree of computer anxiety might not have a high level of website anxiety. For example, a person who has never used a computer previously might have no problem using a mobile phone to browse a website. The differences between website anxiety and computer anxiety are summarized in Table A1 (Appendix). Their differences are also supported by empirical evidence. For example, in a study using teacher candidates as a sample, their correlation was found to be 0.36 [33]. All of these indicate that website anxiety deserves a study of its own.

## 2.4. Task complexity and technology-facilitated chat

User evaluations, behaviors, task performance, and decision outcomes change as the task complexity faced by users increases [125,69]. Wood [134] proposed a comprehensive framework of task complexity. He specified perceived task complexity as a linear function of component complexity. Perceived component complexity refers to the users’ perceptions of the density and dissimilarity of information cues in the task stimulus. In a website context, dense cues are represented by long text, many images, and colors [87]. In the context of multi-alternative multi-attribute problems, component complexity is the number of attributes for a certain product and/or the number of product alternatives, which represent different information cues [125]. Perceived task complexity is an important subject and has been frequently studied in the IS literature (e.g., [125,69]). For example, Mykytyn and Green [86] examined the effect of perceived task complexity on managers’ attitude toward computers. Lankton et al. [69] investigated the role of task complexity in negatively affecting the formation of IT usage habits. Following Wood’s conceptualization, we define task complexity as the dissimilarity of information cues and use the number of product attributes to characterize a certain product. This perspective has more practical implications for website designers as discussed in the introduction section.

Although studies have suggested that task complexity may have a negative impact on users’ experience of using IT, fewer studies have examined how complexity can be mitigated. In order to deal with this type of task complexity, we examine the efficacy of technology-facilitated chat in reducing the negative effects of perceived task complexity. Some companies (e.g., Landsend.com, Dell.com) have provided technology-facilitated chat, typically through real-time text chatting, on their websites to facilitate interactions between online customers and customer service representatives. The importance of technology-facilitated chat has been further underscored by research showing that it can increase consumers’ cognitive and affective involvement [61], improve perceived personalization [138], and enhance the online shopping experience [66,136]. However, little is understood about the effect of a technology-facilitated chat in alleviating the negative effect of task complexity. As compared to automated technologies, it is very expensive to implement technology-facilitated chat because of the cost of the chat or web contact software, the cost of training company staff to use it, and more importantly, the labor cost of having staff attend to customers. Whether technology-facilitated chat can effectively reduce the effects of unfavorable perceptions of task complexity and moderate the effects of website anxiety on users’ intention to return to a website is an important research question.

The main effects of the perceived three factors on intention to return have already been established and justified in prior literature and hence are not formally hypothesized here. However, for the sake of completeness, we briefly explain why perceptions of performance, enjoyment, and social presence, as representations of utilitarian, hedonic, and relational factors, would influence intention to return to a website. Performance encompasses the capability of technology to perform the task accurately and reliably [141], which can be used to represent the utilitarian element of technology adoption. While using a technology-based service, customers may be especially concerned with the reliability of new technology-based delivery options because they may envision that these options may not work well [35]. Studies have found performance to be an important dimension for technology adoption [141,27,28] and a robust determinant of behavior intention [127,19].

Flow is also pertinent in studying online customers’ behavior, as flow is one of the key components for a compelling online shopping experience [67,90]. Koufaris [67] asserts that enjoyment is a common measure of flow. In addition, marketing literature reveals that shopping and many other consumption activities provide both utilitarian and hedonic value through responses evoked during the experience [128]. Enjoyment embodies the hedonic aspect of shopping [67,11,105,85,124] and has been found to significantly affect user intentions to return to a website [64,67,65].

Social presence is an important criterion for firms seeking to build relationships with customers. Websites displaying a sense of personal, sociable, and sensitive human contact are clearly desirable for customers [40,41]. By contrast, the lower levels of social presence in e-commerce may be more than a mere inconvenience for customers and may actually prevent customers from purchasing such services [40]. Websites with a high level of social presence may result in an increased e-consumer sense that the firm is trying to provide good customer relationship management [40] and thus increase intention to return.

## 3.1. The moderating role of website anxiety

Website anxiety is associated with negative attitudes toward the use of websites. We expect that the effect of perceived website performance on intention to return will be weaker for online customers with high website anxiety. Although they might think highly of the website’s performance (e.g., in facilitating customers in selecting a right product), they are less likely to return due to their fear of the potential risks associated with online shopping such as personal information theft by hackers or even their own ordering mistakes [70]. To be precise, they are more likely to worry that certain things in a website may not work well and envision some risk associated with a website. On the contrary, customers with low website anxiety will form their intention to return to a website contingent more on the performance of the website itself and are less subject to the influence of website anxiety. Thus, we posit the following:

H1a. Website anxiety will moderate the relationship between perceived performance and intention to return such that the relationship is stronger for customers with low website anxiety.

Contrary to website anxiety (i.e., negative affect), perceived enjoyment represents the positive affective response of individuals toward using websites [64,67]. In the website context, website anxiety is a general affective response, while enjoyment is a dynamic, situation-specific affective response contingent which the website customers visit [126]. If customers with higher website anxiety perceive a website as being enjoyable, perceived enjoyment is still expected to have less effect on willingness to return to that website given the global anxiety they feel about websites. Due to the offsetting effect of anxiety on perceived enjoyment, customers might feel ambivalent about returning to a website. However, when customers with lower website anxiety enjoy a website, perceived enjoyment will play an important role in influencing their intention to return, as a website provides a hedonic benefit. Thus, we propose the following:

H1b. Website anxiety will moderate the relationship between perceived enjoyment and intention to return such that the relationship is stronger for customers with low website anxiety.

Website anxiety is derived from the psychological fear of using websites, as customers with high website anxiety are acquainted with the traditional ways of shopping (e.g., interacting with a sales employee). Past studies have shown that anxiety will lead users to seek relational support to overcome their anxiety [8]. ## 3.2. The effect of perceived task complexity on performance, enjoyment, and social presence

In the following section, we draw upon the theory of human information processing [94,95,107,110,104] to hypothesize the effect of perceived task complexity on performance, enjoyment, and social presence in the context of a preferential choice task. According to the theory of human information processing, humans have a limited cognitive capacity to process information, and it is not feasible for them to evaluate all available alternatives in detail before making a decision. While electronic shopping environments make it possible to access large amounts of product information, customers still need to process increasingly more information with the same limited processing capacity [131,135]. When customers perceive task complexity to be low, they are able to complete the preferential choice task with ease. For example, when a product has fewer product features (e.g., USB flash drive), customers are better able to select a product from a website by themselves. On the contrary, when customers perceive task complexity to be high, they are less able to complete a task competently [2]. Previous studies have indicated that users perceiving a higher level of information overload and task complexity hold negative perceptions, including a lower level of decision satisfaction [91,46]. Thus, we propose:

H2a. Perceived task complexity will negatively influence perceived performance of a website.

When the perceptions of task complexity are high, the ability to process all information is low. The result is emotional discomfort, specifically the fear of missing important information [64]. Thus, a user will perceive less enjoyment. For example, in the context of a preferential choice task, a product with many product features will result in perceived task complexity [134]. In this situation, it will be difficult to process all necessary information regarding the product features to make an optimal choice. Given the inability to conduct all the evaluations and calculations necessary to arrive at a choice, stress and frustration from the process will occur [30]. Studies have indicated that task complexity manifested as the number of product alternatives negatively affected the customers’ perceived enjoyment [64]. Similarly, we propose:

H2b. Perceived task complexity will negatively influence perceived enjoyment.

When task complexity is perceived to be high (vs. low), we expect that perceived social presence will be low, with other things being equal. The cognitive psychology literature indicates that computer users react differently when confronted with varied levels of task complexity, for example, simple versus complex [88]. When task complexity is relatively high (i.e., when the number of product attributes is greater), the product alternatives have more dimensions that are different from one another, making it more difficult for users to identify the best option [115]. Previous studies indicate that high task complexity increases an individual’s frustration and confusion [112], stress [101], and even feelings of helplessness [96]. Under the helpless situation, a user is more likely to perceive that the website lacks a feeling of human presence. They might make external attributions by believing that a website is not instrumental in helping them achieve their shopping goal, and they perceive a lower level of social presence from the website. Conversely, when the product is characterized by low complexity (i.e., when the number of attributes is fewer), users have little difficulty finding the best product option. Therefore, their evaluation of a website will be favorable and they are less likely to perceive the website as having low social presence. Thus, we propose that

H2c. Perceived task complexity will negatively influence perceived social presence.

## 3.3. The moderating role of technology-facilitated chat between perceived task complexity and the three factors

When task complexity is high, a customer might face information overload. Using external help for performing resource-intensive information processing tasks, and thus freeing up some of the human processing capacity for decision making, is a method of coping with information overload [53]. In this study, we examine the role of technology-facilitated chat in augmenting customers cognitive capacity. Past studies have found that mere display of the technology-facilitated chat (without any actual interaction with users) is not better than the condition without chat display [23]. Thus, we presume that customers must essentially use technologyfacilitated chat to have the intended effect.

Specifically, technology-facilitated chat is implemented with an intention to pay individual attention to each customer’s concerns. As compared to the absence of technology-facilitated chat,the presence of technology-facilitated chat can not only elicit customers preferences and provide product advice to customers, but it can also answer users’ questions immediately, which is particularly important in the case of high task complexity. As a result, even though customers perceive a high level of task complexity, they are more likely to be able to complete the task smoothly and subsequently to perceive the performance of a website to be high. On the contrary, when technology-facilitated chat is not available, customers perceiving high task complexity are more likely to consider the performance of a website to be low. This effect can be explained by the task-technology fit theory [44,38], where fit is the extent to which the technology features match the task requirements [76]. A complex task requires a technology that can better handle the task’s requirements. In the current context, the technology facilitated-chat can fit the complex task it supports to have a performance impact. In other words, the chat can mitigate the reduction of perceived performance due to a good fit between the task and the technology [64,2]. Thus, we hypothesize that

H3a. Technology-facilitated chat moderates the relationship between perceived task complexity and perceived performance such that the relationship is weaker in the presence of technologyfacilitated chat.

Technology-facilitated chat is distinguished from other online customer support functions in that customers interact directly with human customer service representatives using an online medium [1]. Integrating human assistance into web pages makes a site more fun to use and improves the site’s atmosphere [1]. The key reason is that interactivity facilitated by the technologyfacilitated chat leads to better perceptions of enjoyment. Prior research has found that interactivity has positive effects on the sense of satisfaction [119] and enjoyment [24,60]. Therefore, we expect that the element of fun brought by the technologyfacilitated chat helps attenuate the negative impact of perceived task complexity on enjoyment. Specifically, chat assists users in alleviating the cognitive demands induced by high complexity task environments and reduces the level of stress and frustration. In other words, in the presence of technology-facilitated chat, although a customer would still perceive the task as being complex, the negative effect of task complexity on perceived enjoyment will be mitigated. By contrast, in the absence of chat, the negative effect of task complexity persists and users’ perceived enjoyment will still be low. Therefore, we posit the following:

H3b. Technology-facilitated chat moderates the relationship between perceived task complexity and perceived enjoyment such that the relationship is weaker in the presence of technologyfacilitated chat.

Features of the computer interface can help influence the perception of social presence [51,52,55]. Examples of website features that encourage social presence include personalized greetings [40], personality of online recommendation agents [55], human audio [75], human video [68], socially rich text and pictures [51,52], and combined photo, user review, and chat display [23]. Similarly, the negative impact of task complexity on social presence might be altered by the presence of technologyfacilitated chat. Previous studies have shown that media capability moderates the effect of social technology cues on perceived social presence [55]. Similarly, we expect that technology-facilitated chat moderates the effect of perceived task complexity on perceived social presence, as both social technology cues (e.g., socially rich text, pictures, video, and audio) and task complexity contain dissimilarity of information cues. When technology-facilitated chat is available, even though consumers would still perceive a high level of task complexity, they may engage in communication with sales representatives in the chat and effectively obtain information concerning their online shopping process [137]. Through this interactive process, consumers’ needs can be taken care by the chat, thus attenuating the negative effect of task complexity on perceived social presence. Without the assistance of chat, consumers might remain helpless with the complicated task and perceive the website to have a lower level of social presence. Thus, we posit the following:

H3c. Technology-facilitated chat moderates the relationship between perceived task complexity and perceived social presence such that the relationship is weaker in the presence of technologyfacilitated chat.

## 3.4. The moderating role of technology-facilitated chat between website anxiety and intention to return

As noted earlier, studies have indicated a significant and negative relationship between website anxiety and Internet use [62]. People who are more anxious about using the website utilize the Internet to a lesser degree. For example, they might use a website to browse information but not purchase online. The research question is whether technology-facilitated chat reduces the negative relationship between website anxiety and intention to return. We believe it will be the case. Website anxiety is a user characteristic and a stable personality trait. According to Table A1 (Appendix), two key sources of website anxiety include anxiety about website search and website failure [99,17]. Technologyfacilitated chat can provide a desirable channel for users to express their concerns and inquire about the product/service being considered. The chat can also respond to users’ requirements and tailor the messages and product information to better suit users’ needs so that users’ website search can be reduced. In the case of website failure (e.g., dead link or non-intuitive navigation), users can also turn to the technology-facilitated chat for assistance. Previous studies have found that technology-facilitated chat has a positive effect on users’ website involvement [61]. This is because a website user needs to seek out relevant product information [26], and the technology-facilitated chat can provide consumers with interactive opportunities to seek information about products and obtain information pertaining to their decision-making process. As a result, the possible negative effect of website anxiety on intention to return to a website will be attenuated due to the presence of the technology-facilitated chat. As a user’s anxiety about website search and failure can be handled and resolved by the chat, a user with high website anxiety will be more likely to form favorable impressions of the website and will be more likely to return. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4. Technology-facilitated chat moderates the relationship between website anxiety and intention to return such that the relationship is weaker in the presence of technology-facilitated chat.<sup>3</sup>

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Study setting, treatment manipulations, and procedures

We tested the hypotheses in a study through a laboratory experiment with a 2 (with vs. without technology-facilitated chat in a website) - 2 (low vs. high task complexity)<sup>4</sup> between subject design. The technology-facilitated chat (see Fig. A1, Appendix) was provided by a human customer service representative communicating through a technology medium (i.e., the website). A research assistant with an average level of experience in customer service and of knowledge in laptop computers played the role of the human customer service representative and was instructed to provide consistent communication language to different participants as much as possible. He was informed that his key role was to provide product recommendations to the customers based upon their indicated product preferences.

Task complexity was manipulated to create variations in perceived task complexity. We manipulated task complexity using five (low) and 15 (high) product attributes [60]. People can hold 7  2 pieces of data in their working memory concurrently [32, p. 32,84]. Therefore, the product with five attributes (i.e., within the bottom cognitive limit of 7  2) represents a condition with a low level of task complexity, whereas the product with 15 features (i.e., double the medium cognitive limit) represents one with a high level of task complexity. Previous studies used eight and 54 product alternatives to represent a low and medium level of task complexity from the perspective of the number of product alternatives [64]. Thus, we chose 32 product alternatives to represent a moderate level of complexity to avoid the ceiling effect of task complexity given that the number of product attributes was manipulated at two levels (low vs. high).

Each subject was asked to shop online for a laptop computer for a friend, whose product requirements were described to each participant in a written form. Prior to the study, the subjects were informed that they would each receive \$10 (Canadian) for their participation. In order to motivate participants to view the experiment as a serious online shopping session and to increase their involvement, we offered the top 20 performers an extra amount (\$25). Before they began the shopping task, subjects in the technology-facilitated chat group were informed of the correspondent technology available on the website. Subjects could voluntarily decide whether or not to use the chat. Participants were required to complete a questionnaire in order to record their demographic variables. After the shopping task, the subjects answered questions related to the dependent variables.

## 4.2. Measurement scales

The study adapted the existing validated scales whenever possible. The perceived performance scale was adapted from leading service marketing studies (e.g., [141,25]). Items for social presence and enjoyment were borrowed from the study by Gefen and Straub [40]. Items for intention to return were based on those of Coyle and Thorson [21]. The items for website anxiety were adapted from the items of computer anxiety used by Compeau et al. [20], Heinssen et al. [54], and Venkatesh et al. [127]. Perceived task complexity was measured by the items from Shankaranarayanan et al. [106]. Table A2 (Appendix) shows the measurement scales used in the questionnaire. In addition, we created a dummy variable for the construct of technology-facilitated chat with 0 representing a condition without technology-facilitated chat and 1 representing otherwise.

## 5. Data analysis

The study included 128 subjects recruited from a public university. Among them, 88 were females and 40 males. Five were non-students, 25 graduate students, and the rest undergraduates. The average age was 23.4 years. On average, the subjects had been using the Internet for 9.2 years, spending 28.8 h each week. In general, they were familiar with online shopping (5.20/7). Subjects perceived task complexity to be significantly higher in the high task complexity condition than that in the low task complexity condition (mean difference = 1.4, p < 0.001), thereby indicating the success of task complexity manipulation.

In addition to the 128 subjects, 18 other subjects assigned to the chat condition did not utilize the chat feature and were excluded from further analysis for two reasons. First, it enhances conceptual clarity. What matters is the actual use of chat, not the simple presence of chat. Prior research indicated that the mere presence of chat (no user interaction) is not different from the condition without chat in terms of perceived social presence [23]. We also found that subjects using chat (N = 64) perceived significantly (p < 0.01) higher levels of social presence than those exposed to chat but not using it (N = 18). Second, it helps maintain an equal sample size of 64 across the two experimental conditions (with vs. without chat).

## 5.1. Measurement validation

We analyzed the research model using partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling, a component-based approach [74]. PLS allows the simultaneous testing of the measurement model (the psychometric properties of the scales used to measure a variable) and the estimation of the structural model (the strength and direction of the relationships between the variables). Specifically, software SMART PLS 3.0 [103] was used to conduct our analyses with H3 and H4 tested by implementing the PLS multiple group analysis (PLS–MGA).

Assessments of measurement models should examine (1) individual measurement item reliability, (2) internal consistency, and (3) discriminant validity [7]. We examined the loadings of the individual measurement items on their intended constructs to support individual item reliability and compared these to the recommended tolerances of 0.70 [7,12]. All the measurement items met this threshold (see Table A2, Appendix). We calculated composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha for each construct to support internal consistency of the constructs. All met suggested tolerances (>0.70, [36]) with results reported in Table 1.

Discriminant validity is further supported when the loadings for the items on its targeted construct are higher than those on other constructs in the model [12]. Gefen and Straub [41] provided a restrictive guideline stating that the minimum difference between item loadings and cross loadings should be .10 in order to establish discriminant validity in addition to the AVE analysis shown in Table 1. This restrictive guideline has been widely adopted in the IS literature to assess discriminant validity [41,133,144,114,111,71]. Table A3 (Appendix) contains the loadings and cross-loadings. All items load higher on their constructs than they load on any other constructs measured at the same time, and in all 120 cross loadings, the differences are >0.20. These results, therefore, support the discriminant validity.

To address the potential concern for common method bias, we performed three tests with details presented in the Appendix. The results suggest that common method bias is not a major concern in this study.

## 6. Discussion and contributions

## 6.1. Discussion

Our results indicate that (1) perceived performance, enjoyment, and social presence positively influence user intention to return to a website; (2) website anxiety moderates the effects of performance and social presence on intention to return; (3) perceived task complexity negatively influences the three factors; (4) the presence of technology-facilitated chat attenuates the negative effect of perceived task complexity on the three factors; and (5) technology-facilitated chat moderates the effect of website anxiety on intention to return.

Considering these three factors simultaneously in a single study, it is also noteworthy that the path coefficient of social presence on intention to return is the highest among the three factors. The significant effect of perceived social presence on intention to return suggests that the human service component is the key ingredient for retaining customers to an online retailer. The greatest constraint most online businesses suffer from is the inherent lack of helpful online retail personnel [45]. Thus, social presence in a website gives customers a better experience that resembles a physical store. The salient effect of social presence stresses the importance of considering the social relational perspective in addition to the utilitarian and hedonic perspectives.

Regarding the moderating effects of website anxiety, the findings suggest that customers with high website anxiety rely more heavily on high social presence than high performance to signify their intention to return to a website. This is understandable as website usage is a relatively new way of shopping for those with high website anxiety. They tend to be less efficient in their selection and assessment of information, are in greater need of assistance from external help, and therefore tend to appreciate a greater degree of social presence on the website. A strong social presence in the website might give them a feeling of ‘‘returning to the good old days’’ when they could depend on customer service representatives for help. By contrast, customers with less website anxiety have gotten used to online shopping, and hence website performance will be of greater concern to them.

Website anxiety was expected to attenuate the positive effect of perceived enjoyment on intention to return. However, we found no support for this hypothesis. One possibility is that enjoyment and anxiety are of an affective nature so that they are not independent of each other. However, the low correlation coefficient (r = 0.03, Table 1) between them almost excludes this possibility. A more likely explanation is that perceived enjoyment takes over website anxiety in shaping user perceptions toward the website, as website anxiety is an individual difference affective variable, while enjoyment is a situation-specific affective concept. Prior research has shown that after users have direct experience with the target system, individual-difference affective factor (i.e., computer playfulness) plays a diminishing role and systemspecific perceived enjoyment plays a dominant role in forming use perception [126]. Thus, the effect of perceived enjoyment on intention to return does not depend much on website anxiety.

As theorized, the findings support that technology-facilitated chat is effective in lessening customers’ negative perceptions of performance, enjoyment, and social presence induced by the task complexity. The moderating effect of chat between task complexity and performance lends support to the existing literature that IT artifacts can help increase customers’ cognitive capacity and remove the cognitive limitations [53], thereby improving perceived performance. The moderating effect of chat between task complexity and enjoyment is congruent with prior findings that active control over the exchange of information (i.e., technologyfacilitated chat in our case) leads to better perceived enjoyment [60]. The positive influence of chat in reducing the negative effect of perceived task complexity on social presence is consistent with the classification of communication media by social presence theory. That is, the degree of social presence is equated with the degree of awareness of the other person in a communication interaction. When a human service employee is available through a website (i.e., technology-facilitated chat) and assists customers with selecting products, it is understandable that the negative impact of task complexity on social presence can be greatly lessened.

## 6.2. Theoretical contributions

This study accomplishes several theoretical objectives. First, the present study lends substantial support to the utilitarian and hedonic consumption literatures [11,16]. Perhaps, more importantly, the results support that the relational benefit – social presence – contributes significantly to intention to return. This finding further demonstrates that enhancing the social aspect should be targeted as an important consideration in website design [45]. Overall, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to theoretically integrate and empirically test the role of hedonic, utilitarian, and relational dimensions in predicting intention to return to a website.

Second, we contribute to the IS literature by theorizing the moderating role of website anxiety in customers’ evaluations of utilitarian, hedonic, and relational benefits to determine their return intention. This is an important contribution as previous studies have primarily focused on computer anxiety and the main effect of website anxiety. As computer anxiety usually causes computer use avoidance [18], website anxiety may also be a barrier to shopping online. We explain how the three antecedents of intention to return vary across individuals. Our findings suggest that perceived enjoyment is equally important for customers with either high or low website anxiety. We also observe that perceived performance is more important for customers with low website anxiety, yet perceived social presence is more important for customers with high website anxiety.

Third, we found that the perceived task complexity has significant negative effects on perceived performance, enjoyment, and social presence. Although previous researchers studied the effect of task complexity on decision confidence and satisfaction [125,46,47,91], they did not examine the effect of perceived task complexity on perceived social presence. In addition, only limited research has been undertaken to study task complexity from the perspective of the number of product attributes. This study thus contributes to the task complexity literature by demonstrating the negative effect of perceived task complexity on perceived social presence.

Another contribution is that our research addresses a gap in extant IS research by highlighting the importance of technologyfacilitated chat. Although task complexity arising from many product alternatives available could be reduced by limiting the number of products presented to the users, task complexity due to an inherently larger number of product features (e.g., hard drive) is not easy to reduce. The design of IT artifacts needs to be considered and evaluated to identify viable ways of changing customers’ negative perceptions. To this end, we study technology-facilitated chat and shed light on the impact of chat on mitigating the damaging influence of task complexity on the formation of individual perceptions of the three factors and moderating the effect of website anxiety on intentions to return to a website.

The study of the role of technology-facilitated chat in mitigating the negative effect of task complexity also contributes to the diagnosticity literature [60].<sup>6</sup> Perceived diagnosticity is defined as the extent to which consumers believe that particular shopping experiences are helpful to evaluate products [59]. Jiang and Benbasat [60] found that the superiority of videos and virtual product experience over a static picture format in terms of perceived website diagnosticity did not change significantly when product understanding tasks become highly complex. However, our study reveals that as the shopping task becomes more complex, customers are in greater need of technology-facilitated chat that can help improve the diagnosticity. We reason that video and virtual product experience demand a significant amount of customers’ attention to understand the product, while technologyfacilitated chat demands less by tailoring its response to customer’s specific requirements.

## 6.3. Practical contributions

Practitioners can also benefit from this investigation in several ways. First, we demonstrate that utilitarian, hedonic, and relational dimensions all play important roles in influencing intention to return: these three factors explained 61% of the variance of intention to return. Therefore, designers are encouraged to enhance all of these three aspects. Given the significant effect of social presence, it implies that managing social presence should be at the forefront of website development and management, no less than performance and enjoyment.

Second, as perceived performance and social presence have different effects for customers with various degrees of website anxiety, website designers need to consider the heterogeneous nature of the potential customers. Firms can benefit from understanding the circumstances in which the three factors are likely to succeed or fail. In addition to focusing on selling superior service products, firms need to be increasingly oriented toward the consumers of those products and the fulfillment of their needs. Specifically, customers who have high website anxiety are influenced more by social presence in shaping their intention to return, while those with low website anxiety are more likely to rely on website performance. Targeting a customer group with the wrong type of service attributes may be counterproductive. These results provide managers with useful insights into monitoring and improving user intention to return to a website, by taking into account the level of their customers website anxiety.

Third, our findings suggest technology-facilitated chat can play a positive role in reducing the negative impact of task complexity on perceived performance, enjoyment, and social presence of a website and in changing the impact of website anxiety on intention to return. Online service firms are therefore advised to incorporate technology-facilitated chat into their websites. Firms should also implement technology-facilitated chat to target customers with high website anxiety, as chat can reduce the negative perceptions of task complexity on social presence, and social presence in turn carries more weight in influencing intention to return for customers with high website anxiety.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that provide avenues for future research. First, the study participants were mainly university students, as they represent a major segment of the market in computers [129]. They also had relatively low website anxiety, thus possibly leading to a floor effect. Readers should exercise caution in generalizing the results of this study to other demographic groups. In addition, given the nature of the laptop shopping task, we believe most of the users are goal-directed users in the study. Thus, the results might not be applicable for experiential browsing users. Future studies should examine how the model results will change with a different shopping task that facilitates experiential browsing. Second, the existing study focuses only on intention to return; further studies could extend the research to explore simultaneous effects of utilitarian, hedonic, and relational benefits on other aspects such as actual and unplanned purchases. Third, we did not control for subjects general intention to use the Internet, and hence we are unable to disentangle the effects of website anxiety and the three factors on intention to return from one’s general intention to use the web. However, as 87% of American adults use the internet [97], the general intention to use the web is not expected to play a significant role in influencing the results found in the study.

Other avenues for future research include the following: First, there is considerable diversity in the devices through which customers can access websites, ranging from a mobile phone to a personal digital assistant. Hence, a natural progression for the next phase of research is to test the model through a mobile device interface. Second, future research should extend this study by distinguishing website anxiety from computer anxiety conceptually and empirically. In addition, a mixed-method approach could be adopted to illustrate both qualitatively and quantitatively how implementation of technology-facilitated chat leads to attenuated negative perceptions and increased return intentions. Finally, future research should also examine whether a three-way interaction exists among website anxiety, chat, and user perceptions in terms of their effects on intention to return in other contexts (e.g., different sample and/or product types).<sup>7</sup>

## 6.5. Conclusions

This study makes several important contributions to the IS literature. First, we contribute to the IS research by studying the moderating role of website anxiety between user perceptions of the three factors (performance, enjoyment, and social presence) and intention to return to a website. The results have increased our understanding about the factors crucial for customers with high website anxiety. Online merchants can also develop approaches for tailoring their presence to better fit the unique characteristics of their customers. Second, we fill a void in the literature by examining how the utilitarian, hedonic, and relational factors are negatively influenced by perceived task complexity and how such negative perceptions can be attenuated by the implementation of technology-facilitated chat. Although technology-facilitated chat is being adopted by some companies, little is known concerning the contributions of technologyfacilitated chat in reducing the negative effect of perceived task complexity and changing the effect of website anxiety on customers’ online shopping experience. This study elucidates the significant role of technology-facilitated chat in retaining online customers.
