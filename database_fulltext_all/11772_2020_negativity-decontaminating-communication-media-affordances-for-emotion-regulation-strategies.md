# Negativity decontaminating: Communication media afordances for emotion regulation strategies

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Emotion regulation, emotion regulation strategy, technology afordance, computermediated communication, enabling condition, inhibiting condition, releasing condition, suppressing condition, emotional contagion

## A B S T R A C T

A challenging part of many occupations is dealing with negative emotions from customers, coworkers and other communication partners on a daily basis. This paper describes a case-based, inductive study of information technology (IT) help-desk workers within a Fortune 500 energy company and the communication media and emotion regulation strategies (ERSs) they employ for dealing with negative emotions from communication partners. Using the technology affor. dance perspective as the theoretical lens to understand the role of communication media, the present study is the first of its kind to document empirically how employees and management can leverage communication media to ease the strain of emotion regulation upon members and the IT help-desk group—an original concept we label negativity decontaminating. Here, individuals technologically enabled ERSs are metaphorically likened to the techniques used by medical workers to avoid contamination from viruses. This negativity decontaminating potential includes several media afordances existing at two levels: a group level afordance (negativity filtering) and individual level afordances (negativity isolating, negativity barriering, and negativity containing). Moreover, the tech-organizational contexts at the case organization gave rise to a set of condi tions that afected the exercising and actualization of media's negativity decontaminating potential.

## 1. Introduction

Help-desk work would be fantastic, if it weren't for the help-desk part. We've all been there: dealing with users who are impatient, unreasonable, and often downright abusive. – IT Veteran Comment on Help-desk Abuse

The business itself and the people who ran it were cause for far more stress than the customers ever provided. And the customers provided plenty.

– Ubuntu Forum Post for the Thread “Ever had a help-desk job?”

Communicating with angry customers, coworkers, and bosses can take a heavy emotional toll on working adults (Ashforth & Humphrey, 1995; Brotheridge & Grandey, 2002; Grandey, 2000; Morris & Feldman, 1996), who must manage their emotional experiences and/or expressions during such encounters. Managing emotional experiences and expressions is referred to as emotion regulation, defined here as the attempt to influence which emotions we have, when we have them, and how these emotions are experienced or expressed (Gross, 1998). Without question, many industries and tasks (explicitly or implicitly) require emotion regulation from employees (Grandey, 2000; Hochschild, 1983). Those working in service industries (including technology support such as IT help-desk) engage in emotion regulation as they attempt to coordinate with colleagues and serve customers who may be experiencing frustration and anger (Rutner, Hardgrave, & McKnight, 2008; Rutner, Riemenschneider, O'Leary-Kelly, & Hardgrave, 2011). Stress, burnout, absenteeism and turnover are just some of the individual and organizational outcomes known to result from frequent and challenging emotion regulation at work (see Gross, 1998, 2002 for reviews).

Emotion regulation, apart from being a frequent requirement at work, also helps with managing emotional contagion defined as the tendency for individuals to converge emotionally (Hatfield, Cacioppo, & Rapson, 1994). Emotional contagion has been observed at diferent levels and in both face-to-face and computer-mediated communication (CMC) environments (Barsade & Gibson, 2007; Cheshin, Rafaeli, & Bos, 2011). Emotional contagion may occur directly on individuals who are targets of the emotion or who are communication partners of the emotion source, or indirectly on individuals who are bystanders or third-party observers (Hareli & Rafaeli, 2008; Rupp & Spencer, 2006)—these bystanders are also referred to as “outsiders” (Gofman, 1959). Failure to manage emotional contagion may lead to various negative consequences for the employee and the organization (Maitlis & Ozcelik, 2004).

Considering the prevalence of emotion regulation requirements at work and the potential impacts on employees and organizations, it is not surprising that significant scholarly efort has been spent on understanding how to facilitate emotion regulation. Extensive attention has been given to understanding the diferent emotion regulation strategies (ERSs) that individuals may use (Elfenbein, 2007; Gross, 1998). Researchers have also examined individuals' capabilities related to emotion regulation such as selfeficacy (Brotheridge & Grandey, 2002) and eforts designed to improve individuals' capabilities such as training (Grant, 2013).

Existing research examining how to facilitate emotion regulation largely focused on the traditional face-to-face environment (Elfenbein. 2007: Grandey. 2000: Gross. 1998). The small number of research studies on emotion regulation using CMC often treat CMC simply as the study context and pay inadequate attention to the potential influence of communication media, for example a study about 911 call-takers and their emotion regulation (Tracy & Tracy, 1998). Notably absent from the existing emotion regulation research is an understanding of how external tools like communication media may afect (facilitate or inhibit) emotion regulation. In other words, do communication media make emotion regulation more or less challenging?

Some of the existing research may pessimistically suggest that emotion regulation in CMC is likely to be more challenging. For example, CMC research on flaming suggests that features of the CMC environment such as anonymity may reduce individuals awareness and motivation to regulate their emotional expressions, consequently encouraging more negative emotional communication (Kiesler, Siegel, & McGuire, 1984; Spears & Lea, 1994). On the other hand, the CMC literature (Te'eni, 2001; Walther, 1996) also indicates strongly that communication media may be exploited to facilitate intra- and inter-personal processes, such as emotion regulation. For example, communication media's facilitating role for selective self-presentation (Walther, 1996) could apply to selective expression of emotions.

The contrasting views regarding communication media's influence on emotion regulation might be reconciled by examining their diferent assumptions and foci. The pessimistic view directs attention to message senders and how CMC afects senders' awareness and motivation related to emotion regulation. The optimistic view directs attention to message receivers and how CMC afects their capabilities related to emotion regulation (when they already have the awareness and motivation to do so). In this paper, we are interested in understanding what individuals might do to leverage communication media to mitigate the efects of being the receivers of negative emotional communication—we recognize that receivers of negative emotional communication may need to respond to communication partners (and hence be senders in the response communication back to partners); yet in the initial negative emotiona communication that individuals seek to regulate, they are receivers (of negative emotional communication from partners). Thus. we ask,

RQ: How do communication media support the use of emotion regulation strategies (ERSs) for individuals working in a negative emotionladen environment?

To answer the above research question, we conducted semi-structured interviews with IT help-desk employees at a Fortune 500 energy company. Analysis revealed that IT help-desk emplovees' emotion regulation when interacting with communication partners expressing negative emotions is akin to resisting contamination from viruses and, more importantly, that communication media may facilitate emotion regulation via its potential for negativity decontaminating: Negative emotion starts with negative communication partners, akin to patient zero, and spreads to IT help-desk employees via emotional contagion. Our data demonstrate that IT helpdesk employees utilized the negativity decontaminating potential of communication media to avoid or treat negativity contamination and their eforts operated at two levels (group and individual). At the group level, IT help-desk leaders used media to filter negativity out of the IT help desk, avoiding or reducing negativity exposure for the whole group. We labeled this negativity filtering. At the individual level, IT help-desk employees used media to avoid negativity exposure (negativity isolating), to reduce or delay negativity exposure (negativity barriering), or to control the scope of negativity exposure and prevent crossover onto others (negativity containing). Moreover, the exercising and actualization of communication media's negativity decontaminating potential were afected by the tech organizational contexts at the participating organization. By documenting the negativity decontaminating potential, this research provides insight into the protective socio-emotional qualities of communication media

The present study directs scholarly attention to the ways individual employees and the whole group work within communication media to regulate emotions in a negative emotion-laden working environment. Below, a review of the literature on emotion reg. ulation and ERSs is ofered, followed by an explanation of a new theoretical lens for understanding the facilitating role of communication media, the technology afordance and constraint theory (Markus & Silver, 2008). Next, we discuss the research setting, data collection, and analysis. We then present our findings and a set of figures that situate our findings within the technolog afordance literature. In the end, we discuss limitations, theoretical contributions, practical implications, and transferability of our findings to other like contexts.

## 2. Background literature

## 2.1. Emotion regulation and emotion regulation strategies

Emotion regulation has been examined in many contexts, such as the interaction between employees and customers (or emotiona labor, Hochschild, 1983), between supervisors and subordinates (Fisk & Friesen, 2012), and between coworkers (Kramer & Hess, 2002). Although research on emotion regulation largely focused on the individual level, emotion regulation has also been examined at the dyadic, group, and organizational levels (Ashkanasy, 2003). Further, leadership and coworkers also play an important role in regulating ones' emotions (Mainiero & Gibson, 2003). For example, Rey (1996) showed the importance of both leadership and coworkers in helping social workers cope with negative emotions due to client violence such as verbal attacks.

Emotion regulation strategy (ERS) refers to the group of methods individuals employ in their attempt to manage what emotion they have, when they have the emotion and how the emotion is experienced or expressed (Gross, 1998). The reference to “group of methods” captures the fact that there may be multiple specific methods employed under a given ERS. Syntheses of the emotion regulation literature (see Elfenbein, 2007; Gross, 1998 for reviews) suggest that there are five major ERSs distinguished by the point in the emotional process when they have their primary influence (Gross & Thompson, 2007, p. 14). Table 1 provides definitions and examples for the five major ERSs. Note that individuals do not have to use all ERSs sequentially to regulate their emotions.

The earliest ERS to be identified by researchers is situation selection and modification, in which individuals may select or modify the situation to regulate their exposures to emotional stimuli (or cues). Situation selection refers to avoiding a situation with negative emotional stimuli; for example, an individual may delete an email without reading it when perceiving—because of who it is from—that this email may evoke a negative emotion. Situation modification refers to modifying the situational features to reduce exposure to negative emotional stimuli; for example, an individual may prefer emails (over face-to-face communications) to deliver bad news to avoid seeing the receiver's reactions to the bad news because witnessing the negative reactions may cause stress for the messenger (Sussman & Sproull, 1999).

After individuals are exposed to emotional stimuli, attention deployment can be used because attending to emotional stimuli is a necessary condition for an emotional feeling to arise (Elfenbein, 2007). In organizational contexts, attention deployment often takes the form of temporary internal redirection of attention (Gross & Thompson, 2007). For example, an individual may decide to read an email later when perceiving—because of who it is from—that this email may make him or her experience negative emotions.

After individuals attend to emotional stimuli. the kinds of emotions that arise depend on how individuals interpret emotional stimuli. Reappraisal, defined as altering emotional feeling rules or emotional schema, can be utilized during the interpretation process in which individuals interpret the emotional stimuli in a new way, resulting in a different emotional feeling (Elfenbein, 2007; Gross 1998). For example, an individual may decide to believe that, because emotions in email are often subject to misinterpretation (Byron, 2008), what the sender intended to convey was not as aggressive as the message seemed

Experience regulation requires deliberate changes in emotional states outside of the registration process via “a host of psychodynamic defense mechanisms” (Elfenbein, 2007, p. 336) such as suppression, denial, venting like punching a desk (Sutton, 1991), and social sharing, which refers to talking about emotions with others in order to change one's emotional state (Rimé, Philippot, Boca, & Mesquita, 1992). For example, an individual may talk with a colleague about the content of an email as a means of coping with the negative emotions triggered by the message

Display regulation concerns the managing of external emotion expressions without changing internal emotional states. Display regulation often involves two sub-tasks, hiding undesired emotion expressions (that one is not supposed to display) and displaying desired emotion expressions (that may or may not be genuinely felt). Continuing the example using email, display regulation might take the form of sending an email response that is upbeat even though the sender is irritated.

Research on ERSs in organizational communication mainly focused on individuals utilizing ERSs to regulate their own emotions. The ERSs reviewed above may also be utilized to regulate the emotion of other organizational members such as coworkers. For example, leadership may volunteer to deal with dificult customers so that subordinates do not need to deal with those customers themselves. In this case, leadership uses situation selection and modification to manage subordinates' exposures to dificult customers.

Moreover, the ERSs discussed above may also be used in CMC (Tracy & Tracy, 1998). One needs only look to Twitter or Blog comments to know that CMC is becoming increasingly emotion-evoking. However, the IS literature suggests that communication media may be a double-edged sword—empowering and impeding individuals' use of ERSs (Sussman & Sproull, 1999; Walther, 2007). A potentially fruitful investigation into technology support for ERSs may be found using the technology afordance and constraint lens.

## 2.2. Technology afordance and constraint theory

Technology afordances refer to possibilities for (Markus & Silver, 2008) or the ease of (Leonardi, 2011; Strong et al., 2014; Treem & Leonardi, 2012) taking certain actions provided by a technology for goal-oriented individuals. Technology constraints, in contrast, refer to a lack of possibilities for or the dificulty of taking certain actions in the achievement of goal-oriented behaviors. In other words, technology afordances are action potentials provided by a technology for individuals who seek to achieve certain goals while technology constraints are potential stumbling blocks. Also, consistent with Gibson (1986)'s original afordance perspective, technology afordances are initially discussed at the individual level. Recent theoretical development extends technology afordances to higher levels such as group and organizational levels (Leonardi, 2013; Rice et al., 2017; Strong et al., 2014; Treem & Leonardi, 2012; Vaast, Safadi, Lapointe, & Negoita, 2017). Hence, the technology afordance perspective could be applied to understand the facilitating (or inhibiting) role of technology at multiple levels.

Despite the existence of different affordance perspectives (Norman. 1988). researchers in the area of technology use and consequence generally agree that technology afordance is a relational concept that depends on the interaction among technology and individuals' goals (Strong et al., 2014; Volkof & Strong, 2013; Zammuto, Grifith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). Importantly, technology afordances are distinct from technology capabilities. Technology capabilities are what a technology allows individuals to do and are the same across all individuals' usages or goals. Technology afordances, in contrast, are what a technology allows individuals to do in pursuit of their goals. The same technology may ofer diferent afordances for individuals with diferent goals. For example, email ofers the capability of sending files to others. For individuals who want to speed up work progress, email ma aford rotating the responsibility of working on a project among distributed group members with diferent working schedules; for individuals who want eficient discussion, email may aford sharing files with members sitting next to each other.

Moreover, IS research on technology afordance has moved beyond the afordance itself to also examine what may afect the exercising and actualization of affordances —stated another way how do goal-oriented individuals bring the action potentials of technology afordance to reality (Volkof & Strong, 2013)— as well as the relationships or dependencies among afordances (Strong et al., 2014). In their proposed framework for data analysis using afordance as an analytical lens (Fig. 1), Bygstad, Munkvold, and Volkoff (2016) suggested researchers, after identifving affordances, also identify conditions given rise to by the tech-organizational context that may afect the exercising and actualization of identified afordances. Specifically, researchers are recommended to identify potential enabling conditions that initially allow the exercising of affordances, stimulating conditions (often organizational arrangements that make it easier to actualize the afordances), and releasing conditions (often decisions that release constraints for the actualization of affordances) (Demetriou, 2009; Fleetwood, 2011). Using technology affordance as a theoretical lens to understand the role of communication media, this study seeks to understand how communication media may support the use of emotion reg ulation strategies (ERSs) for individuals working in a negative emotion-laden environment. Specifically, we seek to identify media afordances for regulating negative emotions and associated conditions that may afect the exercising and actualization of those affordances.

## 3. Research design and methods

We employed a case-based, inductive, and qualitative study approach (Elliott & Lazenbatt, 2005), which allowed us to investigate media afordances in support of emotion regulation without needing to test preconceived notions. The existing literature provides insights regarding the diferent ERSs individuals may employ and suggests the technology afordance perspective as a suitable theoretical lens to understand the potential role of communication media. The existing literature, however, does not provide an extant and systematic understanding of media afordances for emotion regulation, the focus of this paper. In the following, the research setting is explained; then, data collection and analysis processes are described in detail.

## 3.1. Research setting

Interviews were conducted with IT help-desk employees of a Fortune 500 energy company (EnergyCo hereafter) headquartered in a Midwestern US city. The IT help desk is the first point of contact when users have technical problems and is often the intermediary between users and some other IT groups that are responsible for solving users' problems. Users contacting the IT help desk often experience negative emotions such as anger and frustration and may (intentionally or unintentionally) express those negative emotions to help-desk employees. Also, just like other occupations, IT help-desk employees may need to deal with negative emotions from coworkers and supervisors. IT help-desk employees are (explicitly or implicitly) required to regulate their emotions when interacting with others such as users and coworkers expressing negative emotions, and are considered emotional laborers (Rutner et al., 2008).

At EnergyCo. IT help-desk emplovees within the service center and within two groups that work closely with the service center (the end user support group and the business partner group) participated. The service center is the first point of contact for employees (referred to as “customers”) who have problems with technology. When fielding a request for support, service center members can (a) solve problems themselves, (b) assign tickets to initiate work by other IT groups based on the information provided by customers, or (c) attempt to solve the problems and then later assign them to other IT groups, if unable to resolve those problems. The end user support group—apart from helping customers solve hardware problems physically—interacts frequently with almost all IT groups including the service center via communication media. The business partner group is viewed as the messenger between the IT side and the business side of the organization and interacts frequently with the service center via communication media.

A general understanding of the media ecosystem at EnergyCo is integral to understanding how interviewees described the affordances of communication media in regulating emotions. The first author visited and observed the service center prior to the forma data collection. The service center manager, who was not one of the interviewees, was asked to describe the service center structure, job duties, group goals, and individual performance matrices. The manager also demonstrated how communication media were used.

A description of media available at EnergyCo, media features, media usages, and related communication norms or rules is summarized in Table 2. Take as an example the self-service ticketing system. The ticketing system is one major way for customers to contact the service center: Customers create their own tickets, which are included in the ticket queue with the ticket number and customer identity displaved. Tickets in the queue are not automatically assigned to service center members. Instead, members pick tickets to work on whenever they have time (usually between phone calls). The service center has a 1-hour time limit norm for the ticketing system. That is, a ticket should not be sitting in the queue for longer than 1 hour. In addition, the ticketing system could als be used to communicate with other help desk employees by adding notes in the tickets.

## 3.2. Data collection

Prior to formal data collection, the first author conducted pilot interviews with four MBA students with varying levels of work experience and were familiar with CMC at work. The purpose of pilot interviews was to expose unexpected issues created by the schedule and/or wording of questions. After pilot interviews, one ERS (reappraisal) was tentatively dropped from the schedule of questions because pilot results suggested that media do not aford or constrain the use of reappraisal. It is necessary to point out that interviewees during the formal data collection voluntarily mentioned their use of reappraisal at work. Yet, like pilot interviews, interviewees' comments indicated that media did not afect their use of reappraisal.

Data collection and analysis occurred iteratively according to recommendations for improving the quality of qualitative research (Eisenhardt, 1989; Tracy, 2010). Formal interviews were conducted in two phases: Eleven service center members were interviewed during the first phase. While the majority of information provided during the first phase focused on emotional interaction with customers, some focused on emotional communication with peers. Of note, interviewees seemed to hold a different attitude towards emotion regulation in peer-peer interactions, for example, the ERS of situation selection and modification was mentioned more often in interactions with peers than those with customers. Hence, the second phase of the interviews focused on peer-peer interactions The service center manager helped to identify appropriate interviewees—those who interact frequently with peers via media—for the second phase. Nine more help-desk emplovees (three service center members. four members of the end user support group, and two members of the business partner group) were interviewed during the second phase. Interviewees together provided a total of 204 stories or incidents regarding using ERSs during CMC at work. Each interview lasted about 1 hour. The interviews rendered ap proximately 461 double-spaced pages of transcripts. Primary interview questions are listed in the Appendix A. Four (out of twenty) interviewees were female. Participants' ages ranged from 30 to 50. Average organizational tenure was 4.70 years (SD = 4.61 years).

The first interview questions asked interviewees to think about emotion broadly (negative and positive, weak and strong) and to discuss emotional experiences due to interpersonal interactions with customers, peers, supervisors, or subordinates at work. Interviewees' answers to these questions focused overwhelmingly on negative emotions. Hence, subsequent questions (recalling incidents of using a certain ERS during computer-mediated interactions) inquired about how participants regulated those negative emotions.

All interviews were semi-structured, which means the researchers followed a schedule of questions but were also free to ask follow-up questions and probe for elaboration (Rubin & Rubin, 2011; Tracy, 2010). The semi-structured interview enabled deep exploration of the research questions and the researchers could check their understanding of interviewees' remarks throughout the interview itself (Furneaux & Wade, 2011).

## 3.3. Data analysis

To identify media afordances used by interviewees for emotion regulation, transcriptions were analyzed using a modified version of constant comparative analysis (Charmaz, 2006; Glaser & Strauss, 1967) in which data were sorted inductively without working from preconceived categories of media affordances for emotion regulation (cf. Kelley & Bisel, 2014: Kramer & Crespy, 2011). Analysis proceeded in the following steps: after data reduction (Lindlof & Taylor, 2011), the first author conducted open coding for all 461 pages of transcripts. Next, the researcher engaged in a process of sorting all open codes into similar categories. This process, sometimes labeled, “focused coding,” involves an ongoing and iterative comparison of codes to codes, codes to categories (sets of similar codes), and categories to categories in order to find the best-fitting category. In our case, categories were identified corresponding to media afordances facilitating emotion regulation. Focused coding took several rounds and continued until no new category was identifiable from interviewees' descriptions and existing categories remained stable, achieving theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). A label was then created for each media afordance category that emerged from the data. Finally, in a process similar to axial coding – analyzing data as a coherent whole in relation to emerged categories (Charmaz, 2006), the interrelationships among identified media affordance categories were determined.

Analysis suggests that negativity coming from communication partners can be thought of as having contagious qualities, and that individuals' attempts to regulate emotion when interacting with negativity is akin to resisting contamination with contagious viruses (progctively and regctively) in the medical context. Identified media affordances for emotion regulation suggest that communication media may counteract emotional contagion. However, no existing theoretical framework is able to account for the dynamic interviewees described regarding the facilitating role of communication media suficiently. Hence, we borrowed from the medical metaphor when labeling identified media affordances for emotion regulation—Table 3 summarizes the identified media affordances, one at the group level and three at the individual level. These four identified media afordances together suggest that communication media may facilitate emotion regulation via negativity decontaminating, a concept original to this study

After the afordances and the interrelationships among them were identified, another round of analysis was conducted to look for enabling conditions, stimulating conditions and releasing conditions as well as concrete outcomes should the afordance be actualized using Fig. 1 as the guidance. Interestingly, though Bygstad et al. (2016) only explicitly discussed how the tech-organizational contexts give rise to stimulating and releasing conditions, this study found that the tech-organizational contexts at EnergyCo may act the opposite way—giving rise to inhibiting condition (making it harder to actualize afordances and the opposite of stimulating condition) and suppressing condition (adding constraints for the actualization of afordances and the opposite of releasing condition)—or even act both ways (stimulating and inhibiting, releasing and suppressing) in some cases. Detailed discussion will be provided in the findings section below.

## 4. Findings and analysis

Understandings obtained in this study are specific to emotion regulation when interacting with communication partners such as customers and peers expressing negative emotions at work. The most frequently mentioned negative emotions expressed by communication partners in this study were “frustration” (mentioned by 19 interviewees), and “anger” (mentioned by 10 interviewees) We use negativity to label the negative emotions encountered by our interviewees. Further, results pointed to two types of negativity, one targeted at a specific service center member while the other targeted at the service center group (labeled group hereafter) as a whole–this latter type of negativity largely came from customers.

In the following, we discuss first the group level media afordance (negativity filtering), and then the three individual level media afordances (negativity isolating, negativity barriering, and negativity containing). For each of the identified afordances, we discuss elements (i.e., media and ERSs) giving rise to each afordance (Table 4); we also diagram our findings using the afordance structure (Fig. 1) provided by Bygstad et al. (2016) as a template.

## 4.1. Negativity filtering

Negativity filtering refers to media' potential to reduce the amount and the intensity of negativity reaching the group. Negativity filtering is a group level media afordance, which may be exercised by individuals who are seeking to support group-level goals (Strong et al., 2014: Volkoff & Strong, 2013). Fig. 2 below shows its affordance structure

The negativity filtering affordance applies to negativity targeting the group prior to its arrival (Table 3) such as frustrated customers who are about to contact the group but have not made the call or are waiting in the call queue. When there is negativity targeting the group (enabling condition) —specifically, an outage afecting many users —group leaders exercise this afordance to control the group's exposure to negativity, facilitating the use of situation selection and modification for the entire group. Should the negativity filtering afordance be actualized, some (if not all) of the negativity targeting the group will be stopped outside the boundary of the group, and the group (all members) will not need to interact with (as much or as intense of) negativity (concrete outcome), thereby preventing or reducing the contamination on the group as a whole.

This study identified two stimulating conditions that make it easier to actualize the negativity filtering afordance. The first stimulating condition is the technical setup (1st bullet point in Fig. 2), specifically, CA Service Desk is set as the default browser page and the phone system plays the announcements (if any) prior to connecting callers with the group. This technical setup makes it easier for communication partners to visually or audibly notice problem announcements. The other stimulating condition is acknowledgement and assurance (2nd bullet point in Fig. 2). That is, problem announcements indicate that the service center is aware of the issue(s) and reassure users that actions are being taken. For example, an interviewee explained,

if there is a front-end message and says, yes we do know about it. Yes, we are working on it and yes there is a plan in place. [Then, customers think to themselves] “Oh, good, they got it. Perfect”.

This study also identified a condition that could be both stimulating and inhibiting, the organizational norm regarding posting problem announcements (3rd bullet point in Fig. 2). The norm at EnergyCo is that if an outage afects many users, then a problem announcement will be posted on CA Service Desk; if the outage afects many users and multiple tools, then an announcement will also be posted on the phone system. One interviewee mentioned an on-going outage at the time of the interview,

We don't have a front-end message [posted on the phone system] right now because it's not afecting everything, just one tool. [If] it's jus afecting one tool… We put up an announcement on our service desk [CA Service Desk]

Thus, in situations where outages afect a large number of users but only one tool, this norm about posting announcements makes it easier to actualize the negativity filtering afordance provided by CA Service Desk (stimulating condition) but harder to actualize the negativity filtering afordance provided by the phone system (inhibiting condition)

In addition, this study identified knowledge and habit as a releasing condition (4th bullet point in Fig. 2) that removes constraints for actualizing the negativity filtering affordance provided by CA Service Desk. Specifically, users should have the knowledge about and form a habit of checking problem announcements on CA Service Desk before calling the group, as commented by one interviewee,

…the first thing I do when I come in is I'll check the announcement board (on CA Service Desk). Now a lot of people don't do that, especially end users. It's very rare that an end user actually will check the announcements before calling in. I don't know if it's just because of lack of knowledge on how to get there or something like that.

Thus, individuals' knowledge about and habit of checking CA Service Desk for problem announcements removes constraints for actualizing the negativity filtering afordance provided by CA Service Desk (releasing condition). Without such knowledge and habit, individuals may still contact the group about issues that the group is aware of and has posted on CA Service Desk, hence failing to actualize CA Service Desk's negativity filtering potential.

The above discussion and quotes point to the value of efective group level negativity decontamination and various conditions given rise by the tech-organizational context that may facilitate or hinder such group level efort. In the face of negativity targeting the group, if group leaders are able to actualize the group level afordance of negativity filtering, then the number and intensity of negativity that reaches the group is reduced, hence eliminating or reducing the need for individual group members to deal with the negativity targeting the group. If group leaders fail to actualize the negativity filtering afordance in the face of negativity targeting the group or if the negativity initial targets a certain group member, then group members may leverage individual level media afordances, as discussed below.

## 4.2. Negativity isolating

The afordance of negativity isolating refers to media's potential to help individuals avoid interaction with negativity, either negativity initially targeting the group (and then possibly being assigned to oneself) or negativity targeting oneself (Table 3). Hence, negativity isolating has two subcategories applicable to diferent types of incoming negativity; both may facilitate the use of situation selection and modification to control one's exposure to negativity. As shown in Fig. 3, when there is anticipated interaction with negativity (enabling condition) — either negativity targeting the group arrives or potential negativity targeting oneself — individuals may exercise this negativity isolating afordance. Should an individual actualize either subcategory of the negativity isolating affordance, the individual does not need to interact with the negativity (concrete outcome) and hence will not be contaminated. In addition, there are several stimulating, inhibiting and suppressing conditions that afect the actualization of the negativity isolating afordance; these will be detailed below.

## 4.2.1. Avoiding dealing with negativity targeting the group

Consider the situation in which negativity targeting the group has arrived but has not yet been assigned. In this case, negativity isolating refers to avoiding dealing with negativity targeting the group (Table 3). Specifically, when a historically negative communication partner contacts the group, group members may exercise this subcategory of the negativity isolating afordance to prevent the focal negativity from being assigned to themselves. If this afordance is actualized by a group member, then the member him/ herself does not need to interact with the focal negativity and, instead, passes the negativity to another group member, thus avoiding the contamination on him/herself

The afordance of avoiding dealing with negativity targeting the group may be enacted in the ticketing system and the group chat (Table 4). When communication partners submit a ticket to the ticketing system or send a message to the group chat, the ticket or chat message will not be assigned. Instead, group members choose voluntarily whether to pick up that ticket or chat. An interviewee provided an example of negativity isolating behavior in the ticketing system,

We don't tell anybody to take tickets…. You'll see that maybe a certain ticket will stay there because of who it is. So we have certain people [who]… are always a problem. [For example]… every time there is a ticket for Paul, nobody will pick that up because you know if you call Paul, it will be an hour-long phone call, and he will be yelling and mad the whole time.

Two stimulating conditions and one inhibiting condition are identified. The first stimulating condition is group member's autonomy to engage in interactions in the ticketing system and the group chat (1st bullet point in Fig. 3), which can be seen from the quote above. From another vantage point, this stimulating condition is also supported by the observation that there is an absence of “cherrypicking” behavior in the phone system, where phone calls are automatically assigned based on availability. One interviewee commented on the inability to pick phone calls to avoid dealing with certain customers,

## 4.2.2. Preventing negativity from targeting oneself

In the case of potential negativity targeting oneself (enabling condition), negativity isolating refers to preventing negativity from targeting oneself (Table 3). If this afordance is actualized, then the member has no negativity targeting oneself to manage (concrete outcome).

There were two specific methods used by interviewees to prevent negativity from targeting themselves (Table 4). The first is taking advantaging of IM status. The IM status, which is supposed to indicate individuals' true availability, may be strategically utilized to stop negative partners from contacting oneself. An interviewee mentioned an incident in which peers from another IT group tried to utilize IM status to stop receiving emotional messages from him,

there is one time where I was trying to communicate and trying to get something fixed and it was one of those bounce back and forth times and they went ‘away’ on their (IM) status.

The other way to prevent negativity from targeting oneself is to contact communication partners using media through which partners are unlikely to express negativity. Employees at EnergyCo are careful with what they write in email because email is documented. For example, one interviewee mentioned.

when I know it's going to be a problem, I usually try and avoid written communication… I don't want to document something that they pull up on me later.

Hence, when expecting potential negativity from partners, individuals may purposively contact partners via email as partners are less likely to be (highly) negative in email communications. For example, an interviewee named Austin explained his preference for email to contact dificult customers,

if you email someone, I think they might have an easier tendency just to say, “Oh, whatever. Austin is a hassle. I don't want to type this.”

One obvious stimulating condition that can be seen from the above quotes is media use autonomy (4th bullet point in Fig. 3), so that group members may choose their preferred medium to interact with partners or to manipulate IM status as they wish. The other stimulating condition is physical distance (5th bullet point in Fig. 3). One interviewee described a time when physical distance with a partner made it easier for him to manipulate IM status and how this is not feasible if the partner were collocated.

you can't really ignore a direct peer because they are in the same room most of the time. So, if you ignore them, they can stand up and kind of point at you and say, “I know you're there.” But with other peers, there is one time where I went “Do not disturb” on [IM status].

Leader intervention (6th bullet point in Fig. 3) also serves as a suppressing condition that adds constraints for actualizing this subcategory of the negativity isolating afordance. As can be seen from the following example, seeking leadership intervention constrained the peer's actualization of the negativity isolating afordance that s/he sought to utilize via the IM status changes.

there is one time [when my peer] went “away” on their (IM) status… and I knew they are still there. So, I then emailed the supervisor because they weren't responding.

## 4.3. Negativity barriering

When interaction with negativity is unavoidable (enabling condition), either because negativity initially targeting the group is assigned to oneself or because negativity targeting oneself arrives, the media afordance that individuals may leverage is negativity barriering (Fig. 4). Negativity barriering refers to media's potential to act as a bufer or cushion between oneself and the unavoidable negativity (Table 3); it works like personal protective equipment such as gloves and masks that healthcare workers use when interacting with contagious material. Negativity barriering has three subcategories (Table 3), reducing the duration of the interaction with negativity, reducing the extent of the interaction with negativity, and delaving the interaction with negativity. Should the negativity barriering afordance be actualized, individuals' interaction with the negativity is reduced or delayed to a later and more preferred time (concrete outcomes), thereby weakening or delaying contamination on oneself. Related stimulating and inhibiting conditions are detailed later.

## 4.3.1. Reducing the duration of the interaction with negativity

The afordance of reducing the duration of the interaction with negativity refers to media's potential to reduce the total amount of time individuals spend interacting with the unavoidable negativity. Reduced interaction time may reduce individuals' total exposure to negativity, facilitating the use of situation selection and modification and weakening the contamination on oneself (concrete outcome).

Several communication media may aford reducing the duration of the interaction with negativity (Table 4). Email, which allows individuals to communicate asynchronously, the extent to which individuals are working together at the same time with a common focus (Dennis, Fuller, & Valacich, 2008) without being co-present with communication partners, may reduce the total time individuals spend interacting with negativity. For example, one interviewee explained her preference for email when contacting a dificult customer,

I just want to e-mail you, you know… I don't want to talk to you on the phone about this, I don't want to instant message you, because you're just going to keep bugging me if I instant message you, because it goes ‘ding, ding, ding, ding.

Here, media selection is driven, in part, by the desire to avoid synchronous communication with and the constant interruptions from negative partners.

The phone, though not preferred by the above interviewee, may also aford reducing the duration of the interaction with negativity. The phone at EnergyCo has an on-hold button (which terminates bidirectional exchange between group members and communication partners) and an on-mute button (which terminates unidirectional stimuli transmission from group members to partners). When group members put partners on-hold, communication cues from partners will not be received and hence the actua interaction time with negativity is reduced. In contrast. group members are still exposed to partners' negativity when the on-mute button is used. For example, an interviewee commented that when the on-mute button is used,

I can hear if they're frustrated, I can hear they're saying something, cussing or whatever… (I feel) insecure, very uncomfortable, but I'm not

## 4.3.2. Reducing the extent of the interaction with negativity

The afordance of reducing the extent of the interaction with negativity refers to media's potential to reduce the closeness (or intimacy) of the interaction with the unavoidable negativity. Should this afordance be actualized, interaction with negativity is less close/intimate, weakening the contamination on oneself (concrete outcome). The less close/intimate interaction with negativity helps to limit cues or stimuli transmitted between group members and negative partners, consequently facilitating the use of diferent ERSs.

For the ERSs of situation selection and modification and of display regulation, reducing the extent of the interaction helps via reducing the amount of negativity transmitted from negative partners to group members (situation selection and modification) and the amount of expressive cues that group members need to hide or paint on when responding back to partners (display regulation). Email and chat. which transmit fewer symbol sets compared to the phone, provide such an affordance. Consider. for example, the comments below,

when you have… [a] cranky [client]… going through written communication is sometimes easier because of the fact that you do not have to listen to their sarcasm that they have when you have that verbal and face-to-face communication.

that technology bufer between us, helps to control. By bufer I mean… sometimes it is harder when you are talking to someone face-to-face to hide the physical emotions, to hide those physical tells… I am upset, I do not like you, this is boring.…That is tough to hide when you are right next to each other. But in an email. it is easy to hide that.

Here, members' comments illustrate how email reduces the amount of negativity transmitted to individuals (facilitating situation selection and modification), and it reduces the amount of expressive cues to be hidden (facilitating display regulation) respectively.

For the ERSs of attention deployment and experience regulation, reducing the extent of the interaction helps by hiding group members' use of ERSs from partners. Interviewees reported two specific attention deployment methods (multitasking during on-going interaction with negativity and delaying attending to negativity) and two specific experience regulation methods (venting and socia sharing, see Table 1). A common obstacle inhibiting the use of these methods is the potential negative reactions from partners upon finding out about individuals' attention deployment or experience regulation behaviors (Tracy & Tracy, 1998). Take multitasking behavior as an example, an interviewee commented,

my problem with the video camera on the phone is the multitasking and that someone would think that I'm not paying attention to them or

not listening to them because I'm not looking at them straight in their eyes.

Here, partners' negative reactions to the group member' multi-tasking behavior is an obstacle for group members' use of multitasking, and the other attention deployment or experience regulation behaviors. Communication media that can help hide group members' use of such behaviors may be leveraged to remove the obstacle.

Similar to the above discussion for situation selection and modification and for display regulation, email and chat may facilitate the use of attention deployment/experience regulation via reducing the extent of the interaction with negativity and hiding members use of ERSs from negative partners. Compared to other means of communication such as face-to-face, email and chat transmit fewer symbol sets, which makes it easier to hide group members' use of attention deployment/experience regulation. Consider, for example, the following comment, which shows that chat can help members hide social sharing behaviors (with peers) from negative customers (on the phone).

we have a couple of users that I don't think anybody in the service center likes to take those calls…. “Oh, we just got [a known and problematic client] on the phone,” [and our peer will say] “Oh poor you. Alright well deal with it.”

When the communication is via the phone, members may still utilize this afordance via pressing the on-hold/mute button or via turning of the camera on the phone. Take venting as an example, some interviewees commented,

over the phone I don't really control a lot of my facial expressions or my mannerisms. You know sometimes I throw my hands around or touch my head like that…but obviously the users can't see that [because the camera is of].

So yes, a lot of the times I have thrown my headset or my pen or sometimes I use bad words. Of course I mute.

Here, these physical outbursts are masked from communication partners by the media and hence allow group members to freely vent or express frustrations without worrying about it afecting partners directly, and importantly, the potential backlashes from partners.

Two stimulating conditions are identified. The first is media use autonomy (1st bullet point in Fig. 4), which includes both the autonomy to choose media for interactions and the autonomy to use media features such as the on-mute/hold button and the camera on/of. Take as an example the autonomy to choose media for interactions, an interviewee explained his preference for chat over the phone,

If I know there is a subject that will be emotional….I will use instant message at that point, so that way it will give me a chance to think about what I am going to say next. So that gives me my 10 or 15 seconds to come up with the right answer instead of the emotional answer, which would probably end up being the wrong one.

Interviewees' autonomy to use the on-mute/hold button or to turn off the camera on the phone also makes it easier to actualize the afordance of reducing the extent of the interaction. Several interviewees described their frequent use of the on-mute/hold button when interacting with negative partners. For example, an interview commented,

So you kind of have to sometimes put the phone on mute and just (makes growl noise) and then go [and say to the client] “Okay, let's start over again.”

The importance of media use autonomy is probably best shown via the contrasting requirements and members' experiences with the camera on the phone. A few months prior to the start of this study, EnergyCo instituted a policy that required the camera to remain on. This requirement added emotional burden to group members, as mentioned by interviewees,

when we had to turn our cameras on all the time. You pretty much just had to be (happy voice) yea!!! the whole time.

And then you need to make sure that if somebody is upset and you're seeing them and they're seeing you. You don't want to look like you don't care. So you have to show emotion. You have to show that you care about their situation otherwise it could get worse. They would get mad because you don't care.

Eventually, leadership removed the requirement, due to negative feedbacks from group members. Interviewees felt that emotion regulation was easier without the camera-on requirement. One interviewee commented on her ability to mask emotional expressions,

That's why I [like that ability to turn] the camera of. Because it's hard to stay composed when it's a[n emotional] situation… my husband would say, you could see my tells, like when somebody gets under my skin, sometimes he can just read it on my face, that's why I'm kind of glad the camera's of.

The other stimulating condition is physical distance (4th bullet point in Fig. 4). Group members and their partners are usually not collocated. This physical distance makes it easier to actualize the afordance of reducing the extent of the interaction. For example,

even though on the phone you could have a frown and you could be doing the scream emotions on your face, all your coworkers know you're pissed of but the person at the other line, you're “Oh that's very helpful. I'll be happy to help you with that.”

Here, the interviewee's comment shows that while s/he could “make faces" when on the phone with a customer located else where, s/he can't do the same thing without the physical distance between themselves and the partner.

## 4.3.3. Delaying the interaction with negativity

When interaction with negativity is unavoidable, media also may help individuals avoid interacting with negativity now via afording delaying the interaction with negativity, which facilitates the use of attention deployment, experience regulation, and display regulation. If this afordance is actualized, then the member's interaction with negativity is delayed (to a later and more preferred time). For example,

I would purposively delay the communication if I know I am going to respond emotionally or that person is going to respond emotionally. I will put more thoughts into it and use certain words into that email.

Thus, time delay is seen as key to establishing one's own (and possibly partner's) self-control in communication and managing the negative consequences of the partner's emotional messages directed at the group member.

A common obstacle inhibiting the use of attention deployment, experience regulation and display regulation is that individuals do not have the time to use these ERSs when interacting with negative partners. The afordance of delaying the interaction with negativity comes to the rescue. Such afordance could be provided by the ticketing system and especially by email and chat, as shown below,

I had a ticket this last week [with a challenging] person… every time you talk to him there is something else wrong, there is something else going on, he needs something else. Those types of people… definitely make you want to put it of until the end of the day or put it of until tomorrow or do it the first thing in the morning to get it over with.

the nice thing about written media is, if it comes in as an e-mail or an IM, is that I can have that. It is something that is in front of me and I can mark it to say “Hey I got to go do this but I do not have to do it right now”.

This afordance could also be provided by the phone. Although customers are told to call the 800-number (which puts them in the call queue) when they have technical issues, some customers may occasionally call a certain group member's direct extension if they had worked with that member in the past. Group members, however, may not pick up this type of calls. For example, an interviewee shared how he/she dealt with a frequent and demanding caller,

so when I see her call particularly on my smart phone which identifies her. I will let the call go to voicemail and then call her, return the call probably an hour later, maybe two hours later. When I had a chance to clear everything else that I'm working on and then I call her and it also enables me to send out myself so that when I deal her I can deal with her in a complete manner.

Thus, the ability to delay and return to a demanding partner later is seen as helpful in managing the emotionally-taxing nature of the IT help-desk job.

We identified three stimulating conditions. The first is partner identity (5th bullet point in Fig. 4), as can be seen from the above quote—Without the partner identity revealed by the smart phone, it would be dificult for the group member to delay the interaction with the demanding customer. The second stimulating condition is media use autonomy (1st bullet point in Fig. 4), specifically the autonomy to choose media for interactions. One interviewee explained his/her preference for email,

Email is the best because not only do you have time to think about it but also it takes a delay before when you can respond to. So even if they respond to you, they can expect the delay before they get another response; whereas when a chat window it up, it's right there and it's real time.

In other words, the asynchronous nature of certain media such as email can strategically slow the growth of negative emotions, and group members' autonomy to choose those media helps them actualize such potential in negative emotional communication. The third stimulating condition is physical distance (3rd bullet point in Fig. 4). Not being collocated makes it dificult for communication partners to confirm that group members are purposively delaying the interaction.

In summary, the afordances of negativity isolating and negativity barriering may be leveraged by individuals to counteract negativity contamination on oneself, either avoiding the contamination from partner's negativity-laden messaging or dampening/ delaying the contamination. However, negativity-laden messaging in organizations does not occur in a vacuum where only individuals and their negativity-expressive partners are present; emotion-laden messaging, instead, occurs in contexts where there are outsiders (Gofman, 1959) such as peers, subordinates, and customers who are not involved in the focal negativity-laden messaging. If the manner in which the negativity-laden messaging occurs is not well regulated, outsiders may be contaminated. The last individual level media afordance identified (negativity containing) may help counteract negativity contamination on outsiders.

## 4.4. Negativity containing

Negativity containing refers to media's potential to prevent contaminating outsiders who are not involved in the negativity-laden messaging (Table 3). Here, individuals are trying to manage outsiders' exposure to negativity (situation selection and modification). This afordance could be exercised when negativity (initially targeting the group or oneself) may potentially crossover onto others (enabling condition). Should this affordance be actualized, outsiders do not need to interact with the negativity, avoiding contaminating outsiders (concrete outcome). Fig. 5 shows the afordance structure for negativity containing.

Media that group members frequently utilize to aford negativity containing are email and individual chat (Table 4). Compared to face-to-face and group chat communications, email and individual chat provide better compartmentalization, or the extent to which a medium restricts the concurrent availability of communicative cues from an interaction to only those participating in the interaction (Reinsch, Turner, & Tinsley, 2008). Such compartmentalization helps to contain the negativity and to prevent the negativity from spilling onto outsiders. Take email as an example. ## 5. Discussion

We started with the premise that emotion regulation strategies are likely increasingly being enacted during computer-mediated encounters (Tracy & Tracy, 1998). As such, understanding how technology afords strategic eforts may be important to help describe and understand emotion regulation in the modern workplace. Using the technology afordance perspective, we documented communication media's potential to facilitate emotion regulation in a negativity-laden working environment, i.e., negativity decontaminating; this negativity decontaminating potential includes four media afordances operating at two levels. Specifically, we documented that, at the group level, negativity filtering may be exercised by group leaders to prevent all members from being contaminated by negativity targeting the group. At the individual level, media may aford negativity isolating, negativity barriering, and negativity containing. Individuals who actualize negativity isolating do not need to interact with the negativity themselves, hence avoiding being contaminated. Negativity barriering may be leveraged to add a cushion between individuals and the unavoidable negativity to impact the duration or the extent of the interaction with negativity or to delay the interaction, weakening or delaying the contamination on themselves. Finally, negativity containing may be leveraged to prevent contaminating outsiders.

Like all studies, this study has limitations. One limitation is the lack of triangulation (or, the use of multiple techniques to collect or analyze data). This study relies on interview data. Future research may add additional data collection methods such as observation for cross-checking (Eisenhardt, 1989; Jick, 1979). To compensate for the lack of triangulation, we used one validation strategy, member checking (Creswell. 2007). Another limitation is tentatively excluding reappraisal from the interview protocol. Although our decision to exclude reappraisal can be justified theoretically (according to the definition of and the literature on reappraisal) and empirically (according to our pilot interview and interviewees' voluntary mentioning of their use of reappraisal during formal data collection), it might be better to keep it in the interview protocol during formal data collection.

Despite the limitations, this study ofers several theoretical contributions. In the following sections we compare our findings to similar findings in the interaction management literature to draw conclusions with “stronger internal validity, wider generalizability. and higher conceptual level” (Eisenhardt, 1989, p.544). Our findings both enfold (or confirm) and extend the literature on managing unexpected or unwanted interaction via communication media. Below we discuss how our findings enfold the interaction management literature, extend the interaction management literature, and contribute to other literatures.

## 5.1. Enfolding the interaction management literature

Managing interaction has received extensive attention in the management, communication, and IS literatures. For example, research on the connectivity paradox faced by distributed workers indicates that technology both enables distributed work benefits such as flexibility while also undermines those benefits by the constant telepresence and interruptions of work and communication partners (Fonner & Rolof, 2012; Leonardi, Treem, & Jackson, 2010). As such, distributed workers are forced to manage this paradox by strategically increasing “distance" with communication partners. Other examples include the extensive research on impression management (O'Sullivan, 2000) and the hyper-personal model of CMC (Walther, 1996, 2007) that both suggest individuals often take advantage of communication media to control how they present themselves in front of others. Inherent in the efforts to manage interactions is deliberate decision making about whether, when, and how to interact with others. Much of what we glean from our data regarding media afordances for emotion regulation may be best viewed as an efort to manage interactions (the whether, the when, and the how) with negativity-expressive partners, as shown in Table 5.

## 5.1.1. Managing whether to interact

This study found that communication media have the potential to prevent interaction with negativity at the group (negativity filtering) and individual (negativity isolating) levels, helping with managing whether to interact (Table 5). Utilizing communication media to manage whether to interact has received some (though limited) attention—existing research often started with the assumption that there is a communication exchange and then examined how media afect the exchange (Dennis et al., 2008). The possibility that media may afect whether the communication exchange occurs or not deserves more attention.

Despite the limited attention to communication media's role in managing whether to interact, the individual level afordance of negativity isolating finds its counterpart in the existing interaction management literature. For example, the connectivity paradox literature suggests that to deal with the over-connecting and constant interruption problem, individuals often use “dissimulating” (i.e., using media to “disguise their actual work status, so as to provide a cover that would allow them to regain flexibility and/or focus,” Leonardi et al., 2010, p., 97) such as changing IM status to be away to avoid interruptions. The group level afordance of negativity filtering, however, cannot find its counterpart in the current interaction management research. This represents a potentia extension to the existing literature and will be discussed later.

## 5.1.2. Managing when to interact

When interaction with negativity-expressive partner is unavoidable, individuals can utilize communication media to manage when to interact. Managing when to interact with partners can also be informed by the research related to dissimulating as mentioned above. For example, setting a deceiving “away from the ofice” memo in email to concentrate on work can serve to avoid the interaction altogether or to delay it (Leonardi et al., 2010).

Communication media may help with managing when to interact via a subcategory of negativity barriering that we have labeled delaying the interaction with negativity (Table 5). The afordance of delaying the interaction with negativity provides insight into how technology facilitates the enacting of all examined ERSs except situation selection and modification. as the timing of the interaction does not afect the total amount of negativity exposure which is the focus of situation selection and modification. When the other ERSs are being enacted, asynchronous media such as email allow individuals to (temporarily) turn attention away from the negativity (attention deployment), to engage in activities such as venting and social sharing to change ones' emotional experiences (experience regulation), or to craft ones' emotion displays prior to responding to partners (display regulation). Take display regulation as an example. We found that media facilitate display regulation via providing individuals the time to craft emotion displays. This is consistent with existing research on impression management and selective self-presentation which suggests that individuals prefer the slowed-down pace in CMC over face-to-face communication so that they can carefully craft how to communicate face-threatening messages to communication partners (O'Sullivan, 2000; Sussman & Sproull, 1999).

## 5.1.3. Managing how to interact

Managing how to interact receives probably the most attention in the existing literature. For example, research on media preference suggests that for certain communication exchanges (e.g., delivering bad news or deceptive messages), individuals may purposively choose CMC (over face-to-face interactions) to control how they deliver the message to partners. Also, the connectivity paradox literature found that distributed workers may take advantage of CMC when interacting with partners to regain flexibility and focus, such as replying to emails during phone calls with coworkers.

Media may help with managing how to interact via two subcategories of negativity barriering, reducing the duration and the extent of the interaction with negativity (Table 5). The two subcategories of negativity barriering both find support in the literature. For reducing the duration of the interaction, for example, early research on electronic meeting systems found that the use of CMC may reduce the overall elapsed meeting time (Grohowski, McGof, Vogel, Martz, & Nunamaker, 1990). For reducing the extent of the interaction, research on the cues-filtered-out perspective suggests that certain media such as email may reduce the social cues transmitted, making the interaction less “rich” (Daft & Lengel, 1986;Sproull & Kiesler, 1986; Walther, 1996).

Media's potential to manage how to interact may facilitate the use of all examined ERSs. For situation selection and modification and display regulation—two ERSs related to emotional cues (received from partners or sent to partners)—the potential for managing how to interact helps with controlling the amount and types of negative emotional cues individuals receive from/send to partners. This mechanism could be supported by the extensive research on the cues-filtered-out perspective (Sproull & Kiesler, 1986; Walther, 1996): For situation selection and modification, for example, research on bad news communication suggests that message senders prefer CMC (over face-to-face communication) to avoid seeing receivers' reactions to the bad news, which are negative emotiona cues to senders (Riordan & Kreuz. 2010: Sussman & Sproull. 1999): for display regulation, for example. deception research suggests that CMC may help deceivers by masking their rehearsing, planning and editing of communication from message receivers (Carlson, George, Burgoon, Adkins, & White, 2004).

For attention deployment and experience regulation, media's potential for managing how to interact helps via hiding individuals use of ERSs from partners. A major restriction on the use of attention deployment/experience regulation is partners' potential negative reactions upon finding out individuals' attention deployment/experience regulation behaviors (Côté, 2005; Markus, 1994; Martin, Knopoff, & Beckman, 1998; Sutton, 1991: Tracy, 2000). Bv hiding individuals' uses of ERSs, media remove individuals concerns about potential negative reactions from partners (Leonardi et al., 2010; Markus, 1994). Such role of media can be seen from the existing literature. For example, research on butler lies (i.e., a linguistic strategy for availability management to avoid social interaction or to account for a failure to communicate, Hancock et al., 2009) suggests that media may be leveraged to excuse communication behaviors without disclosing the true reason. for example, using the excuse of being in a meeting (which is not the case) to explain one's delay in responding.

## 5.2. Extending the interaction management literature

In addition to enfolding the existing interaction management literature, our findings suggest two extensions. First, our findings point to the important role of leadership in utilizing communication media to manage interaction (specifically, whether to interact with negativity-expressive partners) for subordinates. Existing research on interaction management focuses on individuals utilizing media to manage unwanted or undesired interactions for themselves. One exception is Hiltz and Turof (1985)'s study which found that, in group conferencing systems, “Members whose contributions are not desired for one reason or another can be restricted to a ‘read only’ status…” (p.686). Implicit in this finding is leadership's role as restricting a group member's status to be “read only” is typically done by leaders. Such proactive action by leadership is similar to the negativity filtering afordance identified in this study. Future research should pay more attention to leadership's strategic use of media at higher levels to manage whether to interact, for example. how leaders of (distributed) teams use media to prevent all team members from being interrupted by others such as coworkers from other teams.

The other extension suggested by our findings is that future interaction management research needs to pay more attention to the potential involvement of outsiders. The potential involvement or presence of outsiders, despite being vividly and convincingly discussed by Goffman (1959. 1981) and related work (Levinson. 1988). has received far less attention in the existing literature. For example, a review of research on impression management found that “relatively little attention has addressed … the efect IM [impression management] has on team members who witness IM attempts but are neither the target nor the actor (i.e., bystanders)” (Bolino, Kacmar, Turnley, & Gilstrap, 2008, p. 1089). There are two related exceptions: one is the research on how outsiders perceive and respond to workplace bullying or mistreatment (Harvey, Treadway, & Heames, 2007; Lutgen-Sandvik & Arsht, 2014; O'reilly & Aquino, 2011: Paull, Omari, & Standen, 2012: Sheppard, Lewicki, & Minton, 1992: Skarlicki & Kulik, 2004), and the other is emotional contagion. Witnessing workplace mistreatment may lead to emotional contagion (Harvey et al., 2007; Paull et al., 2012). Although researchers recognized that emotional contagion may happen to both intended and unintended parties (Hareli & Rafaeli, 2008), the former received most of the research attention. However, the limited attention to outsiders of communication exchanges does not suggest it is insignificance, especially in the context of managing negative emotions at the workplace.

Negativity, apart from being transmitted between intended or ratified (Gofman, 1981) parties of the communication exchange, may also spill over onto those unintended/non-ratified outsiders. What we found in this study is that IT help-desk employees also sought to control the scope of contamination and manage the potential crossover onto outsiders (often coworkers) who should not be involved in the focal negativity-laden communication, negativity containing, Managing crossover on outsiders has received some (though limited) attention in the literature (Gofman, 1959, 1981). Very often, the literature indicates a lack of such managing and its subsequent impact on emotional contagion (De Dreu, West, Fischer, & MacCurtain, 2001; Felps, Mitchell, & Byington, 2006; Hareli & Rafaeli, 2008; Harvey et al., 2007; Paull et al., 2012). For example, a case study about communicating toxic message suggests that failures to managing crossover on outsiders may lead to widespread negative emotions across the organization which can linger for years (Maitlis & Ozcelik, 2004). Future research should pay more attention to the potential involvement of outsiders and the role of communication media in containing the potential crossover onto outsiders.

## 5.3. Contribution to the technology afordance literature

Our findings contribute to the technology afordance literature by suggesting a more comprehensive way of understanding the various conditions given rise to by the tech-organizational context that afect the actualization of technology afordances. Bygstad et al. (2016) explicitly discussed how the tech-organizational context gives rise to stimulating and releasing conditions, which facilitate the actualization of technology afordances. Our findings suggest that the tech-organizational context at EnergyCo may also give rise to inhibiting and suppressing conditions, which hinder the actualization of technology afordances; further, in certain situations, the tech-organizational context may impose contrasting influences. For example, the organizational norm about posting problem announcements may facilitate the actualization of negativity filtering affordance provided by CA Service Desk (stimulating condition) and, at the same time, inhibit the actualization of negativity filtering afordance provided by the phone system (inhibiting condition). Similarly, interviewees' personal opinion on the use of copying/forwarding could remove (releasing condition) or add (suppressing condition) constraints for the actualization of the negativity containing afordance.

The simultaneous existences of stimulating and inhibiting conditions and of releasing and suppressing conditions point to the complexity (and sometimes dilemma) around the use of technology (including media) at the workplace. In the context of our IT help desk, the dilemma lies in the help desk's struggle between protecting help desk employees and providing good service to others. On one hand, help-desk employees need to provide timely and courteous service to those facing technical issues; on the other hand, helpdesk employees need to protect themselves to avoid being bombarded and stressed by the negativity, which may sometimes come at the cost of providing timely and courteous help. As a result, the tech-organizational context gives rise to various conditions that may facilitate (stimulating and releasing conditions) the actualization of negativity decontaminating afordances in certain situations while hinder (inhibiting and suppressing conditions) its actualization in other situations

Take the autonomy to pick interaction (a stimulating condition), the 1-hour time limit norm (an inhibiting condition), and leader intervention (a suppressing condition) as examples. Generally, the service center allows group members to pick tickets/chats to work on between phone calls. This allows the service center to quickly answer phone calls while at the same time take care of some tickets/ chats. This autonomy, however, also allows group members to cherry-pick around certain tickets/chats (from historically negative partners). The 1-hour time limit norm, to some extent, prevents those cherry-picking behaviors from happening all the time and from going to an extreme. And when noticing that the 1-hour time limit norm is seriously violated, leaders may step in and assign unpicked tickets/chats to certain group members. IS researchers have long-recognized that technology may be a double-edged sword. Our findings suggest that the tech-organizational context may also be a double-edged sword, facilitating or hindering the actualization of technology's action potentials (or, afordances). Such complex influences of the tech-organizational context around the actualization of technology afordances deserve more attention in future technology afordance research.

## 5.4. Contribution to the contagion theories literature

This study contributes to the broader contagion theories literature. Contagion theories argue that the attitudes and behaviors of influence targets such as individuals and organizations are afected by others to whom they are linked, or influential others. Examples of contagion theories include social information processing theory (Fulk, Steinfield, Schmitz, & Power, 1987), social influence theory (Schmitz & Fulk, 1991), mimetic process of the institutional theory (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983), and social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986). At the intra-organizational level, contagion theories have been used to explain workplace attitudes such as commitment (Hartman & Johnson, 1989), attitude towards technology (Schmitz & Fulk, 1991), and behaviors such as turnover (Feeley & Barnett, 1997). Contagion theories have also been used to explain phenomena at the inter-organizational level such as innovation adoption (Westphal, Gulati, & Shortell, 1997) and corporate acquisition (Haunschild, 1993). The contagion theories literature suggests that there are two diferent (and in some cases complementary) contagion mechanisms (Monge & Contractor, 2003), contagion via cohesion (the extent of direct interaction with influential others) and contagion via (structural or regular) equivalence (the extent to which influence targets have similar pattern of relationships with influential others).

Despite the prevalence of contagion studies, researchers have also recognized the existence of anti-contagion in which influence targets reject an attitude or behavior adopted by influential others (Bovasso, 1996). However, anti-contagion research received far less attention. Moreover, the current literature focused on contagion on intended influence targets and paid inadequate attention to contagion on outsiders

This study contributes to the contagion theories literature by suggesting ways of strategically utilizing media to counteract contagion via cohesion. Specifically, leaders may utilize media's negativity filtering afordance to prevent subordinates' direction interactions with influential others, hence preventing contagion on subordinates; individuals may also utilize media's negativity isolating and barriering afordances to prevent, weaken, or delay direct interaction with influential others and consequently avoid, reduce or delay the contagion on themselves; the negativity containing afordance could be utilized to prevent direct interaction between influential others and outsiders, hence preventing contagion on outsiders. Future (anti-) contagion research may apply identified media afordances to understand how may leadership and individuals utilize communication media to counteract contagion via cohesion for the many workplace attitudes or behaviors examined in the extant contagion theories literature.

## 5.5. Practical implication

Findings of this paper ofer several practical implications. First, this paper ofers insights regarding how individuals may leverage communication media to help with emotion regulation when interacting with negative partners at work. Complementing (and even replacing) face-to-face communication, CMC is likely to be increasingly dominant for workplace communication. Both employees and managers need to be aware of communication media's potential for facilitating emotion regulation (and other similar purposes, as discussed in the following section on transferability) and utilize the potential to their advantages. Findings of this study ofer insights that may help with technology adoption (or purchase) decisions, technical and ofice space set up, and employee training.

As managers think about technology purchase decisions (especially for workers in emotion-laden roles), communication media that stimulate and/or inhibit diferent ERSs should be carefully considered. For example, advanced announcement about technica problems allowed leaders to pre-empt negative emotions targeting the IT help desk. So, communication technologies that allow problem announcements may be particularly useful. In addition to adopting technologies with problem announcement capabilities, managers should also consider making the problem announcements easily accessible, such as making the problem announcement page the default page of browser and train employees to form the habit of checking for problem announcements before contacting the IT help desk. In the face of negativity targeting the IT help desk, help-desk leaders, with the right communication technologies (providing the negativity filtering potential) and accompanying conditions that facilitate the actualization of such potential, can block most (if not all) of such negativity outside of the IT help desk, eliminating the need for individual help-desk employees to worry about and deal with the negativity and instead allowing them to focus on the other help requests.

Our informants also leveraged asynchronous tools (like email) to delay and minimize exposure to negativity. Further, with synchronous tools (IM and the phone) they used features such as the IM status and the on-hold and on-mute buttons on their phones to manage their exposure to negativity. Such features should be carefully identified, and their use enabled so that employees can regulate their exposure to negativity. Alternatively, features such as revealing the identification of message senders allowed help-desk employees to avoid or delay exposure to negativity in ways that may be harmful to help-desk productivity. An example in our data was the ticketing system's lack of automatic ticket assigning combined with the displaying of help requester's identity; this combination allowed help-desk emplovees to cherry-pick tickets to avoid known sources of negativity. The leaders in our study managed this by setting a limit to how long a ticket could remain unclaimed and, when necessary, by intervening and assigning unclaimed tickets. As such, managers may need to clearly and intentionally balance the available emotion regulation tools with emplovee task performance expectations.

In addition, managers must consider the physical work environment. Managers who are concerned about contagion resulting from overhearing emotional exchanges could invest in noise-cancellation microphones (and it's better if they come with the on-mute/hold button), reconsider the ofice plan (open versus half-open versus private ofice plan), train help-desk employees to be more cognizant about this issue, and have tools that help raise awareness among coworkers when a help-desk employee is on the phone with a customer such as having signal light flashing.

Second, findings of this study suggest that managers need to pay more attention to the various conditions (given rise by the techorganizational context) that may afect the exercising and actualization of media afordances. Careful thoughts need to be given when establishing policies, rules or norms regarding the use of media. Sometimes those policies may have unexpected and counter-productive impacts. A good example is EnergyCo's camera-on policy. As explained by one leader, the rationale behind the initial requirement to have the camera-on (during phone calls with customers) was simply “Since we have this [camera on the phone] feature, let's use it”. However, many members felt that this requirement added an extra and unnecessary burden. Several interviewees mentioned how they worked around this camera-on policy when it was enforced, such as having the camera facing towards the celling or the floor and putting a teddy bear in front of the camera. After several months' enforcement, this camera-on requirement was eventually abandoned due to negative feedback. It is important to clarify that we are not suggesting a universal best practice; the examples discussed in this paper may ofer some insights that help managers better understand the pros and cons of diferent options; managers may need to solicit opinions from their own employees and likely do some trial-error before finding out what works best for their own organizations.

## 5.6. Transferability

A goal of qualitative research is the inductive creation of contextualized and transferable knowledge claims and concepts, an outcome that is “the most lasting contribution qualitative research can make” (Christians & Carey, 1989, p. 373). Transferring research findings to similar contexts may extend theoretical and practical implications. Although the IT help-desk is an ideal context —an emotional labor occupation with heayy reliance on CMC for work interactions occupied by workers with great communication media familiarity—for executing a study to obtain the desired understanding, we believe that our findings may be extended to other CMC contexts where individuals in other occupations seek to manage emotional contagion. Further, although we use “negativity” to describe the negative emotions that our interviewees encountered at work such as frustration and anger, our findings may be applied to general negativity at work that may be contagious such as stress and conflict (Gump & Kulik, 1997; Riley & Eckenrode, 1986).

## 6. Conclusion

Relying on the qualitative research method, we identified communication media afordances supporting the use of emotion regulation strategies. Our findings suggest that communication media have the potential to counteract the contagion of negative emotions at work, in other words they aford negativity decontaminating. The negativity decontaminating potential exists at two levels (group level and individual level) and has several aspects (negativity filtering, negativity isolating, negativity barriering, and negativity containing). When facing potential negativity from communication partners, individuals may leverage these negativity decontaminating afordances to prevent contamination on the entire group, to prevent, weaken, or delay contamination on oneself, or to prevent contamination on outsiders who are not involved in the negativity-laden messaging. Moreover, for each of the identified media afordances, an afordance structure is provided in which the tech-organizational context gives rise to a set of conditions that may afect the initial exercising (enabling condition) and the actualization (stimulating/inhibiting, and releasing/suppressing) of identified media afordances. Findings of this study may be applied to other computer-mediated contexts where individuals seek to resist the contamination of general negativity such as stress and conflict.
