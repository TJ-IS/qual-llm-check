# WHEN CONSTRUCTS BECOME OBSOLETE: A SYSTEMATIC APPROACH TO EVALUATING AND UPDATING CONSTRUCTS FOR INFORMATION SYSTEMS RESEARCH<sup>1</sup>

Deborah Compeau

Carson College of Business, Todd Hall, Room 570B, PO Box 644750, Washington State University, Pullman, WA 99164-2315 U.S.A. {deborah.compeau@wsu.edu}

John Correia School of Business Adminstration, Gonzaga University, 502 East Boon Avenue, Spokane, WA 99258-0102 U.S.A. {correia@gonzaga.edu}

Jason Bennett Thatcher

Fox School of Business and Management, Temple University, Speakman 210, 1810 N. 13<sup>th</sup> Street, Philadelphia, PA 18122 U.S.A. {Jason.thatcher@temple.edu}

In this paper, we confront a paradox in the IS literature that even though our field focuses on the rapid pace of technological change and the dramatic scale of technology-enabled organizational and societal changes, we sometimes find ourselves studying these changes using—largely without question—constructs that were developed in a vastly different IT, user, and organizational environment. We provide guidelines to help assess whether an existing construct warrants updating and to structure the updating task if it is undertaken. Our three-step process provides for a theoretically grounded and comprehensive method that ensures we balance the need for construct updating against the need to sustain our cumulative tradition. We illustrate our guidelines using computer self-efficacy (CSE) as a case study. We document each of the steps involved in analyzing, reconceptualizing, and testing the revised construct information technology self-efficacy (ITSE). Our analyses show that the new construct better explains both traditional and contemporary constructs with a traditional (postal survey) and contemporary (online panel) sample. We discuss the implications of our work both for research on self-efficacy and more broadly for future updating of other important constructs.

Keywords: IT self-efficacy, IT identity, construct measurement, cumulative tradition, construct obsolescence, updating constructs

## Introduction

As IS scholars, we seek “the enhancement and communication of knowledge concerning the development of IT-based services, the management of IT resources, and the use, impact, and economics of IT with managerial, organizational, and societal implications” (MISQ, 2015). Advancing substantive

IS theory requires attention to construct measurement because theory and measurement are inextricably intertwined (Bagozzi, 1984, 2011). In fact, second-generation multivariate techniques like SEM were developed to analyze theory and measurement together. SEM techniques assume that one cannot understand the measures of a construct without putting the construct into its nomological network. For the same reason, we cannot fully understand the meanings of our nomological networks (our theoretical models) without critically reflecting on the definition and measurement of the models’ constructs.

The connection between theory and measurement is of particular interest in our discipline (Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017) because despite the rapid pace of technological change, the dramatic scale of technology-enabled organizational and societal changes, and the broadening set of theories used to explain these changes (Goyal et al., 2018), IS research routinely draws on measures that were developed during the 1980s and 1990s. This paradox is evident, for example, in the case of computer self-efficacy (CSE). The most commonly used measure of CSE was developed for a dissertation that was completed in 1992 (Compeau, 1992).

Other constructs in the IS discipline may be equally subject to the risks of obsolescence. Technostress creators (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008) was developed when IT mobility was new and creating concern for users. IT was, as a result, conceptualized as a threat and trigger for distress. Recent research acknowledges the potential for IT-influenced eustress (Califf et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2019). Yet the measure of technostress creators includes threat-oriented language (e.g., “I am forced”) in each of the items, which makes it difficult for respondents to express an opportunityoriented view. At the organization level, Chen et al. (2010) propose a multidimensional IS strategy construct reflecting innovative, conservative, and undefined strategies for IS. However, digital transformation efforts, which have been accelerated by COVID-19, challenge some of the preexisting notions about IS strategy as distinct from organizational strategy and might benefit from an assessment of these underlying assumptions and their implications on the conceptualization and measurement of IS strategy.

Such constructs remain important to our understanding of IS phenomena, but as theories evolve, as context changes, and as methodologies improve, it would be unreasonable to expect that our constructs would never need to be replaced or updated. Continuing a scholarly tradition without questioning the reuse of existing constructs, particularly in a changing technology context, threatens our ability to understand the present and emerging phenomena. Thus, we need to proactively question whether our seminal constructs should be updated. On the other hand, our seminal constructs, many developed in the early years of our field, have served and continue to serve our field well. We have developed a strong cumulative tradition (Grover et al., 2006) so we must also balance the need for updating with the need to maintain consistency and continuity with the past. Reusing these measures, one could argue, has provided much needed consistency for researchers to build on and extend the understanding of key IS phenomena, such as IT adoption and diffusion. For example, having a common set of constructs and measures has facilitated meta-analyses, which are necessary to summarize our cumulative understanding of concepts and to identify what needs to be investigated. We face, then, a construct obsolescence dilemma, where we suspect certain existing measures are losing their ability to capture the current phenomenon while also recognizing that replacing or updating a seminal construct comes at a potential cost to our field’s valued cumulative tradition.

In this paper, we provide an approach for researchers to address this construct obsolescence dilemma, by critically assessing whether our theories of measurement (i.e., our conceptualization and measurement of constructs) are inconsistent with the contemporary context and, if so, how to go about the task of refining or replacing them. In doing so, we ascribe to the spirit of Straub’s (1989) second, less prominent, recommendation on constructs: that when the research context demands changing a measure, we must be careful to revalidate “instrument content, constructs, and reliability” (p. 161). Our work directs attention to this problem in the context of latent constructs and survey-based measures, since this is the context within which the authors work most frequently. However, we believe our approach is generalizable to other research methods that operationalize abstract constructs in quantitative models but leave the specific application to scholars more focused on those techniques.

We begin by developing a novel set of guidelines and describing the logic behind our recommended approach for updating constructs and their measures. Our robust process for evaluating constructs integrates and builds on guidelines related to construct measurement (e.g., Boudreau et al., 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2011), construct evaluation (e.g., Osigweh, 1989; Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021), and theory contextualization concepts (Hong et al., 2014). We then illustrate our approach using the concept of self-efficacy, one of the most widely used constructs within (Johnson et al., 2017) and outside of IS (Hekman et al., 2009; Lyons & Marler, 2011).

## The Process of Evaluating and Updating Constructs

Since substantive theory rests on measurement theory, we advocate using a systematic process to evaluate whether existing constructs require refining or updating (Figure 1). Previous literature provides an important foundation to understand how construct meaning can lose relevance (Bartunek & Spreitzer, 2006; Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021), leading to the situation we call construct obsolescence, but none considers the ways that changing technologies can influence the phenomenon. Our review of the IS and referent literatures did not identify a framework that provided specific, pragmatic, guidance on how to evaluate when and how to update an existing construct in a dynamic domain of inquiry such as IS. Therefore, our process connects broad approaches used to develop concepts and theory (e.g., Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011; Osigweh, 1989; Sumpter et al., 2021) with specific guidelines for conceptualizing and operationalizing quality constructs (e.g., Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Podsakoff et al., 2016) in order to address the phenomenon of construct obsolescence.

The process begins (Step 1) by evaluating an existing construct, including its conceptualization and its measurement, to determine whether justification for change exists. If theoretical or practical justifications exist, the second step directs attention to reconceptualization of the construct in the contemporary context for inquiry. This step is followed by a systematic conceptual and empirical comparison between the proposed new construct and the existing construct (Step 3). The construct evaluation process concludes with four possible outcomes.

While the process in Figure 1 is straightforward, construct evaluation demands nuance and is as much art as science. Our process is designed to be used in conjunction with relevant guidance from the measurement literature to improve the quality of theoretical constructs. For example, MacKenzie et al. (2011) and, to a greater extent, Podsakoff et al. (2016) provide a framework (property, entity, theme, and choice of wording) that can be used to evaluate the conceptualization components within our process (steps 1 and 2). Furthermore, MacKenzie et al., and Burton-Jones & Lee (2017) provide guidance on evaluating and developing the connection between theories and their measurement, which can be used to evaluate the measurement components of constructs in our process (step 3). Finally, existing empirical guidelines can be referenced to evaluate the empirical analysis (step 3) within our process (e.g., Hair et al., 2016; Kline, 2016).

Because guidance relevant to our steps may vary depending on the phenomenon and may evolve with the relevant referent literature, we do not provide an exhaustive set of approaches for each step in our process. Rather we provide exemplars that demonstrate how existing methods and theories can be used in conjunction with the proposed steps. Our intention is to provide a structure for future work that evaluates constructs, while affording opportunities for necessary permutations that account for the natural evolution of theories, constructs, and their measurement. In the following sections, we provide guidance and illustrations that clarify how to apply our process to resolving the construct obsolescence dilemma.

## Step 1: Evaluate Existing Construct(s)

How do we know if a construct requires updating? We focus on this question in our first step, providing guidance on how to evaluate existing constructs to determine whether problems exist that warrant further assessment. In some cases, minor modifications (e.g., replacement of dated or context-specific terminology in a definition and/or measure) are sufficient to continue using an existing construct. In other cases, minor modifications will not properly address these limitations; when these scenarios occur, our process suggests a thorough evaluation of whether to replace or update the existing construct. In this section, we provide a broad and diverse set of concepts that can be used to trigger the evaluation and/or further support the need to thoroughly evaluate an existing construct. To determine whether minor editing would suffice or reconceptualization is necessary, we consider both conceptual and empirical evidence.

## Conceptual Limitations

Research constructs are used as a theoretical lens to describe and capture a concept in a phenomenon. As such, they are “meaning-laden classifications” (Osigweh, 1989, p. 581) that cannot be entirely decoupled from the phenomena to which they refer. After a construct has been developed, both the lens and context can be sources of limitations in the capturing of the concept. These limitations occur because of the dynamic nature of the IS research context and can hinder researchers from accurately studying the existing and future phenomenon.

Organizational constructs such as those used in information systems research “tend to be highly sensitive to and contingent on contextual conditions; because most organizational phenomena tend to be temporal … changes in time may affect the expression of any construct” (Suddaby, 2010, p. 349). Because of the pace of change in IT, the IS literature is perhaps at even greater risk of mismatches between the context in the contemporary environment and a long-held conceptualization of a construct. Consider the construct user satisfaction with the information services function, or USISF (Ives et al., 1983). Kettinger and Lee (1994) assessed the USISF construct and showed that its measure was disconnected from the contemporary IS services environment (e.g., focused on centralized transaction processing systems rather than personal and networked computing). They challenged the conceptualization of USISF because it did not capture satisfaction with the entire range of services provided by the IS function. The gap between the conceptualization of USISF and the actual services provided by the IS function in organizations influenced the construct’s reliability and validity. Therefore, respondents could not map answers to questions that did not reflect their experience, which may have resulted in increased error variance in the items. By updating the construct’s conceptualization, conclusions drawn about the IS function would have greater explanatory value since they would capture critical dimensions of performance in the contemporary environment.

To understand whether a mismatch exists, our first step examines the tacit assumptions (Gray & Cooper, 2010) about the phenomenon under study and those inherent in the construct. A variety of models could support such analyses. Benbasat and Zmud (2003), for example, articulate properties of the IT artifact and the context of its use (the tasks for which it is used and the structures in which it is embedded) as central characteristics in the IS discipline. Assumptions about the nature of IT include the devices, applications or services to which it refers and the features it has. Assumptions about the use environment include whether the purpose is work-related or personal, where it is undertaken (place-bound or mobile) and whether it is an individual or collective activity. The concept of affordances (Karahanna et al., 2018; Strong et al., 2014) extends this view, since affordances are relational properties that describe the IT artifact and its use context. Matook and Brown (2017) use systems-theory concepts to identify seven characteristics of IT artifacts (integration, connectivity, complexity, state, adaptation, self-adaptation and synchronicity) that could also support such analysis.

New meanings for concepts can also arise over time, based on different perspectives on a phenomenon. Bartunek and Spreitzer (2006), for example, identify three distinct meanings for the construct of empowerment over its history. Each meaning reflects different tacit assumptions about the necessity of empowerment, the context in which it took place and the intended outcomes. Other types of tacit assumptions may be related to issues of measurement such as the debate over the use of formative versus reflective measurement (Edwards, 2011; Petter et al., 2007).

Sumpter et al. (2021) suggest that premature convergence on a single set of assumptions about the meaning of a construct leads to stagnation in the discipline as promising avenues of inquiry may be cut off because they don’t conform to the dominant meaning. The IS field may be at greater risk of such problematic convergence because of our emphasis on encouraging construct reuse to promote a cumulative tradition. Thus, while the age of a construct is not in itself a limitation, it should cause a pause to wonder if its reuse is due to its continuing relevance, or to problematic convergence. Further, these implicit drivers to reuse a construct combined with the efficiency of reusing existing instruments may result in the application of existing constructs beyond their relative boundaries (Osigweh, 1989).

Regardless of the chosen approach(es) taken to evaluate the relevance of a construct, the analysis at this step seeks to answer the question: Are the assumptions made in the development of the construct still valid in the context to which they are being applied? Surfacing these tacit assumptions and interrogating the extent of their influence on the meaning of the construct and its applicability to understanding the phenomenon under study thus demonstrates whether the construct has stagnated and should be considered “obsolete.”

## Empirical Limitations

The empirical assessment evaluates the extent to which the limitations of a construct are visible in studies of the phenomenon, and whether it is time to update a construct or its measurement. In most cases, empirical limitations are easiest to detect and communicate due to their objective nature. Although advancements in methods may surface limitations in existing constructs (Gefen et al., 2011; Larsen & Bong, 2016), more subtle evidence of a construct’s limitations may arise over time. For example, evidence from the literature may show mixed results, meta-analyses may show temporal changes in effect sizes, mean values of constructs may steadily change, or replication studies may suggest limits on the reproducibility of results.

Such mixed results or changing means do not necessarily indicate construct obsolescence; however, one potential explanation for the mixed empirical performance is the declining relevance of the construct in a contemporary context. For example, if a construct that has shown high predictive power experiences a decline in that power over a series of studies, or if a replication fails after a radical change in context (either the IT context, the user context or both), it is possible that the way the construct is conceptualized and operationalized no longer fits (Marakas et al., 2007). Other empirical evidence to support further evaluation of a construct includes changes to the measurement properties of the construct (e.g., changes in the way items load on the construct or changes in convergent or discriminant validity). If item loadings change sufficiently, then the empirical meaning (Bagozzi, 2011) of the construct is altered and that should trigger consideration of the reasons behind the change in loadings and the implications of the changes for the construct’s theoretical role.

## Summary

At the conclusion of this step, there should be a clearer understanding of how temporal changes in theory, practice, and/or broader research methods limit an existing IS construct’s ability to capture phenomena in a manner consistent with current IT or best practices in research. Analysis of the nature of the construct—its construct specificity and its stability— and assessment of the empirical results of studies using the existing construct, determine the importance and urgency of updating the construct. Two outcomes from this step are possible: If efforts during this step do not reveal justification for further evaluation, then research using the existing construct should continue. Replacing an existing construct with a defined cumulative tradition for the sake of small differences in phrasing may represent an unnecessary cost to the field (Keen, 1980; Straub, 1989). Thus, if only minor differences exist, then continued use of the existing construct is likely desirable, with perhaps minor updating of items. This update would be noted in the relevant paper to signal the nature of and the reasons for updating. If on the other hand, the evaluation reveals substantial limitations that suggest that the construct is hindering the field’s ability to understand contemporary phenomena, the next step is the conceptualization of a new proposed construct, informed by the analysis in Step 1.

## Step 2: Conceptualize a New Construct

Conceptualization of the proposed new construct, in our process, follows the well-defined guidelines found in the literature (e.g., Boudreau et al., 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Podsakoff et al., 2016; Straub, 1989; Suddaby, 2010). In particular, this step in our process equates to the first three steps—conceptualization, item generation, and content validity assessment—of the guidelines provided by MacKenzie et al. Through these activities, we develop the conceptualization of the construct and the corresponding operationalization (item generation) and then assess their match (content validity).We do not repeat this guidance here. Instead, we focus on three unique considerations relevant to conceptualizing a new construct as a potential replacement for one that already exists.

First, it is important to set aside preconceptions of how the proposed construct might look based on existing operationalization. This does not mean ignoring the lessons learned from prior research but does require the ability to consider distinctly different ways of conceptualizing and measuring the construct (Sumpter et al., 2021). For example, a construct that has been previously characterized as multidimensional might be newly viewed as unidimensional or vice versa; a construct that was measured reflectively might be updated with one measured formatively or vice versa, and a construct that had previously been measured using a survey instrument might be updated to draw from unobtrusive measures (Hibbeln et al., 2017).

The evolution of the technostress construct further illustrates the different ways a construct can be conceptualized. In their seminal study, Tarafdar et al. (2007) focused their conceptualization of technostress on the environmental creators of technostress, or technostressors. In extending that work, Ayyagari et al. (2011) emphasized the importance of the strains felt by the individual as a result of the technostress creators. More recently, studies (Califf et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2019) demonstrate that stress can be both positive (eustress) and negative (distress). Each of these ways of conceptualizing stress associated with technology is consistent with theories of stress and coping, yet they result in different ways of thinking about the focal concept that, in turn, influences our theorizing about the phenomena. The technostressors view treats IT as a source of threat, while the distress/eustress view recognizes IT as a source of either threat or opportunity. Thus, setting aside preconceptions based on existing constructs helps overcome the problematic aspects of construct convergence (Sumpter et al., 2021) and allows a novel perspective to emerge that might better fit the contemporary context.

A related consideration in developing a new conceptualization for an existing construct is whether it should be done using the original root theory or using a different theoretical foundation.<sup>2</sup> The first approach is likely straightforward, probably more common, and requires aligning the new conceptualization with updates in theory. Nonetheless, it is possible that the original root theory is no longer applicable and a new theoretical foundation is required. This second path is likely more challenging because it requires explaining the change in theoretical foundation as well as contextualizing a new theory to the domain of information systems.

The final unique consideration in our process is the risk of recurring construct obsolescence. To be sure, not all constructs need to last over long periods of time. As phenomena change and theories develop, some constructs may naturally become less important. Other constructs (e.g., the McLaren et al. (2011) multilevel strategic fit for supply chain management systems) may be explicitly designed with a high level of granularity to facilitate practical application, even though that granularity results in a more limited shelf-life. Nonetheless, for construct measures to have longevity, it is important to build them in a way that will allow them to “travel” (Osigweh, 1989), not just across contexts but also over time. Traveling constructs balance breadth/generality (what Osigweh refers to as extension) and depth (Osigweh’s connotation) to ensure that they can be used in multiple contexts without becoming so broad as to be mere generalities. For example, the construct of workplace commitment was reconceptualized to apply to the full array of workplace targets while also providing a more detailed definition that distinguishes workplace commitment from other constructs (Klein et al., 2012). Thus, “the challenge is to create constructs that are sufficiently narrow … to strip away unintended connotations and surplus meaning but are conceptually broad enough to capture the underlying essence of the phenomenon” (Suddaby, 2010, p. 348).

At the end of this step, there should be an updated definition of the proposed new construct along with an instrument, ready for comparison to the existing construct.

## Step 3: Conceptually and Empirically Compare Constructs

The conceptual and empirical evaluation of the existing and proposed constructs is necessary to determine whether the proposed new construct represents a meaningful change. Beginning with the conceptual comparison, it is important to assess their definitions, their attributes or characteristics, their dimensionality, and their items—all of the elements that go into conceptualizing the construct (MacKenzie et al., 2011; Podsakoff et al., 2016). Are the existing and new constructs defined similarly, or are there substantive differences between them that suggest the existing construct may be limited in one or more ways?

The IT context and the behavioral context in which the existing and proposed new constructs are expected to apply must also be clearly identified and formally compared. We have witnessed at least four eras in the history of IS research (Hirschheim & Klein, 2012), each with distinct characteristics of the IT artifact that could shape the conceptualization and measurement of constructs. We have also witnessed significant changes in the nature of organizations and work over this time and in the breadth of tasks for which IT is used (Burke & Ng, 2006). Since the results of using an IT vary based on the characteristics of the context (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995), identifying the underlying assumptions about the role of IT when a construct was developed versus the contemporary context helps to clarify the potential for differences between the existing and proposed constructs. Although understanding the historical context is challenging, the dialectical method of problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) provides a basis to review prior literature by requiring researchers to identify, question, and resolve discrepancies in assumptions about the IT and use environments. By doing so in a structured, dialectical way, researchers will be better able to highlight and understand the possible alternative assumptions and their implications for the construct.

Dimensionality is also a major component of a construct that should be evaluated across the existing and proposed new constructs. Like the definition and description of a construct, differences in dimensionality may exist due to differences in the technological context and/or behavioral context. Hong and Thong (2013) provide an approach to evaluate differences in dimensions between definitions (e.g., information privacy concerns and internet privacy concerns), technologies (information privacy versus internet privacy), and cognitive aspects (e.g., expectations versus perceptions). In their study, various versions of internet privacy concerns were compared to a more updated conception of internet privacy concerns based on a more contemporary technological context. Through multiple studies, their results supported the updated conceptualization of internet privacy concerns as a refined, contextualized, and modernized multidimensional construct.

Examining differences in measurement involves reviewing the specific items and their relationship with the overarching construct, that is the way in which the items have been developed to reflect or form the constructs. For example, in IS-SERVQUAL the items represent judgments about the overall adequacy of support across various dimensions, reflected in the gap between expected and perceived service. A different view of IS-SERVQUAL might focus on the assessment of the most recent interaction and use a relatively small number of items to assess perceived quality holistically. In both cases, the IS function is viewed as the equivalent of a business with which the individual transacts, and individuals are viewed as able to effectively report on their experiences. Alternative framings of the IS function, such as strategic partner, are also possible and might result in the creation of different types of items.

Once the conceptual comparison is complete, an empirical comparison of the existing and proposed new constructs is needed. Empirically testing the measurement properties (i.e., reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity) and theoretical contribution (i.e., predictive or nomological validity) of the proposed construct compared to the existing construct is important for two reasons. First, it ensures that the updated construct does, indeed, provide improvement over the existing construct. Second, it provides a baseline comparison to help link research findings based on the existing construct with those based on the new construct, thus ensuring continuation of the cumulative tradition.

An ideal comparison will include multiple models to compare the existing and proposed constructs. A baseline model that is as consistent as possible with the original nomological network of the construct should be assessed to determine whether the existing construct continues to perform well in the original model where it was developed. This is, in essence, a replication (Dennis & Valacich, 2015). It is also important to test the constructs in a model using a nomological network that reflects the most current theorizing to assess how well they explain contemporary phenomena. For example, in the case of SERVQUAL, it would be important to test the utility of the construct in both a traditional context (e.g., in-house IT using legacy technologies) and a contemporary context (e.g., cloudbased systems with an external provider), since the notion of IT service quality in the contemporary environment must address all the types of services that exist. Using multiple samples strengthens the comparisons. Using a first sample that closely parallels the sampling frame from the tests of the original measure permits replication of the original results. Adding a second sample permits triangulation with a separate sample that differs on relevant characteristics. We acknowledge, however, that the ideal comparison may not always be practical, or necessary. A single model may be sufficient if the nomological network in which the construct operates has not changed from the development of the initial construct, and a single sample may provide sufficient evidence of change. Given the cost and difficulty of collecting high quality data, the empirical strategy should reflect a sound trade-off between the validity aims of the ideal strategy and the costs and likely benefits of applying that strategy in the particular context of the research.

Assessing the existing and proposed new constructs in a nomological network requires an assessment of the predictive validity of each construct and a comparison of their predictive power in a single model. Does each construct behave as expected in its nomological network? Do the constructs have different predictive power? How would the conclusions drawn from the research differ if we used the proposed versus the existing construct? The analysis thus requires a full evaluation of each model (including measurement and structural model results), a comparison of the measurement characteristics of the two constructs, and a comparison of the theoretical models. For the latter, post hoc tests of the differences in the measured parameters (e.g., t-tests of differences in path coefficients or F-tests of explained variance) help identify whether the observed differences are conceptually meaningful and empirically significant.

## Outcomes of the Process

The insights drawn across the conceptual and empirical assessments determine which of the four outcomes is pursued. If both assessments reveal meaningful differences, the new construct should be used in future research (Outcome 1). If important conceptual differences are found but the new measure does not provide empirical improvement, we recommend continuing to use the existing measure to preserve comparability with previous studies, but advocate the presentation of the updated conceptualization, perhaps with a new name, (Outcome 2) to enhance construct clarity (Osigweh, 1989; Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021). If conceptual differences are small or do not exist, but the proposed measure performs better empirically, we recommend updating the measure while preserving the original conceptualization (Outcome 3). Finally, if neither empirical nor conceptual differences are found, we recommend continuing to use the existing construct with minor changes to item wording if needed (Outcome 4).

We do not expect all of the outcomes to be equally likely. Outcomes 1 and 4 are the most likely since we expect conceptual and empirical differences to go together in most cases. Nonetheless, we have identified situations in the literature where a conceptualization is updated with no or limited changes in the measurement (e.g., performance expectancy as an updated conceptualization of perceived usefulness) and where alternative measures for a construct produce different empirical results (e.g., individual privacy concerns) even though the underlying conceptualizations are similar. Thus, we represent all four outcomes.

In each of Outcomes 1 through 3, the process concludes with the existence of both the original and proposed new constructs. This creates a question of how the two should coexist and requires the development of new boundary conditions (Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021). In some cases, the new or updated construct will completely replace the existing construct, and all future research (excluding replication and meta-analysis) will use the new or updated construct. Recommending the replacement of an existing construct does not invalidate previous results but rather suggests that further extension of knowledge regarding the construct is better served by utilizing a new measure or an update to an existing measure. In this scenario, the boundary conditions of the new or updated construct must also be assessed to determine if the updates create new use scenarios outside the boundaries of the existing construct.

In other cases, the analysis may reveal the need for a new construct (Outcome 1) but may also suggest a continuing role for the existing construct. For example, if the existing construct remains able to adequately predict constructs in a particular nomological network in some contexts, then using it within that context is appropriate. In essence, this creates a new boundary condition for the existing construct. For example, the establishment of the internet self-efficacy construct (Torkzadeh & Van Dyke, 2001) extended previous work on CSE and provided a more focused construct that fits the context of internet use. Its creation did not replace CSE but rather suggested scoping conditions—that is, heuristics dictating when each construct would be most useful (Suddaby, 2010). Articulation of these scoping conditions will not only help future researchers understand the limits of the proposed changes, but also help reviewers understand the opportunity of the update to contribute beyond the current context of the study.

## Summary

The recommendation to update an existing construct, especially one that is well established and broadly applicable, should rest on theoretical and empirical evidence. Our approach to assembling such evidence includes a review of the limitations of the existing construct (Step 1), development of the new construct (Step 2), examination of differences between conceptualization and measurement dimensions of constructs, and empirical results that demonstrate the new construct’s ability to extend knowledge (Step 3). These results inform the understanding of how existing and proposed constructs: capture an underlying concept, relate to other theories/concepts, inform our ability to explain established phenomena, and inform our ability to understand emergent and possible future phenomena. For each step, we highlight the driving questions that guide the activity, the typical approaches and types of evidence that would help answer the question, and the result for the step based on the answers to the driving questions.

## Illustrating the Guidelines: A Case Study of Computer Self-Efficacy

To illustrate our guidelines, we critically examine computer self-efficacy (CSE) vis-à-vis a proposed new construct, information technology self-efficacy (ITSE). CSE has a strong theoretical foundation and has been shown to be important for a variety of use-related cognitions, emotions, and behaviors. We focus on the Compeau and Higgins (1995b) CSE construct developed in the late 1980s (Compeau, 1992) because it is predominant in the literature (Johnson et al., 2017; Marakas et al., 2007) and because we believe it most closely captures the generative capability described by Bandura (1997). As such, it also represents a typical construct obsolescence dilemma in our field.

The CSE construct is rooted in the general psychological theory of Albert Bandura. Bandura (1997) defined selfefficacy as “beliefs in one’s capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments” (p. 3). Self-efficacy is a domain-specific judgment; dependent on individuals’ self-reflective thoughts on their prior experiences, their observations of the world around them, and their internal physiological states.

Compeau and Higgins (1995b) developed the CSE construct to understand how self-efficacy influenced the domain of computing behavior. CSE was intended to capture an indi vidual’s perceptions about the broad domain of computer use, with more specific versions of the measures for word pro cessing and spreadsheets developed in other work (Compeau & Higgins, 1995a). Marakas et al. (1998) further explain the domain of CSE in their multilevel model. They also argue that the Compeau and Higgins measure reflects general selfefficacy within the domain, which is formed by an individual as a consequence of their specific self-efficacy judgments concerning various application domains. We present the analysis of this construct using our guidelines in the sections that follow.

## Step 1. Evaluate Existing Construct

Using the approach presented in Step 1, we evaluate the alignment of CSE’s conceptualization and measurement to the contemporary research and IT use context. Self-efficacy theory applies to various domains—and levels within those domains—of human functioning (Bandura, 2006). In the IT domain, two major forms of self-efficacy have been established, general and specific (Marakas et al., 1998). General self-efficacy constructs capture ones’ self-efficacy related to more broadly applicable attainments while specific selfefficacy constructs are intended for more specific attainments (most commonly associated with specific applications (e.g., spreadsheet) or specific platforms in the field of IT (e.g., internet).

Figure 2 shows how the CSE construct has drifted (Suddaby, 2010) from its initial intent to serve as a general self-efficacy for the field of IT to a more specific view focused on the use of a particular device (the computer) in a specific context (at work).

In the early 1990s, a computer was the primary, if not the only, device through which one undertook “computing behaviors.” Today, however, a computer is just one of many devices with which users interact with and the notion of a “computer” has come to reflect a more restrictive category of information technology. In today’s environment, the idea of self-efficacy with regards to using “a computer” might be considered at the same level of generality as internet selfefficacy (Torkzadeh & Van Dyke, 2001) or perhaps even spreadsheet self-efficacy. But the original intent of the construct was to reflect the broad general domain of computing. The use context for IT is also different today. The items in CSE focus on whether an individual can use a hypothetical new piece of software to complete a job task. This may be appropriate for some contemporary technologies, but not all of the IT studied in our discipline is intended for use at work. For example, how does CSE relate to hedonic technologies or mobile commerce applications? Developing an IT selfefficacy construct to capture the breadth of what, and where, we use contemporary IT may advance IS research, whereas a construct that focuses on what one can do with computers at work may not be as meaningful.

In addition to the drift from a more general to a more specific meaning associated with CSE, our review of the measure identified two additional areas of concern. First, the fundamental premise of the CSE measure is that what truly differentiates more and less confident users (i.e., the psychological confidence that is the essence of self-efficacy) is the ability to cope with the unfamiliar (Compeau, 1992). While many users may be able to complete routine tasks using familiar technologies, Compeau argued that it was the novelty of the hypothetical software package that allowed the instrument to capture the generative capability at the heart of self-efficacy. Changing technology is certainly as much a feature of IT use today as it was in the 1980s, but the idea of dealing with never-before-seen applications, which was central to the experience of the digital immigrant (Prensky, 2001) users of that time, does not have the same resonance today for those who have been using IT in various forms throughout their lives. Thus, framing the measurement of CSE around a hypothetical novel application may not adequately reflect the content domain of self-efficacy as well as it once did.

Second, a review of the items shows that at least some represent conditions that are unlikely to be relevant to most usage contexts today. For example, one asks the user if they could complete the hypothetical task “if [they] had only the software manuals for reference.” Software manuals have not been a feature of most IT distributions for years, at least not as they were created and used in the late 1980s. Another question asks about completing the task using only the built-in help facility. Help facilities are still a major component of computer software but may be less relevant to the world of mobile devices, apps, forums, and wikis. Moreover, a major source of help for today’s user (YouTube videos) is not considered in any item.

In summary, our assessment of CSE against the backdrop of the contemporary computing context suggests a strong conceptual case for updating. We identified several areas of misfit, representing taken-for-granted assumptions (Gray & Cooper, 2010) about the nature of the construct: focus on a narrowly defined IT (computer software packages) and use context (work), fundamental premise of what confidence means based on outdated assumptions, and items that reflect outdated conditions of use (e.g., reliance on software manuals). Continuing to use the CSE construct without evaluating these issues may create problematic convergence (Sumpter et al., 2021), since these misfits may influence our understanding of the role of self-efficacy in contemporary user behavior.

The empirical evidence supporting updating was mixed. On the one hand, a replication study (under review) using student subjects in the context of school-related use of IT was not able to replicate the original Compeau and Higgins (1995b) findings. In addition, a comparison of two scales for spreadsheet self-efficacy that differed primarily in terms of the time of their development (Marakas et al., 2007) showed that the newer measure performed better. On the other hand, a metaanalysis (Johnson et al., 2017) did not find differences in the influence of self-efficacy over time. The mixed empirical evidence suggests that continuing to use CSE might be feasible, but it does not address concerns about the instrument’s content validity nor its likely empirical performance in the future as the technology environment becomes increasingly different. Thus, we found that sufficient justification for updating exists in the conceptual analysis, but the lack of strong empirical distress signals suggests a need for caution.

## Step 2. Conceptualization of IT Self-Efficacy (ITSE)

To develop a new conceptualization of ITSE to contrast with CSE, we followed the process recommended by MacKenzie et al. (2011). Our illustration follows the original root theory of the construct, and thus illustrates the first path we discussed in step 2.

## Defining ITSE

We developed our definition of ITSE by critically reviewing existing definitions (Table 2 shows a representative sample) of self-efficacy both generally and specific to the IT domain. The literature agrees on the essential elements of self-efficacy. First, self-efficacy applies to the entity of a person. While some research has looked at collective efficacy (Bandura, 2000), most IS literature has focused on self-efficacy as the property of an individual. Second, self-efficacy is widely understood as an individual cognition. Most definitions mention terms such as belief or perception. Moreover, the definitions reflect that self-efficacy is a belief in one’s ability or capability. It is a state rather than a trait because it is expected to change based on experience (enactive mastery, vicarious experience, social persuasion, and physiological states), though it is not as dynamic as states such as mood (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002).

Specification of the domain represents perhaps the most complex aspect of defining ITSE. Prior research has debated the relative utility of a general “computer” self-efficacy versus a more focused (e.g., application-specific) self-efficacy (Marakas et al., 2007). The IS literature seems to find value in both, though application-specific self-efficacy measures more strongly predict individual performance outcomes. We argue that general IT use represents a reasonably tractable domain of functioning. Bandura (2006) reports on domains of functioning, such as management and self-regulation, that are equally broad. We accept that more specifically defined selfefficacy (e.g., the ability to use a particular IT) will be more strongly related to some of these behaviors, but we argue, as have others (Johnson et al., 2008), that there is value in being able to assess individuals’ confidence in their ability to use information technologies as a whole.

While the IS literature has often focused on defining the domain in terms of technologies (computers in general versus a specific application), an equally important consideration is the relative emphasis on IT use versus task performance. For example, Compeau and Higgins (1995b) define CSE as a judgment of one’s capability to use a computer (p. 200) and Marakas et al. (2007) define general CSE (GCSE) as “an individual’s judgment of his or her ability to perform across multiple computer application domains” (p. 17). Other definitions, however, emphasize computer use as a course of action (i.e., a means to an end) necessary to accomplish a task. For example, Brown et al. (2010, p. 22) define CSE as an individual’s “belief in his/her ability to use a technology to accomplish a task.” Other definitions (Munro et al., 1997; Venkatesh et al., 2003) also subordinate the use of a computer to the performance of a task for which it is being used.

This distinction is important. If the predominant focus of the measure is on completion of a specific task, then self-efficacy judgments must reflect not only one’s confidence in using IT but also one’s competence in the task domain (Looney et al., 2006). For example, if the task is using a spreadsheet to complete an organizational budgeting task, then the relevant selfefficacy judgment includes not only spreadsheet capability but also budgeting capability (e.g., estimating costs, predicting rates of inflation). While there may be circumstances where this is necessary, to understand how individuals adopt and use technologies in their daily lives, an encapsulated conceptualization (Looney et al., 2006) is more appropriate. Thus, we focus our definition of IT self-efficacy on the behavior of using IT rather than on using IT to accomplish a particular task.

In conceptualizing ITSE, we also reflected on what it means to use IT in today’s environment. When Compeau and Higgins (1995b) developed the CSE measure, they focused on IT use within the work environment of the 1980s, when computers and software were purchased and installed for users at work. Today, using IT requires more than just the interaction with a tool at work. Consider the use of a smartphone app. Use may require individuals to independently perform several necessary activities such as finding the app, installing it, completing the initial setup, troubleshooting problems, and keeping it updated. Activities required to use the app may go beyond the app itself and include tasks such as linking it to other apps to share data or communicate with others regarding its purpose or function. In the case of a device like a fitness tracker or a smart thermostat, similar selection, installation, operation, and maintenance activities are necessary. Thus, when we refer to “using IT,” we mean it in this broad sense, encompassing the various activities (courses of action) involved in using IT across its life cycle.

## Instrument Development

We followed the process outlined in Bandura’s (2006) guide to constructing self-efficacy scales. To ensure content validity, we focused on individuals’ judgments of what they can do (self-efficacy) as opposed to what they will do (intention) and we differentiated the construct from related constructs such as IT outcome expectations, emotion, and self-esteem. We developed items with different gradations of challenge, as judgments about both simple and complex tasks are important for reflecting individuals’ confidence in their overall ability.

Since contemporary IT use requires marshaling resources throughout the entire period of using the IT—from initial acquisition, to learning and using, to sharing and extending, to dealing with problems—we generated items reflecting these different aspects of use. In this sense, our measure of ITSE is consistent with self-efficacy measures focused on engaging in broad repertoires of specific actions such as parenting or teaching (Bandura, 2006).

Building in part on the work of Howard (2014), we generated an initial pool of 40 items related to the different aspects of IT use (acquire, install, etc.). We then worked iteratively to refine the item pool, ensuring adequate reflection of the domain and eliminating duplication of items. We use Bandura’s recommended response scale, ranging from 0–100% confidence. This response scale includes both magnitude (cannot do at all versus can do with some degree of confidence) and strength (variable levels of confidence), two key dimensions of self-efficacy. We focused on items that could apply to the widest range of use cases (e.g., using devices, software applications, etc.) to increase the relevance of the scale across a wide range of situations. While not every item is relevant to every situation (e.g., a clerical staff member would likely not worry about their ability to purchase a new enterprise-wide information system) most have at least some applicability. Appendix A shows the 15 items we developed to measure ITSE.

We argue that ITSE is best modeled as reflective, with ITSE viewed as a “real [entity] that influence[s] scores on the associated measures” (Edwards, 2011, p. 371) rather than a simple compilation of the items as implied by formative measurement. Bandura (1997) notes that “perceived self-efficacy is not a measure of the skills one has but a belief about what one can do under different sets of conditions with whatever skills one possesses” (p. 37). Thus, skills and self-efficacy are differentiated. Modeling the construct as formative, as has been suggested by Marakas et al. (2007), treats the items as the causes of the construct and as measured without error. This emphasizes the differences (the non-overlapping variance) in the items and as such, it more closely represents the individual’s skills. Modeling the construct as reflective, by contrast, emphasizes the shared variance between the items— in essence, treating any differences in skills as part of the error term for each of the items (Edwards, 2011). We believe this is precisely what Bandura meant by the statement that selfefficacy is what one can do regardless of the skills one possesses, and we agree with Hardin et al. (2008) that modeling ITSE as reflective best reflects the underlying psychological construct of self-efficacy.

Having conceptualized the new ITSE construct and developed and assessed the content validity of the instrument, we now turn to the assessment of conceptual differences between ITSE and CSE.

## Step 3b. Empirically Compare CSE and ITSE

## Theoretical Approach to Empirically Evaluating Differences between CSE and ITSE

To empirically assess the differences between CSE and ITSE, we test two models using two different samples (see Table 4). This helped us draw conclusions about traditional and contemporary theories of IT use for different types of IT users. The first model, which reflects traditional IS research in selfefficacy, is rooted in social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997) and is a variation on the original model tested by Compeau and Higgins (1995b). The second model, reflecting contemporary theorizing, is based on identity theory (Burke & Stets, 2009).

We assessed each of the two models with two samples: one from a traditional postal survey and the other from a contemporary online survey. Testing our models with two different samples strengthens the empirical comparison by ruling out sampling artifacts as a basis for the observed results. The online panel is of particular interest because the respondents are individuals who choose to earn money or rewards by participating in IT-mediated research projects. As a result, they are likely to be highly engaged in the use of IT and afford a glimpse into possible differences between how CSE and ITSE will influence studies in the future. As IT becomes more infused into society, and as more IT-centric citizens replace less IT-centric citizens, developing an approach that focuses on these highly engaged users should become more important for IS research (Morris & Venkatesh, 2000). At the same time, there are still many in the US adult population who were not exposed to IT early in their lives (Tams et al., 2014). This subset of the population may be missing in empirical studies that rely on online panels. To reach this type of respondent, we repeated our study using a postal survey. An addressbased sampling method was used to reach a random sample of U.S. households (details provided below). By including this traditional approach, we replicate the mailed survey method used by Compeau and Higgins (1995b) and we reduce the risk of selection bias in the online panel where people low in selfefficacy may be less represented. Thus, including the second sample gives us more confidence in our conclusions about the implications of CSE and ITSE.

Our combination of models and samples enabled us to examine how the different conceptualizations of IT-related self-efficacy relate to what we know (traditional model) and what we are learning (contemporary model) as well as explore differences between CSE and ITSE among samples from two data collection methods (postal and online survey).

We first present the traditional and contemporary models along with their associated hypotheses that describe the impact of IT-related self-efficacy on IT-related concepts. In each model, we focus on the influence of self-efficacy on other cognitions, physiological states, and behavior choice, which are key outcomes of self-efficacy judgments (Bandura, 1997). We then present the two data collection methods that create an opportunity to evaluate the differences between CSE and ITSE in traditional and contemporary models with two different samples.

## Summary

Testing these two models across the two samples provides an opportunity to understand how the different forms of ITrelated self-efficacy inform our understanding across traditional and contemporary contexts with distinct samples of the general U.S. population. In doing so, we can assess how using older and newer versions of a construct influence what we know about the influence of the construct, other constructs in areas of longstanding inquiry and newly opened areas of inquiry using traditional and contemporary data collection methods.

## Data Collection

The two studies included the same constructs, but differed in who they sampled and how data were collected. For the contemporary sample (Study 1), we solicited respondents from a national survey panel using Qualtrics™, an aggregator of market research panels. The 277 respondents were an average age of 47.5 and were equally split between men and women. The online survey included our new ITSE measure, Compeau and Higgins’ (1995b) CSE measure, and each construct presented in our traditional and contemporary models.

In Study 2 we used address-based sampling procedures (Dillman et al., 2014) to solicit responses from a random sample of U.S. households via a postal survey. The instructions requested that the survey be completed by the adult (i.e., over the age of 18) in the household who had celebrated the most recent birthday. Respondents were given the choice of completing the survey online or on paper, and three reminders were sent to encourage participation. Responses from 261 adults were received with 63.5% responding via written survey and 36.5% by web survey. The average age was 53, with 60% being female and 39% male. The survey included a set of measures that was almost identical to the contemporary sample but substituted innovative use (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Wang et al., 2013) instead of perceived innovativeness with IT (PIIT). We made this substitution because while the PIIT items have a behavioral element, their overarching aim is to capture the individual’s broad tendency to be innovative (a trait) rather than the actual innovative behavior. The measure of innovative use is designed to capture individual behavior rather than the trait of innovativeness and this was more consistent with our research model. A full list of measures for each study is provided in Appendix A and more details on the samples are provided in Appendix B.

This analysis shows the extent to which variance explained by each construct is also captured by the other. The approach shows the relative predictive power of each construct and their influence on the conclusions we draw about both the influence of self-efficacy and other constructs in the models.

## Results

We first present descriptive statistics for the model constructs. Then we assess the models using SmartPLS 3.2.6 (Ringle et al., 2015). SmartPLS was appropriate for this since our primary goal with the models was to assess predictive validity of ITSE and CSE. The objective function of SmartPLS is to maximize explained variance (i.e., predictive validity) so using SmartPLS matches the aim of the research to the function of the statistical test. Significance tests were estimated using bootstrapping with 5000 samples. We present the measurement model results first and then the structural model results.

## Descriptive Statistics across Samples

Table 7 provides descriptive statistics for the constructs, computed using equally weighted averages of the items. We report the raw scale mean and its percentage of the maximum to allow for comparison across Studies 1 and 2. In the postal survey, we used a 5-point scale for IT outcome expectations, IT anxiety, IT identity, innovative use of IT, and IT strain based on advice from Dillman et al. (2014). Thus, to remove the effect of scale length, we computed each mean as a percent of the maximum possible (7 or 5).

The mean of ITSE was lower with the postal survey (t = -3.6, p < 0.001). CSE was also numerically lower, but the difference in means was not significant (t = 1.6, p = 0.107). The means for IT use were not different between the two samples. A statistical comparison was not possible for the remaining constructs because of the change from 7-point to 5-point scales. Comparison of the percentages in Table 7 shows the differences in IT OE and anxiety were small. IT strain was higher in the postal survey sample, and IT identity was lower. We could not compare innovative use as we used different measures in Studies 1 and 2. The descriptive results suggest that the online panel sample was somewhat more psychologically engaged in the use of IT. These differences are important to understand in interpreting the model results.

## Measurement Models

Overall, heuristics for reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity were satisfied for all models (Appendix D). In all cases, reflective constructs (i.e., constructs except use) produced composite reliabilities over 0.88. Average variance extracted (AVE) for all constructs was greater than 0.50. The individual item loadings (Appendix E) for all reflective indicators were above 0.70 with three exceptions (marked with a “+” in the appendix). Since the loadings were all above 0.60 and since the overall construct reliabilities were adequate, we retained all three items. The cross-loadings were substantially smaller than the loadings, the Fornell– Larcker test of discriminant validity showed that no constructs were more highly correlated with other constructs than with their own measures and the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) ratios were all below the conservative benchmark of 0.85 (Hair et al., 2016).

Table 8 shows the weights for the formative indicators of IT use in the social cognitive theory (SCT) model for the online and postal surveys. Three of the five formative indicators of IT use were significant in both samples, indicating that each adds unique information in the construction of our use index.

In the online panel sample, neither use of a PC nor use of wearables was significant, while in the postal survey, neither use of a smart speaker nor use of tablets was significant. With different significant indicators across the two models, the measures of use are not directly comparable since the relative importance of the devices to the construct is different; however, we do not view this as problematic since we are not making comparisons of paths between the online panel and the postal survey samples. As a result, we chose to retain the nonsignificant items in each model for reasons of content validity (Hair et al., 2016).

ITSE and CSE were significantly correlated, but distinct. Across the four combined models, they correlate at between 0.58 and 0.70. These correlations suggest a shared variance between 34 and 49%. The HTMT values were below the conservative benchmark of 0.85 (Henseler et al., 2014). Collectively, these results demonstrate that while CSE and ITSE are related, they are conceptually distinct and show discriminant validity.

## Self-Efficacy Case Study Summary

Our analysis of CSE using our guidelines resulted in the development of a new, more general construct, ITSE. Building on the same theoretical foundation as CSE, we demonstrate that ITSE focuses on a broader set of IT use behaviors reflecting the contemporary context of IT use. Our empirical analysis showed that ITSE has higher predictive validity than CSE, which not only impacts our understanding of self-efficacy but our understanding of other theories in our contemporary context. Thus, we believe it provides a better construct to assess an individual’s confidence in their ability to use IT in the contemporary environment and we recommend using the proposed new construct (Outcome 1).

However, while we believe that ITSE better reflects the original intent of CSE as a general measure of IT-related selfefficacy (Marakas et al., 1998), there remains a role for CSE as a more specific form of IT-related self-efficacy, akin to application-specific self-efficacy constructs such as internet self-efficacy. If the goal of a study is to understand the use of computer-based software packages in a work context, using CSE may still be appropriate, particularly if the population of interest represents the digital immigrants that were the population of interest when CSE was developed. Thus, the scope conditions (Suddaby, 2010) for the CSE construct have narrowed as IT and its use have evolved and ITSE constitutes a preferred construct for use when seeking to understand broad judgments of self-efficacy in the IT domain.

In addition, while we believe our ITSE measure represents a good general self-efficacy measure for the domain of IT, further validation is needed since all the items may not apply to every context. Items related to environmental scanning, purchasing, and installing new IT are relevant to a great many contexts, but they are most obviously relevant to personal devices and applications. Thus, it remains to be seen whether construct validity would be as high with a sample of organizational workers using enterprise-wide systems.<sup>5</sup> Our expectation is that users’ experiences with personal devices is what will drive their overall sense of confidence with IT, even where the IT use context is different, but this expectation requires empirical testing.

The illustration provided by the CSE case study shows how the guidelines can be applied to specific contexts. We demonstrate (step 1) how to identify the tacit assumptions surrounding a construct, and how to test their continuing validity in the contemporary context. In the case of CSE, this involves examining the IT context and the use context in which the construct was developed and how these have changed. It also involves identifying the underlying logic of the measure as it was originally constructed, in this case, the fundamental premise of CSE. We further show (step 2) how a new conceptualization and operationalization can be developed that addresses these limitations but is not bounded by the original conceptualization approach. Our new instrument includes different types of questions than those posed in the CSE instrument, focused on tasks rather than levels of support, because that was more consistent with the current environment. In step 3 we demonstrate a robust empirical comparison, and how traditional theorizing and contemporary theorizing may be differently affected by the conceptualization and measurement choices and we propose a specific outcome (replacement) that addresses the conceptual limitations identified in step 1.

## Discussion

This paper critically reflects on how we think about the constructs used in IS research and the potential for their obsolescence in a changing technology and organizational environment. Recognizing the construct obsolescence dilemma, we present and illustrate a structured process designed to balance the need for updating obsolete constructs with the need to sustain the cumulative tradition that scholars in our field have worked hard to build. Each step in our guidelines presents a driving question to consider, key approaches and evidence for completing the assessment, and direction regarding the potential outcomes of the step. Our case study, which updates general CSE to a more contemporary IT selfefficacy, shows how to apply the guidelines in a particular theoretical and methodological context.

The concept of construct obsolescence and the guidelines to address it are especially important in a dynamic, technologycentric discipline such as information systems. This is in contrast with a discipline such as psychology, where measures of personality traits, for example, would be expected to remain relatively constant. Even measures of attitudes or beliefs such as commitment and job satisfaction may be expected to also stay constant. In IS research, however, while a concept such as self-efficacy or effort expectancy may not change in meaning, it may be important to change the way we apply the concept to fit a changing IT or behavioral context. This is important because, as we show in our illustration, construct obsolescence can result in reduced explanatory power, which can influence both our conclusions about the construct and our conclusions about other constructs in the theoretical model. Such conclusions might lead us to abandon fields of inquiry or change our advice for practice based on results deriving from measurement limitations rather than from a substantive change in the construct’s importance.

Our guidelines present a high bar for change because of the potential costs of construct change, such as lost comparability with prior research. Only by undertaking a thorough conceptual and empirical updating of the construct can we (1) ensure the need for updating is real and (2) provide a basis for comparing the old and new approaches to help researchers make sense of any differences. A detailed conceptual analysis of the old and new constructs, and recommendation to retheorize the construct absent the constraints of the existing one, helps to delineate the specific ways in which the existing construct may be obsolete and understand the implications of those differences for theory. The demand for empirical evidence, and as our illustration shows perhaps multiple studies, also ensures that updating projects are not undertaken lightly. But more importantly, the empirical comparison of the existing and new measures provides a baseline for future research that can help to maintain comparability with prior research by showing how the existing and new constructs are similar and different.

Our case study raises an interesting question about the expected level of generality of updated constructs. ITSE emerges at a higher (i.e., more generalized) level than CSE.<sup>6</sup> Because IT has changed over time and has become broader both in terms of the tools available and the opportunities for their use, general self-efficacy concerning IT must, by definition, also be broader. We do not, however, believe that this would necessarily be the case for all constructs. Jasperson et al. (2005), for example, advocated a narrower conceptualization of use (feature-centric view) because “the set of IT application features recognized and used by an individual likely changes over time, and it is the specific features in use at any point in time that influence and determine work outcomes” (p. 529). IS-SERVQUAL (Kettinger & Lee, 1994), on the other hand, added dimensions of IT service but did not fundamentally change the generality of the construct. Thus, we believe researchers should be open to considering new constructs that emerge at a lower, similar, or higher level of generality as compared to existing constructs.

## Contributions

Our research contributes to the extant literature in multiple ways. First, we contribute to the management literature which has explored notions of change and drift in the meaning of constructs (Bartunek & Spreitzer, 2006; Osigweh, 1989; Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021) by elaborating a new form of drift, based on a mismatch between an evolved phenomenological context and a static construct. We term this form of drift obsolescence and argue that it is distinct from previous forms which focus on the ways in which constructs develop new meaning as they are applied to study different phenomena. We further argue that obsolescence is especially relevant in a sociotechnical field such as information systems because of the rapidity with which the context evolves. Providing a language for the concept of construct obsolescence helps researchers across the management disciplines and beyond to address the construct obsolescence dilemma that we highlighted at the start of this paper, by acknowledging its existence and delineating its character.

Second, we contribute to the IS literature in a similar way by extending arguments about the importance of contextualization (Hong et al., 2014). However, we go beyond their logic because while they provide an approach for adapting research models to different contexts, we also direct attention to how established constructs should be recontextualized or updated to address changes within the context.

Finally, we contribute to the interdisciplinary literature on construct development. Straub (1989) and Boudreau et al (2001) articulate the importance of balancing reuse of existing measures to develop and maintain a cumulative tradition with measurement adaptation to improve quality. The question of how to address this construct obsolescence dilemma has not been addressed, however. Guidelines for the creation of measures provide some direction but tend to assume that measures are being created for new constructs, rather than to update existing ones. Thus, they do not provide guidance on how to assess whether existing constructs require redevelopment (our first step in the process) nor do they address how to compare existing and new measures (our step 3). By connecting these seminal papers with the literature on conceptual clarity (e.g., Osigweh, 1989; Suddaby, 2010; Sumpter et al., 2021), construct development (Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017; MacKenzie et al., 2011), and contextualization (Hong et al., 2014), we present a cohesive model that helps to address this important dilemma.

## An Agenda for Research

Our guidelines provide a basis for formally evaluating whether a construct suffers from obsolescence. In our agenda for research, we discuss the challenges in doing and publishing such work as a guide for researchers. We also develop an agenda for expanding our guidelines beyond reflective Likert-type measures to a wider range of measures (multidimensional scales, formative measures, non-survey-based measures).

## Expanding the Use of the Guidelines to Other Constructs

The frequency with which constructs should be changed and the degree to which they should be changed depends largely on the extent of IT change that has occurred over a given period and the extent of contextualization that is theoretically required to align a construct with a changing IT environment. The passage of time itself is not a motivation for change. Rather, it is the extent of change in the IT artifact, the context for use, and how the artifact is used that matters, along with the strength of the evidence demonstrating the impact of that change on behavior. Moreover, change in IT and behavioral contexts of use matter only to the extent that the construct’s conceptualization is tied to these contextual factors. For a concept like self-efficacy, which is formally defined as a context-dependent belief, contextual fit is important because the courses of action and the attainments have a propensity to change as technology evolves. For other constructs, such as PIIT (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998), addressing contextual changes may be less important, since such constructs reflect individual traits that are less context-dependent. Because a formal determination that a construct requires updating should not be made outside the scope of our proposed process, we do not develop a list of candidate constructs, beyond the few we mentioned in the introduction as motivating our work.

We would be remiss, however, if we did not note the degree of effort, challenge, and risk necessary to undertake this work.<sup>7</sup> Our preliminary work to develop the ITSE construct began in 2016 and we spent about a year considering alternative ways to conceptualize and measure the new construct and testing our chosen approach. Our revision process with the journal pushed us to collect additional data to support our claims which further extended the time invested. And it should not be lost on the reader that this paper is NOT a standalone measure development paper for ITSE. In order to develop a sufficient contribution, we chose to focus on our broader methodological contribution in relation to updating constructs and consider the ITSE work as an example of the technique. We raise this not as a complaint—we have received valuable reviews and supportive guidance at each step in the process—but rather as a caution to scholars (especially more junior colleagues) about the difficulty of publishing measure development work. The IS discipline demands pairing contributions in substantive theory with contributions in measurement in order to merit publication in top journals. Thus, developing—or updating—the theory of measurement for a construct must be considered as only the first step in building a theoretical contribution.

## Expanding the Guidelines

Work is needed to further articulate a systematic process for multidimensional and formative constructs. For multidimensional constructs, differences might occur in one or more of the first-order dimensions OR in the relationship between the dimensions and the higher-order construct (or both). The basic logic of the updating process should hold in this case, but the specific details of the steps (especially the empirical analysis) may differ. Similarly, the different relationships between the construct and its formative measures would require additional empirical evidence in order to remain faithful with our guidelines.

Further, we believe that the logic of our process can be applied across different traditions in IS research to assess whether theory and measurement remain aligned as IS phenomena evolve. We believe that the general principles underlying our process are equally relevant to research using secondary measures or other methods that are becoming increasingly common (Rai, 2018). In fact, they are consistent with the practice of testing for endogeneity, evaluating robustness, and analyzing alternative models in econometrics based IS research and/or mixed-methods research. Our approach provides theory-based guidance for why such methods should include periodically re-evaluating measures derived from archival and other secondary data sources, as the underlying construct of interest may tend to drift over time as the context changes. Nonetheless, further research is needed to adapt our process for these other types of methods or measures.

## Conclusion

Critically evaluating how we define and measure concepts does not mean that our cumulative traditions as a discipline should be abandoned. By highlighting how to systematically consider when to update the tightly woven theory and measurement that characterize these traditions, we provide a tool to update our topics, theories, and methods across the behavioral, economic, technical, and design threads of IS research. We can only do this because our field has heeded the advice of Keen, Straub, and our founders, emphasizing a focus on building a cumulative tradition rooted in shared theories, definitions, measures, and methods.

Our process articulates an essential logic of how to mindfully update our constructs through assessment (Step 1), innovation (Step 2), and the empirical comparison of alternatives (Step 3), demonstrated through a case study of CSE, one of the seminal constructs in our field. In so doing, we illustrate how our discipline can integrate new perspectives, build on existing understanding, and further our sociotechnical tradition.

Such a process and illustration are important because our current technological environment looks less and less like the world the founders of our discipline knew and the time has come for the IS community to not only reevaluate key elements of our tradition, such as the use of our seminal constructs, but also investigate how these changes impact our discipline’s ability to speak to practice. We believe that doing this requires affirming our tradition of reflexive analysis to create a bricolage of old and new principles that is capable of challenging problematic assumptions that hinder our ability to update the fabric of our field while adding value to our understanding of IS-related phenomena. We hope that future scholars find our process useful for the thoughtful reconsideration of constructs and hope that it reminds them of the importance of considering not only the theories of measurement but also the methods and epistemologies that are central to understanding the substantive phenomena that we study.
