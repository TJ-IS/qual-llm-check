# The longitudinal impact of enterprise system users’ pre-adoption expectations and organizational support on post-adoption proficient usage

John F. Veiga<sup>1</sup>, Marcus M. Keupp<sup>2</sup>, Steven W. Floyd<sup>3</sup> and Franz W. Kellermanns<sup>4,5</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Management, School of Business, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Military Academy, Department of Military Business Administration, Swiss Federal Institute of Technology, Zurich, Birmensdorf ZH, Switzerland; <sup>3</sup>Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts – Amherst, Amherst, MA, U.S.A.; <sup>4</sup>Department of Management, Belk College of Business, The University of North Carolina-Charlotte, Charlotte, NC, U.S.A.; <sup>5</sup>INTES Center for Family Enterprises, WHU, Düsseldorf, Germany Otto Beisheim School of Management, Vallendar, Germany

## Abstract

Although enterprise systems (ES) are ubiquitous, many firms report less than stellar payoffs from these costly investments, with underutilization often attributed to failures in the implementation process. Unfortunately, research has not provided sufficient insights into these failures, in part because it has focused on actual usage, as opposed to proficient usage, as the benchmark for successful implementation. Moreover, research has not generally examined how the adoption process unfolds over time, thus overlooking potential underlying mechanisms that may help explain how adopters achieve proficiency. To begin addressing these shortcomings, we study how adopters’ pre-adoption expectations, enacted over time, can influence their post-adoption proficiency, by shaping how and why they spend time using the system during the adoption period. We analyzed time-lagged survey data from 153 financial analysts, required to adopt new ES-based software, at a multinational bank. We found that adopters who hold pre-adoption expectations reflecting greater internal and external motives to adopt the system as well as systematically integrate it into their work routines are more apt to use the system in ways that enhance their cumulative knowledge of it, and subsequently develop higher levels of proficiency post-adoption. Moreover, greater organizational support enhanced the impact of adopters’ expectations on proficiency, except when their actual use is low in which case organizational support had an adverse effect. ## Introduction

Investment in enterprise systems (ES) that range from enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems to system-based software platforms has been substantial worldwide (Saeed et al, 2010). In the U.S. alone, the market has been estimated to be \$24 billion and growing (Hestermann et al, 2009). While these systems have generally yielded suf<sup>fi</sup>cient returns to justify further investment, for many <sup>fi</sup>rms one perennial question remains: why do some adopters become highly pro<sup>fi</sup>cient (i.e., use the system’s core applications as intended by its designers) while many others fail to leverage the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of even the most elementary applications (Jurison, 1996; Saeed et al, 2010)? Indeed, the importance of this question is ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed by research suggesting that ES implementation failures occur over 60% of the time (Devadoss & Pan, 2007) resulting in continuous underutilization and lower than expected investment returns.

Clearly, it is unrealistic to expect individuals engaged in different organizational functions to be highly pro<sup>fi</sup>cient users of all the software applications embedded in their <sup>fi</sup>rm’s system, just as it is for users of Microsoft Of<sup>fi</sup>ce who are more apt to use only a few of its applications that directly relate to their jobs. However, it is far more realistic to expect employees in speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational functions, such as accounting, <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis, logistics or manufacturing, to become pro<sup>fi</sup>cient users of system-based software whose applications are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed to enhance their performance on important job-related tasks – and upon which their <sup>fi</sup>rms rely to conduct day-today business (Cotteleer & Bendoly, 2006). That said, to date, there has been a dearth of research on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. Thus, in this study, we speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focus on pro<sup>fi</sup>- cient usage of customized, system-based software adapted to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs of users.

Much of the research on technology adoption at the individual level, largely built on the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) as well as the more recent attempts to provide an integrative acceptance model by Wixom & Todd (2005), has focused on actual usage, or the amount of time a system is used, as the benchmark outcome for successful adoption. Indeed, even recent studies based on the uni<sup>fi</sup>ed theory of acceptance and use of technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003) continue this focus on actual usage (e.g., Jeyaraj et al, 2006; Sabherwal et al, 2006). However, ‘… while it is assumed that [actual] usage will result in positive outcomes, this remains to be tested (Venkatesh et al, 2003, p. 470).

Several scholars have discussed user competence as a way to link usage to positive outcomes (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, 1986; Straub et al, 1995; Munro et al, 1997; Burton et al, 2006). In addition, others have called for empirical studies to examine the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency that users need to develop for their organizations to bene<sup>fi</sup>t (Ginzberg, 1981; Trice & Treacy, 1988; Lassila & Brancheau, 1999; DeLone & McLean, 2003; Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006; LeRouge et al, 2007). However, little has been done to explain the factors leading to pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Indeed, after an extensive review of the literature, Venkatesh concluded that ‘little research has focused on individual employees and studied the drivers of process adoption by employees’ (2006, p. 501). Hence, as a <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward addressing these concerns, we propose a longitudinal model of the sequential, enacted impact of users’ pre-adoption expectations on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved postadoption. While the impact of expectations on individual motivation is the cornerstone of expectancy theory (Vroom & Deci, 1992), in developing our model we followed Weick’s (1988) enactivist approach viewing ‘individual motivations as not straightforward antecedents to the action but as being shaped during the action’ (Meissonier et al, 2012, p. 39). Consequently, we examine how pre-adoption expectations, enacted into the ongoing processes embedded in usage of a system during a threemonth adoption period, subsequently impact post-adoption pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst present our theoretical rationale for our conceptual model and hypotheses. Building on the technology acceptance and ES technology adoption literature, we identify three salient, pre-adoption expectations including intentions to use a system, intentions to systematically integrate a system into work routines, and performance outcome expectations. Next we detail the longitudinal research design and sampling frame that led to our collection of time-lagged survey data, gathered prior to adoption and three months after adoption, from 153 <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts who were required to adopt an ES-based global ledger software application speci<sup>fi</sup>cally developed for analysts based upon industry-best practices. Then we test this model using hierarchical regression, following recommended procedures for testing indirect effects, including testing the resulting conditional sequential effects, using 5000 bootstrapped re-samples. Finally, we discuss the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for research and practice; noting in particular that to our knowledge this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst research to focus on the development of adopter pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency over time and to empirically demonstrate how these three enacted pre-adoption expectations signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly impact post-adoption pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency through their emerging in<sup>fl</sup>uence on how and why an adopter’s time is actually spent using a system during the adoption period.

## Theory and hypotheses

Although technology research has relied extensively on intentions to use as the primary driver of actual usage (Venkatesh et al, 2003), after an extensive review of the literature Wu & Du (2012) found that some studies have simply used intentions as a surrogate for usage. Nevertheless, based on their meta-analysis of 195 studies, Wu and Du conclude that intentions ‘may not serve as a good surrogate for usage’ (2012, p. 690) and urge researchers to follow-up to insure that intentions result in actual usage. However, beyond intentions to use, there has been a dearth of individual-level process adoption research to further explain actual usage (Venkatesh, 2006). More importantly, because extant research has been silent about the consequences of actual usage, we propose to move this discourse forward by focusing on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage.

Given our focus on further understanding the adoption process at the individual level, in which actual usage impacts pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, we relied on technology adoption research from two streams. In particular, we examined ES technology adoption research, which focuses on antecedent of achieving system bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (cf. Seddon et al, 2010), and technology adoption research (Venkatesh et al, 2003) to guide our choice of pre-adoption expectations that could play a key role in explaining actual and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage behavior. While the ES adoption research has focused primarily on the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of implementing change at the organization level, it does offer insights into key drivers of ES bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that affect individuals’ usage decisions. In particular, after an extensive review and integration of extant ES adoption literature, Seddon et al conclude that in the short run there are two ‘primary determinants of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from all ES implementation projects’ (2010, p. 317), functional <sup>fi</sup>t and overcoming organizational inertia, that is, adopter resistance.

Although functional <sup>fi</sup>t, or the match between functional capabilities and organization needs (Seddon et al, 2010), is important, we did not include it in our model because research has shown that it is not apt to be an obstacle to achieving organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts when, as in the present study, a system has been extensively developed based on industry-best practices and then adapted and pre-tested by end-users in the <sup>fi</sup>rm prior to adoption (Brown et al, 2002; Robey et al, 2002). Indeed, the system we studied was developed for the banking industry, after extensive testing and usage by <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts at other banks, based on best practices. This system was also further modi<sup>fi</sup>ed and re<sup>fi</sup>ned, and further pre-tested for functional <sup>fi</sup>t by 15 expert end-users in the company we studied before it was rolled out for adoption, thereby insuring a good <sup>fi</sup>t. Hence our primary focus was on overcoming organizational inertia because, as Seddon et al suggest, it is the ‘key driver [of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts] for ES projects in the <sup>fi</sup>rst few years after [they] go live’ (2010, p. 313) and, although organizational inertia has been recognized as multidimensional (Besson & Rowe, 2012), we focus in particular on adopter resistance because Seddon et al (2010) singled it out as the most important factor underlying whether or not a system is fully utilized. That said, we also recognize that resistance can be active or passive (Coetsee, 1999) while not being equivalent to non-acceptance (Van Offenbeek & DongBack, forthcoming).

Using adopter behaviors to ground overcoming organizational inertia, Seddon et al cite several ES scholars to conclude that ‘unless people … are motivated to use the system, and [assimilate] suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge of how to use the system effectively (Purvis et al, 2001; Liang et al, 2007), the organization is unlikely to gain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts it might’ (2010, p. 313). Following their lead, and given the import placed on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of adopter motivation and knowledge in overcoming resistance and achieving the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an ES, we included three pre-adoption expectations to capture this in<sup>fl</sup>uence in our model. In particular, as shown in Figure 1, we speci<sup>fi</sup>ed intention to use a system to capture adopters’ internal motivation, as suggested by Venkatesh et al (2003). To capture knowledge assimilation, we speci<sup>fi</sup>ed intentions to systematically integrate a system to re<sup>fl</sup>ect adopters’ intent to methodically and purposefully assimilate knowledge into their work routines (Purvis et al, 2001; Liang et al, 2007). And <sup>fi</sup>nally, to more fully capture adopter motivation, we also speci<sup>fi</sup>ed performance outcome expectations to re<sup>fl</sup>ect adopters’ external motivation to use a system, as suggested by Venkatesh et al (2003).

Essentially, we propose a sequential, indirect effects model (MacKinnon et al, 2002) whereby pre-adoption expectations are not directly linked to post-adoption pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency but are instead ‘sequentially related through signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships with a linking mechanism [actual usage]’ (Mathieu & Taylor, 2006, p. 1039). We do so for three reasons. First, we are proposing a longitudinal model that follows a temporal sequence. Second, pre-adoption expectations are more transient in nature and are therefore more apt to play a near-term role by impacting actual usage as the adoption process unfolds. And third, paralleling other sequential learning models that focus on more long-term, distal outcomes such as achievement (e.g., Mayer, 1992; Lodewyka et al, 2009; Phan, 2009, 2010), our outcome, level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved, is the cumulative result of three months of actual usage during adoption that already carries the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of preadoption expectations. Hence, our core thesis is that actual usage during adoption will serve as the primary mediating mechanism through which the effects of pre-adoption expectations are transmitted to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage post-adoption. In addition, owing to the contingent role of organizational support (Seddon et al, 2010), we propose that the indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pre-adoption expectations as transmitted by actual usage to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage will be enhanced by organizational support. To develop the theoretical rationale for our model, we <sup>fi</sup>rst establish the link between actual usage and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage.

## Pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage

Unlike actual usage that has been operationally de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the literature as the amount of time individuals use a system (Venkatesh et al, 2003), pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, perhaps because it is too application speci<sup>fi</sup>c, has not been explicitly operationalized. However, within the broader context of an ES, level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage has been viewed as a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the extent to which individuals fully utilize a system (Marcolin et al, 2000) as ‘designed and intended by the designers’ (Venkatesh, 2006, p. 501) to perform an array of relevant, job-related tasks (Tate, 1995; Saeed et al, 2010). Hence, combining these perspectives we operationalize pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage as the extent to which individuals fully utilize the core, integrative applications within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c software platform that are designed and intended to enhance their performance on essential, job-related tasks. Essentially, in arriving at this operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition, we stress full utilization, as suggested by Marcolin et al (2000) which, consistent with a balanced score card approach (Kaplan & Norton, 1996), emphasizes an adopter’s ability to perform an array of important applications (Tate, 1995; Saeed et al, 2010). We also specify ‘core, integrative applications’ because each is, by design, essential to integrating non-core, peripheral elements that are part of the larger ERP system in which the software platform is embedded. And <sup>fi</sup>nally, we specify ‘designed and intended’ to underscore that we are assessing pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage using software developers as subject matter experts. It is also important to note that this operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition applies to speci<sup>fi</sup>c job-technology combinations where the <sup>fi</sup>t between job-related tasks and the software platform is presumed to be high.

Clearly, during the early adoption stage, some amount of actual usage is necessary in order to even begin developing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. However, just as more extensive usage during adoption does not ensure pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency, limited usage does not ensure ineptness. Put differently, the amount of time spent using a system during adoption is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to explain the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency achieved post-adoption. As Saeed et al point out, ‘Even when usage of an enterprise system is mandated by the organization, users may differ in the extent to which they will leverage [its] various functionalities’ (2010, p. 671). Indeed, there are numerous examples in early system adoption in which users spend considerable amounts of time using a system primarily to develop their own ‘tweaks’ and ‘workarounds’ so as to eventually avoid using it as much as possible (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Robey et al, 2002; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006; Liang et al, 2007), thereby developing minimal pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. On the other hand, there are also many cases in which adopters devote considerable amounts of time conscientiously using a system in order to enhance their understanding and pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency using it. For example, some adopters review training documentation or embedded support manuals while they are interacting with a system, while other adopters create their own intuitive operating manuals to clarify and streamline interactions with the system thereby further enhancing their knowledge and pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). Conversely, when adopters lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient motivation to use a system during the adoption period, they may use it sparingly and develop little pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency, or use it just enough to develop a moderate level of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency – perhaps because they are able to quickly grasp suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information with limited use or expect more pro<sup>fi</sup>cient coworkers to eventually show them how to use it (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Boudreau & Robey, 2005).

That said, we reason that cumulative learning and knowledge acquisition, essential to increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, is more likely to occur as a result of greater actual use during early adoption (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). Hands-on experience is particularly critical for the transfer and development of knowledge (Argote et al, 1990), both tacit and explicit (Nonaka, 1994). In addition, greater knowledge absorption is more likely to occur as a result of increased, re<sup>fl</sup>ective interaction with a system in the early stages of adoption (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000) as individuals gain the requisite knowledge and know-how (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Rogers, 1995; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000) essential to increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Kraiger et al, 1993). Hence, because system complexity has been found to have a negative impact on actual use (Thompson et al, 1991; Igbaria et al, 1995), we reason that an individual’s pre-adoption expectations will be critical in determining the extent to which actual use results in greater pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. In particular, greater actual use that is the result of stronger intentions to use a system, more systematic approaches to assimilating operating knowledge of the system, and higher outcome expectations should enhance understanding of the system during adoption and thereby positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved post-adoption.

## Intention to use

Research on the link between intentions to use and actual usage has generally supported this relationship. For example, Wu & Du’s (2012) recent meta-analysis reported average, sample-size weighted correlations between 0.35 and 0.49 between intentions and usage, depending upon the method used to assess usage. In addition, based on an extensive review of the literature, Venkatesh et al (2003) conclude that intentions to use, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting an adopter's internal motivation to use a system, are linked to actual usage, in both organizationally mandated and nonmandated systems. Moreover, unlike most of the extant research that has relied upon cross-sectional studies to examine this link, Venkatesh et al (2003) found that this link is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant over time.

Further, as suggested by Venkatesh et al, ‘intention captures internal motivations’ (2003, p. 470) to use a system, which is consistent with IT research using motivational theory to explain usage behavior (Davis et al, 1992; Venkatesh & Speier, 1999). In addition, Boudreau & Robey (2005) describe numerous case examples of adopters with stronger intentions to use a system who then subsequently devoted considerably more time and effort to conscientiously using it. Conversely, in explaining underutilization of ES, adopter resistance has been signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly attributed to lack of internal motivation (Wagner & Newell, 2007; Seddon et al, 2010) prior to implementation. Consequently, given this preponderance of evidence, we would expect that adopters with stronger intentions to use a system prior to adoption will be more internally committed and persistent in using it, thereby increasing actual usage, whereas for those who are less internally motivated, actual usage will decrease.

Given this and our prior argument linking actual usage to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, we reason that the strength of adopters’ intentions to use a system prior to adoption will increase the extent to which they use it during the adoption period and thereby, because of more committed and dedicated usage, such intentions will have a positive, indirect effect on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved post-adoption. Formally stated:

H1: Adopters’ pre-adoption intentions to use a system will positively influence actual usage during system adoption and thereby have a positive, indirect effect on the level of proficient usage achieved post-adoption.

## Intention to systematically integrate

The literature has tended to view the process of system adoption as an organizational level phenomenon (e.g., Bagozzi et al, 1992; Holland & Light, 1999; Marchand, 2000), focusing primarily on adoption as an organizational change process (Orlikowski, 1993). As a consequence, while extant organizational ES adoption studies have examined organizational members attitudes toward system change and readiness to change (Kwahk, 2006; Kwahk & Kim, 2008), researchers have focused limited attention on how individuals assimilate change into their work patterns, with some exceptions. Saeed et al (2010) suggested that more re<sup>fl</sup>ective use of a system during the assimilation stage could enhance overall sensemaking of it. Boudreau & Robey (2005) examined group social processes that in<sup>fl</sup>uence individual assimilation after a system has been fully implemented for 14 months. And, based on 14 case studies of <sup>fi</sup>rms adopting ES, Robey et al (2002) concluded that a more incremental approach to system implementation by organizations could reduce barriers to knowledge assimilation. Hence, despite the ‘substantial burden on would-be adopters in terms of knowledge needed’ (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1346) that is imposed by ES adoption, there has been a dearth of empirical research on how adopters integrate systems into their work routines. Indeed, beyond introductory training, adopters are generally expected to develop pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency with a system by integrating it into their work routines through continued use. How this is accomplished is left largely up to them (Due, 1994).

Extant adoption research has suggested that successful integration necessitates methodical ‘extended periods of practice’ (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997, p. 1360), noting that those who resist adopting a system are often prone to do so after only a ‘quick look’ (p. 1359). Beyond these observations, literature on cognitive style and informationseeking offers some insights into how individuals generally prefer to approach processing and organizing information (Hunt et al, 1989). In particular, the analytical cognitive style, that is, a general disposition toward approaching work environments more systematically, gradually, re<sup>fl</sup>ectively, and methodically, has been linked to task and learning performance in organizations (Allison & Hayes, 1996). Similarly, research has also found a positive correlation between more systematic approaches to learning and effective self-study among adult learners involved in cognitively demanding tasks (Harper & Kember, 1986; Schmeck, 1988; Tennant, 1988; Ackerman & Woltz, 1994).

Moreover, from an information-seeking perspective (Heinstrom, 2006), individuals who pursue information more systematically tend to spend more time engaging the task (Kember et al, 1995), persist in the pursuit of information in order to ‘create personal understanding of information content’ (Tanni & Sormunen, 2008, p. 900), and thereby create absorptive capacity routines by incorporating this information into internal and external routines or meta-routines (Lewin et al, 2011). On the other hand, individuals who utilize a less systematic, surface approach tend to be less persistent, spending less time and effort engaging the task (Kember et al, 1995) in order ‘to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a momentary information need rather than to expand existing knowledge’ (Tanni & Sormunen, 2008, p. 900). Hence, extending this line of reasoning, and given the complexity of integrating an ES into work routines, we reason that adopters who expect, prior to adoption, to be more systematic in their approach to integrating a new system into their work routines, will tend to spend more time interacting with it in ways that contribute to learning and, as a result of more systematic usage, enhance their understanding of it. Conversely, those who anticipate being less systematic in assimilating a new system prior to adoption, primarily to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll a momentary need, will spend less time interacting with it and, as a result of super<sup>fi</sup>cial usage, fail to enhance their understanding of it.

Given this and our previous argument linking actual usage to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, we reason that the stronger the adopters’ pre-adoption intentions to systematically integrate a system into their work routines, the more they will systematically use it during the adoption period and thereby, as a result of more enhanced and extensive usage, such intentions will have a positive, indirect effect on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved post-adoption. Hence we posit that:

H2: Adopters’ pre-adoption intentions to systematically integrate a system into their work routines will positively influence actual usage during system adoption and thereby have a positive, indirect effect on the level of proficient usage achieved post-adoption.

## Performance outcome expectations

Venkatesh et al suggest that beyond intentions to use a system ‘future research should investigate other potential constructs such as behavioral expectations’ (2003, p. 470) particularly expectations that capture external motivation. This conclusion was motivated by their <sup>fi</sup>ndings that found performance expectations associated with current job performance served as antecedents to intention which ‘only captures internal motivations to perform the behavior’ (2003, p. 470). Davis et al also suggest that external or extrinsic motivation involves behavioral expectations that are ‘instrumental in achieving valued outcomes that are distinct from [performing] the activity itself’ (1992, p. 1112). Accordingly, our model also includes behavioral expectations that capture valued outcomes that are not related to current job performance. In particular, we focus on performance outcome expectations that encompass longer term outcomes such as opportunities for preferred job assignments, <sup>fl</sup>exibility in changing jobs or increased job security (Thompson et al, 1991; Chau, 1996).

Based largely on social cognitive theory, which suggests that the more individuals believe that pursuing a particular course of action is likely to result in obtaining a desirable outcome the more likely they are to continue pursuing it (Bandura, 1986), researchers have empirically demonstrated the impact of outcome expectations on actual usage. Studies have consistently suggested that outcomes expectations are important determinants of use behavior (Davis, 1989; Davis et al, 1989; Thompson et al, 1991; Thompson et al, 1994; Bergeron et al, 1995; Morris & Dillon, 1997) and several report a positive association with actual usage (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1978; Lucas, 1978; Maish, 1979; Robey, 1979; Swanson, 1987; Davis, 1989; Igbaria, 1990). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, research has also suggested a positive association between performance outcome expectations and actual usage. For example, Compeau & Higgins (1995) and Compeau et al (1999) found that performancerelated outcome expectations, particularly those with long-term importance to the individual, such as a pay increase or promotion, were positively associated with how extensively individuals used a computer. Consequently, although research to date has not examined performance outcome expectations in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of adopting an ES, the preponderance of theory and evidence suggests a strong positive link between outcome expectations and actual usage.

Given this reasoning and our previous argument linking actual usage to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, we reason that stronger pre-adoption performance outcome expectations will increase the extent to which adopters use a system during system adoption and thereby, as a result of more committed, extensive usage, such expectations have a positive, indirect effect on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved post-adoption. Formally stated:

H3: Adopters’ pre-adoption performance outcome expectations will positively influence actual usage during system adoption and thereby have a positive, indirect effect on the level of proficient usage achieved post-adoption.

## Organizational support

Although prior research posits organizational support as an antecedent to technology acceptance, extant longitudinal studies have not found this. For example, Compeau et al (1999) examined the level of technical support that was offered to managers prior to using a computer, such as equipment and software selection choices, and found that it had no impact on computer usage 12 months later. In addition, Jelinek et al (2006) examined the level of social support provided by management over a 6-month period and found it had no direct impact on software adoption by salespeople. Given these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, researchers have begun to call for longitudinal research that examines change management interventions that foster system adoption (Venkatesh, 2006), such as the contingent role of organizational support in facilitating system adoption (Seddon et al, 2010).

It has been suggested that organizational support, or the extent to which individuals believe they have received the requisite socioemotional and technical support from management, plays a contingent role in pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency-building (Schermerhorn et al, 1990). When adopters are provided greater support they are more likely to internalize management’s expectations (Mirvis et al, 1991; Grover, 1993) as well as have a more positive attitude toward adopting a system (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Consequently, during the adoption period, when organizational support is higher, the impact of actual usage on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage post-adoption is more likely to be stronger because adopters are more apt to persevere when they interact with a system, enhancing the transfer of knowledge (Argote et al, 1990) essential to increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. Moreover, given the inevitable frustrations and strains that arise from complex, technical knowledge acquisition, adopters are better able to dissipate such emotions and <sup>fi</sup>nd answers more quickly to their most pressing questions about the intricacies of a system when organizational support is higher, thereby enhancing the impact of actual usage during adoption on post-adoption pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. When organizational support is lower, on the other hand, we would expect that the stresses of adoption will not be as readily dissipated, such that the link between actual usage during adoption and post-adoption pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency will be weaker as growing frustration increases confusion and misunderstanding (Grimley & Banner, 2008), compromising the adopters’ depth of understanding. Therefore we posit that:

H4a: The relationship between actual usage during system adoption and the level of proficient usage achieved postadoption will be moderated by organizational support, such that the relationship will be stronger when support is higher.

And, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, given previous arguments indirectly linking pre-adoption expectations to pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage it also follows that:

H4b–d: The positive, indirect effects of adopters’ pre-adoption expectations, including (b) intentions to use a system, (c) intentions to systematically integrate a system, and (d) performance outcome expectations, on proficient usage will be stronger when organizational support is higher.

## Methods

## Sample and procedure

Our study focused on a large multinational <sup>fi</sup>nancial services corporation, ranked in top 50 of Fortune Global 500 banks, with 75,000 employees and over \$1 trillion in assets that was implementing a new, mandatory global ledger system. The ledger system was part of an SAS ES that involved software applications speci<sup>fi</sup>cally developed for <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts based upon best-practices in the banking industry and customized by a core team of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s designers and end-users. Although the company has a presence in over 40 countries, it was initially rolling out the new system in multiple locations within six countries, including the United States, Switzerland, Hong Kong, Japan, Singapore, and the United Kingdom. After a week of introductory training, a senior executive endorsed and solicited voluntary responses to our web survey via an email from 485 <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts, two weeks prior to roll out. Although the company’s expectation was that all of their analysts understood the English language, respondents were instructed not to complete the survey if they were not conversant in English. Respondents were assigned a random four-digit code which they inserted in the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey and were told that they should make a record of it since it would be used again in a follow-up survey 3 months later.

While some have suggested an adoption period can last as much as 6 months or more (Seddon et al, 2010), we chose a 3-month follow-up time frame for two reasons. First, based upon extensive, developmental, pre-rollout user trials and customization of the system with <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts, the designers believed that this should be ample time for adopters to achieve a reasonably high level of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency with its key applications. And, second, as with any time lagged data, we concluded that extending the time frame further, posed the risk of introducing other exogenous variables that might confound <sup>fi</sup>ndings and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly deplete sample size. Hence, after 3 months an email was sent requesting completion of a second web survey in which respondents provided a four-digit code which allowed the matching of the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second questionnaire. Initially, 240 analysts responded to the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey, and of those 153 responded to the second survey for a response rate of 64% (or 32% of the original population). We compared the demographics and pre-adoption expectations between the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second survey respondents and found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences. Given the 3-month time lag from the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey, the <sup>fi</sup>nal response rate was deemed acceptable. Respondents were 59.2% male, averaged 34.2 years of age, and had 2.3 years of tenure in their current position.

## Pre-adoption measures

Originally developed by Mathieson (1991), we used a widely adopted three-item measure to assess intention to use (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Using a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree) respondents indicated the extent to which they agreed with the following statements: (1) I intend to fully use the global ledger system to do my work, (2) I have every intention of completely adopting the global ledger system in my work, and (3) My intention is to use the global ledger system whenever I can. The three items were averaged to create the <sup>fi</sup>nal score with higher scores re<sup>fl</sup>ecting stronger intentions (α = 0.83).

While measures of <sup>Intention to systematically integrate</sup>cognitive and learning style have proliferated, many have not been validated or are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to administer in organizational surveys because of their length (Hayes & Allinson, 1994; Rayner & Riding, 1997). Moreover, because these measures focus broadly on assessing an individual’s general style and personality, they do not re<sup>fl</sup>ect speci<sup>fi</sup>c situational demands and requisite behaviors (Grigorenko & Sternberg, 1995; Armstrong, 2000; Grimley & Banner, 2008). Hence, to capture our global ledger system context, we adapted four items from Cools & Van den Broeck’s (2007) measure of knowing style and from Kember et al’s (1995) re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking questionnaire, intended to capture the extent to which individuals anticipated using a systematic approach toward integrating a system into their work routines. Respondents were presented with four statements and asked to indicate their level of agreement using a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, they were asked: Integrating a major job change like the global ledger system into my job will: (1) necessitate that I prepare well in advance and thoroughly, (2) require approaching integration slowly and deliberately, (3) tend to be most successful when it is absorbed gradually, (4) occur only after extensive information has been reviewed by me. To ensure the factor structure of this measure, we conducted both exploratory and con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis. Both analyses con<sup>fi</sup>rmed a single construct, and the measure $( \chi ^ { 2 } [ 4 , \mathrm { \dot { N } = 1 } 5 3 ] = 2 1 . 8 0$ P<0.001) had acceptable <sup>fi</sup>t indices CFI = 0.91, IFI = 0.89, NFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.03. The four items were averaged to create a total score, with a higher score re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a stronger intention to systematically integrate into work routines (α = 0.78).

We used a four-item <sup>Performance outcome expectations</sup>measure developed by Venkatesh et al (2003) adapted from Compeau & Higgins (1995) and Compeau et al (1999).

Using a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), respondents indicated the extent to which they agreed with the following statements: Knowledge of the global ledger system can increase my (1) <sup>fl</sup>exibility for changing jobs, (2) opportunity for meaningful work, (3) opportunity for preferred future job assignments, and (4) opportunity to gain job security. The four items were averaged to create a <sup>fi</sup>nal score, with higher scores re<sup>fl</sup>ecting greater performance outcome expectations (α = 0.86).

## Post-adoption measures

Based on broader measures of per-<sup>Organizational support</sup>ceived organizational support (e.g., Rhoades et al, 2001; Eisenberger et al, 2002), using a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), respondents were asked: Please evaluate the organizational support you have received during the previous 3-month adoption period on the following statements: (1) I have received suf-<sup>fi</sup>cient training to understand the global ledger system, (2) I have been given all the tools I need to help me effectively use the global ledger system, (3) Assistance has been readily available to help me with the global ledger system, (4) I have been encouraged by my supervisor to use the global ledger system, (5) I feel my supervisor has fully supported me in developing my skills with the global ledger system. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis veri<sup>fi</sup>ed a single factor structure (χ<sup>2</sup>[5, N = 153] = 15.41, P<0.01; CFI = 0.95, IFI = 0.95, NFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.02) and responses to the <sup>fi</sup>ve items were averaged to create a <sup>fi</sup>nal score (α = 0.81).

While some have assessed usage behavior <sup>Actual usage</sup>by capturing duration of use via system logs, such data were not available for this study because of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s security and proprietary constraints. Moreover, while system logs measure the total amount of time individuals are logged onto a system, they do not necessarily capture active usage of it by the end-user (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Thus, based on Compeau & Higgins (1995) and Compeau et al’s (1999) measure, to obtain a <sup>fi</sup>nal measure of actual usage, respondents were asked to estimate, in minutes, the duration of time on a typical workday during the previous 3-month adoption period that they spent using the global ledger system.

<sup>fi</sup> Given that adopters had to utilize seve-<sup>Pro cient usage</sup>ral different applications in the ledger system software in order to do their jobs, we developed a weighted composite (formative) measure to capture pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage of the system. Researchers have shown increased interest in using such composite measures in organization research (Podsakoff et al, 2006), strategic management (Fiss, 2007), marketing (Jarvis et al, 2003), organizational learning (Gonzalez-Padron et al, 2010), and information systems (Diamantopoulos, 2011).

Given that most pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency tests are commonly developed by subject matter experts and because using their judgments has been frequently recommended as a way to improve the operationalization of a measure (e.g., Gandhi & Sauser, 2008; Hollenbeck et al, 2012), we impaneled 15 subject matter experts to operationalize pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage and establish the content validity of our measure. The panel consisted of the <sup>fi</sup>ve original designers of the global ledger system and 10 senior <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysts who had extensively pre-tested the system as end-users and were highly pro<sup>fi</sup>cient users. Each member of the panel was asked to create a complete list of the system’s core, integrative applications that if used extensively in performing job functions would demonstrate greater pro<sup>fi</sup>- ciency with the ledger system. Members then reviewed each list and collectively reached consensus on <sup>fi</sup>ve core applications – establishing content validity. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal measure, using a scale from 1 (Never) to 5 (All of the time), respondents were asked: Please indicate how extensively you currently use each of the following applications in the global ledger system to do your job, (1) <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, (2) management reporting, (3) making journal entries, (4) RFA reporting: daily material interest calculation analysis of material back valuations, and (5) global ledger system trial balance reporting.

Because frequency of usage differs between applications, some would be utilized more extensively (e.g. management reporting) than others (e.g. trial balance reporting), the panel was asked to establish a normative cutoff point as to what level of utilization constitutes achieving pro<sup>fi</sup>- cient usage for each application. In making this determination, the panel noted that pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency usage of the system, as operationally de<sup>fi</sup>ned, involves the extent to which individuals fully utilize all the core, integrative applications thus a relatively high standard would be essential to meaningfully delineate pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Consequently, the panel recommended that we use frequency scores in the top quartile as a cutoff point in order to delineate adopters who have clearly achieved pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency with an application. While the panel’s recommendation was subjective, it was also an informed choice based on considerable expertise with the system. While the panel also considered using average scores (or top 50%) as the cutoff point, the consensus was that this would not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently demarcate achieving pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. In particular, the panel concluded that they would have far less con-<sup>fi</sup>dence in using average, and above, levels of use as demonstrative of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency, particularly after 3 months of use when adopters should be using these applications fairly extensively. We concurred with the panel’s judgment largely because central tendency bias has been shown to distort performance appraisals intended to discriminate top performers (Ilgen & Feldman, 1983; Martin & Bartol, 1998; Keeping & Levy, 2000; Muchinsky, 2012).

With the top quartile cutoff point established, respondents were categorized as having either achieved pro<sup>fi</sup>- ciency (top quartile), or not, on each application. Then, to create a composite index that re<sup>fl</sup>ected the extent to which adopters were fully utilizing all <sup>fi</sup>ve of core, integrative applications of the system, we used a binary approach (e.g. Ragin, 2000, 2008; Fiss, 2007) to combine the performance indices of these applications. This approach was deemed particularly suitable because it is commonly used to score discrete, categorical variable, such as ours, and is consistent with the balanced scorecard approach (see Kaplan & Norton, 1996 for a description of an organization-level balanced scorecard), in which different, but thematically related, performance indices are combined to yield an overall performance composite (e.g., Kirkman et al, 2004). As a result, adopters who scored in the top quartile on an application were awarded 0.2 points, while those not in the top quartile received 0. Because the panel had concluded that performance on each of the <sup>fi</sup>ve indices (one for each application) should be weighted equally, the awarded points were summed resulting in a <sup>fi</sup>nal score between 0 (low pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage of the system) to 1.0 (high pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage of the system). Following Bollen (2011) and Diamantopoulos (2011) suggestions for assessing the construct validity of a composite indicator, we estimated a second-order model for pro<sup>fi</sup>- cient usage, setting the variance of the error terms to zero and allowing residuals to correlate. This model showed a good <sup>fi</sup>t with χ<sup>2</sup> [4, N = 153] = 7.553, P = 0.109; CFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.94, NFI = 0.88, RMSEA = 0.076) providing evidence of our composite measure’s construct validity.

## Analytic approach

We utilized the approach commonly recommended for testing an indirect effects model (Mathieu & Taylor, 2006; Preacher et al, 2007). Although structural equation modeling (SEM) has been employed to test for indirect effects, we chose to use hierarchical regression for two reasons. First, Preacher et al (2007) caution against using SEM for conditional effects in these models because it only reports effects using <sup>fi</sup>rst-order variance and testing conditional effects necessitates using second-order variance which would require a considerable amount of error-prone, hand-calculations. And second, the results from both approaches are virtually the same (Pedhazur, 1982). Consequently, we used the regression approach recommended by Preacher et al (2007) to test indirect conditional effects, using an SPSS macro developed by Hayes (2008). This macro estimates the indirect effect of an independent variable on the dependent variable through an intervening variable, conditional on a moderator of the path from the intervening variable to the dependent variable. In addition, we estimated a 95% bias corrected bootstrap con-<sup>fi</sup>dence interval for the resulting conditional sequential effect, using 5000, bootstrap, re-samples.

Following Mathieu & Taylor (2006) we <sup>fi</sup>rst used the Sobel test to assess indirect effects, and then we used the product of the unstandardized paths to quantify such effects and test the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of their magnitude (MacKinnon et al, 2002; MacKinnon et al, 2004; Preacher &

Hayes, 2004; Preacher et al, 2007). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, to further verify that the intervening variable carries the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of independent variables to the dependent variable we used the bootstrapping procedure recommended by Preacher and Hayes (2004) and further described in Preacher et al (2007).

Given this analytic approach, we assess the indirect effects hypothesized in H1 to H3 as follows. First, after control variables are accounted for, we test for a relationship between the independent variable (IV) and the dependent variable (DV). Second, we test for a relationship between the IV and the mediator (M). If these two paths are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant we initially conclude that there is an indirect effect between the IV and the DV. Third, to further verify this <sup>fi</sup>nding we then test for a relationship between the IV and the DV. If this relationship is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we then proceed to bootstrapping to further verify that M carries the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the IV to the DV. However, if this relationship is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant we fourthly test to determine whether or not it remains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant once the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the M is accounted for (i.e. whether or not the mediator fully carries the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the IV to the DV or only partially carries that in<sup>fl</sup>uence). Table 2 presents the results of this analysis including changes in R-squared (ΔR<sup>2</sup>) for each model as well as standardized regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, and Figure 1 summarizes the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that intentions to use a system have a positive indirect effect on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage.

## Discussion and conclusions

While pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage should be a salient yardstick for assessing successful system adoption, extant literature has not examined it. To begin to address this omission, we proposed and tested a longitudinal, indirect-effects model to examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pre-adoption expectations, including intention to use a system, intention to systematically integrate a system into work routines, and performance outcome expectations, on the level of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage achieved by adopters post-adoption. In particular, we posited that actual usage during the adoption period serves as the linking mechanism through which the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pre-adoption expectations enacted over time during the adoption period in<sup>fl</sup>uence actual usage and, in turn, impact pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage post-adoption. Overall we found that actual usage was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant link between preadoption expectations and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage post-adoption, as evidenced by the positive, indirect effects of adopter expectations on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. Moreover, we found that these indirect effects were positively moderated by organizational support during adoption.

## Summary of <sup>fi</sup>ndings

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, although adopters may spend equal amounts of time using a system during the adoption period, those who are internally motivated by stronger intentions to use it are more apt to do so in ways that enhance their understanding and cumulative knowledge acquisition (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997) that are essential to increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Kraiger et al, 1993). Consistent with Beaudry & Pinsonneault’s (2005) research on users adaptation strategies, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that as adopters enact their stronger intentions to use a system over time they are more apt to follow coping strategies, particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>t maximization, that result in enhanced user effectiveness. Indeed, when adopters are internally motivated, greater knowledge absorption is more likely to occur as a result of increased, re<sup>fl</sup>ective interaction with a system during adoption (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Lewin et al, 2011) thereby enhancing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Beyond con<sup>fi</sup>rming the impact of intentions to use on actual usage over time (Venkatesh et al, 2003), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that researchers should explore how adopters’ intentions to use a system might also serve to enhance the likelihood of other organizationally bene-<sup>fi</sup>cial outcomes.

Moreover, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that intentions to systematically integrate a system into work routines play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in enhancing pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage postadoption. In particular, as a result of more methodical hands-on, periods of practice during adoption, adopters with stronger systematic integration intentions are more apt to gain greater operating knowledge of a system critical for the transfer and development of both tacit and explicit knowledge (Argote et al, 1990; Nonaka, 1994). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings lend support to Fichman & Kemerer’s (1997) and Beaudry & Pinsonneault’s (2005) contention that successful assimilation of complex systems necessitates methodical, extended periods of practice, and clearly suggest that less disciplined approaches are not only a major obstacle to system adoption but may also be a major deterrent to fully accruing its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Clearly, longitudinal research that is designed to examine the nuances of the assimilation processes deployed by adopters during adoption, particularly as they spend time acquiring the knowledge and know-how essential to developing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency, would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial.

External motivation, driven by performance outcome expectations, was also found to play a role in enhancing adopter pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. In particular, when adopters believe adopting a new system could be instrumental in achieving longer term valued outcomes such as greater job opportunities or job security, they are more apt to be motivated to learn how to use a system as pro<sup>fi</sup>ciently as possible. Consistent with Venkatesh et al’s (2003) call for more research on behavioral expectations, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that performance outcome expectations play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in increasing the likelihood that adopters will achieve system pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Given this, we would encourage research that not only examines other bene<sup>fi</sup>cial outcomes as well as bene<sup>fi</sup>t maximizing coping strategies (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), but also explores other potential mediating links between performance outcome expectations and pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. For example, greater performance outcome expectations could motivate adopters to work more collaboratively with others during adoption and thereby develop a greater shared understanding of how a system operates (Saeed et al, 2010), further enhancing pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

Finally, we found that when system adopters experience higher levels of organizational support, the indirect in<sup>fl</sup>uence of pre-adoption expectations on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher. However, when organizational support is below mean levels its impact is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that at lower levels of support it is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to conditionally enhance the impact of expectations on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. This underscores the contingent role of organizational support in facilitating system adoption (Seddon et al, 2010) and the importance of the ‘project sponsor’ role that managers should play in ERP project implementation (Markus & Tanis, 2000). Conversely, as shown in Figure 2, when actual usage by adopters is low, higher levels of support result in even lower levels of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency than when lower levels of support are provided. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that greater organizational support has an adverse, dampening effect on achieving pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency when actual usage by adopters is low. Although research has demonstrated that reducing adopter resistance to using a new system is a key driver of ES bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Seddon et al, 2010), greater organizational support may exacerbate resistance. Indeed, over time, increasing organizational support may simply be viewed by adopters who are less willing or able to use the system as an unwanted or even burdensome intrusion particularly when their managers are motivated by stronger ‘cognitive convictions’ and a ‘false sense of shared agreement’ (Sproull & Hofmeister, 1986, p. 58) about the <sub>’</sub> fi

## Limitations

To enhance the validity of our causal inferences, we measured pre-adoption expectations prior to system rollout and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage 3 months later. In addition, while our measures were subjective in nature, we took a number of recommended steps to ameliorate concerns over informant bias, common method variance, and measurement error. None of these steps suggested signi<sup>fi</sup>cant bias. However, our study has limitations. In particular, our research design cannot fully rule out the possibility of reciprocal causality between actual usage and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. For example, it is possible that as adopters increase their level of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency they may be inclined to use a system more extensively during adoption. However, despite this potentially reinforcing loop, there is no reason to believe that adopters who achieve higher levels of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency will necessarily use a system more, especially since more pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage is likely to increase their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency as well.

In addition, while we examined the role of performance outcome expectations involving longer-term expectations, we were guided by prior research on extrinsic motivation (Davis et al, 1992; Venkatesh et al, 2003) that ruled out near-term behavioral expectations speci<sup>fi</sup>cally related to current job performance. However, we believe it may be fruitful for future research to concurrently examine both near-term and long-term expectations to determine the extent of their combined in<sup>fl</sup>uence on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. For example, do near-term, job-related expectations also in<sup>fl</sup>uence pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage over time or, as Venkatesh et al (2003) suggest, do they primarily serve to enhance internal motivation as antecedents of intention to use a system? Or, perhaps, do differences in adopters’ cultural values (e.g. individualistic compared with collectivistic) place greater emphasis on performing one’s job and less emphasis on achieving personally valued outcomes distinct from performing one’s job (cf. Walsham, 2001)? Ideally, answers to these questions would help clarify the role of outcomes that adopters value in explaining pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage over time, particularly in large multinational organizations.

In addition, because organizational support is apt to have greater impact early in the adoption period, we did not directly examine its moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the link between pre-adoption expectations and actual usage during adoption. Instead, we theorized that the impact of pre-adoption expectations on post-adoption, pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage would be primarily transmitted through their effects on actual usage during adoption. Hence, we only assessed organizational support retrospectively over 3 months, and not during the adoption period. Going forward, however, we would strongly encourage future researchers to longitudinally explore the moderating in<sup>fl</sup>uence of organizational support at various intervals during the adoption period such as during the early stages of the adoption process (perhaps after the <sup>fi</sup>rst few weeks), when support may actually be even more critical to continuing usage of a system.

Finally, our study was conducted in a mandatory use context and may not generalize to voluntary settings. While Brown et al (2002) suggest that this may not be a problem in mandatory situations involving stand-alone software applications, we nonetheless suggest that future research on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage consider the role of these contexts. In particular, we also call for further research, consistent with Brown et al’s call for more nuanced studies to ‘better understand the nature of voluntariness and mandatoriness’ (2002, p. 292). In addition, while we have suggested that pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage should be a salient benchmark for assessing system adoption, particularly within the context of ES-based software platforms designed for speci<sup>fi</sup>c functional applications, whether it is generally applicable to all ES remains an open question. Although we believe using a panel of subject matter experts to validate the development of a composite pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency index is a viable approach for future researchers to consider, we are mindful that a more generalized pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency measure, while ideal, would be very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to develop.

## Management implications

With respect to management implications, managers need to recognize that how much time individuals spend using a system during adoption does not ensure the development of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency. While time spent clearly plays a role, what is perhaps more important is how adopters are motivated and prepared to spend that time during system adoption. For example, are they motivated internally and externally to dedicate whatever time it takes to conscientiously adopt a system into their work routines, or are they primarily motivated to spend time <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to use it as little as possible? Are they prepared to systematically integrate a system into their work routines through methodical, extended periods of practice (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997), persisting until they create a tacit understanding of it (Tanni & Sormunen, 2008) or are they simply following less systematic, hit or miss, surface approaches (Kember et al, 1995) simply to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll momentary information needs (Tanni & Sormunen, 2008)?

Beyond this, managers adopting costly ES should recognize that unlike personality characteristics that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to change, context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c expectations related to motivation and preparation are potentially more malleable. Indeed, in motivating adopters, we believe that managers need to go beyond emphasizing organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts typically proffered in training programs. Perhaps, for example, by developing more persuasive adoption arguments, which focus on payoffs that serve to strengthen the longer-term performance outcome expectations of adopters, managers might signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhance pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage? Such expectations might involve potential pay increases or annual bonuses, upgrades in job level or class, increased job <sup>fl</sup>exibility to facilitate changing jobs within the <sup>fi</sup>rm, more preferred job assignments, or increased job security as a result of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s improved competitive advantage. Although such bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may be fully supported and sought after by adopters, as a caveat, managers need to be aware that in conveying these expectations it is important not to overstate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts because this could inadvertently increase adoption resistance, particularly if adopters have reason to suspect that such bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may pose negative, longer-term consequences for themselves (e.g. increased workloads, or losing their jobs).

While some adopter skepticism is probably unavoidable, managers can work toward minimizing such unintended consequences by offering socioemotional and technical support to adopters during the adoption period. As Venkatesh (2006) and Seddon et al (2010) suggest it is imperative for managers to engage in supportive management interventions as the adoption process unfolds. However, managers must also be aware that higher levels of support early in the process can have adverse consequences when adopters are minimally using the system. Ideally, early in the adoption period, managers should actively encourage adopters to vent their frustrations and concerns with the system as they arise and then move quickly to provide the necessary information and support to minimize them and thereby increase the likelihood of actual usage of the system. By providing opportunities to readily air their feelings and concerns in an atmosphere of open and honest dialogue, mangers should be able to mitigate and alleviate adopter frustration and skepticism.

Perhaps too, providing adopters some initial training on how to more systematically integrate the new system into their work routines could be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. More promising still, we believe managers can play a major role in facilitating the development of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage. Clearly, strong support during adoption, and not merely token support, can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhance the development of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage, particularly when managers informally monitor usage levels and frustrations with a system, and intervene in a timely manner with both technical and emotional support.

## Conclusion

Despite its limitations, to our knowledge this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to articulate the sequential enactment process through which adopters’ pre-adoption expectations in<sup>fl</sup>uence the development of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage postadoption over time. As such, we believe that our research provides an important <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward understanding how organizations can increase the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial outcomes of their ES. To date, most research has been concerned with changes brought about by introduction of a system, with a commensurate focus on acceptance and resistance. However, there has been little research on the ongoing use of an ES, especially on whether or not it is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial or detrimental, and on how to improve the quality of use. As an aside, we suspect that much of the early research that focused on actual usage (and avoided issues of pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency) was motivated by the reality of earlier times when it was an uphill battle just to get employees to use information systems. Now that such systems are ubiquitous in the workplace, instead of focusing on actual usage, it is essential that more attention be paid to the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial individual and organizational outcomes that accrue as a result of more pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage.

In addition, while actual usage and pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage are correlates, we believe it is important to understand the distinction between them. More importantly, going forward, we need to explore the impact of pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage on the long-term bene<sup>fi</sup>ts sought by organizations which justify their investment in new ES. For example, examining the longer term relationship between pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage and improved job performance, customer satisfaction, process optimization, cost reduction and so forth, would not only provide concrete evidence of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage can yield, but also demonstrate the importance of focusing on pro<sup>fi</sup>cient usage when a new ES is introduced.
