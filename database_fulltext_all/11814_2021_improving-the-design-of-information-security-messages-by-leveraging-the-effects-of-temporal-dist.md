## Abstract

A substantial amount of previous research has examined the efficacy of fear appeals to elicit securityenhancing behaviors from users. However, despite more than a decade of research on fear appeals in security contexts, researchers have yet to understand which factors drive users’ responses to fear appeals. Instead, the literature is riddled with inconsistent findings on the antecedents that predict fear-appeal outcomes, fueling controversy and inhibiting progress on the problem. This research addresses the inconsistent findings by using construal level theory (CLT) to explain how temporal distance and argument nature affect fear-appeal appraisal. Based on two online experiments, we report evidence showing that temporal distance determines which antecedents drive fear-appeal outcomes, which helps explain inconsistent results found in prior literature. Moreover, we found that depending on the temporal distance condition, argument nature (i.e., “how” or “why” arguments) can impact the effectiveness of fear appeals. Overall, our findings refine the understanding of when certain factors influence users’ responses to fear appeals and provide guidance for future research on how to create more effective fear appeals.

Keywords: Information Security (ISec), Security Threats, Fear Appeals, Construal Level Theory (CLT), Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), Fear-Appeal Design, Argument Nature, Temporal Distance, Organizational Security, Behavioral Security

## 1 Introduction

Behavioral information security (ISec) research seeks to find ways to encourage users, employees, and consumers to protect their information assets (Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Boss et al., 2015; Siponen, 2000). To that end, much attention has been devoted to the study of fear appeals (e.g. Boss et al., 2015; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2019; Johnston et al., 2015; Marett et al., 2011; Menard et al., 2017; Warkentin et al., 2016), which are short messages that communicate severe threats as a means of motivating recipients to perform a specific coping behavior (Witte,

1992). When fear appeals are effective, they raise recipients’ protection motivation, that is, their intention to comply with the behavioral change the fear appeals recommend (Floyd et al., 2000; Maddux & Rogers, 1983; Rogers, 1983). Ironically, most ISec studies do not manipulate fear appeals and thus undermine their ability to explain how to foster protection motivation. However, the ISec studies that do manipulate fear appeals corroborate that they work. Examples of such evidence include the successful use of fear appeals to raise users’ motivation to install anti-malware (Boss et al., 2015) or change their passwords (Johnston et al., 2015) (see Appendix A for a comprehensive review).

Despite evidence that ISec fear appeals motivate users, which factors influence their effectiveness remains unclear. Although most of the ISec fear-appeal literature is based on protection motivation theory (PMT) (Maddux & Rogers, 1983; Rogers, 1975), studies often report contradictory results on the significance of PMT variables as predictors of protection motivation (see Wall & Buche, 2017 for a comprehensive review). For example, whereas some studies find threat perceptions to be significant and thus important predictors (e.g., Boss et al., 2015; Marett et al., 2011), others have not found such evidence (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016). This has created controversy regarding the appropriate focus of fear appeals: Should they focus on communicating strong threats or abandon threats and instead focus on response variables? For example, Boss et al. (2015) argued that strong threat statements are important because they lead to motivational fear, which is a key driver of protection motivation (Floyd et al., 2000; Tannenbaum et al., 2015), while other studies have contended that threat statements carry less importance than statements that stimulate coping efficacy (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016). Clearly, the overall findings of the ISec literature paint a disjointed picture of the drivers of protection motivation.

The lack of clarity in ISec research regarding which factors are important determinants of protection motivation is problematic. First, without resolution on this matter, the literature may become stuck in debates over which factors are more important and why (Wall & Buche, 2017) rather than building a cumulative understanding of how to improve fear appeals. Second, without understanding the causal mechanisms of fear appeals, researchers cannot make credible recommendations to practitioners on how to use fear appeals effectively in the security management processes. Third, and perhaps most importantly, the conflicting evidence suggests that the literature suffers from lurking variable bias (Joiner, 1981)—that is, the variance in findings may be explained by thus-farunobserved variables that confound the estimated relationships. Hence, to advance the theoretical discourse of fear appeals, an explanation for the conflicting evidence is required.

To address these issues, we use a fresh theoretical angle to understand when which PMT appraisal factors influence fear appeal effectiveness. Using construallevel theory (CLT), a theory that explains users’ judgment of actions, events, or objects, we examine temporal distance and argument nature, two previously unexamined factors, as explanations for when and why judgments of fear appeals may vary from user to user. Temporal distance refers to the point in the future at which users should perform a specified behavioral response. Argument nature refers to the basis of fearappeal arguments in either how or why a behavioral response should be performed. CLT suggests that temporal distance can affect the focus of users’ judgment and that argument nature can influence the effectiveness of messages. Hence, these two novel factors may explain the inconsistent findings because, as we will demonstrate, ISec fear-appeal studies often differ in terms of the temporal distance and the nature of arguments used in their fear appeals.

Hence, we ask: What are the effects of temporal distance and argument nature on fear-appeal appraisal? We hypothesize that temporal distance moderates the effect of fear-appeal-appraisal variables on fear-appeal outcomes and that argument nature influences fearappeal outcomes if it matches the temporal distance. Two online experiments provide evidence that supports our hypotheses; we found that temporal distance moderates many of the relationships stipulated in PMT. Thus, the novel variable of temporal distance helps explain and reconcile the contradictory findings in the ISec literature. Furthermore, we found that argument nature can improve fear-appeal outcomes if it matches temporal distance. Importantly, this means that if message arguments do not carefully match temporal distance, fear appeals may yield weak effects when compared to matching conditions.

Our study has several important implications for research and practice. First, we introduce a new perspective to ISec fear-appeal research that explains the mixed findings plaguing the literature. Specifically, we found that the previously omitted variables of temporal distance and argument nature can account for some of the differences in findings between previous studies, thus confirming our suspicion that the ISec PMT literature suffers from lurking variables that confound the estimated relationships (Joiner, 1981). Our theory and findings provide researchers a means to reevaluate these findings and account for them in future research to build a more unified cumulative research tradition. Second, we show that researchers and practitioners can create more effective fear appeals by explaining which factors (e.g., severity, self-efficacy) need to be manipulated by which kind of argument (how or why) and for which type of temporal distance (proximate or distal). This helps researchers craft more effective experimental treatments and prevents practitioners from wasting their efforts on ineffective fear appeals.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Protection Motivation Theory

We begin with a discussion of the theoretical basis of fear-appeal research in the security domain. ISec research has relied predominantly on PMT to explain the effect of fear appeals on user compliance with ISec training and policies (see Appendix A) (Boss et al., 2015; Wall & Buche, 2017). Fear appeals are persuasive messages that communicate personally relevant threat events to stimulate users’ protective motivations (Rogers, 1975, 1983). PMT explains that fear appeals trigger sequential cognitive appraisal processes, which jointly inform protection intentions. The threat appraisal process evaluates threat severity perceptions (i.e., recipients’ beliefs that an ISec threat is serious and potentially severe) and perceived threat vulnerability (i.e., their expectation that they will be susceptible to a particular threat) (Rohm & Milne, 2004). Moreover, the threat appraisal process accounts for perceptions of maladaptive rewards, which are rewards gained from not mitigating the suggested threat—for example, not complying with a security policy (Floyd et al., 2000). PMT predicts that higher levels of threat severity and threat vulnerability perceptions lead to higher levels of protection motivation, whereas maladaptive rewards undermine the effects of threat and vulnerability on protective motivation (Floyd et al., 2000).

PMT predicts that if a personally relevant threat sufficiently stimulates threat severity and threat vulnerability, recipients will experience fear, which will lead to protection motivation (Leventhal, 1970). For example, Boss et al. (2015) showed that the more users feared losing data, the greater their motivation to perform data backups. Similarly, several meta-analyses have corroborated the motivational role of fear (e.g., Sutton, 1982; Tannenbaum et al., 2015; Witte & Allen, 2000).

PMT suggests that, once threatened, users will engage in a coping appraisal process that estimates a coping strategy’s value vis-à-vis the response efficacy (i.e., the user’s belief in the efficacy of the action) and its associated self-efficacy (i.e., the user’s perceived ability to actually perform the action) versus the response cost (i.e., all the costs or expected costs of carrying out the protective response) (Floyd et al., 2000; Maddux & Rogers, 1983). PMT predicts that recipients will engage in recommended responses only if the threat’s noxiousness outweighs the associated maladaptive rewards and if the recommended coping response is executable, efficient, and affordable (Boss et al., 2015; Floyd et al., 2000; Maddux & Rogers, 1983).

## 2.2 Mixed PMT Findings in ISec Contexts

The past decade of IS research on PMT and fear appeals has failed to produce a cumulative understanding of which PMT factors matter in security contexts. Despite a shared theoretical perspective, PMT, the ISec literature is riddled with conflicting findings. For example, whereas some studies find threat perceptions to be important predictors of protection motivation (e.g., Boss et al., 2015; Marett et al., 2011), others do not find such evidence (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016). Another example relates to the importance of response perceptions. Whereas one group of studies find response perceptions to be significant drivers of protection motivation (e.g., Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Menard et al., 2017), others have determined that response perceptions are insignificant (e.g., Boss et al., 2015; Johnston et al., 2016; Marett et al., 2011). As Table 1 makes evident, there is a plethora of contradictory evidence for every variable in the PMT model. If anything, the ISec literature appears to agree to disagree.

These mixed findings are problematic. First, they have occasioned a debate about which factors are important for the efficacy of fear appeals. For example, the original literature suggests that protection motivation is predominantly the result of threat susceptibility perceptions (Floyd et al., 2000; Rogers, 1983; Tannenbaum et al., 2015). Whereas some ISec fearappeal studies agree with this conclusion (e.g., Boss et al., 2015), others have find this factor to be insignificant (Table 1) and conclude that fear appeals should instead focus on response perceptions (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016).

Because the literature is currently divided on whether threat or response perceptions matter, it is likely that future studies will merely provide additional corroborating evidence for one side or the other without resolving the underlying conundrum. This will inhibit the development of cumulative knowledge, which is why an explanation that reconciles the two sides is necessary.

Second, because of the lack of agreement regarding what drives fear-appeal efficacy in ISec contexts, the literature does not provide clear guidance on how to craft effective fear appeals. This is problematic for scholars, who may rely on suboptimal treatments in their experiments, which may thus yield smaller effects. As a result, their conclusions may suffer from Type II errors. Moreover, without a consensus on what drives fear appeals, the literature cannot make sound recommendations for practitioners. The goal is to inform practitioners on how to craft effective securityenhancing behavioral interventions; however, as a whole, research has made little progress because it remains unclear which factors are important drivers of behavioral change.

Third, the prevalence of mixed findings suggests that researchers have omitted important variables. This is unsurprising because most studies rely on the variables prescribed by PMT (Wall & Buche, 2017). Virtually no other theories have been used to explain users’ judgments of fear appeals. The overreliance on PMT has resulted in a narrowly defined theoretical approach to fear appeals. Given the mixed findings and the overuse of PMT, it is likely that ISec researchers have omitted lurking variables that play a key role in the effectiveness of fear appeals in ISec contexts. To develop a more holistic understanding of why fear appeals work, fresh theoretical perspectives are in order.

## 2.3 Construal Level Theory (CLT)

To understand the mixed findings, we draw upon CLT, a general theory that explains how users make judgments about future objects, events, or actions (Bar-Anan et al., 2006; Trope & Liberman, 2010). CLT posits that users form mental representations—that is, construals—to represent these objects, events, and actions. As such, CLT is concerned mainly with theorizing the gestalt of these construals and their implications for judgments. To understand how messages influence users’ judgments about future behaviors—the main purpose of ISec fearappeal research—the notion of construals is useful but not necessary. Thus, we follow CLT-based persuasion research in using a somewhat simpler representation of

CLT that does not require construals (Fujita, 2008; Hansen & Wänke, 2010; Katz & Byrne, 2013; Kim & Kim, 2017; Nan, 2007; Semin et al., 2005). It is important to note that this representation is nonetheless true to the basic CLT approach (Fujita et al., 2008).

CLT maintains that users can judge future actions (e.g., download a vulnerability scanner) in terms of their feasibility or desirability (Liberman et al., 2002; Liberman & Trope, 1998; Trope & Liberman, 2010). Judgments of feasibility involve an assessment of whether or how an action can be executed, whereas judgments of desirability involve evaluations of why an action should be executed (Liberman & Trope, 1998). Thus, a single action can be judged in two ways: whether it is feasible (e.g., how can I download a vulnerability scanner?) or whether it is desirable (e.g., why should I download a vulnerability scanner?).

CLT suggests that whether actions will be judged in terms of their feasibility or desirability depends on the user’s temporal distance to the behavior (Fujita et al., 2008). If behaviors need to be performed proximately (i.e., immediately), users must assess whether they can perform the behavior. In such cases, users will judge the behavior based on information that informs the how (e.g., How long does it take to install a vulnerability scanner? Do I have time? Is it free?). By contrast, if a behavior needs to be performed distally (e.g., three months from now), such feasibility concerns are less relevant. Rather, users will assess whether the behavior is of relevance to them and make a judgment of desirability by considering information that informs the why (e.g., Is the vulnerability scanner effective in protecting me?). Figure 1 depicts this relationship. Thus, if fear appeal studies vary in their temporal distance, users might respond based on judgments that consider either feasibility or desirability factors. If true, then temporal distance could offer a potential explanation of the mixed findings.

Because CLT proposes a connection between message arguments and temporal distance (Trope & Liberman, 2010), it is reasonable to expect that messages with arguments that match the temporal distance will be more persuasive. Thus, users’ distance-induced focus on feasibility or desirability judgments can be exploited to build more persuasive messages (Fujita, 2008; Hansen & Wänke, 2010; Katz & Byrne, 2013; Kim & Kim, 2017; Nan, 2007; Semin et al., 2005). For example, in proximate conditions in which users are concerned with feasibility (e.g., How long does it take to install a vulnerability scanner?, Do I have time?, Is it free?), messages that provide feasibility arguments (e.g., It just takes a few seconds, it can run in the background, and is free) may be more persuasive. Analogously, in distal conditions in which users are concerned with desirability (e.g., Is the vulnerability scanner effective in protecting me?), messages that provide desirability arguments (e.g., A vulnerability scanner is effective in protecting your network) can be more effective. Thus, CLT provides a new perspective that clarifies how to improve the effectiveness of fear appeals in ISec research.

## 2.4 Issues in ISec Fear-Appeal Design

To determine whether temporal distance and argument nature explain the mixed findings in the previous literature, we reviewed the findings of some key studies regarding these two factors (Table 2). It is evident that there is substantial variation among the studies with respect to these two factors. In terms of temporal distance, some studies have called for immediate responses (e.g., back up your data within the next week) (Boss et al., 2015), whereas others have not specified a response time (e.g., Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Menard et al., 2017) or leave undefined the meaning of temporal language such as “always,” “frequently,” (Warkentin et al., 2016), or “in the future” (Beitelspacher et al., 2012; Marett et al., 2011). This may be problematic because temporal perceptions substantially influence behavioral intentions and decision-making (e.g., Bar-Anan et al., 2006; Chandran & Menon, 2004; Förster et al., 2004; Venkatesh et al., 2006). Given the variation in temporal distance across the studies, the factor of temporal distance is a prominent candidate for explaining the mixed findings.

In terms of focus, some studies stress desirability arguments (e.g., Anti-spyware software provides a proven method for protecting against spyware) (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010) while providing few or no feasibility arguments. Conversely, other studies stress feasibility arguments (e.g., We recommend using Dashlane as your password manager) (Menard et al., 2017) that inform participants about how to perform the response without detailing why the response is particularly desirable. However, many studies provide a mix of feasibility and desirability arguments and sometimes stress desirability. For example, Boss et al. (2015) offered a detailed account of why a virus should be removed but also provided instructional information about how to do so. The variation in focus indicates that there is no clear understanding of or consensus on what arguments should be included in fear appeals to make them most effective. But because users’ judgments emphasize disparate factors (feasibility or desirability), such understanding is needed.

## 3 Operationalized Theory and Hypothesis Development

Reconciling the mixed findings in the literature requires an understanding of the effects of temporal distance and argument nature on fear-appeal appraisal. We begin by theorizing the specific effects temporal distance has on users’ judgments of fear appeals (H1). We then develop hypotheses about how argument nature can aid users’ judgments and thereby improve fear-appeal outcomes when they match (H2) or mismatch (H3) the temporal distance.

## 3.1 Temporal Distance on PMT

An integration of CLT and PMT could potentially shed light on how fear appeals stimulate proximate or distal responses, as well as reconcile the conflicting findings of existing ISec research. PMT suggests that several appraisals shape protection motivation: threat severity, threat vulnerability, maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response cost. Consistent with the ISec literature and PMT, we use this parsimonious set of appraisal factors to predict protection motivation (i.e., the user’s intention to engage in the protective response) (Boss et al., 2015; Floyd et al., 2000).

Whereas existing PMT research suggests that these appraisal variables are salient factors of users’ judgments about performing a protective behavior, CLT research has shown that the temporal distance of the behavior under consideration influences which factors are important (Kankanhalli et al., 2015; Lutchyn & Yzer, 2011; Stratham et al., 1994; Trope & Liberman, 2010). Specifically, CLT suggests that under proximate conditions, users are more likely to make judgments of feasibility, which are predominantly concerned with considerations of carrying out an action (Liberman & Trope, 1998; Trope & Liberman, 2010). For example, Fujita et al. (2008) show that users respond better to arguments related to ease of use when considering the purchase of a DVD player now vs. in the future.

In that vein, we argue that several PMT variables correspond to “how” judgments (see Table 3). Specifically, we argue that threat vulnerability, selfefficacy, response cost, and maladaptive rewards are concerned with the “how” of performing a protective response. This can be derived from the concepts to which these constructs relate. Threat vulnerability captures one’s perception of susceptibility, thereby evaluating whether a threat will affect oneself. This is a judgment of feasibility, as it necessitates an assessment of how a threat may affect oneself (e.g., How are home-network vulnerabilities threatening me today?). Analogously, self-efficacy perceptions similarly necessitate an assessment of how one can perform the protective response (e.g., How can I install a vulnerability scanner today?). Response cost refers to the costs that incur when performing the response, such as financial cost, time, or effort. Making judgments about response cost also necessitates a judgment about the affordability of the cost (e.g., How can I find time to do this today?). The same applies to maladaptive rewards, which refer to the rewards gained from not performing the response. Judging maladaptive rewards also requires a judgment of feasibility (e.g., How do I benefit from not installing a vulnerability scanner today?). Notice that all these considerations are self-related, meaning that they make assessments that put the threat and response in relation to the self, which become important when the requirement to react to a threat or perform an action are proximate to the self (Liberman & Trope, 1998; Trope & Liberman, 2010). Thus, we hypothesize that these PMT variables will be stronger drivers of proximate intentions than for distal intentions.

Some evidence supports this hypothesis. For threat vulnerability perceptions, research has shown that proximate framing makes health risks appear more concrete and strengthens threat vulnerability perceptions (Chandran & Menon, 2004). For selfefficacy perceptions, evidence indicates that judgments of difficulty are salient in decisions to perform temporally proximate actions (Liberman & Trope, 1998). For response cost, scholars have demonstrated that effort perceptions are more salient for understanding responses to temporally proximate events (Peetz et al., 2009). Finally, for maladaptive rewards, researchers have shown that users emphasize immediate rewards over distal rewards (Smithson, 2008). Thus, we propose:

H1a-d. When judging a proximate coping response, recipients’ (a) threat vulnerability, (b) self-efficacy, (c) response cost, and (d) maladaptive rewards perceptions will have stronger effects on protection motivation than when judging a distal coping response.

In contrast, CLT suggests that in judgments of distal responses, judgments of desirability are of central concern (Trope & Liberman, 2010). A judgment of desirability essentially assesses the valence of the end state (Liberman & Trope, 1998), which corresponds to two PMT variables: threat severity, which assesses the negative consequences of an object (e.g., In general, are home-network vulnerabilities problematic?), and response efficacy, which concerns the positive outcomes of engaging in the protective response (e.g., In general, are vulnerability scanners effective?). Notice here that these variables relate to considerations that are objective in nature. Whether a threat is severe or a response is efficient can be determined without relating these to the self (e.g., a man can make an assessment of whether breast cancer is deadly without considering whether he is susceptible to this threat or not). Consequently, threat severity and response efficacy perceptions refer to general judgments of core attributes of the object under consideration. CLT suggests that such judgments are made when events or actions are perceived to be distal (Trope & Liberman, 2010). Consequently, we argue that for distal responses, these two perceptions will be significant drivers of protection motivation. This is in line with some evidence, as it has been shown that users emphasize outcomes (Liberman & Trope, 1998) and consequences (Stratham et al., 1994) when considering distant goals. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1e, f: When judging a distal coping response, recipients (e) threat severity and (f) response efficacy perceptions will have stronger effects on protection motivation than when judging a proximate coping response.

## 3.2 Message Arguments, Temporal Distance, and Fear-Appeal Efficacy

Next, we conceptualize how considering temporal distance and argument nature can lead to crafting more effective fear appeals. CLT persuasion research has suggested that the nature of arguments (e.g., feasibility vs. desirability arguments) can influence message efficacy if it matches the temporal distance to the targeted response (Nan, 2007).

Thus, following the logic of CLT, when users begin to judge a proximate response (e.g., installing a vulnerability scanner right now), feasibility arguments aid that decision-making process because they feature specific details that inform how the behavior can be performed. Conversely, when users judge a distal response (e.g., installing a vulnerability scanner three months from now), they will respond to desirability arguments that omit peripheral details and specify why a behavior should be considered. Consequently, the matching of arguments to temporal distance has been suggested as a means of increasing processing fluency (i.e., the ease with which users cognitively process these messages) (Shmueli et al., 2016), which influences whether messages are perceived as true (Hansen & Wänke, 2010).

Several studies have shown that messages that match the purpose are more persuasive (Semin et al., 2005). For example, Fujita et al. (2008) showed that individuals with proximate goals are more effectively persuaded by messages conveying specific details and feasibility arguments. In their experiment, individuals in a proximate goal situation (e.g., choosing a class for the next semester) were more effectively persuaded by feasibility arguments focusing on specific, peripheral details (e.g., the seats are nice in that classroom), whereas individuals in the distant behavior condition (e.g., choosing a class for the next academic year) were more persuaded by desirability arguments focusing on central features of the class (e.g., this class has fair grading). Katz and Byrne (2013) proposed that for this reason, individuals faced with immediate choices (i.e., psychologically proximate choices) may be more receptive to specific messages. Thus, we propose:

H2a: When judging a proximate response, a feasibility message will lead to higher levels of protection motivation relative to a desirability message.

H3a: However, when judging a distal response, a feasibility message will lead to lower levels of protection motivation relative to a desirability message.

We also propose the reverse interaction:

H2b: When judging a distal response, a desirability message will lead to higher levels of protection motivation relative to a feasibility message.

H3b: However, when judging a proximate response, a desirability message will lead to lower levels of protection motivation relative to a feasibility message.

## 4 Study 1

## 4.1 Study 1: Experimental Designs

To test the effect of temporal distance and argument nature, we conducted two randomized online experiments. We employed a 2 x 2 full factorial design. The factors were temporal distance (proximate vs. distal) and argument nature (feasibility vs. desirability). Full factorial design has been upheld as the gold standard of experimental design because it enables an examination of the effect of each factor in isolation (in our context, the effect of proximate vs. distal distance or that of feasibility vs. desirability arguments) as well as the effect of the interaction between the factors (Collins et al., 2009; Price et al., 2015). Thus, in a full factorial design, the treatment conditions vary from each other in exactly one key dimension: the manipulated factor. To establish that the treatments aroused appraisal, we further include a no fear appeal condition as an additional control, thereby following recommendations of Boss et al. (2015) that pertain to the operationalization of PMT. In total, our experimental design included five groups (Table 4).

## 4.1.1 Study 1: Procedures

The experiment was hosted on Qualtrics, an online survey platform. Participants were recruited and randomly assigned to one of the five experimental groups. Next, participants received the treatments in sequence (the following section on manipulations provides further detail). Then, participants answered manipulation checks, responded to the main survey instrument, and provided demographic information. It is important to note that our design and treatments were approved by the corresponding institutional review boards.

## 4.1.2 Study 1: Sample

Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk)™ workers were recruited for both the pilot and main study. They were offered a monetary incentive of US\$2.50, and 450 responses were completed. Studies have shown that results based on data gathered from MTurk are comparable to those based on data acquired through other means (Horton et al., 2011; Mason & Suri, 2012; Paolacci et al., 2010; Steelman et al., 2014). We restricted the subject pool to workers who had an approval rate of at least 90%, who were otherwise employed (Lowry et al., 2016), and who were based in the United States (Steelman et al., 2014). We evaluated participants’ attentiveness by employing multiple attention checks, monitoring the time spent on the survey and manipulations, and asking questions to ensure that the manipulations were well received.

## 4.1.3 Study 1: Manipulations

Temporal distance was manipulated by varying the temporal distance of the response. The manipulations were derived from CLT studies (Chandran & Menon, 2004; Fujita et al., 2008; Hansen & Wänke, 2010; Liberman et al., 2002). Specifically, we asked participants in the proximate condition to consider an immediate time frame (i.e., immediately, now/today, or in the next seven days) when thinking about installing a vulnerability scanner. Conversely, we asked participants in the dista condition to consider a distal time frame (i.e., in the near future, the coming months, or the next three months) when thinking about installing a vulnerability scanner.

We developed messages with feasibility and desirability arguments by focusing on how or why compromised routers are a threat and how or why a vulnerability scanner should be installed. This manipulation followed the operationalization guidelines of existing research on

CLT and persuasion (Freitas et al., 2004; Han et al., 2016; Hansen & Wänke, 2010). In line with CLT, we formulated “how vs. why” arguments pertaining to each PMT variable—threat severity, threat vulnerability, maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response cost (Boss et al., 2015). The messages were of similar length and provided the same number of arguments. Appendix B presents the treatments. Potentially, a threat to validity could arise if one set of arguments was generally more persuasive than the other. However, as our findings show, this was not the case, so this confound is falsified.

We included a no-fear-appeal control condition. This condition did not contain any arguments (neither how nor why). However, we had to provide a succinct explanation of what vulnerability scanners do so that participants could understand the survey questions. The brief explanation is somewhat different than the words used in the treatment conditions, as we wanted to exclude the possibility of delivering a “mini fear appeal.” In following the recommendations of Boss et al. (2015), our goal was to establish a baseline for participants protection motivation to contrast with our real fear appeal conditions, thereby validating the effectivity of our fear appeal. This was necessary because Boss et al. (2015) have shown that PMT only leads to valid results if effective fear appeals are employed. Importantly, this no-fear-appeal condition was not necessary to test the effects of our manipulations, since we employ a 2 x 2 factorial design, in which the cells provide contrasting conditions to (and thus control) each other (Bagozzi, 1977; Collins et al., 2009).

## 4.1.4 Study 1: Construct Measures

We used established measures to gather data on PMT variables and fear (Boss et al., 2015; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015). We operationalized protection motivation as users’ intention to download a vulnerability scanner within seven days and within three months (in line with the treatment condition). The persuasiveness (or effectiveness) of our treatments was measured through gains in protection motivation. In line with other fear-appeal studies, we gathered several control variables: suspicion (Wright et al., 2014), computer self-efficacy in information security (Rhee et al., 2009), risk propensity (Meertens & Lion, 2008), and prior media exposure (Malhotra et al., 2004). Appendix B presents the instrument.

## 4.1.5 Study 1: Pilot

Our pilot study evaluated the research design. We collected 166 valid responses from MTurk workers. The pilot revealed that our research design was sound. Through open comment fields, we solicited feedback from the participants, who stated that they found the topic interesting and valuable, that the survey was not lengthy, and that the issued reward was not insufficient. Analysis of the data helped us identify redundant measurement items, which we removed, and to identify opportunities for minor improvements of certain measurement items and stronger wording of the temporal framing responses.

## 4.2 Study 1: Analysis and Results

Analysis proceeded in several steps (see Figure 2). We first assessed the validity of our research design. Next, to determine whether our randomization was successful, we evaluated whether sample characteristics were evenly distributed across treatment groups. We then established that our manipulations led to the desired effects and examined whether our instrument yielded reliable and valid measurements. To test the effect of temporal distance (H1), we conducted a partial least squares regressionbased multigroup analysis (PLS-MGA). The use of PLS was necessary because the groups had insufficient sample sizes for the use of covariance-based methods (Hair et al., 2017). Then, to test whether the nature of message arguments influences fear-appeal effectiveness (H2 and H3), we conducted an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Finally, we addressed the possibility that our findings were influenced by or artifacts of common method bias (CMB). The following sections provide in-depth detail for each of these steps.

## 4.2.1 Study 1: Demographics

The main study collected valid responses from 450 participants. On average, the participants took 19.95 minutes to complete the survey. We removed 25 participants whose responses were extremely short in duration (e.g., seven minutes or less), which left us with 425 usable answers. The removed responses were distributed across multiple treatment groups. Table 5 presents sample demographics. About 55% of the participants were male; participants had an average age of 38.6, an average of 21 years of computer experience, an average of 18.5 years of internet experience, and an average home network size of 9.9 connected devices. Importantly, about 15% of participants held a job in IT, 53% had experience with malware, and 19% had a vulnerability scanner installed. A comparison of sample characteristics across the treatment groups indicated that the differences in age, gender, computer experience, internet experience, and home network size were minimal. To ensure the comparability of samples in terms of prior victimization, experience with vulnerability scanners, and jobs in IT, we conducted an analysis of variance (ANOVA). Although we did not find evidence of differences across groups (even though computer experience was borderline significant, at $p = 0 . 0 5 )$ , we controlled for these demographic variables in later analyses. Overall, the preliminary analysis suggested that we successfully randomized the assignment of subjects.

## 4.2.2 Study 1: Manipulation Checks

Manipulation checks serve the purpose of gathering additional evidence that it is indeed the manipulation (X) that leads to the measured outcomes (Y) (Bagozzi, 1977). To that end, manipulation checks verify (1) that the manipulation (Y) has been “taken” (Perdue & Summers, 1986) and (2) that the actual manipulation (Y) corresponds with the intended manipulation as specified by the construct in the theory (Boudreau et al., 2001). Thus, in our research design, manipulation checks were needed to verify that (1) participants had received the manipulations and (2) that response framing led to differences in temporal distance (i.e., proximate or distal) and that message arguments were perceived as focused more on how (feasibility) than why (desirability).

To ensure that our manipulations were received, we measured the time spent on each manipulation screen. The average time spent on the temporal distance manipulation and the message argument manipulation was 42.5 seconds and 110 seconds, respectively. These values indicate that participants spent sufficient time on the manipulations. To further validate whether our fear appeal messages were effective, we compared the conditions in which messages were delivered to the condition in which no message was delivered. As shown in Table 6, our fear appeals led to significant differences compared to the no-fear-appeal control condition, thus satisfying the conditions necessary to explore PMT (cf. Boss et al., 2015). Thus, we conclude that our manipulations were received and effective in stimulating participants’ cognitive appraisal.

Next, we tested whether the delivered manipulations corresponded with the intended manipulations (construct validity). In terms of the response frame manipulation, we tested whether the response was seen as more proximate or more distal. In terms of the message argument manipulation, we evaluated whether the treatment messages were seen as emphasizing “how” or “why.” We drew from prior literature to develop these manipulation-check questions. As Table 6 shows, our manipulations led to significant differences in subjects’ responses in the intended dimensions. We, therefore, conclude that our manipulations were successful.

## 4.2.3 Study 1: Measurement Validation

We used confirmatory factor analysis to evaluate the measurement model (Straub et al. 2004). We estimated an initial PMT model and examined item loadings. We dropped items with weak loadings, resulting in all constructs being measured by three items, except response cost, for which we dropped one item because of low loadings (Straub et al., 2004). We subsequently reestimated the model. All item loadings were then above the 0.50 threshold (0.60 and significant) (Hair et al., 2006) (see Appendix C). For each construct, the Cronbach’s alpha was above the 0.70 threshold, and the average variance extracted (AVE) was above 0.50 (Gefen et al., 2000), indicating convergent validity. We assessed discriminant validity by comparing construct correlations to AVE-squared (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and found that for each construct, the square root of AVE was greater than its correlation values. Moreover, we estimated variance inflation factors (VIFs) to evaluate multicollinearity. All VIFs were below the recommended 3.0 threshold (Hair et al., 2017). Item-construct loadings and cross-loadings were also higher on their designated constructs than on others (greater than 0.27 difference in all cases) (Henseler et al., 2016a). Appendix C presents item loadings and cross-loadings. Collectively, these findings suggest that the measurement model meets established standards.

The estimated PMT model displayed a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) score of 0.049, which represents a good fit to the data (restrictive cutoff at < 0.080) (Henseler et al., 2016a). The χ²(38) value was 2,331.30 and thus significant $( p \leq 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . Unfortunately, other covariance-based model fit criteria could not be applied directly to components-based structural equation modeling (SEM) because of the lack of established thresholds (Henseler et al., 2016a). An upcoming criterion for PLS-SEM is the root mean square error correlation, abbreviated as RMS theta (Henseler et al., 2014), which for our model scored 0.128.

## 4.2.4 Study 1: Analysis and Results

Step 1: Testing the effect of temporal distance on message appraisal. To understand the effect of temporal distance on users’ judgment of fear appeals, we conducted a PLS-MGA to compare the drivers of protection motivation between proximate and distal response conditions. Thus, H1a-f were tested via a PLS-MGA using SmartPLS v3 (Ringle et al., 2015). SmartPLS 3.0 is a partial least squares path modeling approach to structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM). A PLS-MGA is appropriate for identifying moderation effects arising from dichotomous variables, such as our manipulations (proximate vs. distal) (Fassott et al., 2016). For dichotomous variables, interaction terms and PLS-MGA have been shown to have comparable results (Sarsted et al., 2011). The PLS-MGA allowed us to compare the structural models between the proximate and distal conditions. A PLS-MGA analysis requires measurement invariance, meaning that the measurement instrument must be identical and have identical meaning for all respondents. We expected measurement invariance because, in collecting the data of both groups, we used a single measurement instrument, randomly assigned participants to the groups, and analyzed both datasets using a single structural model. According to Henseler et al. (2016b), this establishes configural invariance, which constitutes Step 1 of their three-step procedure for establishing measurement invariance. Hence, we followed their recommended procedure for establishing compositional invariance (Step 2) and the equality of composite mean values and variances (Henseler et al., 2016b). We calculated the necessary statistics using permutation with 1,000 cases (see Appendix Table C3). Using their criteria, we found that all item-loading correlations (between the two groups) for each construct were larger than its 5% quantile, and we therefore assumed compositional validity (Step 2). We then validated the equality of composite values and variances (step three) by verifying that the 95% confidence intervals included zero, which they did. Based on these criteria, we confirmed that the measurement between the two groups was invariant and thus excluded measurement variance as a potential cause of structural differences between the groups (Henseler et al., 2016b).

Finally, we conducted the PLS-MGA to compare the structural model between the proximate $( n = 1 6 4 )$ and the distal condition $( n ~ = ~ 1 6 7 )$ We relied on the established structural nomology of (Boss et al., 2015), which suggests that fear is a partial mediator of threat severity and threat susceptibility. This is the most recent and complete model of PMT. We estimated the model for both groups using bootstrapping with 5,000 samples (Hair et al., 2017). Our data did not include any missing values. The model for the proximate group explained 0.391 of the variance of subjects’ protection motivation and 0.115 of the variance of subjects’ behavior (both values were adjusted for the number of predictors in the model). The SRMR statistic for the proximate model was 0.065, which is below the recommended cutoff of 0.08 (Henseler et al., 2016a). Similarly, the model for the distal group explained 0.419 of the variance of subjects’ protection motivation and 0.171 of the variance of subjects’ behavior. The SRMR statistic for the distal model, 0.061, also indicated good model fit. Figure 3 shows the model for both groups.

We tested the difference between the models using a non-parametric test (Henseler, 2010) as recommended by Hair et al. (2017). Table 7 details the outcomes of this test. We found that the PMT models were similar insofar as threat severity and threat vulnerability yielded a significant positive effect on fear: fear led to a significant positive effect on protection motivation.

## 4.2.5 Study 1: Common Method Bias

We used several procedures to limit the influence of CMB. First, in the study design, we implemented procedural remedies that, according to research, mitigate the potential influence of CMB (Podsakoff et al., 2003). For example, we randomized the sequence of questions in the survey, used different response formats between criterion and predictor variables, and reassured the respondents regarding their anonymity. Because these remedies may not fully prevent CMB, we performed robustness analyses. Moreover, as Siemsen et al. show, interaction effects cannot be an artifact of CMB, because the bias introduced through a common method may only inflate the main effects, thereby deflating the competing interaction effects and making them harder to detect. Thus, we can be confident that our ANCOVA findings are not an artifact of CMB because the main findings (H2 and H3) belong to this type of (potentially deflated) interaction effect.

In the PLS analysis, we further used the measured latent marker variable (MLMV) approach to “partial out” potential CMB (Podsakoff et al., 2003). We followed the method of MLMV with a construct-level correction (Chin et al., 2013), which was developed because the unmeasured latent method construct approach has been discredited (Chin et al., 2012). We used resentment as a theoretically unrelated MLMV and measured it with four items. As Chin et al. (2013) demonstrated, a four-item MLMV can reduce CMB confounding structural path estimates by about 72%; it therefore represents a good trade-off between added instrument length and effectiveness. We are thus confident that our PLS analysis provided sound estimates that were largely free of bias.

## 5 Study 2

Study 1 showed some compelling results supporting that users’ responses to fear appeals are affected by the temporal immediacy of the action and can be better explained by PMT factors that inform proximate thinking. However, these findings were made in a specific empirical setting for which generalizability is unknown (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Thus, to strengthen the generalizability and usefulness of our contributions (Hubbard et al., 1998), our second study conceptually replicates Study 1. Conceptual replications are considered adequate for expanding the generalizability of findings because they retest existing hypotheses using different treatments, measures, analyses, and/or context (Dennis & Valacich, 2014). Such replications use a new method and context in order to extend an existing theory (Berthon et al., 2002). To that end, in Study 2, we vary the treatments and sampling context from Study 1. The next sections describe the details of that replication.

## 5.1 Study 2: Experimental Design

Since Study 2 aims at testing the same hypotheses as Study 1, we replicated the research design. Specifically, Study 2 also employs a 2 x 2 full factorial design, manipulating temporal distance and message arguments. Again, we also included a no-treatment control group, and thus had five groups in total.

Compared to Study 1, the first difference lies in the treatments administered. Whereas Study 1 tested our hypotheses using a fear appeal that targeted stimulating users’ motivations to install vulnerability scanners to protect against threats of home networks, we changed the fear appeals and targeted outcomes in Study 2. Specifically, in Study 2, we created fear appeals aimed at promoting the use of hard drive encryption to protect against the threat of unauthorized access. This combination of threat and targeted outcome is aligned with previous research that has already studied fear appeals featuring unauthorized access threats (Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2016). Thus, with the choice of this targeted outcome, we make our findings more relatable to prior literature. Analogous to Study 1, we manipulated message arguments using “why” and “how” arguments and manipulated temporal distance by either framing the response to be needed to be conducted immediately or in the future. Appendix D details our manipulations.

The second difference lies in the sampling. Unlike in Study 1, where we recruited participants via MTurk, in Study 2, we recruited participants from the online participant pool Prolific Academic. Unlike MTurk, Prolific Academic is not a crowdworking platform but an online subject pool. In a comparison with MTurk participants, Prolific Academic participants have been found to be more diverse, more naive, less dishonest, and produced comparable data quality (Peer et al., 2017). Consequently, Prolific Academic is used as a subject pool in various disciplines (cf. Palan & Schitter, 2018), including IS (e.g., Schneider et al., 2020).

The experiment was again hosted on Qualtrics and the procedure was as follows. First, we recruited participants from Prolific Academic offering US\$1.20 as compensation, resulting in an effective hourly pay of over US\$8, which meets the platform’s criteria for “good” compensation. We then randomly assigned participants to one of the five experimental groups. Next, participants received the manipulations for temporal distance and message arguments. Participants then answered manipulation checks, outcome intentions, followed by the main survey instrument and controls. Finally, participants were asked to provide demographic information.

For the main survey instrument, the measurements of Study 1 were used but adapted to fit the new threat and response context. In addition to the covariates measured in Study 1, we included further covariates that we expected to be relevant to participants’ responses to the treatments—namely, whether participants owned a laptop, whether they already used hard drive encryption and, if so, what type, as well as their daily laptop use (in hours) and how frequently they brought their laptop outside of their workplace or home office. Appendix D provides the full questionnaire.

## 5.2.3 Study 2: Measurement Validation

Next, we assessed the validity and reliability of our measurements. To that end, we estimated a measurement model using SmartPLS 3.0. Again, we dropped items with weak loadings, resulting in all constructs being measured by three items, except for maladaptive rewards, for which we dropped one item because of low loadings (Straub et al., 2004). After reestimating the model, all item levels were above the 0.5 threshold and significant (Hair et al., 2006). Except for maladaptive rewards, Cronbach’s alphas were above the 0.70 threshold (Gefen et al., 2000). For maladaptive rewards, Cronbach’s alpha was borderline, with a value of 0.66—an issue that resurfaced later in the multigroup analysis, leading us to drop this construct from the analysis. For all constructs, AVE was above the 0.50 threshold (Gefen et al., 2000), indicating convergent validity. Again, we assessed discriminant validity through the Fornell and Larcker (1981) criterion and found that for each construct, the square root of AVE was greater than its correlation values. Moreover, we estimated VIFs which were all below the recommended 3.0 threshold (Hair et al., 2017). Item-construct loadings and crossloadings were also higher on their designated constructs than on others (Henseler et al., 2016a). Appendix E presents construct validity statistics, as well as item loadings and cross-loadings. The estimated model presented good model fit, with a SRMR value of 0.069, below the recommended 0.080 threshold (Henseler et al., 2016a).

## 5.2.4 Study 2: Multigroup Analysis

To test hypotheses H1a-f, we conducted a multigroup analysis (PLS-MGA). Again, this necessitated the establishment of measurement invariance. Since we used the same measurement instrument for all groups, configural invariance is given (Henseler et al., 2016b). Compositional invariance was assessed via permutation analysis (see Appendix E). Again, all item-loading correlations were larger than their 5% quantile, indicating compositional invariance (Henseler et al., 2016b). Since the 95% confidence intervals of the group means included zero, the measurement indicated equality of composite values and variances. Overall, measurement invariance can thus be excluded as a potential cause of structural differences between the groups (Henseler et al., 2016b).

With measurement invariance established, we next conducted the PLS-MGA to estimate and compare structural models between the proximate $( n = 1 7 4 )$ and distal $( n ~ = ~ 1 6 7 )$ conditions. We followed the same procedure as in Study 1. However, it was here that we encountered issues with our measurement of the maladaptive reward construct. When examining the measurement model for each condition, we found that the Cronbach’s alpha and AVE values had respectively dropped to 0.63 and 0.40 in the distal condition. Thus, we were not confident in the validity of our measurement in this condition. Using a distinct set of indicators did not resolve this problem; thus, we refrained from including maladaptive rewards in further analysis. Consequently, H1d could not be tested in Study 2. With maladaptive rewards removed, the SRMR was 0.078 for the proximate condition and 0.072 for the distal condition, thus slightly worse than for the overall model but still below the threshold (Henseler et al., 2016a). The estimated models are depicted in Figure 6. Again, the estimated models controlled for CMB using the MLMV technique and construct-level correction (Chin et al., 2013).

We tested the differences between the models using a nonparametric test (see Table 11). The test showed that the models are different in substantial ways. First, analogous to Study 1, we found no evidence supporting a direct relationship between threat vulnerability and protection motivation, indicating no support for H1a. However, our results showed that self-efficacy and response cost yield significant effects on protection motivation in the proximate condition but not in the distal condition, indicating support for H1b and H1c. These results thus provide evidence reconfirming H1b and new evidence for H1c, which was insignificant in Study 1. Further, in terms of threat severity, the results showed similar effects in both groups, indicating no support for H1e. However, the effects of threat severity were stronger in the distal group, although the difference remained insignificant. Finally, in terms of response efficacy, the results showed a significant difference. Since the effect was only significant in the distal group, the results indicate support for H1f, thereby reconfirming the findings of Study 1. Thus, overall, the results of Study 2 provide evidence that reconfirms results from Study 1 (H1a, H1b, H1f), evidence that leads to new findings (H1c), and insufficient evidence to reconfirm findings from Study 1 (H1e).

## 5.2.5 Study 2: Message Arguments, Temporal Distance, and Fear-Appeal Efficacy

Next, we tested whether matching (H2) and mismatching (H3) message arguments affect fearappeal effectiveness. Following Study 1, we conducted an ANCOVA and introduced our manipulations as factors and PMT constructs as covariates (see Table 12). Our results only show evidence of a strong direct effect of temporal distance $( \mathfrak { p } \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . However, unlike in Study 1, the interaction effect between temporal distance and message arguments remained insignificant $( p = 0 . 7 6 5 )$ , thus not showing support for H2 and H3. In terms of covariates, only gender (β = - 0.486, p = 0.004) was significant.

## 6 Discussion

To address the conflicting ISec findings on which PMT factors drive protection motivation in the security context, we investigated the role of two novel factors: temporal distance and argument nature. We conducted two online experiments in which we manipulated temporal distance and argument nature. The results of the experiments showed that temporal distance moderates the effects of fear-appeal appraisal factors on fear-appeal outcomes (see Table 13). Specifically, we found evidence that supports our hypotheses that in proximate conditions, protection motivation is driven by self-efficacy (H1b), response cost (H1c), and maladaptive rewards (H1d) perceptions, whereas in distal conditions, protection motivation is driven by threat severity (H1e) and response efficacy (H1f) perceptions. Our experiment in Study 1 also showed that argument nature can influence fear-appeal outcomes. Here, we found that fear appeals were more effective when they used “how” arguments in proximate conditions (H2a). In contrast, “why” arguments in proximate conditions (H3a) led to lower levels of protection motivation. This demonstrates the importance of considering temporal distance and argument nature in crafting effective fear appeals.

## 6.1 Implications for Research

These findings help reconcile some of the conflicting findings in ISec fear-appeal research. As we have shown, our findings on temporal distance can help explain why previous studies might have reported contradictory findings on the significance of PMT factors such as selfefficacy (H1b), response cost (H1c), maladaptive rewards (H1d), threat severity (H1c), and response efficacy (H1d). This new knowledge sheds light on the controversial findings. For example, Menard et al. (2017) and Marett et al. (2011) found that self-efficacy did not predict protection motivation, whereas Johnston et al. (2015) found that self-efficacy significantly predicts protection motivation. Our study suggests that these contradictory findings could have been caused by different conditions of temporal distance: whereas Menard et al. (2017) and Marett et al. (2011) used distal conditions (i.e., perform a behavior in an unspecified time frame / the future), Johnston et al. (2015) used proximate conditions (i.e., perform it within one week).

Another example is Boss et al. (2015), who found that response efficacy is an insignificant predictor of the proximate outcomes used in their first study (e.g., perform a data backup next week) and a significant predictor of the distal outcomes used in their second study (e.g., use anti-malware in the next three months). Hence, with temporal distance, our study introduces a new variable to the ISec fear-appeal literature that can help explain the contradictory findings and effectively unify the current body of ISec research, which has failed to establish a consensus on why fear appeals are effective. Thus, this study can help the ISec fear-appeal literature form a more cumulative body of knowledge.

Our findings also suggest new ways of creating more effective messages. Whereas the existing literature has relied almost universally upon a single theory, PMT, to explain and predict fear-appeal outcomes (Wall & Buche, 2017), our study provides a new perspective by drawing from CLT in persuasion research. With this fresh theoretical angle, we proposed and validated the effect of two previously unconsidered variables: temporal distance and argument nature. As Study 1 shows, the interaction between these two variables can significantly improve (H2) or reduce (H3) the effectiveness of fear appeals. This suggests that future fear-appeal studies should consider these two factors when designing and examining fear-appeal treatments. Furthermore, matching temporal distance and message arguments can bring about substantial improvements in message effectiveness in proximate conditions. Because these conditions are especially relevant for security messages that require immediate responses (e.g., Please perform the following crucial update now!), this finding suggests important improvements of current message designs that have previously not been considered. However, the effects were not observed in Study 2. A potential explanation might lie in the behavioral context (i.e., hard drive encryption) in which the theory was tested in Study 2. As hard drive encryption software is now part of standard operating systems, users’ might have had fewer concerns about “how” to enable encryption than concerns about “why,” meaning that “how” messages were found to be equally persuasive in this context. Thus, much remains unknown and further research is needed to fully understand how matching argument nature and temporal distances can be leveraged for creating more effective security interventions.

We further add theoretical nuance to PMT. Whereas PMT has mostly differentiated between threat and response constructs, we use CLT to further distinguish between feasibility-related (e.g., self-efficacy, response cost) and desirability-related (e.g., threat severity, response efficacy) constructs. Because we show that depending on the temporal distance, users consider these constructs differently based on their judgment needs (judgments of feasibility or desirability), we identify when certain PMT constructs are needed to inform users’ decision-making. This may help researchers create more effective fear appeals by identifying the constructs on which fear appeals should focus (feasibility or desirability constructs). Future research will need to test whether such focused fear appeals will be more effective than lengthier, more general fear appeals that use arguments of a mixed nature. Because users often prefer not to read lengthy messages, such work may facilitate the improvement of the effectiveness of fear appeals. Based on CLT, this study provides a preliminary theoretical basis for such work.

Finally, we contribute to CLT research on persuasion (Chandran & Menon, 2004; Kim & Kim, 2017), which has focused largely on the temporal effects of threat distance. In this study, we shift the focus to temporal distance because it is the response that enables users to attain the desired end state (i.e., being safe and free from threats). Thus, our study aligns more closely with the original CLT notion that construals are goal driven (Fujita et al., 2008; Nan, 2007; Semin et al., 2005), and we provide evidence that the construed response behavior may influence message appraisal (H1b, H1d, H1e, H1f) and message effectiveness (H2a and H3a). Our findings contribute especially to the growing body of research on the cognitive outcomes of construals (Liberman et al., 2002; Liberman & Trope, 1998; Trope & Liberman, 2010) by showing that desirabilityfocused cognitions indeed predict distant motivations, whereas feasibility-focused cognitions predict proximate intentions.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

Our findings offer guidance to practitioners and regulators seeking to employ fear appeals in their ISec campaigns. Specifically, the findings suggest that “how” arguments should be used when targeting immediate responses, whereas “why” arguments should be used when targeting distal responses. This is an important finding because, as our results indicate, mismatching arguments with temporal distance may lead to weak messages. Such ineffective design undermines the effectiveness of the ISec function in organizations.

More specifically, to craft “how” and “why” messages, we recommend that practitioners follow the principle “show, don’t tell.” In constructing “how” messages for their target recipients, practitioners should focus on how the recipients should perform the targeted response by giving specific examples and instructions. In parallel, our findings indicate that practitioners should emphasize statements that stimulate self-efficacy and maladaptive rewards when targeting proximate responses and that they should emphasize statements that stimulate response efficacy and threat severity when targeting distal actions. Thus, our findings provide actionable guidance to practitioners on how to craft more effective messages.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without its limitations, which, however, point toward important future research opportunities. The first limitation relates to the security behaviors being studied. Since behaviors may differ in the degree to which they constitute commonsense behavior and in terms of how difficult they are to promote (Posey et al., 2013), these differences might influence the effects observed from fear appeal treatments on different behavioral outcomes. Thus, there is a good chance that the differences observed between our Study 1 and Study 2 findings are artifacts of such differences. However, across these two studies, we also observed a consistent pattern that suggests that temporal distance moderates the relationship of PMT variables in the hypothesized ways. Given that our findings are based on a strong theoretical foundation and largely confirmed by the evidence reported in our studies, one might expect that these findings could be replicated in the context of other, similar security behaviors. However, this expectation should not be assumed and must be validated through empirical testing (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Further research should thus explore the effects of temporal distance and argument nature in the context of other security-relevant behaviors.

Moreover, additional design factors need to be investigated. Although our findings provide guidance for practitioners and researchers on how to craft fear-appeal messages that motivate proximate or distal responses, we recognize that a wide range of other design decisions may influence the effectiveness of fear appeals. For example,

Johnston et al. (2019) showed that differences in rhetorical framing, such as “you” and “we,” can affect fear-appeal appraisal. Because recommendations on how to effectively design fear appeals transcend the ISec context, we encourage researchers to take this opportunity to make contributions to science beyond the field of IS. Along these lines, our findings could inform politicians who want to employ “why” messages that raise voting intentions in advance of voting day or software engineers who need to craft effective warning messages (i.e., “how” messages) that stimulate immediate action.

A final limitation relates to our use of PLS-SEM. While PLS-SEM has its detractors (see Sarstedt et al., 2016 for a discussion), we found it useful for estimating a complex model, such as PMT with nine latent variables (plus covariates) (Ringle et al., 2012) and its use helped us keep our analysis consistent with prior ISec studies that estimate PMT models and model variants (e.g., Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010; Johnston et al., 2015; Menard et al., 2018). Nonetheless, to eliminate the possibility that method artifacts influenced the analysis, future research should seek to further validate our findings using covariancebased SEM techniques.

## 7 Conclusion

The ISec literature is riddled with mixed findings on the efficacy of fear appeals in changing users’ securityrelated motivations. These mixed findings have fueled a debate about which factors are important in security contexts and have inhibited the development of a cumulative research tradition on how to create effective security interventions. It appears that because of an overemphasis on PMT, ISec research has overlooked alternative variables that explain the mixed findings of previous studies. Based on CLT, we theorize that temporal distance may account for the contradictory findings. We also theorize that when temporal distance matches argument nature, fearappeal effectiveness can be improved, whereas mismatches can make fear appeals virtually ineffective. Because we found evidence for these hypotheses, this study introduces new factors to the ISec discourse on fear appeals that can help reconcile some of the contradictory findings and also lead to more effective fear appeals.

## Appendix B: Study 1 Research Design

## Decision to Manipulate the Temporal Framing of the Response Instead of the Threat

Logically, threat and response are separate entities, and hence may occur at independent times. Hence, it could make sense to explore the effects of independently framing the occurrence of threat and response. However, we did not choose to follow this logic in this study. Rather, we take the recipient’s perspective, for which we argue that threat and response are inherently connected: The recommended response is evaluated considering the communicated threat. Thus, the time frame in which a response needs to be performed is delimited by the expected point in time in which the threat event will unfold. For example, when considering applying sunscreen, one would imagine doing that before going into the sun, which poses the threat of skin cancer, not after. Any recommended response will always need to take place before any threat event, as there would be otherwise no purpose of performing the response. Given that logic, we assert that the response time manipulation will infer a temporal threat distance, in that coping behaviors that need to be performed now suggest that the threat is imminent (temporally), whereas coping behaviors that are recommended to be performed within a longer time frame (e.g., three months) suggest that the threat is rather distal (temporally). Therefore, we choose to only manipulate the response time frame as we expect recipients to primarily assess their attitudes towards the response, and because the temporal threat distance will be implicit.

## Message-Level Manipulation Checks Using the Behavioral Identification Form (BIF) from Vallacher and Wegner (1989)

We would like to note here that the necessity for manipulation checks in research on persuasive messages is disputed. The argument is that if manipulations to the message itself lead to effects, then these effects can readily be observed in the message’s persuasiveness (O’Keefe, 2003). One example of this is Wright et al.’s (2014) study, which manipulates statements in phishing messages and observe theirs effect by measuring the likelihood of victims responding to these messages. Thus, the logic is that if the manipulations lead to the hypothesized effects, then they must be valid.

Although we concur with that logic, we would also add here that manipulation checks ensure that the manipulations unfolded their effects in the predicted fashion—and not in any other unexpected way. Hence, manipulation checks in persuasion research can help in ascertaining that the manipulation led to the expected effect in an expected way. In our study, we find evidence that the message-level manipulations of temporal framing of the response and message abstractedness interacted in a way that messages raised lower and higher levels of protection motivation (thus corroborating our hypotheses). The manipulation checks consequently help improve confidence that the messages were indeed regarded as more or less abstract, and that the temporal framing indeed made people feel that the responses were temporally more proximate and distal. Our gathered evidence supports this.

One could be even more confident in the presence of the hypothesized mechanisms if the mechanism itself could be observed. However, this has proven to be severely difficult with construal-level research. Consequently, construallevel theory research has not directly observed or directly measured construal levels. Rather, the presented evidence reports outcomes of manipulations that would support the construal-level hypotheses (cf. Trope & Liberman, 2010). Although some scholars have suggested that subjects’ construal level could be observed through the BIF, this index has been specified to measure individuals’ personality traits (Vallacher & Wegner, 1989), not outcomes of manipulations. Consequently, as expected, we could not find significant effects on BIF. Given that BIF is a trait variable, and our evidence supports our hypotheses which align with the evidence of several related studies, our manipulation checks validate the success of our manipulations. Finally, since existing construal-level research has abstained from using the BIF, we conclude that this is not an adequate measure to assess construal-level manipulations.

## Table D3. “How” Message

Your laptop’s hard drive stores valuable private information, such as your passwords, credit card details, private photos and videos, browsing history, and emails. This data is at risk of being stolen.

How your data is at risk of being stolen:

Anyone can easily gain access to your laptop when you leave it in public spaces, when it is lost or stolen, or when you sell it or return it.

If that happens and your hard drive is not encrypted, criminals can easily copy your data from your hard drive, without even knowing your login credentials.

Then, criminals can look through the copied data for sensitive information that can be used for fraud (identity theft) or blackmail (extorting cheating husbands, selling back private and/or work data).

To protect your data, we recommended that you encrypt your laptop’s hard drive.

How you can encrypt your laptop’s hard drive:

• Set up freely available encryption software that encrypts all your data with a password.

• This password will only be required when booting up your machine.

• Without that password, your data will be extremely difficult to decrypt, even when it has been copied.

Daniel A. Pienta is an assistant professor at Baylor University. He received his PhD in information systems from Clemson University. His primary areas of research are behavioral and technical cybersecurity. He worked as managing director at a cybersecurity and due diligence consulting firm, specialized in servicing some of the largest commercial lending institutions in the United States. He has extensive experience in penetration testing, information system design, development, and user experience. Jason Bennett Thatcher holds the Milton F. Stauffer Professorship in the Department of Management Information Systems at the Fox School of Business of Temple University. Dr. Thatcher’s research examines the influence of individual beliefs and characteristics on technology use, cybersecurity, and IT human resource management in organizations. His work appears in the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Applied Psychology, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes. He currently serves as senior editor at Journal of the Association for Information Systems and Information Systems Research. He has served as senior editor at MIS Quarterly. His current projects are tutoring high school algebra, overcoming his irrational fear of trains, and mastering fusion Pad Thai.
