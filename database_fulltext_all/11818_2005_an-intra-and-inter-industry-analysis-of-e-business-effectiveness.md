# An intra- and inter-industry analysis of e-business effectiveness

Luvai Motiwalla <sup>\*</sup>, M. Riaz Khan, Shenghan Xu

College of Management, University of Massachusetts Lowell, One University Avenue, Lowell, MA 01854, USA

Available online 21 January 2005

## Abstract

Electronic business (EB) on the Internet has been promoted as a revolutionary technology that will transform the way we do business. This paper presents an intra- and inter-industry financial performance (FP) analysis of three industries: retail (R), consumer products (CP), and food beverages and tobacco (FBT). Financial statements of 165 companies selected from these three industries extending over a period of 10 years were reviewed to determine whether the EB activities have any significant impact on their FP, and whether this impact varied across industries. This comparative examination indicated that the EB companies did better than their non-EB counterparts in the post-EB period and the FBT industrial sector performed better than the R and CP sectors.

Keywords: IT investment; Electronic commerce or electronic business impact; Corporate performance; Financial impact

## 1. Introduction

The Internet (or World Wide Web) has experienced a phenomenal growth in attracting people and commerce activities over the last decade. Expanding from a few thousand people in 1993 to more than 150 million in 1999, the Web was expected to attract over one billion by 2004 [1]. This growth attracted a variety of organizations to take advantage of the Web-based economic model, which offers opportunities for internal efficiencies and external growth via electronic business (EB) activities. For the purposes of this study, EB activities were considered to be a simple implementation of Internet website or a more complex implementation, such as business-to-consumer (B2C), business-to-business (B2B), consumer-to-consumer (C2C), or Intranet and/or Extranet applications like Web-CRM, Web-ERP, etc. This broad definition allowed us to group the companies with varying degrees of EB activities and conduct financial performance analysis at the aggregate level rather than focusing on individual firms. Firm level performance is often influenced by good management and leadership styles [7] or the quality of EB implementation. Our goal was to determine the broad influence of EB on corporations and industries, not on a specific company’s performance.

According to the Census Bureau of the U.S. Department of Commerce [21], the retail e-commerce sales in the third quarter of 2000 was over \$ 6 billion, up by 15.3% from the second quarter and more than 2.5 times the 1998 holiday Internet sales. Internet retail sales increased more than 66% to \$ 44.5 billion in 2000 despite the demise of many dot-com companies in the slowing economy. According to Porter [20] the Internet made revolutionary changes to the fundamental business model. For example, Hoffman and Novak [11] found many researchers and business practitioners claiming that the Internet ‘‘will change the way we do business’’ and make a dramatic impact on performances of companies in many industries. However, many investors and executives felt that they were duped into investing millions of dollars more than they needed. Consequently, they do not want to invest more on EB projects of dubious merit. This does not mean that investors are reluctant to make strategic technology investments, but, it does mean closer monitoring of investment in new EB activities.

Nonetheless, the Web-based economic model is much more efficient at the transaction cost level [28]. For example, the traditional cost of processing an airline ticket is 8 \$ but is only 1 \$ through the Web. Similarly, other efficiencies can be derived from marketing and advertisements. Elimination of the middleman in the distribution channel (disintermediation) also can have a big impact on the market efficiency [19]. Other efficiencies are generated by smaller or no inventory, resulting in smaller storage or real-estate space, a larger customer base, and 24 h access for all 7 days per week with no additional cost [26]. Marketing on the Web can result in additional unit sales at very low unit cost. In addition, it also enables higher level of customization to the needs of individual clients [6]. Auto manufacturers, such as Ford and GM, are experimenting with custom designed cars that can be delivered in less than two weeks at a customer’s home [27]. Thus, web-enabling business processes is attractive in the new economy where product life cycles are short and efficient and the market for products and services is global. Similarly, management expects a better financial performance.

EB also allows organizations to expand their business reach. One key benefit of the Web is access to and from global markets [8]. The Web eliminates several geographical barriers for a corporation that wants to conduct global commerce. But political, trade, and cultural barriers may still make this difficult. While traditional commerce relied on value-added networks (VANs) or private networks, which were expensive and provided limited connectivity [22], the Web makes e-commerce cheaper. Corporations can produce goods anywhere and deliver them electronically or physically via couriers. This enables organization the flexibility to expand into different product lines and markets quickly, with low investments. Secondly, full time availability, better communication with customers, and sharing of the organizational knowledgebase allows the organization to provide better customer service. This translates to better customer retention rates and repeat orders. Finally, the rich interactive media and database technology of the Web allows for unconstrained awareness, visibility, and opportunity for an organization to promote its products and services [24]. This enhances an organizations’ ability to attract new customers, increasing their overall markets and profitability. Despite the recent dot-com failures [10], EB has made tremendous in-roads in traditional corporations. Forrester research found that 90% of the firms they surveyed plan to conduct some e-commerce, B2C, or B2B, and it predicts that EB transactions will rise to about \$ 6.9 trillion by 2004. However, there are no macro-economic studies that demonstrate whether the companies in general can benefit from EB.

This paper introduces an exploratory measurement model to study the impact of EB activities on financial performance and to conduct an intra- and interindustry level analysis. The model was evaluated by using financial data from 165 companies in the years 1991–2000; the companies were grouped and analyzed in a comparative analysis. Eighty three of these companies implemented EB activities from 1996. The companies were not pure ‘‘click’’ enterprises, like Amazon.com, e-Bay, or Yahoo, because we were interested on the financial impact of EB. Also, we needed pre-EB activities data, which was not available for pure click companies. Instead, they were traditional brick corporations, such as AutoZone, Fastenal, and others that had decided to compete in the new economy with EB activities and the so-called, brick & click companies. Specifically, our objective was to determine whether the financial data support the claims that EB has boosted the bottom-line performance.

## 2. A financial performance model for e-business

## 2.1. Measuring financial performance

The contemporary IT evaluation approach began in the 1980s [9]; it focused on return on investment (ROI) and return on sales (ROS) and relied on quantitative assessment of IT costs, benefits, and risk during the systems development life cycle, with very few post implementation evaluation [15]. There were inherent problems to the cost–benefits approach; e.g., in quantifying future savings, assessing the cost of large overruns, making arbitrary estimates of systems life, risk, and inflation, and in assessing the effect of the erosion of executives’ confidence [17]. This led to studies that focused on intangible costs-benefits, longterm ROI studies or ROE, and the productivity paradox [4]—which questioned why IT investment had not shown significant impact on worker productivity. Rai et al. [23] suggested that while IT improves organizational efficiency, its influence on administrative productivity and business performance depends on other organizational factors such as leadership and corporate strategy. The difficulty of dealing with intangibles, narrow focus, or using traditional productivity measurements led to innovative approaches for measuring the influence of IT investments. In addition, organizations had not kept accurate records on IT costs-benefit and in many cases did not want to reveal any data for security and competitive reasons [3].

James Collins performed a qualitative study of 28 corporations in his search for reasons on why some companies have outperformed their competition. The independent measures of his study were leadership, technology, and strategy, while the dependent measure was ratio of cumulative stock returns to general market returns similar to ROE. He found that 11 of the 28 good-to-great organizations had flexible leaders with personal humility and professional will; they placed the right people on their teams very early, did not deviate from their vision regardless of any difficulties, provided an environment of culture of discipline, used IT to accelerate the quality of their core products and services but never for revolutionary business process transformations, and their corporate strategy was of sustained evolutionary change on an ongoing basis.

Kivija¨rvi and Saarinen’s study [14] looked at 200 Finnish firms across various industries over a 2-year time period. They collected data on three major (dependent) variables—profitability, funding position, and growth—with eight sub-variables. Growth included net-sales, growth rate, and personnel growth rate ratios, profitability comprised of gross margin, net-income, and ROI, and funding consisted of equity to total assets and debt to net-sales ratios. The independent variables were organization size, as measured by means of net revenues and number of employees, organization type, which was determined by the industry and the ratio of office workers (admin staff) to all employees, and organizational IT investment strategies, which were indirectly determined through the financial analysis of IS cost vis-a\`- vis growth, profitability, and financial position of the firm. The study found that return on IT investments depend on breadth of IS applications and maturity of its use, which suggested that investment in IT must be broad-based and continued over some time. Shortterm measurement cycles and narrow focus may not show healthy ROI.

Bryd and Marshall [2] studied the financial performance of 350 companies over a 4-year period. Using a structured equation-modeling method they were able to find causal relationships between the independent variable IT investments, as measured by number of PCs/terminals as percentage of total employee, amount spent on IT staff training, IT budget as percent of total revenue, percent of IT budget spent on IT staff, value of organization IT as percent of revenue, and percent of IT budget spent on IT staff training. The organizational performance or dependent variables were ROS, ROI, sales by employee and by total assets, and market to book value. These data were standardized by industry to control the inter-industry variance [5]. They found that there was a positive relationships between number of PCs and sales by employee, but the amount spent on IT staff training and the value of organizational IT had negative relationship with sales by assets. Also, the relative IT budget had a negative relationship with sales.

Rai et al. [23] examined the relationships between IT investment and organizational performance of 497 companies. The three IT investment variables were the three budgets for: aggregate IT, client/server systems, and IT infrastructure. The dependent variables on organizational performance were total sales, return on assets (ROA), and ROE. Intermediate performance variables were labor and administrative productivity. The results indicated that while there is a positive relationship between IT investment and ROA there was no relationship with ROE. This suggests that ROE may not be a proper measure for judging IT investment. While large IT investment can increase a firm’s sales and lower costs, it does not guarantee better equity performance ROE. Further, investment in client/server and infrastructure had a positive impact on labor productivity but not on administrative productivity.

Li and Ye [18] focused on the moderating effects of the firms’ environment, strategy, and CEO/CIO relationship on the impact of IT investment and organizational performance. The three IT investment or independent variables were environmental dynamism, a regressed sales revenue index over 5 years by industry, strategic orientation, a ratio of marketing and R&D expenses over total capital expenses, and IT-strategy integration, and the reported relationship between the CEO and CIO. The organizational performance or dependent variables were ROA, namely, net income divided by total assets, and ROS, namely, net income divided by total sales. The study also had three control variables: debt-to-equity ratio, environmental munificence (regressed industry revenue numbers against time), and firm size in number of employees. Their sample of 216 companies over a 3-year time period found a positive relationship between IT investments and ROA and ROS in firms with a dynamic environment a proactive externally oriented firm strategy, and closer ties between CEO/CIO.

Thus, there was no consistent approach for measuring the impact of IT on financial performance. While some studies have measured IT influence at the firm level, others have measured it at the group level.

## 2.2. Financial performance evaluation model

The studies helped us in framing our FP evaluation model. We used some of the same performance measures or dependent variables, such as ROI, ROA, ROS, ROE and revenue growth. However, based on the recommendation from the Rai et. al. study, we did not use ROE ratios. These ratios measured the financial performance of a company and were influenced by its business strategies. The impact of EB activities on, for example, sales revenues, cost of producing goods or services, global sales, and quality of services have been identified and reflected in income and ROI from previous studies. An analysis of these revealed that the independent measures can be classified into measuring either operational performance [2] or profitability and growth. These two measures in turn impact the overall financial performance, as shown in Fig. 1.

The financial health ratios allow the identification of EB impact on the internal operational efficiency [2], whereas, the growth ratios are indicative of the external market growth of the firm. EB activities are expected to enhance the internal efficiency and, at the same time, extend the reach of company’s products in global markets. These ratios, as dependent variables, are sensitive to the company’s financial performance. On the other hand, the EB activities, as an independent variable, would be expected to favorably influence the ratios. However, the degree of emphasis that a company places on a product or service ultimately leads it to outshine its competitors and drives its economic engine.

A comparison between the values of these ratios before and after the adoption of any new business strategy (like EB) may provide an indication of their effectiveness. Similarly, a comparison between the ratios of companies in EB and non-EB (nEB) groups during the same time period could allow us to attribute the observed variation, barring any other major shifts in the business environment, to such activities. Thus, the initiation of EB activity is treated as a strategic event whose impact on the financial performance is classified as favorable (F), unfavorable (UF), or neutral (N). An improvement in a ratio is favorable, a decline is unfavorable, and no change is neutral. The comparative FP model, as shown in Fig. 2, was formulated to identify the impact of EB activities across the three industries included in this study and within each industry by examining:

FP-I Financial performance of EB companies in the pre-EB period

FP-II Financial performance of EB companies in the post-EB period

FP-III Financial performance of nEB companies in the pre-EB period

FP-IV Financial performance of nEB companies in the post-EB period

Arguably, there may have been other factors that influenced the performance of the companies. It is almost impossible, however, to isolate them with any degree of certainty at an aggregate level without detailed data on the specific business environment in which each company operated. Also, the proposed model was designed to implicitly neutralize or minimize such effects. This was accomplished by analyzing the longitudinal financial data aggregated across many companies operating under the same general economic conditions.

The companies were placed in one of two groups: one had adopted the EB activities and the other that had not. Further, two time intervals were defined as pre-EB and post-EB periods. The financial performance of each group was studied separately over each time period. This four-way performance grid, in which the EB companies were essentially treated as a control group during the post-EB period, set the stage for a four-way comparison between EB and non-EB groups and within each over the two periods.

## 3. Methodology

Our purpose in this study was to assess, at the group (or industry) level, the impact of EB activities on the financial performance by reviewing the movements,

P1 (FP-II > FP-I)

P2 (FP-II > FP-IV)

The performance of EB companies is better than nEB companies in the post-EB period; that is, the EB impact is favorable

P3 (FP-III = FP-IV)

The performance of nEB companies in the post-EB period is not different from their performance in the pre-EB period; that is, the EB impact is neutral

P4 (FP-II <sup>S</sup> FP-IV) > (FP-I <sup>S</sup> FP-I)

The difference between the performance of EB and nEB companies is greater in the post-EB period than the similar difference in the pre-EB period

The intra-industry propositions were based on the assumption that EB activities had, in the long run, a favorable impact on the performance of the companies through higher net income, gross profit, inventory turnover, sales, and lower operational costs. Therefore, following the EB activities, each industry and all of them as a group should have experienced an overall improvement. if the data supported Propositions P1–P4, then a plausible explanation for the observed change in the performance of the EB group during the post-EB period could be the impact of EB activities.

over a period of 10 years, in selected financial ratios, averaged by the EB and nEB groups in each industry. The grouping dissipated some of the fuzziness from the performance concept which could be problematic, no matter how accurate the measures. Also, it appeared reasonable to assume that companies within each industry category would be at varying degrees of EB activities and implementation; no attempt was be made, however, to differentiate between these stages because the analysis was conducted at the group level.

For similar reasons, the company size with an industry was not considered pertinent to the study.

Also, the year of website registration was used as the delineating point between pre-EB and post-EB periods. This does not mean that companies with just a website domain registration were selected as EB companies. In fact, we investigated all the companies’ website to assure their EB activities met the criteria we had laid out in our definition of EB.

The implementation of EB activities was determined by using a three-step process. First, based on their SIC code for the three industries a little more than 300 companies were identified from Hoovers Online<sup>1</sup> database service, which provides profiles of Fortune 1000 companies and the company’s website address (URL). Second, the URL for each was checked by the WHOIS<sup>TM</sup> function.<sup>2</sup> Finally, each website was visited to ascertain the level of EB implementation and other company characteristics.

A review of these profiles revealed that the most frequently observed year of EB implementation was either late 1995 or early 1996. Thus, 1995–96 was used as a demarcating point. The periods before and after this cut-off year are defined as Pre-EB and Post-EB periods. This allowed us to review data over a 5-year post-EB period leading up to 2000. Consistent with this for comparison, data was also reviewed over the pre-EB period for years, tracing back to 1991. In the data collection process, companies in the focus industries were filtered to remove those which did not have comparable financial statements available from 1991 through 2000. This resulted in the selection of a total of 165 companies.

The WHOIS<sup>TM</sup> function from the Network Solutions website provided details on the creation of the website domain, when it was last updated, and the company contact information. The domain creation date was used as the year of initiation. The companies included in our analysis were found to be at varying stages of EB implementation. However, when aggregated, they appeared to be at a similar level. A classification of companies by implementation phase was not included as it was not our focus at the group level. The companies were of different sizes in terms of their sales revenue as shown in Table 1.

The companies that went online with their website in 1995–1996 were defined as EB companies, while those that registered their domains in June 1999 or later were nEB. The historical (1991–2000) financial performance data, such as sales, cost of goods sold (CGS), operating costs, income, cash, inventory, current assets and liabilities were collected through various sources, including FIS Online,<sup>3</sup> and the individual company’s website.

In terms of net income, P1 is confined in the Retail and FBT industries and for all industries combined. The movements in other areas within each industry and as a group are mixed. The sales growth provides the most consistent results against Proposition P1. The spread of data in some of the areas, as indicated by large S.D. values noted within parentheses, is wide.

Similar to EB companies, all nEB companies are also classified by industry with their 10-year financial performance in the post- and pre-EB periods. ## 4.2. Test of significance

The analysis based on the available data suggests that the companies that have adopted EB measures have improved their financial performance. The degree of this impact, however, varies across industries as well as within each industry. Whether the observed impact is statistically significant is a question to be addressed next. Three null hypotheses were formulated for this purpose. These were tested by means of ANOVA at $\alpha = 0 . 0 5 \mathrm { : }$

$\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } ; \mathrm { M } \mathrm { d } _ { 1 } = 0$ No difference exists in the key financial measures between EB and nEB companies during post-EB period, where $\mathrm { M d } _ { 1 }$ is the mean difference

${ \mathrm { H } } _ { 0 } \colon { \mathrm { M } } { \mathrm { d } } _ { 2 } = 0$ No difference exists in the key financial measures of EB companies in each industry and across industries between the two periods; where $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { d } _ { 2 }$ is the mean difference

$\mathrm { H } _ { 0 } \colon \mathrm { M } \mathrm { d } _ { 3 } = 0$ No difference exists in the key financial ratios of EB companies within each industry and across industries between the two periods; where $\mathrm { M d } _ { 3 }$ is the mean difference

## 5. Discussion

Although the evidence was inconclusive, our research model allowed us to test our propositions, which were based on a review of e-business literature. Proposition P1 stipulates that the FP of all companies with EB activities would in general be favorable during the post-EB period. The results were somewhat mixed: the biggest impact seems to be on the internal operational efficiencies. On the other hand, unfavorable sales growth ratios suggest EB had some negative impact.

For Proposition P3, the results were again somewhat mixed. In this case, for nEB companies, one growth ratio was favorable and the other unfavorable, but the health ratios, except for gross profit, were unfavorable. Only the current ratio was neutral This suggests that these companies had no short-term technology disruptions but they were not very efficient in their operations. However, only a minority of the companies in each industry sector, see Table 12, have implemented online transaction processing capabil ities (such as B2C or B2B) on their websites. The economic/financial benefits are derived primarily when companies offload their business processes to the Web environment.

The most interesting results are for P2 and P4. Here, the EB companies performed better than the nEB companies in the post-EB period. In the financial health ratios, out of the six, five ratios are favorable and only the inventory/CGS is neutral. The most impressive gains EB companies have made over the nEB group is in the area of gross profit by a margin of 24%. In the growth area, the sales growth is even, but the pre-tax income growth for the EB group has exceeded its counterpart by as much as 22% in the post-EB period. Overall, data suggests that the EB activities did have some favorable impact on the performance of the EB companies. These results provide some basis for management of these companies to believe that there is some value to the brick and click approach widely used by many traditional commerce companies.

A key limitation of this study is that it could not be possible to identify or control other factors that might have had some impact on the financial performance of companies; but an attempt has been made to minimize their influence, however. Some aspects include limitations of historical data collection, using websites like Free Edgar, FIS Online, and Hoovers Online, which do not always provide accurate historical data. However, since the same data source was used for both parts of the sample, the bias in the overall results is expected to be minimal. Nevertheless, the results of EB investments should be reflected in the financial statements of the companies.

## 6. Conclusion

A financial performance analysis of 165 companies over a period of 10 years, as a group and by industry, partially supported the view that EB activities have had favorable impact on the macro performance of the industries. Based on the sample data, no conclusions can be drawn at the firm level. The proposed model may be suitable for measuring the financial impact of EB activities at the group level. By comparing the post-EB performance with the pre-EB performance of the industry, the direction and the magnitude of the impact of such activities on the end result of business functions was determined. Further, by contrasting the performances of EB companies against nEB, the findings reinforced our belief that the EB companies have benefited from innovation and strategic integration, though this was not shown to be statistically significant.

While the EB initiatives appear to have had some beneficial impact on business performance, the results have apparently been exaggerated in the e-business literature. If any, the benefits are neither substantial nor similar across an industry or even within a company.

Despite these ambiguous results, companies would be well advised to pursue a strategy that includes an EB activity. It is beneficial to enter this arena to remain competitive. This mode of conducting transactions will become just as essential to business as telephones and fax machines were. However, some companies in the industry will always do better than others due to innovative leadership, recruitment, retention, disciplined culture and constant innovation, among other elements.
