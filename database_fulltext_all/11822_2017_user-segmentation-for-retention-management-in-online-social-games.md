## A B S T R A C T

This work proposes an innovative model for segmenting online players based on data related to their in game behaviours to support player retention management. This kind of analysis is helpful to explore the potential reasons behind why players leave the game, analyse retention trends, design customised strategies for different player segments, and then boost the overall retention rate. In particular, a new similarity metric which is driven by players’ stickiness to the game is developed to cluster players. Three feature dimensions, namely engagement features (e.g., log-in frequency and length of log-in time), performance features (e.g., level, the number of completed tasks, coins and achievements), and social interactions features (e.g., the number of in-game friends, whether or not to join a guild, and the guild role), are employed and aggregated to derive the stickiness metric. The applicability and utility of this new segmentation model are illustrated through experiments that are conducted on a realistic MMORPG dataset. The derived results are also discussed and compared against two benchmark models. The results reveal that the new segmentation model not only achieves better clustering performance, but also improves player’s lifetime prediction by better distinguishing between loyal customers and churners. The empirical results confirm the effects of social interaction, which is usually underestimated in the current research, on player segmentation. From an operational perspective, the derived results would help game developers better understand the different retention-behaviour patterns of players, establish effective and customised tactics to retain more players, and boost product revenue.

## 1. Introduction

With the development and growing popularity of online social games, game data mining has become an increasingly significant topic for researchers, game designers, and game developers. Mining in-game data provides a meaningful way for understanding players and their behaviours, preferences, interests, and game life cycles. This knowledge is beneficial for optimising the game design, improving player experience, and maximising game revenue. Although there is a wealth of information hidden in game telemetry data, discovering and extracting useful knowledge and behavioural patterns are not easy tasks. Because the scale of game telemetry data increases exponentially, and the number of variables related to player behaviour analysis are often very large. Some research has been conducted in this area to address questions regarding the classification and clustering of players, player lifetime predictions, and in-game social network analysis. However, scant research has been done on player retention management for online social games.

Retaining players has been considered one of the most critical challenges in online social games. The retention rate is one of the key performance indicator (KPI) used in the gaming industry to evaluate the success of a game. Normally, to attract new players, game operators spend enormous amounts of effort and money to advertise and promote the game before its oficial launch. It has been well-recognised that acquiring new customers can be much more expensive than retaining existing ones [24]. On the other hand, churners may create negative impacts through unfavourable wordof-mouth reports, and this may result in a further loss of potential and existing players. Effective retention analysis and management can help understand the behaviours of players and reveal the diverse influential factors that cause player attrition, ranging from personal commitment, competing products and services, or shifting interests to social influences. Different players may leave the game for different reasons. By understanding such reasons, game producers can design and implement customised retention campaigns targeted to different player segments.

Besides having important managerial consequences, retention management of online social games is also of great theoretical importance. Currently, conducting retention analyses in online social games is still at its early stage. Compared to other application domains, one of the most characteristic features of online social games is that it often has a relatively low retention rate [8]. For this reason, the class distribution of churners and non-churners is typically very skewed. The existing work of game retention analysis mainly focuses on player churn predictions [24] and player lifetime predictions [2,45]. For player churn predictions, because of the skewed class distributions, it is more challenging to employ conventional data mining techniques to build useful predictive models, and the high classification accuracy does not necessarily capture the true picture of player churn. Also, the current classification model only segments the players into churn and non-churn groups. In reality, the players’ churn behaviour can be more complicated. Different types of players leave the game at different time points and for various reasons. The conventional classification models are incapable of detecting various churn behaviour patterns. Therefore, it is challenging to draw insightful managerial implications for game operators from the classification prediction results. For player lifetime predictions, the approach focuses on the retention behaviour of individual players because it predicts when a certain player will leave the game. From an operational point of view, it is impractical and ineficient to design customised marketing strategies at the individual level. In practice, game operators often employ a general model to analyse the retention behaviours of all players. However, because churners constitute the majority of a social game’s player population, their retention behaviours would dominate the analysis if a general model were to be used. This makes it dificult for game operators to analyse the different reasons behind churn and then conduct retention campaigns targeted to prevent different types of churn. In addition, in the gaming industry, a popular way of conducting a retention analysis is to calculate game retention rates at some key time points (e.g., Day-1, Day-7, and Day-28), using such isolated data points makes it dificult to depict the true picture of players’ retention behaviour.

To complement the existing literature on retention management for online social games and to do so without creating a general retention model or focusing on individual players, the current study proposes an innovative player segmentation model to support retention management for online social games. The characteristics of retention management in online social games are analysed, and a new metric, termed stickiness, which integrates game engagement, in-game performance, and social interaction feature dimensions, is proposed to cluster players. Extant research only considers the engagement- and performance-related features in clustering players, and the merits of incorporating social interaction attributes in player retention management have not been studied thoroughly. This research overcomes this limitation by proposing a stickiness-based fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering algorithm that is used to segment players into five different groups from the given dataset. Each player segment has its own characteristics, and the captured retention behaviours are different. Hence, precise retention management strategies can be made accordingly, and precious resources can be devoted to the right player segments to prevent player churn and boost game revenue. In addition, rather than calculating isolated retention rates at certain time points, this approach monitors the retention trends of different player segments continuously over time. This is beneficial for understanding different types of retention behaviours. To further validate this, the proposed model is ompared against two benchmark models, and the results reveal that the new model not only achieves better clustering performance, but also improves players’ lifetime predictions by better distinguishing between loyal customers and churners.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 reviews the related existing works of customer retention and the segmentation of online gamers. In Section 3, a new approach is proposed to manage the retention of players of online social games. Through an empirical study, the effectiveness and utility of the proposed framework is demonstrated in Section 4. The obtained results are analysed and discussed in Section 5. The final section gives a conclusion and indicates future research directions.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Customer retention

Customer retention is a core dimension of customer relationship management (CRM), and it is gaining particular attention from companies in today’s competitive environment. In general, it is assumed that there is a strong association between customer retention and company profitability. The longer a customer stays with a company, the more loyal and valuable that customer becomes. Long-term customers are beneficial to the company in several ways. For example, the cost to serve long-term customers is usually less than serving new ones, and long-term customers can bring in new customers and are usually less sensitive to price differences [6]. Customer retention has been widely studied in several domains, such as insurance, banking [2], online gambling [7,22], retailing [34], telecommunications [6,43,49], service provider-hosted online communities [23], and other areas. Customer retention consists of three stages:

• Stage 1: analyse the important determinants of customer retention

• Stage 2: apply the determinants to predict customer retention (including customer churn and customers’ lifetime)

• Stage 3: design and implement policies to improve customer retention and prevent customer attrition.

Most of the existing studies on customer retention mainly focus on Stage 1 (e.g., [22,23,26,37,45]) and Stage 2 (e.g., [2,6,7,24,25,34,43,49]). However, little research has been conducted on Stage 3, such as applying the resultant analysis that is drawn from Stage 1 and Stage 2 to improve customer retention. Kim and Moon [25] claimed that predicting churn probability is not suficient to build optimal retention management models, and the probability of retention and expected revenues from target customers should also be taken into account. In addition, Chu et al. [6] proposed a hybrid data mining model for customer retention in the telecommunication domain. Unlike previous research, the proposed model in [6] not only predicts possible churners, but also proposes proactive policies to retain these customers. In existing literature, a variety of retention-analysis techniques have been developed for detecting important determinants of customer retention and for predicting potential churners. However, conducting retention analyses in online social games is still in its infancy.

## 2.1.1. The determinants of customer retention

It is well-recognised that customer retention can be affected by a number of factors; therefore, identifying and analysing the important determinants are crucial to improve customer retention. For example, although competitive pricing may typically drive churn for price-sensitive customers, factors such as service quality, conversion cost, and social ties can also cause customer defection. In addition, the influential factors can be quite diverse in different application domains. For example, Jolley et al. [22] conducted an online gambling experiment to reveal that habit, rather than satisfaction, had a strong impact on customer retention. Weber et al. [45] employed regression models for identifying which gameplay factors had the most significant impact on gamer retention. Kwon and Kim [26] conducted an experiment to explore the effects of four personalisation dimensions (namely, subject, object, level, and learning method) on customer retention.

Several previous studies have showed that customer loyalty has emerged as a strong predictor of customer retention [26]. Customer stickiness is also an important metric of user loyalty. This metric has been widely discussed in the context of e-commerce [9] and social media [21]. For example, A Web site is considered to be sticky to a user when (s)he visits it frequently, spends more time exploring, and digs deeper into the content than other users do [21]. Accordingly, stickiness is employed to represent not only the ability of a Web site to elicit repeat visits, but also the its ability to attract and retain visitors [27]. In general, three indicators have been used to measure stickiness: duration, frequency, and depth [28] although the first two have been suggested more frequently. Duration (or session stickiness) refers to the amount of time a visitor spends on a website during a visiting session, frequency refers to the number of repeat visits by a person over a specific time period, and depth refers to the extent to which a user explores and uses a website. Empirical studies have revealed the relationship between stickiness and diverse positive outcomes [35,46]. For instance, Moe and Fader [35] discussed the significant impact stickiness has on company revenue. Wu et al. [46] stated that stickiness is observed when customers intend to repurchase certain products or services from the same store. The more a site is revisited, the more the site is being used and reviewed. Stickiness not only affects the bottom line of etailing, but also social networking sites. Hsu et al. [21] found out that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between perceived information accessibility and the stickiness of a microblog. Their research also indicated that the negative impact of information overload on stickiness can be reduced by increasing the sense of community.

Recently, more researchers are getting interested in exploring the effects and strengths of social ties in customer retention. For example, Kawale et al. [24] proposed a predictive model of player churn that considers both the social influence of gamers and their engagement of a game. Verbeke et al. [43] developed a classification model that incorporated social network information to predict the probability of customer churn, and the predictive model was tested on a large-scale telecom dataset. Kaur [23] found out that self-eficacy, hedonic motivation, reciprocal benefit, and social influence have a positive impact on teenagers’ intention to continue participating in Facebook-based brand communities. In online social games, communities often exist in the form of guilds that are voluntarily organised by players [19]. Guilds provide a social environment that allows their members to make friends, access within-guild resources, share in-game information, and form groups to accomplish tough quests and complete dungeons. With these advantages, many players choose to join a guild to participate in community activities, build social relationships with other guild members, and obtain social support. Social network analysis (SNA) techniques have been used to explore guild properties (e.g., size, density, centrality, and reciprocity) in MMORPGs. For example, the work of [19] applied the social capital theory to investigate how a player’s guild centrality affects his or her game retention behaviour. An extension work [20] further examined the moderating effects of game knowledge and community size based on the effectiveness of social capital. However, in the work of [19,20], the data were collected through an online survey, the guild properties (i.e., centrality and community position) were measured and provided by participants, and this was subjective information.

Despite the salient beliefs about the business value of stickiness, empirical evidence of player stickiness in online gaming is surprisingly limited. Although conventional wisdom tells that stickiness should be equally important in online games and should greatly impact player retention, research on design factors leading to player stickiness and strategies to increase stickiness in online games remains limited. Having recognising the importances of communities in online social games, this study aims to propose a new retention management model that considers social ties when defining user stickiness to fill the above research gap.

## 2.1.2. Prediction of customer retention

Based on the important determinants of customer retention, it becomes possible to predict customers’ activity and churn. Churn prediction is an important problem in customer retention, and it has been studied extensively in various domains. Customers are labelled as churners and non-churners, and various prediction models that employ different techniques are developed to predict customer churn [6,7,34]. For example, the work of [7] demonstrated that an ensemble classification approach outperformed standalone prediction models when predicting customer churn in online gambling. Zhang et al. [49] proposed a new churn prediction model that is based on information propagation and interpersonal influence, and the impact on the performance of customer churn prediction was also investigated and evaluated. The work of [34] proposed a new partial-churn predictive model for retailing, and considered both the state of trust and demand maturity of customers towards a company.

In the gaming industry, most of the existing research on customer retention mainly focuses on the prediction of players’ lifetimes. For example, Bauckhage et al. [2] developed random process models on historical gameplay data to estimate how long individual players may play a game actively, and this has helped to understand how engagement in a game evolved over time. In addition, a regression model was built in recent research [45] to predict the number of games played based on players’ gameplay features, including model preference features, control usage features, performance features, and play-calling features.

## 2.1.3. Customer retention for online social games

Although customer retention for online social games is attracting the attention of researchers and some initial results have been obtained [2,45], this remains a challenging task. First, compared to other domains, online social games often result in a relatively low retention rate. Not all players will play indefinitely until all the content is exhausted. In fact, for the vast majority of games, a huge percentage of players churn out just after signing up and never play the game at all. It has been reported that approximately 85% of players do not return after the first day, and game companies can expect to lose 96% of their user base within 12 months [8]. This shows that when predicting player churn, the class distribution for players will be unbalanced. Because there are many more churners than non-churners in online social games, the class distribution of churners and non-churners is highly skewed [43]. This makes it more challenging to employ conventional data mining techniques to build useful prediction models that can handle the skewed class distributions. In addition, it is dificult to evaluate and interpret the results of classification models. The high accuracy of predicting potential churners does not necessarily capture the accurate picture of churn behaviours, because the churners’ retention behaviour may dominate the retention analysis, while ignoring the retention behaviours of other loyal and valuable players.

Second, the retention rate, one of the KPIs in a retention analysis, is a more nebulous concept for online social games than for other types of subscription businesses. The cancellation of the subscription is generally a clear indication of departing from a subscription business (e.g., telecom and banking). However, in online social games, it is dificult to identify the exact moment when players leave the game. Indeed, there is no particular time point at which players need to reveal their intention to leave the game. Because the retention analysis is often conducted at the game-testing stages or the beginning of a game’s release, the most commonly used time frames are the Day-1, Day-7, and the Day-28 (some use Day-30) retention rate. However, the actual time point of player departure remains unclear.

Third, because of the massive number of players, it is impractical to design customised promotion strategies for individual players. It has been pointed out in [45] that there are two levels of measurement for player retention based on game telemetry: aggregation and individual. Aggregation retention refers to measuring the retention of the entire player base, whereas individual retention refers to the analysis of player retention or quitting from an individual perspective, and the measurement is applied to specific players only. The current literature (e.g., [2,45]) is mainly concerned about individual-level retention. However, for online social games, it is costly and impractical for the game operator to spend limited operation resources on every player, especially the likely churners. In fact, the churn behaviour can be diverse for different types of players. For example, some players may quit the game straightaway once they lose interest in it, but some players do not churn suddenly; instead, they exhibit partial defection, which may subsequently lead to a complete churn. Therefore, the present study focuses on the exploration of different types of retention patterns for different player segments.

## 2.2. Segmenting online gamers

Market segmentation, initially proposed in the work of [40], is a popular research field with numerous successful applications [17,29]. In a highly competitive market, it becomes challenging for a mass-market strategy to succeed. The objective of market segmentation is to divide a heterogeneous mass market into smaller homogeneous sub-markets in which customers share something in common [17]. This helps the company differentiate customer groups, then, customised and effective strategies can be designed to better match customers’ needs. In the gaming industry, it is crucial to segment online gamers when conducting retention management. In addition, the gaming industry normally has a much higher churn rate, this may result in churners’ behaviour dominating aggregatelevel player retention analyses. Hence, it is essential to apply market segmentation before conducting a retention analysis.

One of the crucial steps in building segmentation models is the selection of segmentation variables. Many researchers identify different segmentation variables, including geographic, demographic, purchasing behaviours [29], and customer value [17]. The set of selected variables reflects the underlying motivation of segmenting the given market. In the literature different segmentation models have been used to detect and identify clusters of online gamers, and they are summarised in Table 1. For example, Drachen et al. [11] applied K-means and simplex volume maximisation (SIVM) clustering algorithms to cluster players based on their gameplay telemetry data. Asteriadis et al. [1] proposed a model to detect different types of players based on their facial gestures and in-game playtime by applying the K-medoids clustering algorithm. Sheu et al. [39] used the ID3 decision tree algorithm to segment online gamers from the perspective of experiential marketing. In short, most of the approaches employ clustering techniques but used different clustering algorithms, segmentation variables, and gaming applications. It is common to use selected performance measures to guide the determination of clustering parameters (e.g., the number of clusters).

Based on Table 1, it is found that researchers have mainly focused on gamers’ gameplay data and their in-game performances as segmentation variables. However, the social interaction attributes have rarely been considered. Only Tseng [42] considered some social-motive-related attributes, but instead of considering it as an independent social-related dimension, these social attributes have been combined with other attributes to construct two motivational factors. Moreover, the social attributes that were used in [42] are motive attributes (e.g., ‘I enjoy chatting with other gamers’ and ‘I am glad to cooperate with others in a group’), and such motiverelated attributes were collected through an online questionnaire. These attributes are subjective and indicate the players intentions rather than their actual in-game behaviours. In sharp contrast to this, the current study employs actual game telemetry data to extract the players’ social behaviour in a game. Hence, the collected social attributes in the current study are more objective.

In summary, prior research efforts mainly focus either on customer retention or player segmentation, but there is no approach that considers user segmentation in retention management for online social games. In an effort to remedy the above issues, this study will address the following central research questions:

• How should players for online social games be segmented so as to investigate the effects of social interaction on player segmentation?

• How should the retention behaviour patterns of different types of online social gamers be analysed?

• How should differentiation strategies be designed to attract and retain more online social gamers based on their retention behaviour?

## 3. The new retention management approach

## 3.1. Overview of the approach

The literature on retention management for online social games uses either aggregate-level retention analysis or individual-level retention analysis. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is no approach that studies the different retention patterns of different groups of players. In addition, the individual key retention metrics at different time points (i.e., Day-1, Day-7, and Day-28 retention rates) may not be suficient to describe the pattern of retention. The current study will uncover the retention patterns of different player segments using daily retention rates. The derived results can be helpful in targeting the right group of players for promotions and for finding the best operational strategies for running the gaming activities. It has been pointed out in [13] that there are several challenges in game data analytics. For example, the in game telemetry data can be multi-dimensional, and there may be a mixture of data types (e.g., numerical and categorical attributes) that make the analysis challenging. Also, the derived results need to be actionable and interpretable. The better the performance of a player, the more powerful and influential (s)he is in the game. This would result in the player being more willing to stay in the game. The character level, the number of completed quests, the number of in-game currency, and the frequency of using in-game currency are often used to measure this dimension.

S \left(c _ {i}, c _ {j}\right) = \sqrt {\left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {E}} E _ {i k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {E}} E _ {j k}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {P}} P _ {i k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {P}} P _ {j k}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {S I}} S I _ {i k} - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {S I}} S I _ {j k}\right) ^ {2}}\tag{1}
$$

where $n _ { E } , n _ { P } ,$ and n represent the number of the engagement features, performance features, and social interaction features, respectively. $E _ { i k } , P _ { i k }$ and $S I _ { i k }$ reflect the kth engagement features, the kth performance features, and the kth social interaction features of player $c _ { i } .$ .

Based on the computed stickiness distances, the next step is to cluster the players according to their stickiness to the game by using a stickiness-based FCM clustering algorithm. The retention analysis in this model is conducted on different player segments, respectively. By doing so, customised operational and marketing strategies can be designed and implemented to improve the overall retention rate. The technical details for accomplishing the relevant subtasks are presented in the following subsections.

## 3.2. Stickiness-based fuzzy C-means (FCM) clustering

Given an input dataset $I = \{ x _ { i } | i = 1 , 2 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , N \}$ with N data points and $C = \{ c _ { i } | j = 1 , 2 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , K \}$ , a vector that contains the centroids of K clusters, the objective function that the stickiness-based FCM aims to minimise is defined as follows:

$$
J (U, C) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} (\mu_ {i j}) ^ {m} S (x _ {i}, c _ {j})\tag{2}
$$

where K is the number of clusters, and m is the fuzzifier that controls the degree of fuzziness, $m > 1$ . The matrix $U = ( \mu _ { i j } ) _ { N \times K }$ represents a fuzzy partition matrix that is composed of the membership degrees $( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . \mu _ { i j } )$ , the membership degree indicates to what extent the data point x belongs to the cluster i and $S ( x _ { i } , c _ { j } )$ is the distance of stickiness between data point $x _ { i }$ and the centroid of cluster $j ( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . , c _ { j } )$

The above objective function satisfies the following:

$$
\mu_ {i j} \in [ 0, 1 ], \text {   for   } 1 \leq i \leq N, 1 \leq j \leq K, \text {   and   } \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} \mu_ {i j} = 1, \text {   for   } 1 \leq j \leq K.\tag{3}
$$

To minimise the objective function and by applying the Lagrangian function, $J ( U , C )$ can be rewritten as the following:

$$
J (U, C) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} (\mu_ {i j}) ^ {m} S (x _ {i}, c _ {j}) - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \lambda_ {i} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {K} \mu_ {i j} - 1\right),\tag{4}
$$

where $\lambda _ { i }$ is the Lagrangian multiplier.

Subject to Eq. (3), the cluster centroid vector C and the membership matrix U can be computed according to the following iterative equations:

$$
\mu_ {i j} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \left(\frac {S (x _ {i} , c _ {j})}{S (x _ {i} , c _ {k})}\right) ^ {1 / (m - 1)}} & \text { if } S (x _ {i}, c _ {k}) > 0, \forall j \\ 1 & \text { if } S (x _ {i}, c _ {j}) = 0 \\ 0 & \text { if } \exists k \neq j, S (c _ {k}, x _ {i}) = 0 \end{array} \right.,\tag{5}
$$

where $i = 1 , 2 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , N \mathrm { a n d } j = 1 , 2 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , K ,$ and the cluster centroids are defined as the following:

$$
c _ {j} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \mu_ {i j} ^ {m} x _ {i}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \mu_ {i j} ^ {m}}, j = 1, 2 \dots , K.\tag{6}
$$

## 3.3. Retention analysis and management

Retention rate is a key indicator that is commonly used in retention analyses; it shows how effectively game products are retaining players. However, there seems to be no universally accepted definition of what retention really means, nor how it is actually calculated, especially for online social games. This is because retention analyses are often for internal use only. In general, the retention rate measures the percentage of new players who join a game and return to continue playing after a predefined time period [13]. Game publishers usually employ their own metrics to calculate the retention rate and are not willing to share such sensitive data with outside parties. There are several ways to compute the retention rate, each of which has its own features and may lead to different results. No method is necessarily better than anothe in absolute terms; it is important to select the most appropriate method based on different analytical goals, and some common methods are summarised in Table 3.

In the gaming industry, when conducting retention analyses, the evaluation time period is predefined (e.g., from 1 March to 31 March), and typically, it is often from the first day of the game’s release. So only the players who join the game for the first time on the first day of the evaluation time period are considered and monitored. For example, if the evaluation time period is March of 2017, then only the players who newly join the game on 1 March are counted in the player pool. All existing players who already exist in the game do not count, even if they played on 1 March. The current study employs the classic retention method to calculate the retention rate for the following reasons: First, it is one of the most commonly used metrics for retention analyses in the industry [31,33]; it has been proved that if the player pool is large enough, this method can generate very smooth retention curves. Second, the current study aims to analyse the retention pattern, so the daily retention rate needs to be calculated. Thanks to the computational simplicity of the classic retention method, it is easier to calculate and understand the retention rate on a daily basis. Therefore, given the total number of players $X _ { j }$ (defined as the number of all newly entered players on the jth day of the evaluation time period), the Day i<sup></sup>s retention rate for the jth evaluation day, $r _ { i j } ,$ , is defined as the following:

$$
r _ {i j} = \frac {x _ {i}}{X _ {j}}, i = 1, 2, \dots , N \text {   and   } j = 1, 2, \dots , N,\tag{7}
$$

where N is the number of days within the evaluation time period, and $x _ { i }$ is the number of players within the player pool who return to the game right on the evaluation day (i.e., Day i). ## 4. Data and numerical experiments

## 4.1. The dataset

In this section, the effectiveness and utility of the proposed approach is verified using an empirical study. A realistic dataset from Dragon Nest<sup>1</sup> is used in this research. Dragon Nest is a free-toplay massively multiplayer online role-playing game (MMORPG) that integrates non-targeting system gameplay to provide players with a real and fast-paced action-filled experience. The players are allowed to choose from a range of playable characters and can equip these characters with customisable gear and weapons to defeat diverse monsters and other players. The dataset contains of one-month gameplay telemetry data from 1 March 2011 to 31 March 2011. In total, the in-game behaviour (including login details, playtime, social activities, achieved levels, coins, etc.) of 4,811,925 unique characters were collected. The stickiness to the game is derived from three perspectives (i.e., engagement, in-game performance, and social interaction). The 19 available and involved attributes used in this research are summarised in Table 5.

## 4.2. Data preprocessing

Only new players who joined the game on 1 March 2011 are selected for this experiment. A total of 16,770 characters with associated 19 in-game behavioural attributes are included in the analysis. To start with, the noisy, incomplete, and erroneous data were filtered out of the raw dataset. Hence, 1349 out of 16,770 characters were filtered out because of missing or incomplete gameplay transaction data. As a result, the remaining dataset includes 15,421 characters and their associated 19 attributes. A typical problem of data mining in games is the mixture of data types in the available dataset. As shown in Table 5, the dataset includes numerical attributes (e.g., level, playtime, and coin) and categorical variables (e.g., guild role type). To address this, it is essential to perform normalisation on these attributes. The current study employs the widely used zero-mean normalisation method, because it helps to reduce the impact of outliers. After normalisation and reordering, the higher value indicates better performance for each attribute

In the raw dataset, there are 277 guilds. Transactional attributes that reflect the guild’s status (e.g., guild age, guild level, and the number of members) are used for analysis. It is well-recognised that the status of the joined guild influences the character’s social interaction in an MMORPG [13]. Players are more willing to communicate and interact with guild members who are in the same active guild. For simplicity, the guild-related attributes (as listed in Table 6) are integrated to derive an overall attribute that represents the guild’s status. Normalisation is also performed on these attributes. Because all used attributes are numerical, the conventional max-min normalization is used. After that, the conventional FCM [4] clustering algorithm is used to group the existing guilds. When K = 3, Xie and Beni’s validation index (i.e., XB coeficient) [47] receives the smallest value, which indicates the best structure of clusters.

Therefore, the existing guilds are grouped into three clusters: inactive guilds, growing and expanding guilds, and mature guilds. The results of guild clustering are shown in Table 6. The inactive guilds receive the smallest values for all performance attributes and are likely to dismiss in the end; the growing guilds are characterised by high accessibility and a growing number of guild members, but other performance attributes (e.g., age, level, coins, and points) receive medium values; the mature guilds have a stable number of guild members and are unlikely to be dismissed, because their performance attributes receive the highest values. To sum up, the aggregated attribute (i.e., guild\_status) takes values within (0, 1, 2, 3), in which 0 indicates that the character does not join a guild and a larger value indicates that the guild in which a character has joined is more active or mature and is more likely to have social interactions.

## 4.3. Principal component analysis

The in-game telemetry data contains high-dimensional attributes (19 attributes). An initial correlation analysis shows that many of the attributes are highly correlated. For example, it is obvious that as characters level up and gain coins, the number of completed quests increases as well. To overcome this issue, the PCA is performed to select the most significant attributes/components and reduce the dimensionality of the data. The idea of the PCA is to transform the attributes into a special coordinate system that optimally describes the variance of a given dataset. By doing this, only the principal components that have important contributions to the total variance are selected, and the others are discarded. In this work, the original 19 attributes are replaced with one of their subsets that accounts for a given proportion of the variance (e.g., 85%). The first 7 components (accounting for 86.61% of the variance) are used instead of the original 19 attributes in subsequent analyses. ## 4.4. Clustering analysis and validation of clustering

After the data pre-processing and principal component analysis, the next step is to group characters into different clusters according to their in-game behaviour. This research employs the stickinessbased FCM algorithm to conduct clustering.

## 4.4.1. Determination of parameters of clustering

In a clustering analysis, determining the number of clusters is crucial to the derived results. In the literature, many metrics have been proposed to measure the performance of clustering. Although these metrics consider the properties of the generated clusters from different perspectives, no metric can guarantee optimal results. In this research, three widely used measures, namely partition coeficient [3], XB coeficient [47], and Fukuyama-Sugeno clustervalidity (FS) [15], are used. For the partition coeficient, a higher value of the index indicates better performance, whereas for the XB coeficient and FS, a smaller value of the index indicates better performance. These measures are calculated using different values of c (i.e., the number of clusters) for the experimental dataset. The obtained results include absolute values and normalised values (i.e., the larger value indicates better performance for all measures), and these values are reported in Table 9. The normalised values of three measures are summed up to derive an overall performance index for different numbers of clusters (i.e., the last column of Table 9). Table 9 shows that when c = 5, the clustering achieves optimal overall performance. In addition, product operational experts suggested that it is impractical to concurrently design and implement a large number of customised marketing tactics to retain players. ## 4.6. Analysis of computational complexity

The computational complexity of the proposed method mainly consists of four parts: 1) attributes retrieval, 2) PCA analysis, 3) FCM clustering analysis, and 4) retention rate calculation. This work employs the MySQL DBMS and MySQL executes joins between tables using a nested loop algorithm. Attribute retrieval requires to join two tables, so that the computational complexity of most attributes is O(nm), where n is the number of new characters within the evaluation time period, and m is the number of records in the corresponding database tables [32]. To retrieve the engagement attributes (e.g., Playtime\_W1 to Playtime\_W4 and Login\_W1 to Login\_W4), the first step is to filter the relevant login records of the new characters, and the computational complexity is O(nm). The second step is to analyse the login data and calculate the time length of game playing and number of logins for each week. Assuming the process time for each record is p, the number of retrieved records from the first step is $q \left( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , q < m \right)$ , and the computational complexity for the second step is O(nqp). Therefore, the total computational complexity for the engagement attributes is ${ \cal O } ( n m ) + { \cal O } ( n q p )$ . In this study, $ { n _ { \mathrm { ~ \scriptsize ~ = ~ } 1 5 , 4 2 1 } }$ , and the value of m depends on the attribute tables, and the value of p is usually very small and can be ignored. Hence, the maximum computation complexity for attributes retrieval is O(nm).

For PCA, according to the analysis of [44], because this study explicitly computes the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, the computation complexity of PCA is $O ( n d ^ { 2 } )$ , where n is the number of characters, and d is the number of attributes. ## 4.8. Prediction of player lifetime

In the gaming industry, it is important to predict a player’s lifetime in a game. An accurate prediction of lifetime can help game operators take actions to retain the possible churners before they leave. It has been pointed out in [2] that based on data about the initial playtime behaviour, it is possible to predict when gamers will stop playing. Therefore, although only one-month game telemetry is available in this study, it is still plausible to use this data for the prediction and analysis of player lifetime. In practice, because of the large number of players, it is impractical to analyse the absolute value of individual player lifetimes. However, if the remaining lifetime of players can be accurately classified as low and high, it will be useful for game operators to implement strategies to retain players with a low remaining lifetime. For this reason, the prediction of player lifetime is modelled as a classification problem in the current study, and the performance of the predictive model is measured in terms of classification accuracy.

The main purpose of this experiment is to validate whether the clustering-based model can help to improve the classification accuracy. In general, a good clustering model can well separate data with different characteristics while grouping similar data in the same cluster. Different clusters that are detected by a good clustering model often contain different data patterns. Hence, compared to using a standalone predictive model for the whole dataset, individual predictive models that build on different data clusters tend to better capture the data patterns. The aggregation of the performances of individual predictive models can improve the overall prediction accuracy, if a good clustering approach is used to separate the data. The improvement of playtime classification accuracy can provide further validation about the appropriateness of the clustering-based model.

The remaining lifetime is measured in hours, and Table 16 lists the distribution of characters using different cut-off parameters. For example, when the cut-off parameter is set to be 25, then 44.69% of the characters in the training dataset are classified as having a low remaining lifetime whereas the remainder (i.e., 55.31%) is classified as having a high remaining lifetime. To obtain a dataset with balanced class labels, the cut-off parameters take values from 25 to 60, respectively. The proposed stickinessbased model, together with the other two benchmark models (i.e., M1 and M2), are used for characters’ lifetime classification.

To start with, the characters are randomly partitioned into two groups: training data (with 70% of characters) and testing data (with 30% of characters). All three models use the same characters as training data to build classification models, and the same characters as are used as testing data to report the performance of classification. In other words, the three models use different input attributes to predict the same thing (i.e., class label of a player’s in-game lifetime). Besides the input attributes and the prediction attribute, each character has an associated cluster label that is derived from previous clustering models, and this label may be different for the varying models. According to these cluster labels, the original dataset is divided into k training subsets $( { \mathrm { i . e . , } } D _ { 1 } \cdots D _ { k }$ in Fig. 4) and k testing subsets $( \mathrm { i . e . , } T _ { 1 } \cdots T _ { k }$ in Fig. 4), where k is the number of detected clusters. Then, k clustering-based predictive models are built from the corresponding training subsets. In this case, 5 cluster-based models are built for the stickiness-based model, and 7 cluster-based models are built for models M1 and M2, respectively. Rather than using a standalone predictive model for testing the whole dataset, each testing subset can employ the most appropriate cluster-based predictive model (i.e., built from the same cluster label) to predict the class label of in-game lifetime. By comparing the actual and predicted class labels of characters in the testing dataset, the classification accuracy for testing can be calculated. The above process is illustrated in Fig. 4.

In this experiment, two classification models, namely naive Bayes and radial basis function (RBF) network, are used for class-label prediction by using different cut-off parameters, and the derived model structure and parameters may be different for different clustering-based predictive models. The key learning parameters for the RBF network include one clustering seed, the minimum standard deviation for the clusters equals 0.1, and the number of clusters generated by K-means is equal to 2. For the purpose of comparison, two prediction approaches are conducted on different models, respectively: one using the above clustering-based classification method and the other using the traditional prediction approach that directly employs a classification model on the original dataset without considering different clusters. The obtained results are summarised in Table 17. The derived results reveal that, compared with the traditional prediction approach, the new clustering-based predictive approach can greatly improve the classification for all three models. Moreover, in all experiments, the proposed stickinessbased clustering model consistently achieves the best classification accuracy among all three models. This clearly illustrates that the proposed stickiness-based model produces good clustering results because it appropriately separates characters with different in-game behaviour patterns, so that the cluster based prediction models can capture them accurately.

## 5. Discussions and implications

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

The key difference between online games and ofline games is that online games usually allow player interactions through forming teams to accomplish quests and missions together, trade, and simply chat. Players not only play the game together, but they also socialise with each other in online games via some in-game functions, such as chatting systems, mailing systems, friends management systems, and community management systems (i.e., guild). It has been wellrecognised that socialising has become an important motivation for players to continuously play online games [42]. However, traditional CRM systems for online games only focus on gaming activities and the in-game performance of players. Although the literature (e.g., [42]) has considered some social-related attributes in segmenting online players, such attributes are combined with others to construct motivational factors instead of being considered an independent social dimension. In other words, the aim is to investigate the effects of motivation rather than social interaction when segmenting online gamers. On the other hand, prior research efforts on retention management have been mainly devoted to analysing the important determinants of customer retention in various domains or predicting players’ activities and lifetimes. However, conducting retention management for online social games remains a challenging task, and it is still in its infancy. In the literature, little research has been done on retention analysis and management for online social gamers. The current study contributes to the existing literature by designing a new framework for customer retention management for online social games by integrating clustering of players with retention management of players. The new framework includes three dimensions (i.e., engagement, performance, and social interaction) when clustering players, and is able to better differentiate between loyal customers and churners among online social gamers.

## 5.3. Limitations

Although the proposed approach is promising, much progress can be made through further research. First, when deriving the stickiness metric, the importances of different dimensions can be considered. The more important game-play dimension can be allocated a higher weight in the calculation of the stickiness clustering distance. Second, in this study, the behaviour of players are regarded as a static snapshot during the observed period of time. To better understand the process of churning, it is necessary to use a dynamic approach that can analyse the migration pattern of players. Although the proposed framework can help in better distinguishing between valuable customers and churning customers, the causality relationship between performance, engagement, social interaction, and the churning behaviour is still not established in numeric terms. Though this is beyond the scope of the current research, it is definitely worth of investigation in future research. Third, because of the availability of the empirical dataset, this study only considers the characters’ social interaction attributes in the segmentation model. When more social data becomes available, it would be interesting to explore the properties of in-game social networks (e.g., density, centrality, and reciprocity) by using social network analysis (SNA) techniques.

## 6. Conclusions

Retention rate is an important measure to evaluate the quality and attractiveness of online social games. It is the starting point of a game life cycle and is essential to revenue generation. This study proposes a new data mining approach for retention management of online social games based on different player segments. The applicability and utility of the proposed model is demonstrated via empirical analysis of a realistic MMORPG dataset. By using the proposed model, the players in the experimental dataset are divided into five clusters that represent five types of players: ‘leaders and aggressive gamers’, ‘churners’, ‘socialisers’, ‘explorers’, and ‘achievers’. By analysing their retention trends within the evaluation time period, the length and start–end time points of different retention phases (i.e., attrition, settlement, and stationary) are found to be different for these gamer clusters, and customised retention strategies are then designed accordingly.

The main contributions of this work include: 1) this work proposes an innovative clustering-based retention management model for online social games. The existing retention management research in the gaming industry either employs a general model to analyse the retention of all players or predicts the retention behaviour of individual players. In reality, because different players quit a game for different reasons, it is impractical to apply one general strategy to retain all players. On the other hand, it is ineffective and costly to design customised strategies that aim to keep individual players engaged for a longer time period. The new model is capable of identifying the retention behaviour patterns of different groups of players, and analysing such patterns can provide insights into how to design customised retention strategies to retain more players and improve customer relationship management. 2) A new metric, named stickiness, is proposed in this study to measure the distance between two players in online social games. The stickiness distance concurrently and explicitly considers the three important features (i.e., game engagement, in-game performance, and social interaction) of online social gamers and integrates such features to derive a global index that can be used to distinguish among players. In particular, this metric innovatively considers the social interaction in player segmentation and retention management and provides an effective means with which to handle high-dimensional segmentation attributes. The new distance is compared against two benchmark models, and the comparative results show that the stickiness-based model achieves more reasonable clustering results and is able to better predict customer lifetime. Moreover, the findings of this research empirically confirm the importance of social interaction, which is usually underestimated in the current literature of online social gaming. Since most of the MMORPGs have similar social interaction features such as, allowing players to chat, make friends, join guilds, share in-game resources, and form groups, the proposed stickiness-based model is general and adaptable to other MMORPGs.
