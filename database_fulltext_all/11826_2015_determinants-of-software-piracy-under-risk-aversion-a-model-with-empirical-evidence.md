# Determinants of software piracy under risk aversion: a model with empirical evidence

Ahmed Driouchi<sup>1</sup>, Mingzhu Wang<sup>2</sup> and Tarik Driouchi<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Institute of Economic Analysis and Prospective Studies, Ifrane, Morocco; <sup>2</sup>Department of Management, King’s College London, London, U.K.

Correspondence: Tarik Driouchi, Department of Management, King’s College London, Stamford Street, London SE19NH, U.K. Tel: +44(0)2078483624

## Abstract

This paper studies the determinants of software piracy in world economies from a risk avoidance perspective. A risk aversion model for the commercialization of pirated software is developed to account for behavioral elements of risk and uncertainty avoidance among countries’ software pirates (i.e., counterfeiters and suppliers) and test empirically for the effects of country characteristics on piracy levels. Panel regression analysis is conducted to identify the determinants of software piracy using this model on a data set of 87 countries during 2007– 2011. The empirical results confirm those obtained in prior research (e.g., the inverted U-shaped relationship between GDP per capita and piracy rates) but divulge that the behavioral-country component capturing the attitudes towards risk of software pirates improves the explanatory power of the statistical regressions after controlling for country performance and institutional factors. We also show that human development and good country governance reduce piracy rates. Besides providing support for our risk aversion-based piracy model and hinting at the need to consider population behavior in policy-making, these findings underline the relevance of human development and country institutions in explaining software piracy rates.

## Introduction

Most reports and publications on software piracy recognize the direct and indirect costs incurred in different world economies (BSA, 2003, 2007, 2011; Ding & Liu, 2009). Such costs are generally assessed to be high in developed countries but are assumed to be more pronounced in developing economies where intellectual property legislation and enforcement are usually weak regardless of international pressure (Gould & Gruben, 1996; Gopal & Sanders, 1997; Awokuse & Yin, 2010). The fact that a culture of intellectual property rights is not yet well entrenched in a number of societies adds also to the problem (Simpson et al, 1994; Angeles, 2011; Chan & Lai, 2011). Lack of fair access to information and communication technologies, inef<sup>fi</sup>cient policies for socio-economic inclusion, poverty and weak country governance all exacerbate this problem in the developing world, turning something accepted as illegal and immoral into an economic necessity and a life choice. Behavioral and country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics such as agents’ economic motives (risk and rewards attributes), legal and political institutions and human development should, therefore, also be part of the existing explanations behind software piracy across nations.

The aim of this paper is to identify and assess empirically the variables that are likely to determine software piracy rates worldwide while accounting for risk avoidance, human development, and country performance or quality of governance as country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics (these key factors are predicted to contribute to piracy by our theoretical model of software piracy decision making under risk aversion) affecting the decision-making process of individuals specializing in counterfeiting and the commercialization of pirated software. In our context, software pirates are part of a network or group of individuals that specialize in counterfeiting and the commercialization/distribution of pirated software in countries. We focus on the typical case of vendors with ‘hybrid’ business models (i.e., combining legal and illegal operations).

Incorporating country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes in the analysis of piracy rates, the paper sheds new light on the role of human development, country institutions, and risk attitudes in explaining counterfeiting phenomena in world economies. Several empirical studies have investigated related issues and established the prevalence of economic and non-economic variables in predicting software piracy (Andres, 2006; Dejean, 2009). However, these cross-country studies do not rely on decision-making models of piracy that support directions of analyses. The case of <sup>fi</sup>rm piracy determinants, on the other hand, has been widely covered in decision making (see, e.g., Berger et al, 2012 for a recent overview of the relevant literature and an investi gation of how R&D strategy can reduce <sup>fi</sup>rm exposure to piracy risk). Among other things, cross-country studies have not accounted for the attitudes towards risk of country software ‘pirates’ nor do they control for human development, a determinant of income and GDP, as a variable explaining national piracy rates. Associated with economic well-being, human development and socio-economic environment are important factors to consider when studying deviating behavior and informal economies. The papers of Kovačic´ (2007), Goel & Nelson (2009) and Yang et al (2009) are examples of recent contributions in this area. Authors highlight the relevance of GDP per capita and other socio-economic variables in explaining piracy. They also recognize that more studies are needed to identify the aggregate factors that affect piracy rates in the economy. Yet, most of the existing studies are based on a list of intuitively selected determinants that potentially affect cross-country variation in piracy rates with reasonable explanatory power (e.g., Glass & Wood, 1996; Husted, 2000). What is often missing from this literature is the prior establishment of an economic decision-making model of software piracy that would support and guide the empirical analysis of country determinants.

Our study contributes to addressing this speci<sup>fi</sup>c gap in research. We present a model of decision making under risk aversion for the commercialization of pirated software. This model, after aggregation, serves as a basis for our empirical analysis of country piracy rates. This is in line with the large set of literature that has covered tax evasion under risk in a number of economic areas (e.g., Sarris, 1985; Van Dunem & Arndt, 2009). Our analysis is conducted at the country level and accounts for the behavior of the typical software pirate or counterfeiter per country by looking at the general risk attitude (or risk aversion tendencies) of individuals in that country. Because we study the aggregate impact of risk aversion on country piracy rates, our risk avoidance variables are indicative of risk aversion tendencies per country (i.e., the risk aversion of a typical group of individuals in that country). To capture this effect in our empirical analysis, we use Hofstede’s (2001) uncertainty avoidance index as a proxy for risk aversion.

The innovations provided herein relate to (1) using a theoretical framework to identify the determinants of software piracy under risk aversion and incorporating the risk avoidance of individuals per country in the empirical analysis, and (2) considering the joint effect of human development and country governance on piracy rates. These two dimensions (i.e., risk attitudes and socioeconomic environment) can be very important in determining someone’s willingness to engage in counterfeiting, and therefore might provide useful policy insights vis-à-vis intellectual property enforcement in world economies (e.g., Schware, 1987). We also go beyond extant empirical research in the area by performing panel regression and factor analyses (i.e., principal component) of piracy rates’ determinants on a data set of 87 countries during 2007– 2011. The majority of past studies rely on cross-sectional regressions. Our main <sup>fi</sup>nding is that country risk avoidance positively affects piracy rates worldwide but, as predicted by our theoretical model, such an association is negatively moderated by human development, intellectual property rights and good country governance. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms that the negative relationship between risk aversion and one’s propensity to engage in counterfeiting is conditional upon high economic well-being and strong country institutions. The paper is composed of four sections. The next section reviews the relevant literature and research streams. The subsequent section presents our theoretical risk-aversion framework and research approach. The empirical analysis is provided in the penultimate section, while the last section discusses <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications.

## Background

In contrast to prior research that has investigated the determinants of piracy (e.g., copying, infringement and counterfeit) for <sup>fi</sup>rms (see El Harbi & Grolleau, 2008; Weatherall & Webster, 2009 and Berger et al, 2012 on the association between <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics and the likelihood of falling victim to piracy), the largest share of available literature on the aggregate determinants of software piracy in countries does not consider parameters related to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c behavior or attitude of agents (i.e., networks of individuals) and population in the analysis. In this speci<sup>fi</sup>c strand of research, economic, cultural and institutional variables such as GDP, intellectual property rights and rule of law are usually viewed as the most relevant determinants of software piracy worldwide.

The gross domestic product (GDP) per capita is the most recurring determinant in extant research. Fischer & Andres (2005) show that the relationship between GDP per capita and piracy is such that piracy rates increase initially with low GDP per capita but decline with higher levels. Grolleau et al (2010) corroborate this relationship and show that piracy in fact follows a Kuznets-like curve. Also, other evidence reveals that the level of economic development in high GDP countries tends to be accompanied by a strengthening of intellectual property protection which explains declines in piracy rates in these countries. For example, Gopal & Sanders (1998, 2000) <sup>fi</sup>nd that every \$1000 increase in GDP per capita leads to a 1.3% decrease in piracy rates. This upward trend is reversed beyond \$6000 GDP per capita. Husted (2000) presents income inequality as another explanatory factor. Andres (2006) use both the Gini coef<sup>fi</sup>cient and income quintiles as measures of income inequality to test whether high-income inequality explains higher levels of illegal copying. If so, it implies that piracy is a middle class crime since it is the middle class population who is more likely to be able to afford computer hardware but not the legal acquisition of the software. Additional economic indicators used to predict the occurrence of piracy in a given country shed light on in<sup>fl</sup>ationrelated metrics based on the assumption that as in<sup>fl</sup>ation increases, software piracy might be expected to rise since expected future prices are increasing (Depken & Simmons, 2004). Other factors include domestic market size, unemployment, economic freedom, share of export of goods and services (Kovačic´, 2007), research and development expenditures (Kumar, 1996; Ginarte & Park, 1997), expenditures on information technology, foreign direct investments (Schneider, 2005), high technology exports (Hogenbirk & Van Kranenburg, 2005), and state tax burden (Bezman & Depken, 2006). The majority of these indicators, used in cross-country multiple regressions, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contribute to explaining piracy rate levels.

In addition to the aforementioned economic variables, cultural factors accounting for personal attitudes towards piracy have been used as explanatory constructs for crosscountry variation in piracy rates (Husted, 2000; Wagner & Sanders, 2001; Chan & Lai, 2011). Depken & Simmons (2004) describe differences between Western and Asian approaches to intellectual property rights and relate them to cultural speci<sup>fi</sup>cities. Individual creation among Westerners is characterized by individual ownership, while in Asian cultures creation might have collectivist implications. The authors combine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of economic and cultural variables to examine the determinants of software piracy in a heterogeneous group of 65 countries for which data on piracy and cultural variables was available (Depken & Simmons, 2004). The piracy construct is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally regressed against economic variables and two societal measures developed by Hofstede (1980, 2001) namely individualism and power distance. Accordingly, countries with high power distance index values are supposed to have higher software piracy rates on average (Depken & Simmons, 2004). The related results show that the individualism metric is overall negatively related to piracy which validates the authors’ initial hypothesis (this is later con-<sup>fi</sup>rmed by Yang et al, 2009). The power distance construct, on the other hand, yields con<sup>fl</sup>icting <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Other studies covering cultural aspects and using Hofstede cultural dimensions include Husted (2000), Shore et al (2001), Ki et al (2006), Marron & Steel (2000), Moores (2008), Shin et al (2004) and Yang et al (2009). Only three of these studies (i.e., Husted, 2000; Shore et al, 2001; Moores, 2008) explicitly investigate the impact of risk or uncertainty avoidance, as a cultural factor, on piracy rates but results are generally inconsistent. Shore et al (2001) <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive association between software piracy and uncertainty avoidance using students’ survey data. Relying on country data, Husted (2000) <sup>fi</sup>nds a negative but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant association between piracy rates and uncertainty avoidance for a data set of 39 economies while Moores (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nds a positive but insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant association between uncertainty avoidance and software piracy on a sample of 57 countries (results also show, however, that uncertainty avoidance is positively related to declines in piracy). Performed on a data set of 87 countries, our analysis examines interactions between risk avoidance and country characteristics and their effects on piracy from an economic and decision-making perspective (see our predictions and risk aversion model in the next section). We reconcile extant <sup>fi</sup>ndings by showing when risk avoidance reduces piracy and when it might not.

Regarding country governance matters, Kovačic´ (2007) explores the determinants of cross-national variation in software piracy rates by performing a quantitative analysis using economic, cultural and legal variables. The study establishes that factors such as economic development and the rule of law make the most important contribution to the variability of worldwide software piracy. Other empirical investigations such as Goel & Nelson (2009) also use cross-sectional analyses. They con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the equation for software piracy determinants contains economic and noneconomic components. The results show that a country stage of development and quality of governance have the largest impact on the incidence of software piracy. Yang et al (2009) produce related <sup>fi</sup>ndings and underline the negative effects of economic well-being, individualism and technology development on piracy rates (see also Marron & Steel, 2000 and Shin et al, 2004 for additional cross-country evidence on how intellectual property protection varies across cultures and national institutions).

Regardless of the extent to which the different variables used succeed in explaining the occurrence of piracy, studies also underline the existence of regional differences in the economies examined (e.g., Fischer & Andres, 2005; Hogenbirk & van Kranenburg, 2005). For example, piracy is shown not to be a middle class crime in all geographic regions or income groups. Also, the explanatory power of the economic factors compared with the cultural variables might depend on the data set studied. Works related to these regional differences include Husted (2000) on the role of national culture and Ding & Liu (2009) on productivity factors in Asia.

Despite the consensus evidence on what determines piracy across nations, the aforementioned studies tend to overlook the process of decision making of country counterfeiters and how their behavior affects the economy as a whole and country piracy rate in speci<sup>fi</sup>c, omitting possible interactions between population attitudes and country economic indicators in the analysis. This is an important element of the piracy rate-country characteristics relationship.

The existing evidence needs to be supplemented by decision-making insights into the behavior of country counterfeiters. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the attitude of software pirates or population towards risk in a given country needs to be accounted for in the piracy rates determinants equations. Given that risk tends to moderate the economic choices of individuals, risk attitudes should play a role in predicting software pirates’ behavior across nations (by not incorporating this important behavioral factor and its economic dynamics, extant research presumes risk-neutrality among counterfeiters). More generally, country characteristics and behavioral factors – including agents’ risk-rewards attributes – are important factors to consider for the study of economic policy issues.

Attitudes to risk will usually be determined by culture, economic well-being and the geographic environment of the software pirate. Interesting economic models with promising applications have been emerging in recent years (Varian, 2000; Peace et al, 2003; Peitz & Waelbroeck 2006; Jaisingh, 2009). Banerjee (2003) and others examine the policy instruments that may be used to reduce software piracy in the economy namely the optimal monitoring rates and the penalty levels that should be charged to infringers. These models account as well for network externalities (Chang et al, 2008). King & Lampe (2003) discuss how <sup>fi</sup>rms can allow piracy to occur in the presence of network effects and analyze when the producer can choose the monetary amount to be devoted to piracy. Results suggest that tolerating piracy cannot always raise pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for <sup>fi</sup>rms (King & Lampe, 2003). Similarly, Crampes & Laffont (2002) analyze the effects of piracy on the pricing policy of a software publisher with an emphasis on cost randomness in the decision to use illegal software and on the risk aversion of users among other issues. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that piracy can have positive social effects in the short run, provided it does not provoke a market breakdown. No clear result is reached concerning longterm effects. Ding & Liu (2009) examine the moderating adverse effect of software piracy on economic ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and productivity growth in a number of Asian nations. Moores (2008) highlights how uncertainty avoidance across countries might determine the rate of decline in piracy. Finally, Kwan et al (2008) and Jaisingh (2009), respectively, discuss the effect of the risk of using pirated software on <sup>fi</sup>rm software protection policies, and the impact of piracy on innovation.

In line with these studies, our paper proposes a decisionmaking framework for software piracy under risk aversion which helps identify and explain the empirical determinants of piracy rates in world economies. In addition to guiding our choice of piracy determinant variables, this approach takes into account agents’ behavior in the economy. With respect to software piracy modeling, frameworks used in previous research for the understanding of tax evasion are relevant to this work (Allingham & Sandmo, 1972; Borck, 2004). This literature has examined a series of issues and sectors that include agriculture, natural resources, <sup>fi</sup>nance and other areas (Pope & Just, 1977). Contributions cover also matters related to informal activities and deviating behavior (e.g., Van Dunem & Arndt, 2009). Several authors have put in place models where risk and uncertainty are introduced under different structures including tax evasion decision models (Yitzhaki, 1974; Slemrod 1989; Elffers et al, 1992). Other variants of these models are enriched to tackle economic policy decisions where risk aversion and risk attitudes are further emphasized with a series of issues that include violation of intellectual property rights, etc. (Schluter & Mount, 1976; Borck, 2004; Gupta, 2005; Acconcia, 2006). Building on this literature, the next section introduces the theoretical framework adopted to explain software piracy (behavior) under risk aversion.

## Theoretical model of aggregate piracy rate under risk

We present a simple but intuitive decision model for software piracy under constant risk aversion. The theoretical model employed in this paper combines those used for decision-making and tax evasion under risk. Following the contribution of Borck (2004), tax and penalty structures are introduced with decisions concerning the amount to be declared by counterfeiters and software vendors to the <sup>fi</sup>scal administration. This amount re<sup>fl</sup>ects the level of piracy under randomness in capturing and <sup>fi</sup>ning violations. The model thus portrays the behavior of vendors who operate the illegal parts of their businesses behind the legal front and declare only a part of their income (e.g., electronics shops that sell TVs, computers and other hardware legally but provide pirated software, games, movies, etc., as part of their offerings). This model is not applicable, however, to entirely illegal operations.

## Commercializing the pirated software

In an economy, some agents pursue activities that are not legally allowed (i.e., constantly shifting between legal and illegal commerce). This can include stealing of goods and assets, tax evasion and other informal businesses such as digital pirating with violation of intellectual property rights. Under these circumstances law violators are assumed to realize levels of income equivalent to y. This level of income will vary across nations because of human and economic development considerations. The latter partly determine the amount y realized by counterfeiters.

As they cannot reveal the real levels of their gains because of the illegal nature of their activities, counterfeiters only declare a lower revenue h to the <sup>fi</sup>scal authorities (among other institutions) with h<y. This underreporting of gains is mainly motivated by the willingness to evade higher tax payments. In this model, informal activities such as pirating of intellectual property are directly linked with another violation that is tax evasion. While such model can be viewed as applying to large cases of violation, the framework is considered mainly for the case of software piracy or software counterfeiters and the related data available for empirical validation. Under the above considerations, software pirates are assumed to search for information and other instruments that trigger piracy and then result in informal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from commercialization. As such, there are costs related to the time and efforts devoted to the activity at both levels of production and sales of the pirated outputs. These outputs are represented by the level h that is declared as a proportion of the real level achieved.

The cost function related to declaring the level h of gains is set to be:

$$
c (h) = \frac {\alpha h ^ {2}}{2} (\alpha > 0).\tag{1}
$$

The taxation rate in the economy is assumed to be at a <sup>fl</sup>at rate $\tau ,$ such that the economic agent declaring h pays:

$$
T (h) = \tau . h.\tag{2}
$$

The penalty rate that applies only if the operator is caught is $\beta > 1$ . This is a random variable that is not fully known to the software pirate. The randomness of $\beta$ is mainly due to a sequence of conditional probabilities related to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the fraud, the agent being caught, auditing and the <sup>fi</sup>nal stages of <sup>fi</sup>ning in case the facts are legally established. In practice, $\beta$ is about enforcing laws and property rights and should be determined by− country institutional factors such as the intellectual property rights regime, rule of law and quality of governance. The penalty in this model applies to the amount (y h) with the multiplicative rate $\beta \tau .$ The penalty β is assumed to be normally distributed with expected value $\mathcal { ^ { \prime } E } ( \beta ) ^ { \prime }$ and variance, ‘var $( \beta ) ^ { \prime }$ . Under this formulation, the net gain of the violator is given by G(h):

$$
G (h) = \gamma - \tau h - \frac {\alpha}{2} h ^ {2} - \beta \tau (\gamma - h) \mathrm{with} \beta > 1.\tag{3}
$$

This says that the net gain from commercializing− the pirated software is obtained by subtracting the evasion costs, the partial total tax paid and the total penalty from the total gain achieved that is (y h). Next, we look at how the economic agent will be attempting to set the level h to be declared based on the maximization of the above objective function (G) under risk aversion.

## Optimal amount to declare under risk aversion

Our model assumes that economic agents exhibit constant risk aversion behavior with coef<sup>fi</sup>cient γ. The maximization of the expected utility of gains is equivalent to the maximization of the value function V based on the expectation of G and its variance. Under the normality of the random variable and with an exponential utility function exhibiting constant risk aversion, the maximization of the expected utility is equivalent to maximizing Eq. (4) (see Hey, 1979, pp. 49). The proof can be easily established given that the density function of the normal random variable is also of the exponential form. The <sup>fi</sup>rstorder conditions are then necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for a maximum to be attained since the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of $h ^ { 2 }$ is negative (V(h) is concave in h):

$$
\begin{array}{c} V (h) = E (G (h)) - \frac {\gamma}{2} V a r (G), \\ V (h) = \gamma - \tau h - \frac {\alpha}{2} h ^ {2} - \tau (\gamma - h) E (\beta) \\ - \frac {\gamma}{2} \tau^ {2} (\gamma - h) ^ {2}. V a r (\beta). \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The optimal amount to be declared in case of constant absolute risk aversion is then determined by:

$$
\frac {\partial V}{\partial h} = - \tau - \alpha h + \tau E (\beta) + \gamma \tau^ {2} (y - h) V a r (\beta) = 0,\tag{5}
$$

$$
h _ {R} ^ {*} = \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma \tau^ {2} \gamma V a r (\beta)}{\alpha + \gamma \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}.\tag{6}
$$

The optimal value of h is given by Eq. (6). This says that besides τ and $\tau ^ { 2 }$ and $E ( \beta ) , \gamma$ and var $( \beta )$ are determinants of the optimal level of h.

## Aggregation and piracy rate determination

Under risk aversion, each software pirate denoted by i = 1 to N is expected to cause a loss to the economy equivalent to:

$$
y _ {i} - h _ {R} ^ {*} i = y _ {i} - \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma_ {i} \tau^ {2} y _ {i} V a r (\beta)}{\alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {i} \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}.\tag{7}
$$

Under this formulation, all infringers are subjected to the same levels of tax and penalty but have different search costs and different attitudes towards piracy as this is measured by the constant absolute risk aversion coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient $\gamma .$ For the overall economy, aside from network effects and transaction costs, the total loss is given by:

$$
L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \left(\gamma_ {i} - h _ {R i}\right) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \gamma_ {i} - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma_ {i} \tau^ {2} \gamma_ {i} V a r (\beta)}{\alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {i} \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}.\tag{8}
$$

If risk attitudes and costs, besides the levels of the real transactions, were homogenous among the software pirates then the aggregate loss can be simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed to:

$$
L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} (y _ {i} - h _ {R i}) = N \left(\gamma - \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma \tau^ {2} y V a r (\beta)}{\alpha + \gamma \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}\right).\tag{9}
$$

For our model to be tested empirically, this assumption of homogeneity is required. As we are comparing piracy rates (and their determinants) across countries, we consider risk aversion per country to be indicative of the risk avoidance tendencies of individuals (e.g., software pirates) in that country and we measure it through Hofstede’s (2001) uncertainty avoidance index. We recognize/caution that this measure is only a proxy of risk aversion as it has been criticized for incorporating both aspects of risktaking (risk avoidance) and preference for <sup>fl</sup>exible rules in decision-making (e.g., D’Iribarne, 1997 and Rowe & Struck, 1999). In Eq. (9), γ stands for the typical risk aversion coef<sup>fi</sup>cient characterizing all agents in the economy (e.g., risk aversion tendencies in a given country). The software piracy rate $p$ based on these losses can be then expressed relative to the total amount of transactions $N \gamma ,$ that is $( L / N \gamma )$ , as:

$$
p = \frac {L}{N y} = N \frac {\left(y - \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma \tau^ {2} y V a r (\beta)}{\alpha + \gamma \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}\right)}{N y},
$$

$$
p = \frac {L}{N \gamma} = 1 - \frac {\tau [ E (\beta) - 1 ] + \gamma \tau^ {2} \gamma V a r (\beta)}{\alpha + \gamma \tau^ {2} V a r (\beta)}.\tag{10}
$$

The aggregate piracy rate $( p )$ is consequently a function of penalties, tax rates (and their squared values) in addition to the variance of the penalty rate, and the levels of transactions and the search costs. These factors interact with the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of absolute risk aversion $\gamma .$ All in all, Eq. (10) predicts that in interaction with income $( \gamma ) ,$ taxes (τ) and penalties $( \beta ) ,$ , higher risk aversion $( \gamma )$ should lead to lower levels of piracy in the economy. Higher (expected) penalties also contribute to reducing piracy. Tax effects are ambiguous. The potential value range of $p$ is $[ 0 ,$ , 1]. Note that the case of $\gamma = 0$ corresponds to that of risk-neutral infringers. Under risk neutrality $( \gamma = 0 )$ , the economic agent maximizes the expected value of G. This corresponds to the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order− − − − − − condition that is necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for a maximum since the objective function Eq. (3) is concave in h:− $G ( h ) =$ $\gamma { - } \tau h { - } ( \alpha ) / ( 2 ) h ^ { 2 } { - } \beta \tau ( \mathrm { y } { - } h )$ with $\beta > 1$ , $\partial E ( G ( h ) ) / \partial h = - \tau - \alpha h +$ $E ( \beta ) \tau = 0 ;$ then the optimal amount to be declared under risk neutrality is given by: $h ^ { \star } { } _ { N } = \tau [ E ( \beta ) - 1 ] / \alpha$ . The corresponding aggregate piracy rate is equal to $p _ { N } = 1 - h ^ { \star } { } _ { N } .$

The next section tests the validity of Eq. (10) predictions and related variables empirically in a set of panel regressions covering the 2007–2011 period. First, we examine the effect of each determinant, its squared term and interaction with risk aversion in a standard (random effect) panel analysis to identify the speci<sup>fi</sup>c contribution of each variable in predicting piracy. Second, after considering the multiple interactions between the various variables, we apply principal component analysis (PCA) to our panel data to construct economic and institutional factors that respect the nonlinear dynamics of Eq. (10). These factors are robust and practical enough to provide meaningful and comprehensive information about (y), (τ) and $( \beta )$ per country, without causing multicollinearity problems. We also control for country human/economic development and performance effects in our analysis. Aside from the fact that they have proved useful in explaining a number of social and economic phenomena worldwide, these extra dimensions are considered in this research because they factor in the regional differences or country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ governance) discussed earlier in the paper, control for rich and poor countries differences, and moderate model outcomes (e.g., high correlation between country performance and penalties, and strong positive association between human development and income).

$$

$$
P = b _ {1} U A I + b _ {2} T a x + b _ {3} \left( \begin{array}{c} G D P \\ H D I \\ I P R I \\ R O L \\ \text {Performance} \end{array} \right) + b _ {4} \left( \begin{array}{c} G D P ^ {2} \\ H D I ^ {2} \\ I P R I ^ {2} \\ R O L ^ {2} \\ \text {Performance} ^ {2} \end{array} \right) + e\tag{12}
$$

The various variables are represented under their logarithms in line with the dynamics described in Eq. (10). $P$ stands for the piracy rate per country. UAI stands for uncertainty avoidance and is used as a proxy for $\gamma .$ Tax (tax rate) is a proxy for τ. GDP per capita and HDI are (substitute) indicators for income (i.e., variable $\gamma$ in the theoretical model). IPRI (intellectual property rights index), ROL (rule of law) and Performance (country performance) are proxies for penalties β. GDP, HDI, IPRI, ROL and Performance effects are not tested together in one statistical model because of potential multicollinearity problems. We address this issue in the section ‘Additional results and principal component regressions’ by performing factor analysis on the data set.

Considering the highly nonlinear dynamics of our risk aversion model and the multiple interactions between the various indicators in Eqs. (11)–(12), we also apply PCA to our panel data to construct three unbiased economic and institutional factors that may serve as (income-based, taxbased and penalty-based) piracy rates’ determinants along risk aversion (UAI). Combining GDP, Tax, HDI, IPRI, ROL and country performance information, these three factors can be considered reliable statistical proxies for (y), (τ) and (β) per country. The PCA-based regressions are, therefore, as follows:

$$
P = c _ {1} U A I + c _ {2} \binom{E c o n o m i c P C 1}{E c o n o m i c P C 2} + c _ {3} I n s t i t u t i o n a l P C 1 + e,\tag{13}
$$

where EconomicPC1 = Δ (GDP, HDI, Tax), EconomicPC2 = Θ (Tax, HDI, GDP) and InstitutionalPC1 = Ψ (IPRI, ROL, Performance). Δ, Θ and Ψ are nonlinear functions.

## Data sources and descriptive statistics

Our dependent variable is the piracy rate P over the years 2007–2011 as provided by the Business Software Alliance (BSA). This rate consists of a complex mix of parameters including the availability of legitimate and pirated software, broadband access and consumer vs business ownership. The highest piracy rates are found in less developed countries in the regions of Asia, Middle East and Africa, and Central and Eastern Europe. Lower rates are in Western Europe and North America. For this and the other reasons cited above (e.g., high association with income), we consider country human development as an incomebased factor explaining piracy rates. We rely on the human development index (HDI) per country to account for human development effects in our models. HDI data are obtained from UNDP HDI reports (2011).

Data on different tax rates are collected to include taxes on goods and services (% value added of industry and services), tax revenue as percentage of GDP, the highest marginal corporate tax rate, the highest marginal individual tax rate, and taxes on income, pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and capital gains. Different types of taxes have been attempted with the selection of the variable that causes the least estimation bias in the regressions.

The tax rates per country are retrieved from the World Bank economic database. GDP per capita is also collected from the World Bank economic database. IPRI, ROL and Performance, all proxies for β in Eq. (10), are taken from the Property Rights Alliance reports (PR Alliance, 2011), World Bank governance indicators (e.g., Kaufmann et al, 2010) and Euromonitor country risk reports (ECR, 2011), respectively. IPRI measures copyrights enforcement in countries by considering their legal and political environment, physical and intellectual property rights (Horst, 2007). ROL is about the extent to which people abide by and have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the rules of society, providing an indication of the quality of contract enforcement and property rights per country (Kaufmann et al, 2010). Since it takes into consideration both de facto and de jure institutions, ROL is a good proxy for the strength of the intellectual property rights regime. Performance is a country risk score based on country <sup>fi</sup>nancial, structural, economic and political data and is indicative of country quality of governance. A low risk score implies weak country governance. We employ this outcome (or de facto) measure as an additional/alternative proxy for β in our analysis. IPRI, ROL and Performance are combined into one institutional β factor in the principal component regressions. HDI, GDP and taxes are combined into two economic factors (i.e., one tax-based and another income-based).

To account for risk attitudes, γ in Eq. (10), and potential regional differences, the logarithm of the index of uncertainty avoidance by Geert Hofstede (2001) is employed as a proxy for the aggregate risk avoidance behavior per country. This measure has been used in prior empirical research examining economic issues of corruption, investment and wealth (Husted, 1999; Robertson & Watson, 2004). It has also been examined in studies dealing with the impact of uncertainty tolerance and risk avoidance on a number of economic factors (e.g., Huang, 2008; Inklaar & Yang, 2012).

Data sources, variable speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and descriptive statistics are all summarized in Table 1. Our data set covers 87 countries, the maximum number for which data was available. The average piracy rate in the sample is 0.59 with a median rate of 0.63. The lowest piracy rate is 0.19 in the U.S. whereas the highest one is 0.95 in Georgia. Correlation analysis of the various independent constructs revealed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive associations (⩾ 0.75) between GDP, IPRI, ROL, Performance and HDI (other correlation effects are in line with standard regression assumptions). This justi<sup>fi</sup>es our choice of statistical models, our principal component approach and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c information structures adopted in Eqs. (11)–(13).

## Regression results

The random effect panel regression method is applied using indicators for all the explanatory factors described in Eqs. (11)–(13). Standard collinearity diagnostics were performed to investigate linear dependence among the independent variables (all variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors are less than 5 and statistical tolerance levels are greater than 0.2). Based on this, we present our regression estimates in Tables 2 and 3 and discuss outcomes in relation to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in each of the selected statistical models. Our choice of statistical models is guided by the results derived in Eq. (10).

Columns (1)–(16) in Table 2 summarize regression results for Eqs. (11)–(12). We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, on its own, risk avoidance (UAI) positively affects piracy rates while GDP per capita (down to a level), human development (HDI), intellectual protection rights (IPRI) and rule of law (ROL) are all negatively associated with software piracy. We also observe that higher tax rates (Tax) tend to increase piracy but not in the presence of or in interaction with income (GDP or HDI). In line with the theoretical predictions of the previous section, Columns (4), (7), (10), (13) and (16) show that, in interaction with income, penalties and governance, risk aversion (UAI) is negatively associated with piracy. This important result underlines the conditional effect of risk aversion on piracy. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that weaker country governance and lower country performance will generally increase piracy (i.e., albeit nonlinearly). Table 3 extends these <sup>fi</sup>ndings by examining the impact of risk aversion on piracy rates after combining income-based, tax-based and penalty-based data into three principal economic and institutional components. The PCA approach is followed to avoid potential multicollinearity problems that may arise from examining the joint effects of HDI, ROL, IPRI, GDP and Performance on piracy in one multivariate model.

Besides providing support for our risk aversion-based piracy model and hinting at the need to consider population behavior (i.e., risk-rewards attributes) in policy making, our overall <sup>fi</sup>ndings underline the relevance of human development and country institutions in explaining software piracy rates. Risk aversion can reduce piracy only under strong country institutions and high economic wellbeing. In the absence of such factors, risk aversion can encourage piracy and tax evasion.

## Discussion and conclusions

Our empirical results are robust and consistent with the predictions drawn from the theory presented in the section ‘Theoretical model of aggregate piracy rate under risk’. The relevant variables (y, τ, β, γ) predicted by the risk aversion model appear in our empirical estimates and contain enough information to explain software piracy rates worldwide. The introduction of the human development factor in the analysis also puts an important emphasis on the relationship between country development and piracy and shows how higher human development and better country governance can lead to lower rates of piracy. This is in accord with the recent conclusions of Yang et al (2009) on the negative effect of economic well-being on piracy rates. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings, while supporting our decisionmaking framework for software piracy under risk aversion, also validate prior empirical investigations that used socioeconomic variables in their analyses. The signs of the various determinants (e.g., rule of law, country governance and intellectual property rights) of piracy rates too seem to corroborate <sup>fi</sup>ndings from previous research. The inverted U-shaped or the Kuznet effects of GDP are also well con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. We extend these <sup>fi</sup>ndings by underlining the conditional negative effect of risk aversion on piracy rates and the role of taxes in the various relationships.

The empirical evidence in this paper can be used to re<sup>fi</sup>ne economic policies and instruments pursued for monitoring economic and social development worldwide. Important insights into the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of income taxes, the level of per capita income besides attitudes towards risk and uncertainty in each country have been revealed to be related to software piracy. It is also shown that human development does have an impact on piracy rates with clear reduced effects under higher HDI. The move of economies towards higher levels of human development and better country governance with enhancement of income provides the major directions of changes for software piracy reduction. This calls for the necessity to strengthen country economic and social policies through further revisions of income and <sup>fi</sup>scal measures as means to ensure successful outcomes on how to manage and deal with deviating behavior, illegal activities and the informal economy. Variations related to country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c context and economic structure should also account for population behavior and agents’ risk attitudes in policy formulation exercises.

But, additional policies might be needed to accelerate population access to better environments, infrastructure and resources. This includes incentives for the development of insurance schemes and safety nets that would provide means of accounting for the sources of risk and uncertainty prevailing in each economy. This is mainly desirable for developing economies where better visibility of economic agents can lead to higher economic and social performance, better socio-economic inclusion and then to lower piracy rates. Other aspects to consider improving in policy making and that have proven to be relevant determinants of software piracy in extant research, but have not been covered independently or explicitly in this contribution, include country economic freedom and literacy levels.

Despite the interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings, a few limitations characterize this study as the use of macroeconomic data has well-known disadvantages. Some of our results should be interpreted with caution. Identifying explicit indicators that fully proxy for penalty levels, penalties enforcement and risk attitudes in a given country is not a straightforward task when examining country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues, population clusters or a certain type of societal behavior. As a result, we had to rely on aggregate data to capture country characteristics and describe software pirates’ ‘average’ behavior per country. Microeconomic and disaggregated data or even large-scale survey data would have provided more detailed and comprehensive evidence. Furthermore, a number of theoretical assumptions such as the elimination of network effects and transaction costs, and homogeneity might also limit some of the conclusions of this research.

Nevertheless, the overall empirical results produced in this paper are clear. We show that risk-reward systems, population behavior, tax mechanisms, per capita income, human development and country institutions are likely to be the major instruments that policy makers need to work on to reduce software piracy in the global economy. A series of questions remain to be answered in prospective research. This covers aspects of counterfeiting that go beyond the software piracy problem. Although related theoretical models can be employed, there are data limitations that constrain the detailed study of these key (human) development issues. In addition, it would be interesting to analyze in future research whether behavioral and experimental economics can help re<sup>fi</sup>ne both the knowledge on software piracy and deviating behavior in world economies.
