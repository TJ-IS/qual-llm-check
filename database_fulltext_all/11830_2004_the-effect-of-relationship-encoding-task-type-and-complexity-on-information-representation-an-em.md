# THE EFFECT OF RELATIONSHIP ENCODING, TASK TYPE, AND COMPLEXITY ON INFORMATION REPRESENTATION: AN EMPIRICAL EVALUATION OF 2D AND 3D LINE GRAPHS<sup>1</sup>

By: Nanda Kumar Zicklin School of Business Baruch College City University of New York New York, NY 10010 U.S.A. Nanda\_Kumar@baruch.cuny.edu

Izak Benbasat Sauder School of Business University of British Columbia Vancouver, British Columbia V6T 1Z2 CANADA izak.benbasat@ubc.ca

## Abstract

Most of the recent research in data visualization has focused on technical and aesthetic issues involved in the manipulation of graphs, specifically on features that facilitate data exploration to make graphs interactive and dynamic. The present research identifies a gap in the existing knowledge of graph construction, namely potential problems in both 3D and 2D graphs that will impede comprehension of information when three or more variables are used in a graphical representation. Based on theories regarding perceptual issues of graph construction (Bertin 1981; Pinker 1991), we evaluate specific cases where 3D graphs may outperform 2D graphs, and vice-versa. Two experiments have been conducted to test these hypotheses, and 3D graphs have been found to consistently outperform 2D graphs in all of our experimental scenarios. A third experiment has been conducted to identify situations where 2D graphs might perform at least as well as 3D graphs, but its results suggest that 3D graphs outperform 2D graphs even for simple tasks, thus leading to the conclusion that 3D graphs perform better than 2D graphs under all task conditions with more than two variables.

Keywords: Computer graphics, 3-D graphics, human information processing, information presentation, information retrieval, information characteristics, information processing

## Introduction

The development of sophisticated data mining and data presentation applications in the last 10 years have led to a new level of interest in the field of data visualization, the art and science of visually representing n-dimensional data. Data visualization consists of two distinct phases: (1) graph construction, including all decisions that go into the construction of a graph and (2) graph manipulation, comprising all decisions that go into the interactive modification and manipulation of graphs by end-users (Buja et al. 1996). Until the mid-1990s, a considerable amount of research investigated effective methods of representing information in two-dimensional graphs (Bennett and Toms 1993; Dickson et al. 1986; Jarvenpaa and Dickson 1988; Spence 1990; Tan and Benbasat 1990). While most of this work focused on constructing 2D graphs, some research in scientific data visualization has also addressed information representation and manipulation using n-dimensional graphs (Colet and Aaronson 1995; Wickens and Carswell 1995; Wickens et al. 1994). More recently, several researchers have addressed image construction methods for visualizing multidimensional managerial data (Jones 1994; Zhang 1996, 1998).

In general, recent research has shifted away from the production of static images and toward features that make graphs more interactive. Indeed, most of the recent data visualization research has focused more on the manipulation of graphs (e.g., filtering, querying and interface design) rather than on graph construction (Buja et al. 1996; Cook et al. 1995; Hurley and Buja 1990; Swayne et al. 1998). Almost invariably, all of these studies have involved high density and high dimensional data (Sutherland et al. 2000). Some of this research has focused on the design of user interfaces, advocating the use of motion or animation as another channel to convey information (Baecker and Small 1990; Bartram 1997; Ware 2000).

Notwithstanding these studies, there is still a surprising paucity of cumulative empirical research concerning the graph construction stage, specifically on information representation using 3D graphs (Card et al. 1999). Past research on information representation using 3D graphs has yielded equivocal results in terms of performance differences among 2D graphs, 3D graphs, and tables (Dull and Teagarden 1999; Meyer et al. 1997; Sollenberger and Milgarm 1993); therefore, the present paper seeks more empirical results by focusing on the first step of the data visualization process: graph construction. In this research we identify relationship encoding, where some relationships are more readily available for comprehension than others, as a limitation that will have an impact on the performance of 2D graphs (Shah and Carpenter 1995). We also believe that relationship encoding will bring into question some of the past research on 2D graphs, specifically research that posits that line graphs are better than bar graphs to understand trends. On the other hand, previous research has noted ambiguity of depth perceptions as a major limitation of 3D graphs (Sollenberger and Milgarm 1993; Wickens et al. 1994). In addition, familiarity and complexity have been known to have strong impacts on graph comprehension (Kennedy et al. 1998; Meyer et al. 1997). Familiar graphs typically take less time to comprehend than unfamiliar graphs. However, the effect of complexity on graph comprehension has yielded mixed results (Meyer et al. 1997). Thus, our objective is to predict conditions where 3D graphs might outperform 2D Cartesian graphs, and vice-versa, and to test these predictions in a series of laboratory experiments.

In the next section, we briefly review past literature on data visualization and introduce a model of the intellectual processes involved in reading and understanding graphs, as discussed by Pinker (1991). We then attempt to situate our research in a broader context, by expanding on the work of Bertin (1981). The limitations of 2D and 3D graphs are briefly discussed. Our research hypotheses, based on the theories outlined and developed in the previous sections, are then enumerated. The general research methodology is described. The three sections that follow present the results of our three experiments. The results of the study are discussed and our conclusions presented.

## Pinkerís Theory of Graph Comprehension

A graph, according to Pinker (1991), is a representation that tries to communicate to readers a set of n-tuples of values in mathematical scales, using objects whose visual dimensions (e.g., length, position, lightness, and shape) correspond to the respective scales in the representation (nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio-scales), and whose values on each dimension (i.e., an objectís particular length, position, and so on) correlate with the values on the corresponding scales. A graph reader must do two things to comprehend a graph, according to Pinker. First, the reader must mentally represent the objects in the graph in a certain way. For example, in a bar graph, one must think of the bars in terms of their heights and their positions along the x-axis, but not necessarily in terms of the jagged edges formed by the tops of the bars. Pinker introduces the concept of visual descriptions to accomplish the first task. This visual description is the output of the mechanisms of visual perception that shows the identity of the sceneís parts and their relationship among them (see Figure 1). When a reader looks at a graph, the information from a graph arrives at his/her nervous system as a two-dimensional pattern of intensities, referred to as visual array, on the retina. This raw information from the visual array is converted to memory representations signifying knowledge of what the visual marks of the graphs mean through visual descriptions.

Subsequently, graph readers must remember, or deduce, which aspects of the visual constituents of the graph stand for each of the mathematical scales that the graph represents. According to Pinker, the correct correlations are made through graph schema, which are remembered knowledge in some domain, consisting of descriptions that contain slots or parameters for as yet unknown

These types of cognitive connections are made by instantiating graph schema, according to Pinker. This involves particular processes (see Figure 1).

Matching: identification of a graph as belonging to a particular graph type.

Message assembly: creation of a conceptual message out of the instantiated graph schema. It is important to remember that not all information on a graph is necessarily translated directly into conceptual messages due to short-term memory capacity limitations, noise in the match process, and bad construction of graphs that highlights unimportant information, among other reasons.

Interrogation: retrieval and codification of new information based on conceptual questions. This process happens when a reader needs some piece of information that is not already in the conceptual message. This initiates a top-down search to find the desired information.

Inferential processes: application of mathematical and logical rules to the entries of conceptual message.

An important implication of the theory is that if the desired information is not in the conceptual message, it will have to be generated by the top-down interrogation process or the inferential process. For a limited-capacity system, such as the human mind, this would mean that a particular type of information would be harder to extract from a given graph to the extent that inferential processes and top-down encoding processes, as opposed to conceptual message look-up, must be used. Clearly, this implies cognitive costs for the graph reader. Thus, extracting information from a graph is easiest when a visual description has those predicates that directly answer the conceptual question asked of the reader. For example, line graphs should be best for illustrating trends and interactions, since there exist many visual predicates for line shapes.

## Graph Construction with Three Components

While Pinkerís work provides a foundation for understanding how users comprehend graphs, there is still a need to understand whether it is possible to use certain fundamental principles to construct graphs that portray n-dimensional data sets. Bertin (1981) makes a case for using the level of organization of the information componentónominal, ordinal, interval, and ratioóas the basis for construction of graphs. A graphic system has eight visual variables to represent these components of information, according to Bertin: the two dimensions of the plane of the graph, in addition to the size, value, texture, color, orientation, and shape of the graph. The visual variables chosen to represent the individual components of information must be at least at the same level of organization as the components they represent. For example, if an information component is ratiolevel in nature, then the visual variable chosen must also be able to represent such information. If the component is age-group categorized into children, adults, and elders, then the visua variable chosen must have at least an ordinal level of organization.

Only the two dimensions of the plane are complete in terms of their levels of organization, such that they can represent any kind of information component. Hence, when a graph to be constructed involves only two components, they should be represented on a 2D plane, which has the highest level of organization and is capable of representing any type of component. When there are three components to be represented, the key to constructing a good graph is choosing the appropriate visual variable for the third component. With eight different visual variables to choose from, there seems to be no dearth of choices. With three components, one can use 3D as well as 2D graphs for representing these three components. Because of the variety of options available, however, it is possible to make many design mistakes when designing a graph with three components.

We use the level of organization of the three components, whether nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio, as the main characteristic that helps in deciding the possible types of graph construction with three components. For all cases when at least one of the components is nominal or ordinal, a single 2D graph can be constructed to represent the same information without any redundancies. For example, information with two ratio-interva scale (continuous) components and one nomina component can be represented either as a 3D line graph or as a 2D line graph. In the case of a 2D graph, the two dimensions of the plane would be used to represent the two ratio-interval scale components, and an appropriate visual variable would be used to represent the nominal component. For instance, if the nominal component were gender, then there would be two lines, one each for males and females.

On the other hand, in the case wherein all three components are interval or ratio (continuous) scale, the information can only be represented in 2D form using a pair of graphs, according to Bertinís rules of construction. In this case, the information about the third ratio component cannot be represented on a 2D plane using visual variables (size, value, texture, color, orientation, and shape). Hence, the information has to be split up into two 2D graphs. In this situation, the information can be represented without any redundancies, or it can be represented with redundancies to make integration of information across the two graphs easier.

In a study of the case where all three components are continuous, Dull and Tegarden (1999) have found that the 3D graphs perform far better than 2D graphs for tasks that required an integrated understanding of information presented. They used two 2D graphs and one equivalent 3D graph for comparison. Other research comparing pairs of 2D graphs against individual 3D graphs has demonstrated that 3D graphs perform better than 2D graphs when emergent properties (e.g., surface visualization of three-dimensional graphs) are required to understand relationships in the data represented (Wickens et al. 1994). In the current research, however, we investigate cases where users have the option of using either a single 2D graph or a single 3D graph using the same data set. Thus, our research covers all cases where at least one of the three components is represented as a visual variable on the 2D planeóthat is, represented in categorical rather that in a continuous form. The implications of this claim of generalizability and its limitations are explored more in detail in the conclusions section by juxtaposing this claim with the specific results of the study.

## Limitations of 2D and 3D Graphs

## xñy and yñz Relationship Encoding

The conceptual limitations of line graphs have been well documented by Shah and Carpenter (Shah and Carpenter 1995), who empirically showed that for a 2D line graph with three components, some relationships are more readily available to the users and require minimal processing while other relationships may not be as readily available and may require considerable processing.

In the example shown in Figure 2, the relationships between the components represented on X and Y-axes are more readily available (e.g., relationships between noise level and test score in Figure 2: As the noise level increases from 10 to 50dB, what is the steepest change in the test score you observe when the temperature is at 70F?). However, the relationships between the component represented on the Y axis and the third component represented using the legend (temperature for Figure 2) are not as readily available and require more processing (e.g., relationships between temperature and test score in Figure 2: As the temperature increases from 50 to 90F, what is the steepest change in the test score you observe when the noise level is at 30dB?). For this 2D graph, we call the encoding of the relationships between the components represented on the X and Y axes as xñy relationship encoding, and those represented on the Y axis and third component represented in the Legend as yñz relationship encoding.

Pinkerís theory of graph comprehension provides an explanation why xñy relationship encoding is perceptually more salient than yñz relationship encoding for 2D line graphs. While the structures and processes described in Pinkerís theory permit any quantitative information whatsoever to be extractable from a graph, in practice, ìlimits on short-term memory and on processing resources will make specific sorts of information easier or more difficult to extractî (Pinker 1991, p. 107).

First, the information represented on 2D line graphs in terms of xñy relationships is directly available as conceptual messages at the message assembly stage (see Figure 1), while that represented in terms of yñz relationships is not. This is due to the fact that in 2D line graphs, xñy relationships use the two dimensions of the plane to represent relationships spatially while yñz relationships use one dimension of the plane to represent the second component and shapes (triangle for 50F, square for 60F, etc.) to represent the third component (see Figure 2). These shapes are organized spatially in a random order (when temperature is used as the third component, 50F is not the lowest point for any given value of noise level; 60F can occur either above or below 50F). This requires users to perform more scanning and search processes to encode the yñz relationships while they have little trouble in encoding xñy relationships directly (10 dB is always before 20 dB; spatially arranged). Of course, this is a problem only when users are interested in exploring and understanding relationships between all three components of information (comparison, trend, and pattern identification). When the intent is to extract a single data point, there is no need for significant additional scanning or search processes to recognize yñz relationships.

Furthermore, xñy relationships are represented using a line, which is ideal for exploring relationships between components of information (Pinker 1991; Spence 1990; Tan and Benbasat 1990). For example, a ìVî pattern (a decrease and an increase) in Figure 2 is more obvious than a similar pattern in yñz relationships. Hence, when we are dealing with three components of information, for a 2D line graph it becomes very important to understand which component should be represented as the legend. In Figure 2, noise level is represented on the x-axis and hence the relationships between noise level and test scores are more readily available than the relationships between temperatures and test scores. To make the relationships between temperatures and test scores more salient, they need to be represented on the two dimensions of the plane. On the other hand, this problem does not exist for 3D graphs as both xñy and yñz relationships are readily available because of the use of three axes to represent the Cartesian plane (see Figure 3).

We would further argue that this phenomenon wherein one set of relationships (either xñy or yñz) does worse than other set of relationships (xñy or yñz) is not just a limitation of line graphs, but is a generic limitation of all 2D graphs due to its use of a two-dimensional plane in depicting the relationship between three components. Pinker uses the term graph difficulty principle to describe this general phenomenon.

This problem for 2D graphs will become more prominent as we move away from traditional business graphics software to more advanced visualization tools, and as data mining tools are used to unearth relationships among three or more variables. If we specifically test for an overall understanding of data, a 3D graph might be more usefu because it facilitates an integrated understanding of data by simultaneously encoding both xñy and yñz relationships. In many data exploration activities powered by sophisticated data mining algorithms, users often ìfishî for unintuitive relationships without knowing ahead of time which relationships are important. Hence, a 3D graph that facilitates an integrative understanding of relationships between three components may be better than an equivalent 2D graph. Therefore, the purpose of the graph might have a significant impact on the choice of the graph format.

## Occlusion and Ambiguity of Depth Judgments

Past research has identified the following factors as typical disadvantages of 3D graphs: (1) occlusion, where the data in front hides the data represented at the back (Munzner 1998; Webber 1998) and (2) ambiguity of depth judgments (Andre et al. 1991; Gregory 1977). Researchers in computer graphics field have identified a number of algorithms and tools to ameliorate the impact of occlusion (Munzner 1998; Webber 1998). The occlusion problem can be addressed by letting the users interactively specify the portion of the graph they would like to view and automatically moving them to the ideal viewing point (Eades et al. 1997; Houle and Webber 1998). In the case of static graphs, this problem can be addressed simply by automatic calculation of best viewing point and subsequent display of the static graph (Webber 1998).

However, a graph displayed using an ideal viewing point will still be unable to support tasks that require depth judgments. In some cases, use of an ideal viewing point may in fact result in distortion of parts of the 3D graph (as this is typically projected over a 2D surface, such as a computer screen or paper) further worsening the situation vis-‡-vis depth judgments. The costs due to the ambiguity of depth judgments can be compensated to some extent through the provision of additional depth cues, such as image rotation and stereovision. However, empirical support for the efficacy of these techniques has been mixed (Dull and Teagarden 1999; Sollenberger and Milgarm 1993; Wickens et al. 1994). Another option in reducing the ambiguity of depth judgments is the use of labeling support: moving the mouse interactively over a data point resulting in the display of appropriate data. However, the use of both labeling and image rotation requires the use of a computer with at least some support for user interactivity, while the use of stereovision typically requires bulky accessories (Wickens et al. 1994).

In the current study, we have classified task types into two overarching categories, borrowing from the work of Tan and Benbasat (1991): elementary (simple data extraction) and advanced (comparisons and trends) task types. Elementary tasks require extraction of a single data point given the fixed values of other two components. For example, a sample elementary task that requires extraction of a specific data value from a graph (see Figures 2 and 3) may be characterized by the following question: What is the value of the test score when the temperature is at 70F and the noise level is at 30 dB?

Advanced tasks require a more integrated understanding of relationships between the components of information represented in a graph, and they may involve comparisons or identification of trends and patterns. The present research is primarily interested in task types that facilitate this kind of understanding. However, to isolate the impact of ambiguity of depth judgments, this research separates advanced tasks that require extraction of specific data values from advanced tasks that do not require extraction of specific data values. For example, a sample advanced task that requires extraction of a specific data value from a graph (see Figures 2 and 3) may be characterized using the following question: As the noise level increases, what is the steepest change in the test score you observe when the temperature is at 70F and at 80F? A different sample advanced task, without requiring extraction of a specific data value from a graph, may be characterized using the following question: As the noise level increases, do you observe any general pattern or trend emerging for the test score when the temperature is at 70F and at 80F? The answer for the second question could be specified using simple predicates such as increase or decrease, whereas the answer for the first question might require extraction of specific values to accurately compute the steepest change in the test scores.

We have generated questions that instantiate these two advanced task types using Tan and Benbasatís taxonomy (see Table 2 for sample questions generated for these two advanced task types). We argue that without any additional support, 3D graphs will be a poor match for advanced (as well as elementary) tasks that require extraction of specific values because of the ambiguity of depth in the graphs. On the other hand, 3D graphs will perform better than 2D graphs overall for advanced tasks that do not require extraction of specific values, as questions about yñz relationships will bring down the performance of the 2D line graphs.

## Hypotheses

This research argues that there is a dearth of empirical research on 3D graphs and their performance in relation to 2D graphs. Hence, graph type (2D and 3D graphs) is one of the variables of interest to this research. Previous research on 2D graphs has demonstrated that complexity of data and prior familiarity with a display interface interacts with the amount of time necessary to comprehend a graph (Meyer et al. 1997). This research will control for the effect of familiarity by training participants with a practice round and presenting two equivalent tests one after the other to allow participants reach a higher level of familiarity with the display and the task types; only the results from the test taken second will be used in the analysis of data. Thus, this research will primarily manipulate the following independent variables to measure their impact on graph comprehension:

Graph type (graph dimensionality): 2D or 3D graphs

ï Complexity of data: data sets with nine points or 25 points

Relationship encoding: xñy relationship encoding or yñz relationship encoding

As explained earlier, 3D graphs do not perform well when specific data values need to be extracted from the display because of the lack of depth cues while projecting a 3D graph onto a 2D computer screen. In fact, our pilot studies have indicated that participants who used 3D graphs to complete advanced tasks (or even elementary tasks) involving extraction of specific data values spent an inordinately long time to find answers for all such questions and most often vocally expressed the futility of such an attempt. Based on this feedback, it was decided to add extra support for these tasks, whereby users could display values associated with data point for both 2D and 3D graphs by interactively moving the mouse over that data point (labeling). This labeling support increases the performance of 3D graphs, ensuring that all of the following hypotheses hold true for all advanced tasks. Line graphs are used in this research because the main intent is to study advanced tasks that require an integrated understanding of all components used in the displays by comparisons and by identification of trends and patterns.

According to Pinkerís theory (1991), encoding of yñz relationships is a problem that is unique to 2D line graphs for advanced tasks (exploring relationships between all three components in a 2D graph). This relationship encoding difficulty for 2D line graphs was also shown empirically by Shah and Carpenter (1995). In addition, this difficulty can also be explained using the theory on elementary information processes (EIPs) and how these EIPs impact on strategy formulation and execution in comprehending information using quantitative displays (Kennedy et al. 1998). Strategy formulation can be defined as the process of determining what needs to be done and devising a sequence of EIPs (such as scan to, search, read, compare, and compute) to accomplish it. For a 2D line graph, the number of EIPs needed to retrieve information encoded as yñz relationships are significantly more than those needed for xñy relationships. More scan and search operations are needed to visually locate the component represented in the legend (than the component represented in the X or Y axes). Further, more operations will be needed to visually order the symbols represented by the third component (they are randomly organized for yñz relationships for any given level of X component). We also argue that the EIPs needed to retrieve information encoded as xñy relationships in 2D line graphs will be very similar to the EIPs needed to retrieve information encoded as either xñy or yñz relationships in 3D line graphs and hypothesize that the time taken to perform a given EIP will be the same across 2D and 3D graphs, an assumption that will be tested empirically. Hence, for a 2D line graph, the information that is represented as xñy relationships will take less time to be comprehended than the information that is represented as yñz relationships. On the other hand, in a 3D graph there is no difference in the time taken to comprehend information that is represented as xñy relationships and information that is represented as yñz relationships.

H1: There will be an interaction effect between graph type and relationship encoding for the time taken to comprehend graphs.

This interaction effect will be due to the prediction that there will be no difference in time taken to answer both xñy and yñz encoded relationships using 3D graphs, while for 2D graphs, yñz encoded relationships will take more time than their xñy encoded counterparts. When a graph is used primarily for data exploration purposes, users do not always know in advance which relationship (xñy or yñz) will be of more interest. If the relationship that a user is interested in is encoded as a yñz relationship in a 2D line graph, then it will take the user more time to detect that particular relationship. On the other hand, there would be no such problem for xñy encoded information in 2D graphs and all information in 3D graphs.

H2: There will be a main effect of graph type. The time taken to comprehend information using 3D graphs will be less than that for 2D graphs.

H3: There will be a main effect of relationship encoding. When 2D and 3D graphs are taken together, xñy relationship encoding is expected to take less time than yñz relationship encoding.

In general, as graph complexity increases, the time taken to comprehend the graph also increases, as previous studies have demonstrated (Casali and Gaylin 1988; Meyer et al. 1997; Spence and Lewandowsky 1991). However, graph complexity can be conceptualized in three ways, according to Meyer et al. (1997): (1) the number of data points in a graph, (2) the way in which this data is configured (whether 12 data points appear as one line of 12 data points, two lines of six data points or three lines of four data points), and (3) the regularity of displayed data (whether there are lines crossing over each other resulting in cluttering, or whether the lines appear to follow a less cluttered, easily perceivable pattern). When complexity is conceptualized based on the number of data points, the time taken to comprehend the graphs increases as complexity increases (Meyer et al. 1997). In the present study, therefore, we conceptualize complexity based on the number of data points and hypothesize that as complexity increases, the time taken to comprehend a graph will increase.

## H4: There will be a main effect of complexity. As the complexity of a graph increases, the time taken to comprehend information from the graph will increase.

Complexity affects performance for various tasks and displays differentially, according to previous studies (Casali and Gaylin 1988; Meyer et al. 1997). While it takes more time to comprehend information encoded as yñz relationships than xñy relationships, this performance difference is further augmented as the complexity of the graph increases. We also posit that a similar effect will be observed for the dimensionality of the graph, and we predict that complexity will differentially impact the comprehension performance of 2D and 3D graphs. Complexity, decision task types, and graph types have been shown to exert an interactive effect on the strategy formulation process in trying to comprehend information from a display (Kennedy et al. 1998). We believe that as complexity increases, yñz encoded relationships and 2D graphs (graph type) entail much longer scanning and visual search EIPs in the graph comprehension process. Hence, we hypothesize interaction effects for the following:

H5: There will be an interaction effect between complexity and graph type. As complexity increases, the time taken to answer questions using 2D graphs will increase more rapidly than that for 3D graphs.

H6: There will be an interaction effect between complexity and relationship encoding. As complexity increases, the time taken to answer yñz encoded questions will increase more rapidly than that for xñy encoded questions.

Although third-order interaction between the independent variables (graph type, relationship encoding, and complexity) is expected to occur, no attempt has been made to explain its significance because of interpretation difficulties.

## Research Methodology

Three laboratory experiments using mixed designs have been conducted to test the hypotheses stated in the previous section for different task types. Table 3 shows the independent variables used in each study, their operationalization, and the set of independent variables manipulated in each study.

## Experiments 1 and 2

The results from Experiments 1 and 2 are necessary to justify the experimental set-up and operationalization for Experiment 3; therefore, this section discusses the research methodology for the first two experiments and defers the discussion of Experiment 3 to a later section. Dimensionality of the graphs (2D and 3D) is the between-subjects variable in all experiments. Complexity and relationship encoding, on the other hand, are the within-subjects variables.

The graphs used in all experiments include three components. Participants were asked questions about the relationships between noise levels, temperatures (in an exam hall) and the average test scores of a class taking an exam. Categorical representations of temperature and noise level (three or five levels for each of these components and ordering matters), and test scores were used as the three components of information. The first experiment tested participantsí graph comprehension for advanced task types that did not require extraction of specific data values. The second experiment tested graph comprehension for advanced task types that required extraction of specific data values (please see Table 2 for a sample of questions generated to instantiate these task types).

Complexity of data was operationalized based on the number of data points in a graph. A nine datapoint graph in which the two categorical variables have three levels each (Temperature: 50, 60, and 70 Fahrenheit; Noise Level: 10, 20, and 30 dB) was considered a simple graph. A 25 data-point graph wherein the two categorical variables have five levels each was considered a complex graph. Each participant was given two back-to-back tests (a set of questions that tested graph comprehension systematically) to reduce the impact of familiarity. Within each test, the order of presentation of individual questions was varied randomly. The two tests were administered in reverse order for consecutive participants to balance the effect of individual tests. The test that was administered second was considered to be more familiar to the users and the data from this test was used for all analyses. Each test involved comprehending four xñy and four yñz encoded relationships using simple graphs as well as complex graphs for each graph type (2D and 3D). Figures 2 and 3 show samples of the graphs used in the experiments.

The time required for participants to answer the questions was used as the indicator of their performance. The participants were offered incentives based on their ability to answer the questions as accurately as possible and as quickly as possible. It is possible for a subject to spend a lot of time looking at a difficult graph, and to understand the graph well, and yet to make a mistake because of the time pressure. On the other hand, if the incentives are not offered for both answering

## Experimental Setup and Procedures

Undergraduate and graduate students from a large public university were recruited to participate in the experiments. Each experiment had a sample size of 80, and participants followed the same procedures for each experiment. There were only two differences between the two experiments.

The first study used advanced tasks that did not require extraction of specific data values, while the second study used advanced tasks that did require extraction of specific values. This was tested in two studies rather than targeting another within-subject factor because of time constraints. The experimental task took about 60 to 90 minutes to complete, and addition of another within-subjects factor could result in fatigue.

Both 2D and 3D graphs had labeling support (individual data values shown when the mouse was moved over the data points) for the second study while there was no such support for the first study. The graph in Figure 2 shows an instance where the test score value of 55 is displayed on the top right corner of the graph in response to the movement of the mouse over a particular data point by the user.

Participants were paid \$15 to participate in the study and were offered additional performancebased incentives to ensure that they performed to the best of their abilities. The participants were informed that they would be rank-ordered based on their ability to answer questions accurately and quickly (total time taken/proportion of correct answers) and that the top 25 percent of the participants would receive cash prizes ranging from \$10 to \$32 in increments of \$2. At the beginning of each session, participants were given a tutorial that simulated test conditions to familiarize themselves with the questions and graphs used in the actual test. The tutorial at the beginning of the experiment not only helped participants to familiarize themselves with the graphs, but also, more importantly, helped them understand what the questions asked. This was done to ensure that the time measured in actual experimental conditions reflected the time taken to understand the graph and not the time taken to understand the questions. After completing the tutorial and ensuring that they understood the task at hand, the participants were randomly assigned to either a 2D or 3D graph and asked to complete two equivalent tests one after the other. The order of tests was reversed for the next participant to negate the possibility of actual tests influencing results. The data from the second test was used for further analysis.

## Discussion

The cumulative results from the three experiments lead to the conclusion that when provided with support (in the form of dynamic labeling) for overcoming the ambiguity of depth problem, 3D graphs outperform 2D graphs under all experimental conditions. The results of the first two experiments are consistent with our expectation that for information that is encoded as yñz relationships, 3D graphs would perform better than their 2D counterparts (see Figures 4A and 5A). Similarly, there is little or no difference in performance between information that is encoded as xñy and yñz relationships for 3D graphs. The unexpected result is that for information that is encoded as xñy relationships in 2D graphs, 3D graphs still outperform 2D graphs. We proposed visual cluttering as a possible reason for the dominance of 3D graphs in these two experiments.

Visual cluttering is a problem that afflicts 2D graphs more than the 3D graphs especially when data complexity is high, thus having an adverse impact on the performance of the 2D graphs. The effect of visual cluttering is less pronounced for 3D graphs, as they represent all three components spatially in a three-dimensional plane. The first two experiments operationalized complexity in terms of the number of data points without regard for visual cluttering. An inspection of the graphs used in the first two experiments revealed that many of them were cluttered visually (e.g., too many criss-crossing lines, and two data points occupying the same spatial location). Therefore, a third experiment, which controlled for the impact of visual cluttering by operationalizing complexity based on regularity of displayed data, was conducted (Meyer et al. 1997).

In order to facilitate conditions where 2D graphs should perform at least as well as 3D graphs, the third experiment used elementary tasks (requiring extraction of a single data value) in conjunction with simpler graphs (nine-point data sets). Even under these conditions favorable to 2D graphsó nine-point data set, extraction of single data values, and visually uncluttered graphsó3D graphs performed better than 2D graphs in the third study. The results for all three experiments are in line with the hypothesized relationships in that within 2D graphs xñy relationships are better handled than yñz relationships, and for 3D graphs there is no difference. However, the across-graph comparison shows xñy relationships are better handled by 3D graphs as compared to 2D ones and 3D graphs outperform 2D graphs even for elementary tasks. What accounts for this difference?

Previous research has shown that ìan objectís spatial location has a different perceptual status than its color, lightness, texture or shapeî (Pinker 1991, p. 79; see also Spence 2001, p. 59). Kubovy (1981) has shown that information represented using spatial dimensions has certain indispensable properties (e.g., selective attention; see Pinker 1991, p. 83) that makes it better than representing information using other attributes such as color, lightness, texture, or shape. Accordingly then, in the case of xñy relationships as well as others, since a 3D graph uses a threedimensional plane to represent the three components of information, it is a more efficient way to represent information (for three components) than an equivalent two dimensional representation using a 2D graph. When a 2D graph represents the third component of information as legend, it may take more time to understand which shape represents which level of that third component (e.g., in Figure 2, squares represent data values at 60F).

In our experiments, shapes (triangles, squares, and circles) were used to represent the individual levels of the third component. In the examples we used, this limitation can be overcome to a certain extent by using numbers to represent the different levels of the third component (for example, in Figure 2, 60F instead of the square to represent 60F). This labeling system is also more appropriate considering that (temperature represented as categorical component) ordering matters for the third component (e.g., as temperature increases from 60F to 70F). However, not all situations lend themselves to this solution. For example, it is difficult to represent components with longer labels or descriptions (e.g., high, medium, low) and we have to revert back to using artificial symbols to represent these levels of the third component. In any case, it is worthwhile to conduct a future study that empirically investigates whether or not the performance of a 2D graph in representing xñy as well as yñz relationships can be improved by using the labeling described above (numbers instead of shapes).

Designers need to come up with software that can ameliorate this situation by offering users more flexibility in terms of the symbols they can use to represent the levels of the third component, as most graphical programs currently offer limited number of choices in terms of what symbols can be used. However, even if there is flexibility in terms of symbols that can be used, this still doesnít completely address the cluttering problem. The third experiment created conditions more favorable to 2D graphs and showed that uncluttered 2D graphs still performed worse than equivalent 3D graphs. But if complexity increases in terms of more data points and tasks get more difficult, visual cluttering may yet degrade the performance of 2D graphs even more (a triangle gets hidden by a square; 50 and 60F values overlapping together, thus illegible; lines crisscrossing). Hence, any study conducted to examine the impact of different labeling systems must also include complexity (both number of data points as well as visual cluttering) as an independent variable.

Any study that tests the comprehension of graphs needs to be careful in balancing the need for correctly measuring accuracy with that for measuring time. For example, it is desirable to have an incentive system to induce participants to answer questions as accurately as possible, but not at the expense of spending an excessive, and unusual, amount of time in studying the graphs, more so than they would in normal circumstances of graph comprehension.

The validity of using time as a dependent variable hinges on the participants getting most of the answers correct, thus indicating that they made an honest effort. The questions used in the three experiments were designed so that they could be answered correctly using both the 2D and 3D graphs. In fact, this design objective is supported by the results from all three experiments that show that accuracy rates are high overall and do not differ significantly among treatments. Thus, the key performance issue is what graph formats will lead to such results in less time, i.e., time captures the effort needed to understand a graph and answer the question asked. This is why we believe that time is a better indicator of comprehension than accuracy in this context.

However, we still performed additional analyses to confirm the results based on time only, by taking both time and accuracy into account. The composite score used to rank order the participants in terms of their overall performance which takes into account the impact of both accuracy and time (namely, dependent variable = time/percentage of the number of correct answers) was utilized as a second dependent variable to run a similar set of ANOVA analyses, with the same independent variables, for all three studies. While not reported here, the pattern of these new results for all three studies was consistent with the results obtained by using time alone as the dependent variable with one major difference found only in Experiment 1: a nonsignificant interaction between complexity and relationship encoding.<sup>2</sup> However, the magnitude of all of the F-values had decreased for these latter sets of analyses, consistent with our expectation that introducing accuracy will increase the error variance thus deflating the F-values. This relates back to our original contention that since all questions posed could be answered accurately (using either a 2D or 3D graph) with conscientious effort (as the results indicate), time is a better indicator of graph comprehension than accuracy.

Even though, this work specifically investigated line graphs, relationship encoding as a limiting factor applies to all 2D Cartesian graphs. For example, in the case of a 2D bar graph (see Figure 7), if the third component represented using the legend has five categories, then for each level of the information represented as the X component, five bars representing each level of the third component are sequentially located in space. Since all levels of the third component are grouped spatially together for a given value of the X component, this enables comprehension of information that depicts relations between the third component represented using the legend and the component represented using the Y-axis (yñz relationships) more saliently than the relations between the components represented on the X and Y axes. Past research typically examined information that is encoded as xñy relationships for both line graphs and bar graphs and found that line graphs are better than bar graphs to understand trends. However, we would argue that bar graphs might perform better than line graphs in understanding trend information that is represented as yñz relationships (with three components of information on a 2D graph). This shows the usefulness of separating the two different kinds of relationship encoding in understanding graph performance. Further empirical research is needed to show that the performance of bar graphs may be better than line graphs in understanding yñz encoded trend relationships.

This research specifically investigated line graphs, but we believe that the results are generalizable to

Cartesian graphs with three components of information. Since we wanted control (and used experiments), instantiation of graphs is necessary. This limits the generalizability; Cartesian graphs were instantiated as 2D and 3D graphs; 2D and 3D graphs were instantiated as 2D and 3D line graphs. Labeling support was added to Experiments 2 and 3 due to our pilot study results that showed the inability of 3D graphs to support tasks that require depth judgments; this limits the generalizability to graphs with labeling support. We also argued that looking at the individual components of information in terms of whether they are nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio is very valuable in constructing good graphs. Borrowing from and extending Bertinís work, we argue that if three components (be it ordinal, interval, or ratio) are to be represented using Cartesian 2D graphs, then at least one of the components must be represented in, or converted to a, categorical form to be represented as the third component using legends. For example, in this study, temperature and noise level were represented in a categorical form. Our research covers all such cases where it is possible to construct a single 2D graph and a single 3D graph to represent the relationships between these three components. While the performance of 2D and 3D graphs may vary across these cases, we argue that the general pattern of the resultsó3D better than 2D overall, xñy encoded relationships better than yñz relationshipsówill still remain very similar to the results shown in this research. In addition, the results from Experiment 3, where 3D graphs still outperform 2D graphs even under simple conditions, further strengthen our contention of generalizability.

The process of constructing graphs also involves an element of creativity and the designers may come up with better ways to represent information for a particular situation. However, when data exploration using computers is involved, the software program has to offer choices automatically and Cartesian graphs make sense for most of these business applications. Of course, this does not in any way distract from the fact that there are many other ways of representing graphs and in no way precludes the role of creativity in constructing graphs.

## Conclusions

This research has provided part of the much needed empirical support in evaluating the performance of 3D graphs (Card et al. 1999). It introduces relationship encoding as a general limiting factor for 2D graphs when three components of information are represented on a 2D plane. This paper also identifies task types dealing with depth judgments as a limiting factor of 3D graphs and provides labeling support to help ameliorate the problem. Apart from the surprising total dominance of 3D graphs, this research also helps us understand the performance of 2D and 3D graphs in finer detail by decomposing the dominance of the 3D graphs into two orthogonal parts for effect of relationship encoding. When comparing 2D and 3D graphs, it is important to separate questions that test xñy and yñz relationships. Without this separation, predominant use of yñz relationships to compare 2D and 3D graphs would clearly result in superior performance by 3D graphs, while the predominant use of xñy relationships would narrow the differences between 2D and 3D graphs. The results provide empirical support for the superiority of 3D graphs over 2D graphs for both advanced and elementary tasks that require extraction of specific values. The dynamic labeling for all graphs (Experiments 2 and 3) specifically helped the 3D graphs more by mitigating the ambiguity of depth problem. This research also provides evidence for the superiority of 3D graphs over their 2D counterparts as the graphs become more complex. However, the dominance of 3D graphs diminishes as the questions get easier and as the graphs become less complex.

Data visualization research is focusing on developing not only better tools and algorithms to improve the performance of 3D graphs, but also on making these graphs more dynamic by providing interactive features such as zoom and rotation (Buja et al. 1996; Sutherland et al. 2000). While this study provided dynamic labeling support in an interactive manner, the graphs used were essentially static. Further empirical research is needed to investigate the impact of the other interactive features such as zoom, rotation, and filtering.

The present research has addressed an important gap in data visualization literature by focusing on graph construction when the number of components exceeds two. When the primary purpose of using a graph is for data exploration, the users do not know the relationships that are of most interest to them in advance. Designers of data visualization packages might find it fruitful to offer choices to users based on the nature of the data (ordinal, nominal, interval, or ratio scale). The results of our research suggest that when the user is interested in exploring three components of information it may be best to start with 3D graphs and restrict the use of 2D graphs to specific occasions if and when the user desires it. 2D graphs can be used as a follow-up to using 3D graphs and after the user has identified an interesting relationship and wishes to view it between just two components.
