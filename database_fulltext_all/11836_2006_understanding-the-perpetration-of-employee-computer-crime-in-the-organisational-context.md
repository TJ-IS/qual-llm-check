# Understanding the perpetration of employee computer crime in the organisational context

Robert Willison \*

Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60, DK-2000 Frederiksberg, Denmark

Received 6 February 2006; received in revised form 25 February 2006; accepted 29 August 2006

## Abstract

While hackers and viruses fuel the IS security concerns for organisations, the problems posed by employee computer crime should not be underestimated. Indeed, a number of IS security researchers have turned their attention to the ‘insider’ threat. Of this group, several focus on the ofender, either in terms of a series of attributes required for perpetration, or with reference to forms of safeguards aimed at negating such behaviour. These studies are complemented by those texts which examine the organisational context in which rogue employees commit computer crime. Currently, however, there has been a lack of insight into the relationship between the ofender and the context, during the commission process. To address this deficiency, two criminological theories are advanced. This paper illustrates how the theories, entitled the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention, can be applied to the IS domain, thereby ofering a theoretical basis by which to analyse the ofender/context relationship during perpetration. By so doing, practitioners may use these insights to inform and enhance the selection of safeguards in a bid to improve prevention programmes. Furthermore, the importation of the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention into the IS field opens up potentially new areas for future research. - 2006 Elsevier Ltd. ## 1. Introduction

While hackers and viruses fuel the security concerns of organisations, the threat of employee computer crime should not be overlooked. This message is echoed by numerous security surveys which point to the magnitude of the ‘insider’ problem (CSI/FBI, 2004; DTI/PWC, 2004; Ernst & Young, 2004). The 2004 CSI/FBI Computer Crime and Security Survey (CSI/FBI, 2004) revealed that approximately 50% of security breaches occurred within the organisation. From another perspective, respondents to the UK DTI/PWC (2004) survey were asked about the source of their worst security incident. For small size (1–49 employees) organisations, 32% stated the source was internal. However, this figure rose to 46% and 48%, respectively, for medium (50–249 employees) and large (250 + employees) companies.

Against this backdrop, a number of researchers have turned their attention to the security problems posed by employee computer crime (Harrington, 1996; Kesar & Rogerson, 1998; Straub, 1990). Of this group, several focus on the ofender, either in terms of a series of attributes required for perpetration, or with reference to forms of safeguards aimed at negating such behaviour. These studies are complemented by those texts which examine the organisational context in which rogue employees commit computer crime. Currently, however, there has been a lack of insight into the relationship between the ofender and the context during the perpetration of computer crime. To address this oversight, this paper focuses on the stages an ofender must go through in order for a crime to be committed, i.e. the procedural stages. Two criminological theories, entitled the Rational Choice Perspective (Clarke & Cornish, 1985, 2000; Cornish & Clarke, 1986) and Situational Crime Prevention (Clarke, 1997), are advanced to support analysis of the stages comprising employee computer crime. Rather than focussing on ‘why’ and ‘how’ people become criminals, these theories focus on the perpetration of crime. It is argued that the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention may complement existing security strategies by potentially ofering a theoretical basis by which to identify ofender behaviour in all of the procedural stages, and the associated criminal choices which underpin their actions. In so doing, practitioners may use these insights to inform and enhance the selection of safeguards to prevent the successful perpetration of employee computer crime. Furthermore, through the application of the two theories in the IS field potentially new areas for future research are suggested.

The proceeding section of the paper reviews the related literature. This is followed by a discussion which centres on the diference between those criminological theories which focus on the criminal act as opposed to theories of criminality. The discussion serves as an introduction to a description of the two bodies of theory advanced in this paper, namely the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention. The penulti mate section discusses how these approaches can be used to address the procedural stages of computer crime, followed by the conclusion and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Employees and computer crime

Of the related IS security literature which examines the insider threat, a number of these studies focus on the ofender, either in terms of a series of attributes required for perpetration, or with reference to forms of safeguards aimed at negating such behaviour. These texts are complemented by those studies which examine the organisational context in which rogue employees commit computer crime. This section of the paper, therefore, reviews this literature, which can be seen to fall into four specific categories including safeguards, deterring ofenders, attributes for ofending and the organisational context.

## 2.1. Safeguards

Several writers have discussed the range of controls which can be used as a safeguard against computer crime by employees (Backhouse & Dhillon, 1995; Dhillon & Moores, 2001; Dhillon, Silva, & Backhouse, 2004; Kesar & Rogerson, 1998). Dhillon and Moores (2001), for example, while advocating traditional technical safeguards to limit access to computer systems and their programmes, further note the need for formal and informal controls. Formal safeguards include written policies for clarifying the appropriate security responsibilities and roles of staf. These are complemented by informal controls, such as education and awareness campaigns, which directly aim to influence the security behaviour of employees. While the aforementioned papers have proven useful in discussing the need for a focus on the behavioural as well as technical safeguards for IS security, such discussions are held at a high level, and ofer little guidance when considering the choice and application of suitable controls for specific contexts.

## 2.2. Deterring ofenders

A number of researchers have focused specifically on the deterrent efect of safeguards (Campbell, 1988; Cardinali, 1995; Harrington, 1996; Hofer & Straub, 1989; Sherizen, 1995; Straub, 1990; Straub & Nance, 1990; Straub & Welke, 1998). Of this group, several have applied General Deterrence Theory to the IS security domain (Harrington, 1996; Hofer & Straub, 1989; Straub, 1990; Straub, Carlson, & Jones, 1992; Straub & Welke, 1998). This criminological theory focuses on the role of disincentives and sanctions for deterring criminal behaviour.

Given the above, deterrent safeguards advanced by writers in the IS security field include, for example, detection and monitoring activities (Hofer & Straub, 1989; Straub et al., 1992), security awareness programmes (Straub & Welke, 1998) and codes of ethics (Harrington, 1996).

General Deterrence Theory provides valuable insights into the deterrent efect of safeguards, but as soon as the ofender moves beyond the point of deterrence and embarks on a criminal act the theory is limited. Admittedly, writers in the IS security field have discussed General Deterrence Theory in terms of preventive controls and the relationship with computer criminals. Indeed, Straub and Welke (1998) argue IS security countermeasures consist of four separate, but related, activities which include: (i) deterrence, (ii) prevention, (iii) detection and (iv) recovery. These four areas are designed to enhance IS security by reducing systems risk. As noted, the initial aim of IS security countermeasures strategy would be to deter such activity. If deterrence proved inefective, the second part of the strategy would aim at preventing the ofender from perpetrating computer crime. Similarly, if preventive controls proved inefective then detection activities are required, and so on. However, with regards to the explanatory value of General Deterrence Theory, preventive controls are discussed only in terms of their deterrent efect. As Straub and Welke note:

. . .all of these organisational responses [the four elements of the safeguard strategy] lead to a downstream efect of deterring future computer abuse . . . From the perspective of general deterrence theory, these four kinds of defense can contribute dynamically to a subsequent deterrent efect. That is, potential abusers become convinced of the certainty and severity of punishment for committing certain acts when the efectiveness of the systems security is obvious or when it is communicated to them (Straub & Welke, 1998, p. 446).

However, as noted, General Deterrence Theory is unable to provide any theoretical insights into the actual act of perpetration, and the behaviour of ofenders during such an act. Hence, what is also required is a theoretical basis for advocating preventive safe guards which focus on the criminal act.

## 2.3. Attributes for ofending

Other writers have considered the ofender in terms of a series of attributes they require for the perpetration of computer crime (Parker, 1976, 1981, 1998; Wood, 2002). Parker (1998) argues that practitioners need to consider all forms of what he calls ‘cyber-crimi nals’ in terms of their skills, knowledge, resources, authority and motives. In other words, what skills, knowledge, etc. does an ofender require for the commission of computer crime, and what are the implications for organisational security? An alternative perspec tive is provided by Wood (2002) who solely focuses on the insider. While similarly urging consideration of skills, knowledge and motives, Wood departs from Parker by advocating examination of ofenders’ methods to avoid risk, the associated tactics and the processes involved in perpetration. Indeed, given the nature of this paper, the latter is particularly relevant. However, Wood fails to explain how risk avoidance methods, tactics and the processes involved in perpetration should be researched in the organisational setting where computer crime may take place. In addition, no theory is advanced to help examine these three factors. This is also the case with regards to the attributes for ofending, where Parker (1998) and Wood (2002) ofer no theory or related guidance for the study of these fac tors in the organisational context.

## 2.4. The organisational context

Closely related to the attributes for ofending is the consideration of the organisational context in which computer crime takes place (Becker, 1981; Sherizen, 1995). Becker (1981), for example, argues for a focus on the organisational surroundings, rather than on an individual’s personality, for predicting and preventing computer crime. He argues dishonest employees perceive the organisational context in a number of ways, and provides a classification of seven ‘criminogenic environments’. By this, Becker means that if the context of an organisation reflects one (or more) of the seven types, then the organisation will be vulnerable to various forms of computer crime. So, for example, one of the seven types, ‘the land of opportunity’, represents an organisational context in which dishonest employees exploit security loopholes spotted during the course of their daily work activities. Unfortunately, Becker (1981) does not elaborate on how the ofender’s perceptions are formed in terms of the organisational context, and hence how some environments are judged as criminogenic as opposed to others.

In a similar vein, Willison (2002) and Willison and Backhouse (2006) examine how the organisational context provides opportunities for computer crime. Their studies advance a model known as the ‘Crime Specific Opportunity Structure’, which provides an overview of those elements in the organisational context which together aford an opportunity. While the model allows some consideration of ofender decisions, a method for identifying and analysing the choices for a specific crime is not forthcoming.

Willison (2006) assesses the use of three criminological theories for understanding ofender behaviour in the organisational context. Events highlighted in the Barings Bank case study are used to assess whether the data supports concepts central to the theories. With varying degrees of success the theories ofer insights in the behaviour of ofenders both prior to, but also, during the commission process. However, the three theories are unable to ofer a method for analysing the specific ofender choices (and the related ofender behaviour) during the perpetration of computer crime in the organisational context.

## 2.5. Deficiencies in the existing literature

Although the reviewed literature has proven useful in highlighting the problem of employee computer crime, and ofering guidance for practitioners, there are as noted, certain deficiencies. Indeed, there is currently a lack of insight into the relationship between the ofender and the context during the perpetration of computer crime. Yet an understanding of this relationship could feasibly address the aforementioned deficiencies. A theoretical understanding of the ofender/context during perpetration, would (like General Deterrence Theory and deterrent controls) provide a theoretical underpinning for the selection of suitable controls. In addition, an appreciation of ofender behaviour in a specific context would also help to identify the associated ofender attributes. Finally, through the application of appropriate theory, an understanding of how ofender perceptions are influenced by the context could be explained.

Insights into the ofender/context relationship would be of interest to practitioners responsible for addressing computer crime. Indeed, Goodhue and Straub (1991) advance a model of managerial perceptions of systems risk. They note how informed management perceptions are based on knowledge of three areas, including ‘organisational environment’ (knowledge of risks inherent to a specific industry), ‘IS environment’ (knowledge of the range of technical and managerial controls that can address systems risk) and ‘individual characteristics’ (knowledge of local systems risk and the threats which form part of such risk). However, existing research notes that managerial knowledge of the ‘IS environment’ and ‘individual characteristics’ is ‘fragmented’ and ‘incomplete’, contributing to situations where eforts at reducing system risk are often less than efective (Loch, Carr, & Warkentin, 1992; Straub, 1986a, 1986b; Straub & Welke, 1998). Hence, an understanding of the relationship between the ofender and the organisational context during the perpetration of computer crime, may feasibly enhance knowledge of the ‘IS environment’ and ‘individual characteristics’. As Straub and Welke (1998) argue, if enhanced knowledge of these two areas can lead to improved security management, then the chances of reducing systems risk are greatly improved.

The following section of the paper discusses the diferences between those criminological theories which focus on the criminal act as opposed to theories of criminality. The discussion serves as an introduction to a description of the two bodies of theory advanced in this paper, namely the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention. It is argued they can help provide an understanding of the relationship between the ofender and the context during the perpetration of computer crime, and thereby address the defi ciencies discussed in the literature review.

## 3. Criminological theories of crime and criminality

Clarke (1997) argues one ‘mistake’ made by modern criminology is that the task of explaining crime has been assumed to be the same as explaining the criminal (Gottfredson & Hirschi, 1990). ‘Dispositional’ criminological theories have been eager to provide accounts of why and how individuals through the assimilation of specific social or psycho logical influences, or the inheritance of traits, are, as a consequence, more inclined to acts of a delinquent or criminal nature. However, this is not the same as explaining the occurrence of crime, which, aside from requiring a motivated ofender, also warrants an opportunity. Simply to explain criminal dispositions, Clarke contends, is only half the equation. What is further required are explanations of how crime is committed (Ekblom, 1994). Through developing such explanations, insights are aforded into the ofender/context relationship, which can be used to inform prevention programmes.

What has therefore emerged in the field of criminology are two approaches entitled the Rational Choice Perspective (Clarke & Cornish, 1985, 2000; Cornish & Clarke, 1986) and Situational Crime Prevention (Clarke, 1997). Unlike other criminological theories, they aford consideration of the criminal act. It is for this reason that the Rational Choice Per spective and Situational Crime Prevention are advanced in this paper for their application to the IS security field. As noted in the literature review, there is currently a lack of insight into the relationship between the ofender and the context, during the perpetration of computer crime. It is argued that the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention ofer a theoretical basis on which to analyse the ofender/context relationship by examining the stages an ofender must go through in order for a crime to be committed. By so doing, the deficiencies cited in the literature review may be addressed. In addition it is worth noting how the advancement of the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention is consistent with previous IS security research which has emphasised the need for analytical tools which are ‘firmly grounded in theory’ (Straub & Welke, 1998, p. 451).

A qualifying point should perhaps be made in terms of defining Situational Crime Prevention and the Rational Choice Perspective as ‘theories’. Dispositional theories have dominated criminology, with the advancement of various versions as to the causes of criminality. Unfortunately, one of the major drawbacks for these theories, concerns the lack of efective policies and preventive practices which can be derived from them. Clarke (1980) notes how:

. . .the dispositional bias remains and renders criminology theory unproductive in terms of the preventive measures which it generates. People are led to propose methods of preventive intervention precisely where it is most dificult to achieve any efects, i.e. in relation to the psychological events or the social and economic condi tions that are supposed to generate criminal dispositions (Clarke, 1980, p. 137).

Indeed, Wilson (1975) notes when discussing the fortunes of the USA during the 1960s, even when attempts (although not directly aimed at decreasing crime) were made to reduce poverty, improve education and provide better housing, crime rates did not fall, bu instead they ‘soared’. Similar problems are faced when attempting to change a person’s temperament, or other biological factors, and it is dificult to envisage what means could be taken to make guardians care for and exercise more discipline over their children. Clarke (1980) notes how these pragmatic problems do indicate the inconsistencies and uncertainties faced when addressing the ‘causes’ of crime. Knowing at which point to intervene in the regressive chain of causal events has therefore been a considerable stumbling block for dispositional theories.

By contrast, Situational Crime Prevention and the Rational Choice Perspective examine the criminal act, with their focus very much on making a contribution toward crime prevention policy and practice (Clarke, 1997; Newman & Clarke, 2003). In comparison to the development of dispositional theories, research into the criminal act is still at a relatively early stage. Indeed, Clarke and Cornish (1985) assert the need for ‘good enough’ theories which provides policy and research guidance. Such is the thinking behind the development of the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention, which have witnessed considerable change in the last four decades. To capture the flavour of this transition, Cornish and Clarke (1986) note with specific regard to the Rational Choice Perspective how it:

. . .should contain the seeds of its own destruction: whatever its immediate practical value the facts it helps to produce ought in time to provide the building-blocks for some more sophisticated and (temporarily) more satisfying theory of crime (Cornish & Clarke, 1986, p. 16).

With the Rational Choice Perspective helping to underpin the theoretical base of Situational Crime Prevention, it is perhaps not surprise to learn, either, how the latter has evolved in terms of the form, and number of, preventive measures (which will be discussed shortly), which it advocates. It is perhaps also worth mentioning how in terms of prevention Situational Crime Prevention stands alone. Newman (1997) and Newman and Clarke (2003) note how as a theory of crime it is unique in that central to Situational Crime Prevention is the explicit value of reducing or preventing the crime problem at hand.

Hence, while Situational Crime Prevention and the Rational Choice Perspective are constantly evolving and may currently lack the theoretical sophistication ofered by dispositional theories, their development should be encouraged given that the Achilles’ heal of dispositional theories is their limited prescriptions for policy and practice.

In keeping with the above, a final point to be discussed in this section concerns the application of the two theories at a more practical level. As will be noted in the next section of the paper, central to the two approaches, and as discussed earlier, is their focus on the criminal act. Together, the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention provide a theoretical underpinning for the selection of suitable controls in specific contexts. This is central to Situational Crime Prevention’s goal of reducing the opportunities for crime. A key role in achieving this goal is played by those individuals who implement the various controls. A not so dissimilar task is performed by IS security practitioners. Of fundamental importance and a common denominator between those individuals who employ Situational Crime Prevention techniques and IS security practitioners who address the insider threat, is their ability to manipulate the context in which crime takes place. Therefore, at a practical level, the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention appear well suited to addressing the insider threat. Whether or not the approaches could be applied to an external threat is debatable. A potential stumbling block is the extent to which practitioners are able to influence the context for these types of crime associated with this group of threats. This could, however, form an area for future research.

The two approaches will now be described, followed by a discussion of how they may be applied to address the procedural stages of computer crime.

## 4. The rational choice perspective and situational crime prevention

## 4.1. Rational choice perspective

Central to the Rational Choice Perspective advocated by Clarke and Cornish (Clarke & Cornish, 1985, 2000; Cornish & Clarke, 1986), are a number of propositions. These include the assumption that crimes are deliberate and purposive: that is, those who commit crimes do so with the intention of deriving some type of benefit from such acts. Obvious examples are cash or material goods, but a broader reading of the term ‘benefits’ allows for the inclusion of other forms such as prestige, fun, excitement, sexual gratification, and domination. Joyriding is an example of how the benefits may take the intangible forms of fun and excitement.

Another of the propositions relate to crime specificity. The factors considered by crim inals and the related variables that influence the decision-making process, vary considerably with the nature of the ofence. Thus an analysis of decision-making needs to be made with reference to specific categories of crime. Legal categories of robbery and auto-theft are too generic, because these umbrella terms cover diversely motivated ofences undertaken by a broad spectrum of ofenders utilising a plethora of skills and methods. For example, the theft of a car for temporary transport is diferent from the theft of a car for joyriding, which is again diferent to the theft of a car to be sold locally or overseas.

Of further importance to the Rational Choice Perspective is the proposition that crim inal choices can be categorised into two groups, viz., ‘involvement’ and ‘event’ decisions. The former relate to the three stages of the criminal or delinquent career. The ofender must make decisions about embarking on criminal activities, whether or not to continue these activities over a period of time, and when, if at all, to cease ofending. The latter refers to those decisions made during the commission of a crime. These choices are framed within the crime-specific focus.

The final proposition to be discussed centres on the sequence of event decisions which an ofender faces during the commission of a crime. Original work in this area focused solely on choices made in terms of potential target selection (Clarke & Cornish, 1985; Cornish & Clarke, 1986), but as a result of theoretical advancements it was realised that as the criminal act unfolds the perpetrator is required to make a series of decisions about other stages in the crime commission process. Clarke and Cornish (2000) note, for example, how:

In the case of suburban burglary, the event may be sparked by some random occurrence, such as two burglars meeting up, both of whom need money . . . Plans begin to be made and a car or van may be stolen for transport. The next step involves travelling to the neighbourhood selected and identifying a house to enter. Ideally, this holds the promise of good pickings without the chance of being disturbed by the owners. A point of entry that it not too dificult or risky must then be found. Getting into the house and rapidly choosing the goods to steal follow this stage. The goods must then be carried to the car without being seen by neighbours or passers-by. Afterwards, they may have to be stashed safely while a purchaser is found. Finally, they must be conveyed to the buyer and exchanged for cash (Clarke & Cornish, 2000, p. 31).

As the burglary example illustrates, within the commission process, there are a number of stages and at each stage choices are made by the ofender.

## 4.2. Situational crime prevention

Situational crime prevention is a relatively new school of thought. Difering in its focus from most criminology, its starting point is an examination of those circumstances which aford specific kinds of crime. Through an understanding of these situations, measures are introduced to induce change in the relevant environments with the aim of reducing the opportunities for specific crimes. Its emphasis is therefore on the criminal setting. Rather than sanctioning or detecting ofenders, the intention is to stop the occurrence of crime, and rather than seeking to reduce criminal tendencies through the enhancement of certain aspects of society, such as better housing or education, the relatively simple aim is to make criminal action less appealing to ofenders (Clarke, 1997).

Eforts to achieve this goal involve implementing opportunity reducing techniques, which target specific forms of crime and impact on the immediate criminal environment in terms of its design, management or manipulation. As can be seen in Table 1, associated with the techniques are five major aims, which include increasing the efort or risks of crime, or reducing the potential rewards. These are further complemented by removing the excuses for crime and negating provocative phenomena. Examples of the techniques include target hardening (e.g. anti-robbery screens: to increase the efort), utilising place managers (multiple clerks in convenience stores: to increase the risks), target removal (e.g. removable car radios: to reduce the rewards), reducing frustrations and stress (e.g. eficient queues and polite service: to reduce provocations) and the setting of rules (e.g. harassment codes: to remove excuses) (Cornish & Clarke, 2003).

In an attempt to block the commission of specific crimes, measures introduced into the immediate environment are designed to impact the ofender’s perceptions about the potential costs and benefits of crime commission. In addition, it is assumed as part of the decision-making process that some evaluation is made with respect to the possible moral costs of ofending. While some ofenders may be prepared to shoplift, this does not mean they are prepared to mug the elderly. In an attempt, however, to overcome any feelings of guilt or shame, ofenders may try to neutralise such feeling through the construction of excuses such as ‘everybody else does it’, ‘I’m just borrowing it’, etc. (Clarke, 1997). Situational Crime Prevention theorists have further acknowledged how the immediate environment may not only aford potential opportunities, but also provoke criminal behaviour. Hence a number of techniques have been developed to assuage such phenomena (Cornish & Clarke, 2003).

A final point to mention, and in keeping with the Rational Choice Perspective, is Situational Crime Prevention’s crime specific focus. Forgoing, for example, a discussion of crime prevention at the level of ‘burglary’ or ‘robbery’, greater emphasis is placed on those specific crimes that fall under these broader categories. The argument advanced is that only a detailed understanding at the level of ‘specific crimes’ will aford insights for prevention programmes. Hence, Poyner and Webb (1991) assert that preventive measures, needed for tackling burglary of domestic electronic goods, difer from those required to prevent the burglary of household cash or jewellery, owing to the diferences in the way these crimes are committed.

## 5. The application of the rational choice perspective to IS security

While underpinning the techniques advocated by Situational Crime Prevention, at a broader level the Rational Choice Perspective provides a framework for helping to explain all forms of crime. The framework acts as a basis for modelling criminal decision-making (Clarke & Cornish, 2000).

## 5.1. Involvement decisions

As previously considered, involvement and event decisions form the two main groups encompassed by the Rational Choice framework. The former focuses on three stages of the criminal career, which include initiation, habituation and desistance. The extent to which modelling these three stages would provide prevention insights for the IS security field is problematic. This is largely due to the fact that these stages are themselves influenced by ‘background factors’, ‘current life circumstances’ and ‘situational variables’. Clouding the issue further are additional problems related to the category of ‘background factors’. Citing computer fraud as a case in point, Cornish and Clarke (1986) note how with certain forms of crime, the ofender’s ‘background factors’ (which include upbringing, social class, ethnicity, educational opportunities, etc.) appear to have little influence on involvement decisions. Hence, attempting to model the three stages of involvement decisions may prove dificult and ultimately fruitless. However, it is believed that greater inroads can be made into modelling the criminal behaviour associated with event decisions.

## 5.2. Event decisions

These types of choices are made during the commission process and are framed within a crime specific focus. As noted earlier, when discussing the example of burglary, a sequence of choices is made by the ofender in relation to the stages of a crime. For the burglar these include decisions concerning the neighbourhood, house, point of entry, etc. If these stages and the associated decisions can be identified, the preventive scope for many diverse contexts could feasibly be extended. Safeguards could accordingly be implemented which influence the potential ofender’s choices, leading to the cessation of the criminal act.

In an attempt to correctly identify the stages in the commission process, Cornish (1994a, 1994b) advances the concept of crime scripts. The origins of this concept can be found in the field of cognitive science, which has addressed the production and understanding of sequences of events and actions (Gardner, 1985). Scripts form part of a group of hypothesised knowledge structures, or schemata, which are believed to help arrange our knowledge of people and events. More specifically, the script is defined as an ‘event’ schema since it is concerned with how individuals retain and organise context-specific knowledge with regard to behavioural routines or processes (Taylor & Crocker, 1981).

Examples of these routines or processes include visiting the doctor, attending lectures (Graesser, Gordon, & Sawyer, 1979) and going to a restaurant (Nakamura, Graesser, & Zimmerman, 1985). Common to these forms of behaviour is the attainment of a goal. In addition, each script comprises event sequences extended over time. The events in the sequence are interrelated given that events at the early stages of a script aford the occurrence of later ones.

Hence the scripts concept focuses on behavioural processes involved in rational goal oriented actions. Moreover, the concept afords ‘concrete explanations about specific actions in specific domains’ (Hewstone, 1989, p. 103). Given this, Cornish argues that the script concept can act as a useful tool for analysing the ‘event’ stages in the commission of a specific crime, i.e. scripts can be used to address the procedural stages of an ofence. As he notes:

A script-theoretic approach ofers a way of generating, organising and systematising knowledge about the procedural aspects and procedural requirements of crime commission. It has the potential to provide more appropriately crime-specific accounts of crime commission, and to extend this analysis to all the stages of the crime-commission sequence (Cornish, 1994b, p. 160).

Within the field of criminology, the scripts method has been used to analyse a diverse group of crimes ranging from check fraud (Lacoste & Tremblay, 2003), body switching (the stealing of motor vehicles for resale purposes) (Tremblay, Talon, & Hurley, 2001) and public transport ofences (Smith & Clarke, 2000). In their study of check fraud ofend ers, Lacoste and Tremblay (2003), for example, use the method for identifying the procedural stages of three diferent forms of this ofence, which include the use of counterfeit, altered and true checks. Their research helps to explain how, although the number of check frauds decreased (in Montreal during the period 1991–1996), the payof per fraud increased. They argue this is due to more innovate or creative groups of fraudsters who alter their behaviour (their scripts) in a bid to lower the odds of arrest and increase the gains from each ofence.

For the purpose of script generation in the IS context, knowledge held by security practitioners can act as a useful source. This can be complemented by ofender accounts and secondary sources of data such as published research, security surveys, newspaper accounts, etc. (Cornish, 1994a, 1994b). While there are obvious practical problems associated with obtaining ofender accounts, preliminary eforts could be initiated by the construction of ‘draft’ scripts through the use of secondary sources and practitioner knowledge. To aid in their development, Cornish (1994a, 1994b) argues that the universal script can act as a useful guiding framework. Common to all scripts are a set of generalised scenes, which form the basis of the universal script. The separate elements of this type of script are sequential in order and together they provide a framework that could be used by researchers or practitioners for modelling the commission of a specific crime. In essence, each ‘scene/function’ stage of the universal script can be viewed as a procedural stage of a crime.

Cornish argues (1994a, 1994b), once the crime scripts have been generated, clearer insights are provided into the procedural stages of the particular ofence. Using the universal framework as a guide to script creation invites consideration of all the procedural aspects of the ofence, ensuring that no aspect of the commission process is overlooked. In addition the universal script permits examination of the process from the ofender’s viewpoint and actions. In this way, common-sense ‘knowledge’ about crime commission can potentially be debunked and a more rigorous understanding of the commission process can be aforded to those addressing IS security.

While examining ofender behaviour, scripts also draw attention to the required attributes for perpetration. As noted, Parker (1998) and Wood (2002) have stressed the need to consider the ofender in terms of certain attributes such as skills, knowledge, access and resources. Cornish and Clarke (1989) define these attributes as ‘choice-structuring properties,’ i.e. those ‘single or multiple features of particular criminal activities which make them diferentially available and attractive to certain individuals at certain times (Cornish & Clarke, 1989, p. 108). The scripts concept helps to enhance the analysis of choice-structuring properties owing to its focus on the ofender/context relationship. Hence, these properties are more clearly identified as specific contexts play a large role in defining and delimiting them. So, for example, in the local council fraud, the rogue employee presumably required accounting skills and knowledge of the particular invoicing system. The ability to undertake the fraud was therefore enhanced through his accounting skills and knowledge of the system. This was complemented by the ofender’s knowledge of the security vulnerabilities created by the failure of his colleagues to lock-down their computers. By systematically working through the script stages, practitioners could feasibly acquire greater insights into the choice-structuring properties associated with the ofence. This would hopefully enhance prevention programmes by looking at ways of denying access to the properties, if this proved feasible.

## 6. The application of situational crime prevention to IS security

Once the criminal behaviour per each script is identified, the next step would involve the implementation of corresponding controls. The 25 techniques advocated by Situational Crime Prevention could feasibly be adopted by security practitioners to complement this process. Indeed, some of the techniques are already implicitly used in the organisational context. Examples include property marking (to identify property), clear desk/screen pol icies (to remove targets), anti-virus detection (to target harden) and firewalls (to screen exits).

Using the Situational Crime Prevention techniques as a guide potentially enables the practitioner to consider safeguard options for influencing the ofender’s decision-making processes. To aid this process, scripts could be merged with the 25 crime prevention techniques to provide a schematic tool for practitioners and researchers. Table 3 represents an example of such a merging, based on the case of computer fraud cited earlier. As can be seen, with each script action there is the aim of implementing corresponding controls. The numbers cited next to each control in Table 3 refer to the type of Situational Crime Prevention technique employed (see Table 1). Unlike the more traditional crimes addressed by Situational Crime Prevention and the Rational Choice Perspective, employee computer abuse which is perpetrated in the organisational context can be termed a ‘specialised access crime’ (Felson, 2002). In other words, only those people who have access to the environment are in a position to commit the crime. It is, therefore, dificult to implement ‘entry’ and ‘exit’ controls, for staf who have access to the criminal context as a result of their employment. But, as noted earlier, the elements of a script are interrelated and the script’s actions in a prior stage aford the existence in a later one. In Table 3, therefore, the ‘entry’ into the organisation is achieved by ‘deliberately gaining access to the organisation’. Hence, specialised access crimes can involve either (i) long range planning, whereby an individual deliberately applies for a job with the intention of committing an ofence or (ii) where an individual applies for a job without criminal intent, but later on, for whatever reason (e.g. becomes disgrun tled, develops an addiction, marriage breakdown, etc.), decides to perpetrate a crime. Table 3 presupposes the former. Therefore, an appropriate control at the ‘Preparation stage would be the screening of prospective employees.

While the discussion of the ‘entry’ into environments, which enable specialised access crimes, may appear pedantic, it is precisely this attention to detail which helps to produce efective scripts and the corresponding identification of suitable controls. Ignoring the realities of the commission process may result in failing to apply appropriate safeguards or applying safeguards which are inappropriate. Suitable controls, therefore, can only be implemented if the actions which warrant their application are correctly identified. In Table 3 there are currently no controls cited in the columns ‘Reduce the Rewards’, ‘Reduce Provocation’ and ‘Remove Excuses’. However, the 25 techniques could be used as a brainstorming tool by practitioners for the consideration of complementary safe guards. Hence, with regard to the case of computer fraud, is it possible that the techniques could be used to ‘deny benefits’ (a technique which aims to ‘Reduce the Rewards’), ‘alert conscience’ (a technique which aims to ‘Remove Excuses’), or ‘reduce frustration and stress’ (a technique which aims to ‘Reduce Provocation)? Not all of the techniques will apply to an individual crime, but this is to be expected given that the techniques (and their associated controls) have been used and developed to address a diverse number of crimes in specific contexts. However, rather than outright rejection, the application of the individual techniques for specific crime in the organisational context require further examination and represent an area for future research.

## 7. Conclusion and future research

IS security represents a growing concern for organisations. While external threats require due consideration, the threat posed by rogue employees should not be ignored. From an academic perspective a modest but growing number of texts have addressed the insider threat. Currently, however, there has been a lack of attention given to the relationship between the actual behaviour of ofenders during the perpetration of computer crime, and the organisational context in which such behaviour takes place. Yet insights into this relationship could feasibly be used to address systems risk. In response to this cur rent deficiency the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention are advanced in this paper, for addressing the procedural stages of crime. Central to the Rational Choice Perspective is an examination of criminal decisions. Event decisions encompass those choices made by the ofender during the crime commission process. By using the scripts method, the various related stages of this process could feasibly be identified. In this way, the goal would be to single out the ofender behaviour per each stage and implement controls accordingly. Hence the IS security strategy would aim to disrup the criminal act through the implementation of safeguards which influence the ofender’s choices and prevent successful perpetration.

The Situational Crime Prevention techniques, based on the conceptualisation of the ofender as advocated by the Rational Choice Perspective, could be used to complement the scripts method. While some of the techniques are already employed in the IS domain, consideration of the complete range advanced by Situational Crime Prevention enables the practitioner to systematically, and explicitly, consider all the alternative safeguard options for influencing the ofender’s decision making processes. To aid this process, crime scripts could be merged with the 25 techniques to provide a theoretical schematic tool for prac titioners and researchers.

Currently organisations can draw on a number of means for guidance on safeguard selection. These include the use of risk assessment techniques (Peltier, 2004), international standards, such as ISO BS17799 (ISO BS17799, 2005), or the ‘baseline security’ approach (Parker, 1998), where controls are selected based on best practice principles. Irrespective of whether an organisation uses one or more of these means, the Rational Choice Perspective and Situational Crime Prevention can both complement existing security practices. The scripts approach can help in understanding the ofender/context relationship, and the opportunity reducing techniques advanced by Situational Crime Prevention can act as a guide for practitioners by enabling them to systematically consider all the safeguard options for influencing the ofender’s decision-making process.

Future research could encompass the development of crime scripts through the use of the action research method (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998; Mathiassen, 2002). More precisely, research could involve the use of the universal script as the basis for such development. In this sense, the action research method would be used to evaluate the feasibility of developing scripts in the organisational context. To complement this, action research could also potentially incorporate the scripts method in conjunction with the 25 Situational Crime Prevention techniques.

Addressing the external threat through the formation of scripts and the application of the Situational Crime Prevention techniques, could form another area for future research. Given that the commission process for some of these threats would originate in cyberspace, research could assess the extent to which the whole commission process for specific crimes might be accurately mapped out. Another stream of research could consider the extent to which the specific context may be manipulated by practitioners to fend of external threats.

Future research could also encompass theory development. While the rational choice perspective advanced by Clarke and Cornish has been examined in this paper, a number of other researchers, with some variations, have applied the theory to examine a diverse range of crimes including drunk driving (Nagin & Paternoster, 1993), sexual assault (Bachman, Paternoster, & Ward, 1992), juvenile delinquency (Paternoster, 1989) and tax evasion (Klepper & Nagin, 1989). An examination of these studies may indicate avenues for future theory development. Nagin and Paternoster (1993), for instance, in their application of the perspective consider not only situational factors pertaining to a crime but also include a series of ‘informal costs’ that may be incurred by the ofender in terms of loss of respect from peers and family members, diminished job prospects and the like. In their study, a sample of college undergraduates were presented with a survey which described three criminal scenarios (sexual assault, theft and drunk driving). The students were asked to estimate the probability that they would undertake the crimes. The study found significant support for consideration of these informal costs in the decision-making process.

In a similar vein Paternoster and Simpson (1996) in their application of a rational choice model, examined these informal costs in the area of ‘corporate crime’. Once again, the research findings indicated support for the inclusion of these types of costs in a rational choice model. Hence further research needs to consider all the potential costs that could be levied against the criminal, and the potential safeguards which could evoke them. Failure to do so may represent a missed opportunity to explore and develop a potential source of control.

The application of the 25 Situational Crime Prevention techniques also opens up new avenues for research. The techniques encompass areas of prevention which are relatively unexplored by IS security practitioners and whose exploitation may prove fruitful. It should be remembered that the techniques (and their associated controls) have been used and developed in a number of diverse contexts in a bid to reduce the opportunities for crime. Hence the extent to which safeguards based on ‘Reduce Emotional Arousal’ and ‘Disrupt Markets’ are suitable for the organisational context is debatable, but this does not mean that they should be rejected outright. Rather they should be examined by looking at how the controls, which fall under these headings, have been used and their potential for the IS context considered. Consideration of the techniques could feasibly encourage innovation in the area of IS security and facilitate the incorporation of controls in areas previously not considered.

One area that appears to ofer great potential is the group of techniques which fall under the heading ‘Remove Excuses’ (see Table 1). An earlier categorisation of the Situational Crime Prevention techniques focussed on attempts to increase the risks and eforts and reduce the rewards of crime (Clarke, 1992). Here, the measures tended to rely on the physical manipulation of the criminal environment in an attempt to reduce the opportunities for crime.

More recently, however, the Rational Choice Perspective has developed to consider how some ofenders assess their own morality, and how they are often able to absolve themselves of the guilt and shame associated with criminal acts. Such absolution is achieved by individuals rationalising their actions in a manner which helps neutralise these negative emotions. Common examples of such rationalisations include ‘I was just borrowing it’ and ‘everybody else does it’. Support for this assertion comes from earlier criminological and psychological research (Bandura, 1976, 1977; Sykes & Matza, 1957). Hence, a group of measures aimed at ‘removing excuses’ (i.e. the rationalisations) has been advocated (Clarke, 1997; Clarke & Homel, 1997). If ofenders can be stopped from rationalising and excusing their criminal actions in specific settings, then they will be open to feelings of guilt and shame.

While there has been some consideration of these rationalisations in the IS security field (Harrington, 1996; Sherizen, 1995), a more systematic consideration and exploitation of these techniques may complement existing prevention practices. For example, Cornish and Clarke (2003) advance five types of ‘removing excuses’ techniques, but similar work in this area has been undertaken by Wortley (1996) who identifies four broad strategies through which IS security methods could possibly be enhanced. These areas include ‘rule setting’, ‘clarifying responsibility’, ‘clarifying consequences’ and ‘increasing victim worth’. So, for example, with regard to ‘increasing victim worth’, such a strategy recognises how ofenders find it easier to perpetrate crimes if they perceive their victims to be ‘unworthy’, ‘sub-human’, ‘outsiders’, ‘anonymous’, or ‘deserving of the fate’. The prevention strategy entails attempts to reduce depersonalisation and develop an emotional bond between potential ofenders and victims. Wortley notes how it is not just individuals but also organisations which are open to this form of ofender derogation. Discussing the example of organisational fraud, he argues:

Employee share schemes, incentive schemes and general attention to reducing job dissatisfaction may increase in employees a sense of attachment to a company and inhibit their ability to portray the company in ways that justify acting fraudulently against it (Wortley, 1996, pp. 122–123).

Applying criminological theories to the IS context has the potential to provide new perspectives and insights for improving security. Yet, to date, such application has been minimal. The failure by IS security researchers and practitioners to view computer crimes as precisely that, i.e. crimes, marginalises criminology, and consequently denies access to useful theories and practical knowledge.
