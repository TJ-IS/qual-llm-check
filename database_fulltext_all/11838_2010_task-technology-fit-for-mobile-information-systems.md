# Task-technology fit for mobile information systems

Judith Gebauer<sup>1</sup>, Michael J Shaw<sup>2</sup>, Michele L Gribbins<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

Mobile information technology (IT) holds great promise to support managerial processes; however, clear guidelines of how to design effective mobile information systems (IS) for use by managers are still lacking. Building on earlier research studies of task-technology fit (TTF) and other areas of IS, this article presents a three-step conceptual model to establish fit between managerial tasks, mobile IT, and the mobile use context, assuming that a good fit positively impacts task performance. We find that use situations characterized by high distraction and poor quality of network connection are particularly challenging for the design of mobile IS, and that the user interface requires particular attention. Our model contributes to IS research by joining disparate streams of research in an effort to help advance our understanding about the development and utilization of mobile IS. From a practical perspective, we contribute to the success of mobile IS and to the effectiveness of mobile work as it becomes increasingly prevalent in our modern world.

## Motivation

he relationships among information technology (IT),<sup>1</sup> organizational processes, and performance are of great interest to researchers (Orlikowski, 2000; Markus, 2004). Recent forms of IT enable employees to ‘stay close to their local situations while engaging in global activities critical to their company’s sustainability’ (Malhotra and Majchrzak, 2005). Installed on portable devices, such as laptops, personal digital assistants, and smart phones, mobile information systems (IS) let users communicate, access information, and use various business applications, when away from a stationary office environment (Basole, 2008). Remote access to corporate IT infrastructures is established over traditional telecommunication networks and/or the Internet (Sawyer et al., 2003). As a group of particularly mobile workers, managers were the intended audience of many early mobile IS (Computerworld, 2003). Managers perform a variety of tasks as individuals or as part of a team, including leadership, consulting, and problem solving (Mintzberg, 1980; Whitley, 1989; Sørensen et al., 2002; Venezia et al., 2008). The benefits of mobile IS on the performance of managers include improvements of flexibility, productivity, and decision-making quality (Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Scornavacca and Barnes, 2008) with additional, indirect impacts on co-workers, team members, and business partners.

The success of mobile IS presents a number of challenges. Immature technology and ongoing technology developments can limit usability and results (Gebauer et al., 2007), while creating risks to the organization (Agrawal et al., 2003). A substantial difference between mobile and non-mobile IS results from the context in which the IS are used (Siau et al., 2001). Associated with the personal and environmental information that influences the user (Kim et al., 2002) and constitutes the use experience (McCullough, 2004), mobile use contexts vary more frequently than the typical use contexts of stationary systems (Kleinrock, 1996; Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999; Sawyer et al., 2003; Tarasewich, 2003a; Turel, 2006). The resulting implications include differences in user requirements and work procedures (Smith et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2002, 2008; Zheng and Yuan, 2007; Gebauer, 2008).

A good understanding of success factors and requirements of a mobile workforce is essential given the oftentimes considerable investments in mobile IS. The application of established IS theories and practices to the design and management of mobile ${ \mathrm { I S } } ,$ however, is non-trivial as a result of differences in technology, business processes and use contexts that distinguish mobile from non-mobile IS (Mylonopoulos and Doukidis, 2003; Strader et al., 2003). In this study, we build on the premise that the effectiveness and success of any IS depends on the extent to which it fits the tasks of the user (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). We seek to develop guidelines of how to design effective mobile IS for managerial use, whereby our primary unit of analysis is the task, rather than the organization or individual user behavior (similar: Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). We include the use context as a key differentiator between mobile and non-mobile IS.

Use context plays a central role in socio-technical approaches to IS (McCullough, 2004), including humancomputer interaction (HCI) (Zhang and Galletta, 2006), and IS usability research (Shneiderman, 1980; Nielsen, 1993). The need to develop a comprehensive understanding of the complex and dynamic network of interrelated factors that include the user, technology, and the world that surrounds both has been emphasized (Sawyer et $a l . ,$ 2003; Markus, 2004; McCullough, 2004; Chen and Nath, 2008). Ideally, a mutual understanding will be established between people and the situated context of their actions (Dourish, 2004) that result in a continuous fit of the IS with the immediate needs, skills, and abilities of the users (Dourish, 2001). We seek to balance the call of the HCI community for comprehensiveness and to join social and technological aspects in order to create a holistic user experience, with a pragmatic approach that advances the theory of task-technology fit (TTF) and develops guidelines for the design of mobile IS (Kim et al., 2002; Turel, 2006; Venezia et al., 2008).

With this article, we hope to contribute to TTF as we apply and extend it, and to the practical success of mobile IS for use by managers. We first review earlier research studies of TTF, managerial tasks, and mobile IT, before we develop design guidelines to establish fit for mobile IS. We then discuss implications of our model for research and practice, and conclude with suggestions for future research.

## Two theories of TTF

Two largely independent theories of TTF have emerged. One focuses on IS use by individuals and establishes TTF as an important concept in assessing and explaining IS success (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995). It has its origin in a technology-to-performance model that includes characteristics of IT, tasks, and individual users as explanatory variables for IS use and individual performance. Empirical results suggested that TTF and usage together better explain the impact of IS on individual task performance than usage alone.

Related studies support the relevance of the TTF construct to assess the value of an IS (Goodhue, 1995) and to predict IS usage (Dishaw and Strong, 1998) and individual performance (Goodhue et al., 2000). Different aspects of TTF have been confirmed relevant for IS in general (Goodhue, 1995, 1998; Goodhue et al., 1997; Ferratt and Vlahos, 1998; Goodhue and Thompson, 1995) and for particular forms of IT (Dishaw and Strong, 1998; Goodhue et al., 2000), and for a variety of tasks (Goodhue, 1995, 1998; Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Goodhue et al., 1997, 2000; Dishaw and Strong, 1998; Ferratt and Vlahos, 1998; Majchrzak et al., 2005).

The second stream of TTF-research emphasizes the development and deployment of group support systems (GSS) to support group tasks (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). Assuming that a fit between tasks and IT supports group performance, fit is developed as ‘ideal profiles composed of an internally consistent set of task contingencies and GSS elements that affect group performance.’ Following early empirical support (Zigurs et al., 1999), the group-oriented stream of TTF has been applied to various collaborative forms of IT with the intent to improve group tasks (Barkhi, 2001–2002; Dennis et al., 2001; Massey et al., 2001; Susman et al., 2003; Murthy and Kerr, 2004). The concept has also found support in the area of electronic commerce (Jahng et al., 2000).

Building on these two theories, we suggest that TTF is a valid construct to explain the success of mobile IS for managerial use and emphasize the use context as a particularity of mobile IS (Mylonopoulos and Doukidis, 2003; Strader et $a l . ,$ 2004; Tarasewich et $a l . ,$ 2008). The idea of TTF has been considered for mobile IS before (Junglas and Watson, 2003; Gebauer and Shaw, 2004; Liang and Wei, 2004), but has not been assessed systematically to achieve fit for particular combinations of tasks and technology.

## Modeling TTF for mobile IS

We apply the concept of TTF to managerial tasks supported by IT in a mobile use context, whereby we consider task performance as a proxy of IS success. We conceptualize fit as pre-defined profiles that consist of a set of internally consistent characteristics (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998) and that we develop in a three-step process (Fit 1–3). Profiles have the benefit of being specific about the suggested areas of application (managerial tasks) and, thus, allow for the identification of opportunities and potential problems. We first describe the main constructs and then outline the steps to derive TTF for mobile IS. Table 1 provides an overview of the research model.

## Managerial tasks

As a subset of knowledge work (Newell et al., 2005), managerial tasks play a constitutive role in ‘establishing, maintaining and changing such organizations as relatively distinct and semi-autonomous units of resource combination and use’ (Whitley, 1989). Two dimensions are frequently used to describe managerial tasks: (1) task non-routineness and (2) task interdependence. A third dimension, timecriticality, appears to be relevant to mobile IS.

## Task non-routineness

The degree of routineness of a task is also evident in characteristics such as repetitiveness and novelty, and indicates the ease with which managerial decision-making can be programmed or requires judgment and intelligent, adaptive, problem-oriented action (Simon, 1960). Related are the number of exceptions that can be handled by organizational IT (variety) and the degree to which a search procedure is analyzable (difficulty) (Perrow, 1967). Non-routine IT is best applied to situations with many exceptions and unanalyzable search results, whereas routine IT is best applied to situations with few exceptions and analyzable search results.

Since task variety and difficulty were correlated and difficult to distinguish in practice, they have been combined into a single dimension, termed task non-routineness (Daft and Macintosh, 1981; Karimi et al., 2004). We view nonroutineness broadly as the level of structuredness, analyzability, difficulty, and predictability of a managerial task. Tasks of low non-routineness (high routineness) include the processing of travel expenses or the procurement of standard items. Tasks of high non-routineness are more varied, and include strategic planning, solving unique problems, and managerial decision-making.

## Task interdependence

Task interdependence is the exchange of output between segments within an organizational subunit and with other units (Fry and Slocum, 1984). Thompson (1967) proposed that different types of interdependence exist in organizations, each requiring different coordination mechanisms. For example, when interdependence increases from pooled to sequential to reciprocal, coordination mechanisms should change from rules to standardization to mutual adjustment, as more communication is needed.

We include task interdependence as the degree to which a managerial task is related to other tasks and organizational units, and thus the extent to which coordination with other organizational units is required (Thompson, 1967; Malone and Crowston, 1994). The level of interdependence determines the need for IS access to perform a task as part of a larger whole, which has a direct impact on individual performance and an indirect impact on the performance of others (Gebauer and Shaw, 2004). Highly interdependent tasks (e.g., project management) require individuals to interact extensively to generate the desired outcome, whereas tasks having little interdependence (e.g., telemarketing) can be executed by a single person (Wageman and Gordon, 2005). Interdependence can be operationalized with the number of regular communication channels and partners that a user interacts with (Simon, 1960), and the pattern of interaction between tasks and resources that are consumed and produced (Crowston, 2003).

## Time criticality

Time criticality, defined as the importance with which a task needs to be performed promptly (urgency), depicts the dynamics of the work environment and tasks of a manager. The need for organizations to respond quickly to changing market requirements has been acknowledged (D’Aveni, 1994; Bradley and Nolan, 1998), whereby the urgency of communication tasks tends to be related with the type of media selected (synchronous vs asynchronous) (Straub and Karahanna, 1998).

Time criticality is very relevant for mobile IS (Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Turel, 2006) that allow for rapid feedback (Jarvenpaa et al., 2003), enable immediacy (Siau et al., 2001), and support emergency services (Yuan and Zhang, 2003; Liang and Wei, 2004). It is also likely a more important trigger for use in wireless than in wired environments (Venkatesh et al., 2003). In practice, support for urgent tasks (e.g., notification of emergency situations) has been among the earliest applications of mobile IT (Ammenwerth et al., 2000).

## Mobile IT

Earlier research on mobile IT (Varshney and Vetter, 2001; Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2002), business models (Haaker et al., 2004), and telecommunications industry (Barnes, 2002) notwithstanding, we emphasize the user perspective as we characterize mobile IT along three dimensions: functionality, user interface, and adaptability (Siau and Shen, 2003). Functionality is conceptualized as applicable to both mobile and non-mobile IT, whereas user interface and adaptability are conceptualized with a particular focus on mobile IT.

## Functionality

TTF research has frequently characterized IT based on functionality, in reference to its capabilities (Cooper and Zmud, 1990; Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). Since mobile IT combines traditional computing and interpersonal communication functionalities (Varshney et al., 2002; Sarker and Wells, 2003; Yuan and Zhang, 2003; Krogstie et al., 2004), we categorize mobile IT functionality with two dimensions: (1) the primary functionality (interpersonal interaction vs computing) and (2) the direction of interaction between user and IT (one-way vs two-way reciprocal) (Balasubramaniam et al., 2002). ## User interface

The user interface relates to the experience to use an IS (‘look and feel’). While functionality applies to both mobile and non-mobile IT, the user interface is more of an idiosyncratic factor. Mobile IT brings attention back to the devices that are used to access and utilize functionality. The result is added complexity for the design of highly usable systems (Buchanan et al., 2001; Palen and Salzman, 2002; Thomas and Macredie, 2002). Mobile devices are smaller, more portable, and more varied than their stationary counterparts. The differences include form factors (weight and size) and features such as processor and battery performance, storage capacity, and bandwidth requirements (Luff and Heath, 1998; Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999). Usability is compounded by the fact that developments are ongoing and new devices and applications continue to reach the market (Computerworld, 2003; Yuan and Zhang, 2003).

The user interface is both a limiting and an enabling factor of mobile IT (Al-Hawamdeh, 2004), which needs to be designed and managed carefully (Siau et al., 2001; Varshney and Vetter, 2001; Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Chan et al., 2002; Smith et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2002; Varshney et al., 2002; Siau and Shen 2003; Tarasewich 2003b; Yuan and Zhang 2003; Krogstie et al., 2004; Lee and Benbasat, 2004). Suggestions have been made for the improvement of sub-optimal interfaces, for example innovative menu structures and scrolling techniques for the smaller screens (Buchanan et al., 2001; St. Amant et al., 2007), fisheye techniques for calendar applications (Bederson et al., 2004), and comprehensive frameworks to understand the usability of wireless communication devices from the user perspective (Palen and Salzman, 2002). We acknowledge the complexity and breadth of the mobile user interface and exemplify its characteristics as related to portability (form factors, support for various use situations), operations and performance (compatibility with other devices, speed, battery life), usability (display, keyboard, ease of use), and network connectivity (Gebauer et al., 2008; Gebauer and Ginsburg, 2009).

## Adaptability

The possibility to adapt to various use situations is a key differentiator for mobile IT (Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Yuan and Zhang, 2003; Kini and Thanarithiporn, 2004; Lee and Benbasat, 2004), whereby technical challenges are associated with providing consistency throughout changing use conditions (Ebling et al., 2002). Applications can be adapted to the physical location of use (Kleinrock, 1996; Computerworld, 2003; Rao and Minakakis, 2003) or to the individual user and use situation. Two task characteristics are particularly relevant: (1) location-dependency (i.e., the users’ location is important) and (2) identity-dependency (i.e., the users’ identity matters) (Junglas and Watson, 2003). We refer to adaptability as the ability of mobile IT to adapt to varying circumstances, such as use locations (location-based services), use situations (disruption management), and users (personalization).

## Mobile use context

Building on Chan et al.’s. (2002) characterization of usability for mobile IS, we focus on the three dimensions of (1) user distraction, (2) network connection quality, and (3) user mobility.

## Distraction

Distraction characterizes the use situations of mobile IS, because mobile users often multi-task (Lee and Benbasat (2004) and experience frequently changing distractors that compete for their attention (Tarasewich, 2003a). Less time and cognitive resources can be spared for the performance of tasks (Chan et al., 2002). We conceptualize distraction as the level of interference with the use of a mobile IS caused by activities and people in the use environment or when outside noise prevents the user from picking up the signal of an incoming call or message.

## Connection quality

Network connectivity and reliability are critical requirements for mobile IS (Kleinrock, 1996; Varshney and Vetter, 2001), yet wireless network connections typically provide less bandwidth, stability, and predictability than their wired counterparts (Siau et al., 2001; Chan et al., 2002). While access speed and availability can drive the adoption of mobile IS (Kini and Thanarithiporn, 2004), network coverage and reliability tend to impact technical feasibility and usefulness (Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Varshney et al., 2002). Connection quality can impact user satisfaction and continued intention to use mobile services (Ebling et al.,

2002). We view connection quality as network availability, bandwidth, and stability. High connection quality suggests that usage is not limited by network connections, whereas low connection quality suggests that usage is affected negatively.

## Mobility

Mobile IS typically assume at least one participating party to be away from a stationary work environment (Luff and Heath, 1998; Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Perry et al., 2001). Mobility has been conceptualized with three modalities, namely traveling (going from one place to another), wandering (mobility in a building or local area), and visiting (spending time in a place for a limited period of time) (Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999); and three dimensions, namely space, time, and context (situatedness) (Kakihara and Sørensen, 2001).

We associate mobility with the extent to which a mobile IS is used at different geographic locations and while the user is in motion. A high level of mobility suggests that the user changes locations frequently and the locations are very different from each other (e.g., noisy vs quiet environments, widely varying access conditions), whereas a low level of mobility suggests that the user rarely changes locations during use and the locations are very similar to each other. Mobility relates to the other use variables of distraction and network connection quality. Besides varying availability and quality of the network connection the level of distraction can change as a user moves (Kleinrock, 1996; Chan et al., 2002; Ebling et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2002).

## Fit

Researchers have suggested different approaches to conceptualize fit between tasks and IT, including a set of dimensions (e.g., data quality, ease of use, reliability) (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995), and a computed match between available IT functionality and the functionality required to complete various tasks (Dishaw and Strong, 1998).

Similar to Zigurs and Buckland (1998), we apply a systematic approach to determine TTF as predefined profiles of inherently consistent characteristics, assuming that fit between managerial tasks and IT has a positive impact on task performance and on system success. We view task performance generically as the ability to reach stated individual or group task goals, to be operationalized in greater detail for specific use situations (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). Whereas Zigurs and Buckland’s (1998) TTF profiles include the independent constructs of group tasks and GSS, we use three independent constructs, and suggest a three-step process to determine fit between managerial tasks, mobile IT, and mobile use context (Tables 1, 3–5).

In the first step, we conceptualize the fit between managerial tasks and IT (Fit 1, Table 3), independent of the use context. In the second step, we focus on the features of mobile IT to determine feasibility in a mobile use context (Fit 2, Table 4), independent of a particular task. In the final step, we join Fit 1 and Fit 2 to derive TTF for mobile IS in support of managerial tasks that are performed in a mobile use context (Fit 3, Table 5).

Fit 1: managerial tasks and IT

To highlight the general conditions of fit between a managerial task and supporting IT, we omit characteristics that are associated primarily with mobile IT. Fit 1 corresponds therefore most closely with ‘traditional’ concepts of TTF where technology has been modeled based on functionality, and is applicable to both mobile and nonmobile IT.

Task non-routineness: Media richness is a concept that links managerial tasks (simple vs complex) with different types of IT in an effort to align both for best support (Daft and Lengel, 1984). Rich media (telephone and face-to-face meetings) is needed to process complex (i.e., non-routine) tasks (e.g., setting organizational goals, strategies), whereas media low in information richness (written information sources, technical manuals) is best to handle simple (i.e., routine) tasks, such as inventory control. The propositions of media richness theory have largely been confirmed for various types of IT applications (Markus, 1994; Lim and Benbasat, 2000; Majchrzak et al., 2000).

We build on two key elements in the theory of mediarichness, namely the intensity of personal interaction and the extent to which natural language is used between the individual and task-supporting IT. Referring to Table 2, IT that predominantly automate data processing (e.g., e-procurement) are regarded as comparatively information poor and best suited to support and enable routine tasks (Gebauer and Shaw, 2004; see also Sørensen et al., 2002). In contrast, IT that allow for interactive communication (e.g., phone and email), and flexible information access (browserbased) are comparatively information rich (Gebauer et al., 2007) and best suited to support and enable non-routine tasks (Table 3). We propose:

Proposition 1a: Managerial tasks of low non-routineness should result in best performance when using an IS that emphasizes data processing.

Proposition 1b: Managerial tasks of high non-routineness should result in best performance when using an IS that emphasizes communication and information access.

We suggest that the extent to which non-routine tasks are relevant for managerial work (Mintzberg, 1980; Whitley, 1989; Markus, 1994) should be reflected in the emphasis on IT to enable managers to communicate and access information (Perry et al., 2001; Venezia et al., 2008). In contrast, support for routine tasks includes productivity tools such as scheduling (Bederson et al., 2004), dispatch and routing (Sørensen et al., 2008), and e-procurement applications (Gebauer and Shaw, 2004).

Task-interdependence: Since task-interdependence requires coordination (Malone and Crowston, 1994), highly interdependent tasks tend to rely heavily on communication and the exchange of information to clarify assignments and project requirements, develop effective performance strategies, make decisions, and obtain performance feedback (Straus and McGrath, 1994; Andres and Zmud, 2002). The higher the level of interdependence, the less standardized the suggested form of coordination can be (Thompson, 1967; Daft and Lengel, 1984; Whitley, 1989). The number of different relationships that need to be supported, and the frequency with which interaction occurs typically, increase the complexity and difficulty of managing task interdependence (Simon, 1960).

Time criticality: To support time-critical tasks, noti fication applications (numeric pagers for emergency alerts) were among the earliest applications of mobile IT (Ammenwerth et al., 2000). Notification about a waiting task can improve task performance and help managers cope with immediacy requirements of prompt response and performance (Gebauer and Shaw, 2004; Gebauer, 2008). As non-time-critical tasks tend to be less demanding, we focus our proposition on time-critical tasks.

Proposition 3: Managerial tasks that are highly timecritical should result in best performance when using an IS that emphasizes notification.

Fit 2: mobile IT and mobile use context

We now develop Fit 2 with a focus on the association between mobile IT and mobile use context. Besides functionality, a characteristic common to all IT, we include user interface and adaptability as features specific to mobile IT (Table 4). The underlying assumption is that compared to a stationary office environment, fit for mobile IS is more difficult to achieve because of user-distraction and - mobility, and a low or varying level of network quality.

Level of distraction high: Situations of distraction and limited attention often characterize mobile use contexts (Lee and Benbasat, 2004). When too much demand is placed on the user’s memory, information overload can occur and reduce usability of the system (Chan et al., 2002). Research has emphasized the need to design interfaces with clear visual cues (Chan et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2004), to limit information analysis and processing by the user (Ebling et al., 2002; McCrickard et al., 2003), and to limit the complexity of system transactions (Lee and Benbasat, 2004). We suggest that communication functionality is best suited to support mobile users in situations of distraction and limited attention. In contrast, powerful desktopcomputers used in a non-mobile environment may provide a more appropriate platform for needs of extensive and uninterrupted information access and exchange, and to support complicated processing.

Even though the active use of mobile IT often occurs in calm environments with low distraction and while the user is not walking (Kim et al., 2002), distraction and movement are undisputed characteristics of mobile work (Zheng and Yuan, 2007). Even if a user manages to stay focused during the interaction with an IS, environmental factors naturally demand attention from the non-active (potential) user who carries a mobile device outside of a stationary office environment. Kim et al.’s (2002) results emphasize the need for effective notification to get the attention of a mobile worker who subsequently may or may not retreat to a quiet area to attend to the waiting task (McCrickard et al., 2003). Moreover, verification features can acknowledge to the sender that a message has been received and viewed by the mobile user. For situations of high distraction, we propose:

Proposition 4a: A mobile use context characterized by high user-distraction should be supported by notification functionality in combination with verification, and by communication functionality for best overall task performance.

Proposition 4b: A mobile use context characterized by high user-distraction should result in suboptimal task performance when primarily supported by information access functionality and data processing functionality.

Proposition 4c: A mobile use context characterized by high user-distraction should be supported by a well-designed user interface that provides targeted cues and requires minimal attention for best overall task performance.

Quality of network connection low: The quality of a wireless network connection can be low or non-existent because of limited coverage, bandwidth, and network instability (Kleinrock, 1996). Poor network quality prevents the user from accessing the corporate IT infrastructure and can hinder the usefulness, feasibility, and success of the mobile IS (Beulen and Streng, 2002; Varshney et al., 2002; Chan et al., 2002; Gebauer and Shaw, 2004).

We propose that notification of the mobile recipient combined with verification to the sender as to whether a message has been received is easiest to provide in cases of limited network connectivity. More complex functionalities that require greater bandwidth and more stable connections are more difficult to achieve (Chan et al., 2002). Recommendations for the user interface in situations of poor connection quality include limitations of required up- and download times and bandwidth, and distraction-minimizing indicators of connection quality (Ebling et al., 2002). We propose:

Proposition 5a: A mobile use context characterized by limited quality of network connection should be supported by notification functionality in combination with verification for best overall task performance.

Proposition 5b: A mobile use context characterized by limited quality of network connection should result in suboptimal task performance when primarily supported by communication, information access, and data processing functionalities.

Proposition 5c: A mobile use context generally characterized by limited quality of network connection should be supported by applications with a specially designed user interface that includes limited bandwidth requirements and indicators of network quality for best overall task performance.

User mobility: Situations of mobility present a challenge primarily because they are associated with unpredictable changes in user distraction, network availability, and stability (Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999; Balasubramaniam et al., 2002; Tarasewich et al., 2002). The adaptability and location-awareness of a mobile IS can support its use in varying locations and use contexts (Rao and Minakakis, 2003; Liang and Wei, 2004), and in situations of active movement during ‘travel’ and ‘wandering’ (Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999). Among the suggestions for adaptability are ring-mechanisms that change depending on whether the user is outdoors or indoors, the automatic adjustment to local time, and location-based services, such as finding a hotel or allowing others to locate the moving user (Kristoffersen and Ljungberg, 1999). Usability can be limited when moving users are not aware of insufficient or weakening signal strength (Chan et al., 2002), which prompted suggestions of IT applications that display connectivity information (Ebling et al., 2002). For cases of high user-mobility, allowing the IS to continue tasks that were disrupted because of sudden changes of network quality and user distraction is recommended (Kleinrock, 1996). We propose:

Proposition 6a: A use context characterized by high user-mobility should be supported by a user interface that can accommodate changes in the levels of user distraction and of the quality of network connection for best overall task performance.

Proposition 6b: A use context characterized by high user-mobility should be supported by adaptability (location-awareness and customization) of the mobile IS for best overall task performance.

Table 4 summarizes the previous discussion, and highlights that use situations of high user-distraction and low quality of network connection are particularly difficult to support. Situations of high user-mobility tend to be defined by requirements of the user interface and adaptability. In contrast, a mobile use context that is characterized by low distraction, high quality of network connection, and low mobility does not require particular functionality, user interface, and adaptability features beyond what is required in a regular, stationary use context.

## Discussion

In an effort to apply and extend TTF to mobile IS in support of managerial tasks and to develop practical guidelines for IS design, we have derived a number of propositions based on previous studies in the research disciplines of organization, mobile IT, and IS. To derive a complete profile of fit requirements for a particular combination of managerial task and mobile use context, we refer to Table 5 and note that all cells and associated propositions that are located at the intersection of a column (representing an applicable task characteristic) and row (representing an applicable characteristic of the mobile use context) need to be taken into consideration.

Our analysis suggests that the mobile use context can limit the feasibility of certain combinations of task and IT. While each dimension of the mobile use context adds to the requirements of a mobile IS, the feasible fit of functional scope is limited in particular in situations of high userdistraction and of low quality of network connections. In an extreme case, a full set of task requirements pertaining to non-routineness, high interdependence, and high timecriticality has to be combined with a full set of restrictions pertaining to the mobile use context, including high distraction, low quality of network connection, and high mobility. The difficulties of such an undertaking are evident. In the following, an example illustrates the applicability of our model.

## Illustrating example

Let us assume a manager travels to locations where the quality of network connection is low because the preferred mobile carrier does not provide adequate network coverage. Let us also assume the manager is expected to receive and approve purchasing requests from her staff, meaning that the task at hand is characterized by high routineness, high interdependence, and possibly high time-criticality.

Referring to Table 5, fit requirements are associated with low quality connectivity (row 2), high task routineness (column 1), high task interdependence (column 3), and high time-criticality (column 4). Thus, Fit 3 calls for data processing, communication, and notification to support a task of high routineness (P1a), high interdependence (P2) and high time-criticality (P3), yet allows primarily for notification in combination with verification because of low quality of network connection (P5a, P5b). Additional user interface requirements include measures to manage the limited quality of network connection (P5c).

Our model can help to identify difficulties with achieving an overall fit for the specific situation, whereby typically more than one solution exists. For example, a decision to equip the manager with a mobile e-procurement system (data processing functionality) can provide adequate support for the task at hand (Fit 1), but may result in a poor fit with the individual use context (Fit 2) and negative implications for system success. A similar case is reported by Gebauer and Shaw (2004) for a Fortune 100 company where the implementation of a mobile e-procurement system was unsuccessful, even though the conditions of Fit 1 were largely met, but not the conditions of Fit 2. Alternative solutions within the model include adjustments of the individual use context or even the task itself. For example, the organization can decide to allow the use of a different wireless provider at the location of travel, even if such a provider is not on the list of preferred vendors and therefore more expensive. In that case the dimension of low quality network connection that is associated with the mobile use context is reduced, and a fit between task and technology can be established. As an alternative, the task can be adapted to the mobile use context: Instead of providing a traveling manager with an application to perform data processing (approval of purchasing request), the manager can merely be notified about a waiting request and subsequently use communication for delegation to a staff-member in a more favorable use context (better network connection). In summary, the conceptual model supports the systematic analysis of a particular use situation for informed decision-making to achieve a three-way fit between IT, managerial task, and use context.

## Implications for research

The model has implications for IS research, in particular for the research area of TTF. First, we have presented profiles for the combination of managerial tasks and mobile IT.

A notable number of previous TTF studies have sought to understand the role that TTF plays as a factor to explain and predict IS success (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Dishaw and Strong, 1998) but have been less concerned with situated requirements of fit. We believe that wellfounded and concrete fit profiles, such as the one presented here will be helpful to advance the practical relevance of TTF as a research discipline, and to add to our understanding about the characteristics, conditions, and implications of fit as a construct of IS research.

Second, our analysis of mobile IT has prompted us to add a third dimension to TTF, namely use context. Even though previous studies of TTF have analyzed a variety of different IT (Goodhue and Thompson, 1995; Dishaw and Strong, 1998; Zigurs and Buckland, 1998), a common assumption has been the stationary use of IT in a regular office environment. To the extent that IT characteristics differ between mobile and non-mobile use contexts, we follow the call of scholars of socio-technological perspectives to apply an encompassing approach to IS analysis (Sawyer et al., 2003; Markus, 2004; McCullough, 2004; Chen and Nath, 2008). We also showcase a connecting link between the rather deterministic concept of ‘traditional’ TTF and the stated need to consider social and environmental factors of IT use. As the workforce continues to become more mobile, use conditions of IT will become more varied. We consequently expect the context of use to further gain importance as a differentiator of IS use and an explanatory variable of performance impacts.

Third, we have presented a three-step procedure to determine TTF that can be applied to many combinations of tasks, IT, and use contexts. For example, instead of analyzing the requirements of managerial tasks as we did, Fit 1 could be based on Zigurs and Buckland’s (1998) analysis to determine the fit of GSS to support group tasks. Fit 3 would then describe a suggested fit profile of GSS to support group tasks in mobile use contexts. The idea of deriving fit by using a procedure similar to matrix algebra can become a useful tool for the analysis of complex situations where individual conditions can be expressed in a vector-like form (Gribbins et al., 2006).

## Implications for practitioners

Mobile IS studies have uncovered poor support for complex transactions and have found that the use of mobile IT is limited in complicated environments (Siau and Shen, 2003). By improving our understanding about the practical requirements of successful mobile IS, our model can support practitioners in charge of the development and use of mobile IS. It can help identify opportunities and limits associated with IT innovations and it provides guidelines for effective and efficient management of mobile IS, whereby fit can be achieved by adjusting more than one TTF factor, including the technology (e.g., more adequate user interface), the individual use-context (e.g., subscribing to a more high-quality network provider), and the task (e.g., completion via notification and delegation instead of direct completion).

The model also re-iterates the notion that in general, the less the mobile use context resembles a ‘regular’ office work environment with a low level of distraction, high level of network connection quality, and low mobility, the more difficult it is to provide adequate IT support. We found that situations of high distraction and low quality of network connection can be particularly challenging (Nicholson et al., 2005) because the functionalities that are required by the managerial task are difficult to provide, while meeting the requirements of the user interface becomes critical.

Practitioners can further use the model to identify areas where the deployment of mobile IT is particularly promising or difficult. Moreover the model can help to explain the success of certain forms of mobile IS, such as applications that provide simple, yet highly task-oriented functionality, for example notification and basic communication systems (Gebauer and Shaw, 2004).

## Outlook

Having identified relevant variables of analysis and discussed the relationships between them, our model stands at the intersection between ‘theory’ and ‘research (Dubin, 1978). It will now benefit from empirical testing to determine its general adequacy, to deepen our understanding of the proposed relationships, and to improve the practical applicability of the suggested design tool.

Perhaps most obvious, the actual impact of the suggested fit profiles of managerial tasks, mobile IT, and use context (Fit 3) on task performance needs to be measured in an effort to strengthen the propositions of the current model (Junglas and Watson, 2003). To that effect, we suggest qualitative and quantitative empirical studies among mobile users who work in a joint organizational setting, such as a division that has recently equipped its managers with smart phones. We also suggest empirical studies among managers to explore the frequency of different combinations of managerial tasks and mobile use contexts (Kim et al., 2002; Venezia et al., 2008). A better understanding about the relative importance of particular work and use scenarios has practical implications in supporting the development and management of effective mobile IS, and also contributes to usability and interface research.

We further suggest empirical research studies on the role and antecedents of TTF-related behavioral variables, such as adoption, use (Lopperi and Sengupta, 2004; Schwarz et al., 2004; Kim et al., 2006), and performance impacts, on individual and organizational levels (Jeyaraj et al., 2006). For example, a research study of TTF for mobile email found that user-perceived IT maturity played a major role to explain user behavior (Gebauer et al., 2007). The results suggest a need to analyze the theoretical boundaries of TTF, and consider idiosyncratic factors, such as the level of maturity of IT innovations, when applying TTF to new areas of research.

Our model will also benefit from the consideration of time as a research construct. Whereas the current model focuses on establishing fit at a given point in time, others have pointed out the importance of maintaining fit in the long run (Boddy and Paton, 2005). Besides changes that are to be expected for innovative IT, task-related and use context-related changes have to be considered. Future studies may want to explore the interdependencies between structural and adaptable elements of TTF that together ensure long-term flexibility (Boddy and Paton, 2005).

Finally, we suggest extending the notion of TTF to assess the viability of mobile IS as proposed by Liang and Wei (2004) for mobile commerce. Rather than focusing on mobile IS that merely provide a good fit with various tasks, it may be beneficial to strive for systems that also promise managerial and financial success.

## About the authors

Judith Gebauer is an Associate Professor of Information Systems at the University of North Carolina Wilmington. She holds both master and doctoral degrees from the University of Freiburg, Germany, and has held positions at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and at the University of California, Berkeley. Her research focuses on the design and management of business information systems and includes projects on the task-technology fit of mobile information systems, information system flexibility and the impact of information technology on product modularity. Her work has been published in such journals as Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Communications of the ACM, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Information Systems Management, Information Systems and e-Business Management, Electronic Markets, Information Technology and Management, Informatik Forschung und Entwicklung, and International Journal of Mobile Communications. For more information, please visit judithgebauer.com.

Michael J Shaw is a Professor and Hoeft Endowed Chair of Information Systems in the Department of Business Administration at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. He is also the Director of Center for IT and e-Business there. He has published extensively with over 140 papers in the information systems, e-business, and decision support areas. He was an Associate Editor of Information Systems Research, 1991–2003, and has been the Editor-in-Chief (with J. Becker) of the journal Information Systems and e-Business Management, published by Springer, since its inauguration in 2003. Mike’s research has focused on studying how to make information technology beneficial to large enterprises. Because of that interest he has been working with companies such as Motorola, GE, Wal-Mart, and Caterpillar on research projects. Mike was a Fulbright Scholar and continues to keep a global perspective in his research and teaching. He oversees information systems programs on all levels at Illinois. He is affiliated with, in addition to his home department, National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) and Information Trust Institute (ITI). His most recent research has focused on the functions of Chief Information Officers, service management, and IT governance.

Michele L Gribbins is a Visiting Assistant Professor in Management Information Systems at the University of Illinois at Springfield and a Ph.D. Candidate in Information Systems at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. Her primary research interests are centered on the process and human factors associated with the adoption, usage, and impact of emerging technologies in enterprise environments. She serves as a mini-track chair for the Americas Conference on Information Systems and as an officer for the AIS Special Interest Group on e-business. Her research has been published in Communications of the Association for Information Systems, Electronic Markets, the International Journal of Management Theory and Practices, and has been presented at the Americas Conference on Information Systems, the International Conference on Information Systems, and at the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences. Before joining the Ph.D. program, she was employed as a computer trainer and as an adjunct business instructor.
