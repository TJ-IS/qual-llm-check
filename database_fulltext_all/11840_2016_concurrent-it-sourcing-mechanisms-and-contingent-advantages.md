# Concurrent IT Sourcing: Mechanisms and Contingent Advantages

AMRIT TIWANA AND STEPHEN K. KIM

ABSTRACT: A growing trend to simultaneously insource and outsource the same information technology (IT) activities (“concurrent IT sourcing”) has not yet received research attention. Although it is widespread and recent empirical studies have detected that in-house IT can complement IT outsourcing, when and how concurrent IT sourcing pays off is not yet understood. This study introduces the notion of concurrent IT sourcing. It then develops two interrelated ideas: concurrent IT sourcing simultaneously enhances in-house and outsourced IT performance: (a) via distinctive mechanisms, but (b) only when vendors’ IT capabilities complement the client’s. Econometric tests using survey data from 233 firms support these ideas. Our novel contribution is to explain when and how concurrent IT sourcing enhances a client firm’s inhouse and outsourced IT performance. The explanatory mechanisms for outsourced IT performance are socialization and modeling of clients’ in-house IT practices by vendors; for inhouse IT performance they are knowledge spillovers and ratcheting. For practice, our study shows that when a firm’s in-house capabilities complement its IT vendors’ capabilities, firms can simultaneously outsource and insource the same IT activities to enhance both in-house and outsourced IT performance.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: concurrent IT sourcing, insourcing, IT capabilities, IT governance, outsourcing, plural governance, seemingly unrelated regression.

Firms have historically chosen either to outsource or insource an information technology (IT) activity. An unanticipated recent trend has been to eschew insourcing and outsourcing as mutually exclusive choices and instead doing both. We refer to the phenomenon of firms simultaneously insourcing and outsourcing the same IT activities as concurrent IT sourcing. Practitioners refer to such a mix of insourcing and outsourcing as “rightsourcing” [53, 62], implying a shift away from the dyadic terminology of past decades.

The concurrent IT sourcing trend crosscuts diverse industries, especially as even nontechnology firms rely more on IT to produce their offerings and to interact with customers. Companies like General Motors, General Electric, Starbucks, NCR, and Caterpillar are recent examples of firms that have brought back in-house some—but not all—IT activities that were previously outsourced [62]. GM, for example, brought back in-house 8,000 IT jobs for developing software embedded in its cars in 2014. This can be viewed as a rebalancing toward concurrent IT sourcing rather than a wholesale reversal of outsourcing (e.g., backsourcing à la [9]) [45].

The premise is that this concurrent IT sourcing strategy allows firms to combine the advantages of in-house IT such as greater responsiveness to strategic opportunities with those of outsourcing such as cost competitiveness [62]. In-house IT skills can be maintained without forsaking vendors’ scale advantages [45, 53]. Yet given the considerable resources that replicating the same IT activities in-house and with vendors plausibly entails, it is important to better understand when it is worthwhile as well as its consequences. Pursuing concurrent IT sourcing without an appreciation of these nuances can be a frivolous extravagance at best and financially devastating at worst. Given its recency, concurrent IT sourcing has yet to receive research attention.

Prior IT outsourcing research has focused on two broad themes: (1) the decision to outsource IT and (2) drivers of IT outsourcing performance. The first stream focuses on why firms outsource some IT activities but not others [2, 9, 63, 71]. This stream predominantly uses a transaction cost logic to explain the decision to outsource IT. The second stream focuses on understanding how relational factors and control mechanisms between client and vendor firms enhance IT outsourcing performance [38, 61, 64]. We know from the IT outsourcing literature that relational quality between clients and vendors enhances IT outsourcing performance [38]. Yet, control mechanisms that clients attempt to use have mixed results [36, 64], in part due to the noncontractible elements unique to IT activities [46]. Whether and how the recent trend toward concurrent IT sourcing mitigates these challenges has yet to be examined.

As practitioners suspect, recent empirical studies have also detected some complementarities between insourcing and outsourcing (e.g. [16, 41]). Some have even questioned the conventional belief that outsourcing and insourcing are substitutes [26]. For example, recent studies have found that more in-house IT increases outsourcing’s payoffs in reducing non-IT operating costs [26]; and outsourcing of complex IT requires increasing investments in in-house IT [41]. Yet, the question of when concurrent IT sourcing—with its commensurate costs—is advantageous remains understudied, as do the mechanisms (the how) through which it influences IT performance [26, p. 327]. We focus on these two theoretical gaps, guided by the following research question: When and how does concurrent IT sourcing enhance a client firm’s performance in its IT activities?

To address this question, we theoretically develop two interrelated sets of ideas. Concurrent sourcing of IT enhances both in-house and outsourced IT performance via distinctive mechanisms, but only when vendors’ IT capabilities complement the client’s. The explanatory mechanisms for outsourced IT performance are socialization and modeling of clients’ in-house IT practices by vendors; for the in-house IT performance side they are knowledge spillovers and ratcheting. Tests using data collected from 233 firms with seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) models provide considerable support for the hypotheses.

Our novel theoretical contribution to the IT sourcing literature is to explain when and how concurrent IT sourcing—conceptualized here for the first time—enhances a client firm’s in-house and outsourced IT performance. Concurrent IT sourcing catalyzes the otherwise-inert potential of partnering with vendors whose IT capabilities complement the client’s. We also unmask two separate pairs of mechanisms through which concurrent IT sourcing and interfirm complementarity in IT capabilities jointly enhance in-house and outsourced IT performance in a client firm. Therefore, this exploratory study offers new insights into learning-related mechanisms through which clients can realize the benefits of concurrent IT sourcing arrangements. Subsequent sections of the paper theoretically develop the concurrent IT sourcing concept and the hypotheses, describe the methodology, analyses and results, and discuss its contributions and implications.

## Theoretical Development

## Concurrent IT Sourcing

We define concurrent IT sourcing as the degree of simultaneous insourcing and outsourcing of the same IT activities by a client, building on the broader notion of concurrent sourcing as simultaneously insourcing and outsourcing the same task [29, 49]. Such IT activities encompass systems development, testing, integration, maintenance, support, and IT services provisioning [7]. A client therefore simultaneously uses both outsourcing and insourcing for its IT activities instead of choosing between them. This increasingly observed arrangement has been also labeled as rightsourcing [53, 62], plural governance [12, 29], mixed sourcing [43], and partial integration [31, 33].<sup>1</sup>

The fledgling body of research on concurrent sourcing—largely outside information systems (IS)—has focused primarily on why firms choose concurrent sourcing over insourcing or outsourcing [28, 29, 49].<sup>2</sup> The premise is that concurrent sourcing simultaneously draws on the strengths of insourcing (e.g., improved coordination and control) as well as outsourcing (e.g., scale economies and opportunities to learn from outside firms). The intuitively appealing—but untested—assumption in the concurrent sourcing literature is that concurrent sourcing ameliorates weaknesses of insourcing and outsourcing [11, 49].

Theorizing the performance consequences of concurrent IT sourcing requires attention to two nuances: (a) The dependent variable and (b) the unit of analysis. First, it requires simultaneously but separately considering in-house and outsourced IT performance. We define in-house and outsourced IT performance as the degree to which the work produced respectively by the client’s in-house IT department and the IT vendors supports the client firm’s business goals and priorities. IT outsourcing studies have historically and legitimately focused on outsourced performance (e.g. [38, 61]). Prior concurrent sourcing studies outside IS simply aggregate both in-house and outsourced performance (e.g. [11, 29, 49]). We cannot understand how concurrent IT sourcing influences in-house and outsourced IT performance without disaggregating them.

Second, we must zoom out the unit of analysis to the IT function level to examine a client firm’s portfolio of vendor relationships. IT outsourcing studies have historically used either a project or dyadic outsourcing relationships as the unit of analysis (e.g. [4, 61, 63]).<sup>3</sup> Exploration of learning mechanisms that maximizes the performance benefits of concurrent IT sourcing requires looking beyond discrete projects at the IT function as a whole. Such zooming out helps unmask performance benefits that accrue over time to the client over multiple outsourced projects spanning multiple vendor relationships.

Concurrent IT sourcing by itself should not be expected to enhance IT performance because it simply replicates the same set of IT activities in-house and with vendors. It acts as a catalyst for enhancing IT performance only when the vendors’ IT capabilities are complementary to the client’s in-house IT capabilities, but it is inert without it. Using the client firm’s IT function as the unit of analysis, we first turn to the four facilitating mechanisms that bridge concurrent IT sourcing to inhouse and outsourced IT performance.

## Facilitating Mechanisms and IT Performance

Our theoretical development focuses first on four facilitating mechanisms that bridge the client and vendors to influence IT performance. Our focus on these four mechanisms stems from Bradach’s [11] early inductive derivation of such mechanisms; we venture theoretically far beyond this starting point.<sup>4</sup> We first conceptually develop them in an IT context and then construct a nomological network around these mechanisms in ways that Bradach did not.

These four mechanisms theoretically belong together because they are learningrelated; they facilitate learning between the client and vendor firms that allows them to build on each other’s strengths in performing IT activities. We theorize that two unidirectional mechanisms—modeling and socialization—influence outsourced IT performance; two bidirectional ones—ratcheting and spillovers—influence in-house IT performance. Directionality is the direction of interfirm learning/knowledge transfer expected. These facilitating mechanisms are the explanatory pathways from concurrent IT sourcing to in-house and outsourced IT performance. Our subsequent emphasis on interfirm IT capability complementarity stems from the learning-related property shared by the four facilitating mechanisms.

## Modeling

We define modeling as the degree to which the IT vendors’ practices are patterned after the client’s in-house IT department, building on prior conceptualizations of modeling [55]. Modeling is by definition a unidirectional process, that is, vendors model their IT practices after the client but the client does not do the same. Modeling mitigates in two ways a recognized challenge in outsourcing [43, 56, 61], verification of the quality of vendors’ work [17]. First, it is often difficult for clients to evaluate work processes and procedures being used by vendors because of the lack of structures and systems comparable to those used by the client firm [43]. Modeling allows vendors to internalize and embed in their own operating procedures and practices those used by the client [11]. This allows the client to more readily evaluate vendors’ work. Use of comparable practices and procedures allows vendors to structure their work for the clients in ways that also permit their outputs to be more readily integrated into the client’s own IT operations. Second, it allows the client to structure outsourcing contracts in ways that make opportunistic behavior by vendors more readily detectable, thus discouraging it. We therefore expect modeling to enhance outsourced IT performance. Since modeling is a unidirectional process that enhances quality verification of outsourced IT activities by the client, we do not expect it to influence in-house IT performance. This leads to our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Modeling enhances outsourced IT performance.

## Socialization

We define socialization as the degree to which the client acclimates its IT vendors to its own goals, values, and norms [61]. Therefore socialization involves vendors’ learning to operate within the client firm’s context, unlike modeling that involves adopting the client firm’s IT work practices. Socialization is by definition also a unidirectional process, that is, vendors are socialized into the client’s norms but not the other way around. Socialization is an interfirm version of clan control [35, 47], with benefits accruing primarily from mitigating the moral hazard problem pervasive in IT outsourcing relationships [17]. Socialization reduces in two ways the need for verification of vendors’ work quality, unlike modeling, which reduces its difficulty. First, it helps create convergent expectations with the vendors—whose absence frequently impedes outsourcing [72]—about what the client expects from them. This encourages a greater level of noncontractible effort by the vendors, increasing the likelihood that they will strive to fulfill even those client expectations that cannot be contractually spelled out [40, 66]. (Noncontractibility is a widespread impediment in interfirm IT sourcing arrangements [46].) Second, vendors who share the client’s goals, values, and norms are also less likely to misrepresent the quality of the work produced for the client, lessening the need for the client to engage in extensive quality verification. Therefore, we expect that socialization, by reducing the need for verification of vendors’ work quality, enhances outsourced IT performance. Socialization does not influence in-house IT performance because it is a unidirectional process that reduces only the need for the client to verify the quality of the work produced by vendors. This leads to our second hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: Socialization enhances outsourced IT performance.

## Ratcheting

We define ratcheting as the degree to which the client uses in-house IT performance data to set expectations for its IT vendors [69]. Ratcheting is a bidirectional process in that in-house IT performance data are used to set vendor expectations; vendor performance data also implicitly facilitate benchmarking of in-house IT operations. A pervasive challenge in managing IT activities is that of setting appropriate performance expectations [17]. Although vendors can be motivated by performance clauses and the threat of contract termination [25, 55], the client relies more on legitimate authority over its own employees to correct performance shortcomings of its in-house IT department. Ratcheting bolsters in-house IT performance by legitimately complementing such hierarchical authority with performance benchmark data from vendors. Such data provide the client a credible basis for setting performance expectations for in-house IT staff and also discourage shirking [27]. Higher vendor performance plausibly allows the raising of performance expectations for the inhouse IT department. We do not expect ratcheting to affect vendors’ performance because they are likely prescreened for their ability to perform the outsourced IT activities better than the client’s in-house IT department [17]. Therefore, we expect ratcheting to enhance in-house but not outsourced IT performance.

Hypothesis 3: Ratcheting enhances in-house IT performance.

## Spillovers

We define spillovers as the degree to which the client and vendors acquire new IT knowledge and skills from each other through their interactions. Knowledge spillovers from IT vendors are an important mechanism to enhance clients’ IT skills [16, 26, p. 327, 41]. Spillovers expose in-house IT staff to vendors’ ideas and different ways of performing IT activities that they can apply to in-house IT operations [14, 44, 48]. This helps the client’s IT department assess and improve institutionalized IT practices in ways that can enhance in-house IT performance. Therefore learning from vendors can improve the client’s in-house IT performance, just as knowledge transfer within the firm does [23, 37, 60]. Since such learning is by definition bidirectional, vendors also gain a better understanding of the client’s business that they can use to better tailor their work to a client’s needs. Therefore, we expect knowledge spillovers between the client and vendors to enhance in-house as well as outsourced IT performance.

Hypothesis 4: Knowledge spillovers enhance (a) in-house and (b) outsourced IT performance.

## Interfirm IT Capability Complementarity

IT capabilities broadly refer to a firm’s portfolio of IT knowledge, skills, competencies, and IT resources [3, 10]. We therefore define interfirm IT capability complementarity as the degree to which the IT knowledge, IT skills, and IT expertise of the client’s in-house IT department complement those of its IT vendors. This builds on established definitions of IT capabilities internal to the firm, such as Bharadwaj’s [10], which encompasses IT knowledge, experience, and skills, and Aral and Weill’s [3] definition as IT skills and competencies.

Although interfirm capability complementarity is implicitly invoked in prior sourcing studies, it is largely absent in theorizing [31].<sup>5</sup> Even in transaction cost economics—the crucible of outsourcing theorizing—Williamson [73, p. 1087] emphasizes the need for theoretically incorporating interfirm capability differences, which he refers to as “the distinctive strengths and disabilities of particular firms.” Barney [6, p. 140] also explicitly laments inattention to the capabilities of a firm relative to its partners in the sourcing literature. We focus on interfirm IT capability complementarity in our model because the four facilitating mechanisms are learningrelated; there are greater opportunities for interfirm learning to occur when the client’s and vendors’ capabilities are complementary.

Firms increasingly outsource to access best-of-breed IT capabilities of IT vendors [4, 53, 62]. Interfirm IT capability complementarity allows the client to access specialized IT skills that it might not internally possess. But interfirm IT capability differences that make such partnerships potentially valuable also exacerbate integration of work between the client and vendor firms [6]. In isolation, interfirm IT capability complementarity introduces coordination and control challenges, making it unlikely by itself to enhance IT performance. But its combination with concurrent IT sourcing is mutually reinforcing; more of one makes more of the other more valuable. Their mutually reinforcing interaction activates the facilitating mechanisms to enhance IT performance. Put differently, IT capability complementarity is the context under which concurrent IT sourcing enhances the four facilitating mechanisms. The two can be viewed as an inert ingredient and a catalyst, useless in isolation but powerful in combination. We theorize next how their mutually reinforcing interplay influences each of the four facilitating mechanisms in our model.

## The Mutually Reinforcing Interplay Between Concurrent IT Sourcing and IT Capability Complementarity

## The Interplay’s Effect on Modeling

Higher complementarity between the client’s in-house IT department and the vendors’ IT capabilities implies that vendors are less likely to use practices and procedures for IT activities similar to the client. This can increase the difficulty for vendors to pattern their own work after the client. Without in-depth knowledge of the outsourced IT activities, it is also difficult for a client to properly interpret information provided by interfirm monitoring mechanisms [29].

Greater use of concurrent IT sourcing mitigates this problem. Carrying out similar IT activities in-house allows the client to better understand the outsourced activity, increasing what Kirsch [34] calls knowledge of the transformation process. This provides the client firsthand knowledge to better interpret and evaluate vendors’ work quality and to help vendors’ model their own IT activities on how the client’s in-house IT department performs them. Therefore, we expect interfirm IT capability complementarity and concurrent IT sourcing to reinforce each other in enhancing modeling (i.e., a positive interaction). The former provides the context for the latter to enhance modeling. This leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5: Concurrent IT sourcing enhances modeling when IT capability complementarity is higher.

The Interplay’s Effect on Socialization

Higher complementarity between the client’s and vendors’ IT capabilities implies that vendors are less likely to share the client’s goals, values, and norms [32, 43]. The absence of preexisting shared norms and mutual understanding thus creates a greater opportunity to create them through concurrent sourcing. For example, pursuing the vendors’ IT activities in-house through concurrent sourcing deepens the client’s own understanding of the vendors’ work. This deepening of mutual understanding facilitates diffusion of the client’s norms and expectations through its interactions with its vendors. It does so by increasing the client’s ability to communicate its expectations about the work for which vendors are responsible and also by reducing the need to overtly micromanage the vendors’ work as it would in the absence of such mutual understanding. We therefore expect that interfirm IT capability complementarity provides a context for concurrent IT sourcing to enhance socialization of vendors. The two therefore mutually reinforce each other in enhancing socialization (i.e., a positive interaction). This leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6: Concurrent IT sourcing enhances socialization when IT capability complementarity is higher.

The Interplay’s Effect on Ratcheting

Higher complementarity between the client’s and vendors’ IT capabilities implies that they specialize in different IT activities. This makes it challenging for the client to use data from its in-house IT department to set performance expectations for vendors; such data might simply not exist. Concurrent IT sourcing provides the client data from comparable IT activities done in-house, facilitating ratcheting. Such in-house experience also provides the client firsthand knowledge of the outsourced IT activity, enabling the client to write better contracts and to better manage them [26, 67]. Such ratcheting is of limited value unless the client’s in-house capabilities are complementary to the vendors’. We therefore expect interfirm IT capability complementarity to provide a context for concurrent IT sourcing to enhance ratcheting. Their mutually reinforcing interaction therefore enhances ratcheting (i.e., a positive interaction). This leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 7: Concurrent IT sourcing enhances ratcheting when IT capability complementarity is higher.

The Interplay’s Effect on Spillovers

Knowledge spillovers between the client and vendors have two prerequisites: (a) there must be knowledge differences between them and (b) the means for spillovers to occur. The more complementary their IT capabilities, the greater the potential to learn new things and skills from each other. The client and vendor are potential sources of novel knowledge for each other only when such differences exist. Yet, mere access to vendors’ dissimilar capabilities does not lead to spillovers; it only creates their possibility. Greater in-house engagement in IT activities similar to the vendors provides both in-house IT staff and vendors the background knowledge and context to absorb new knowledge from each other. We therefore expect interfirm IT capability complementarity to provide a context for concurrent IT sourcing to enhance interfirm knowledge spillovers. Their mutually reinforcing interaction therefore enhances spillovers (i.e., a positive interaction). This leads to our final hypothesis:

Hypothesis 8: Concurrent IT sourcing enhances spillovers when IT capability complementarity is higher.

## Methodology

We collected data using a survey of the IT departments in 233 U.S. firms to test the proposed ideas. Our unit of analysis is the firm’s IT function, similar to other firm-level studies of IT functions (e.g. [5, 58]). Our data collection focused on the client firm’s inhouse IT unit and its portfolio of all IT vendors used by it over a two year period.

## Scale Development, Construct Operationalization, and Refinement

Table 1 provides the operational definitions of each key construct in our model. We adapted existing multi-item, seven-point Likert scales to measure all controls and some theoretical constructs in the model. The scale items for the new measures and the items and sources for the control variables appear in Appendix A. We developed new scales for concurrent IT sourcing and the four facilitating mechanisms—modeling, ratcheting, socialization, and spillovers—building on their conceptualization in the representative references in Table 1. We used a convenience sample of five IT managers and eight academic experts to refine and validate the item pool. The objective of this iterative scale refinement process was to ensure that the scale items were meaningful and that they unambiguously captured the domain of each construct, particularly the four facilitating mechanisms [19]. We then pretested the refined scales with this group. (Items eventually deleted are indicated by an asterisk [\*] in Appendix A.)

To develop a new measure for concurrent IT sourcing, we first generated a pool of items representing IT function activities based on a review of the IT management literature [4, 15, 65, 68] and triangulated it with the practitioner literature [7] to ensure that it was a representative set of corporate IT activities. Concurrent IT sourcing was measured as a summative index constructed by coding ten items (seven retained after factor analysis) that represented the key IT activities in each client firm. The IT activities are shown in Figure 2. Consider the first activity, software architecture development. It could be insourced, outsourced, or concurrently sourced wherein some part of it is done in-house and some part outsourced. One end of the scale captures 100 percent insourcing and the opposite end captures 100 percent outsourcing, and the intervening continuum indicates different percentage mixes of insourcing and outsourcing. Unlike prior studies preceding the recognition of concurrent IT sourcing, our measurement approach recognizes that insourcing and outsourcing are two different approaches to sole sourcing that is qualitatively different from concurrent sourcing. Concurrent IT sourcing is a simultaneous combination of the two. Prior concurrent sourcing studies (e.g. [49]) have consistently used a 10 percent cutoff to measure concurrent sourcing, that is, if over 90 percent of an activity is done internally it is considered insourced; if over 90 percent is done externally it is considered outsourced, and the rest concurrently sourced. Following this established 10 percent heuristic, we dummy-coded each IT activity as insourced if 90–100 percent of the activity was done in-house and as outsourced if 90–100 percent of it was outsourced. These two modes of IT sourcing represent sole sourcing rather than concurrent sourcing. If the level of insourcing was between these two 10 percent cutoffs, that is, 11–89 percent, we dummy-coded it as being concurrently-sourced. (The results are robust to alternative coding schemes.)

That activity was therefore dummy-coded as zero, representing that it was not concurrently sourced. The second activity in Figure 2 met the cutoff for concurrent sourcing; it was therefore dummy-coded as 1, representing concurrent sourcing. We repeated this procedure for all IT activities for each firm in the sample. Each IT activity was therefore dummy-coded as concurrently sourced (= 1) or not concurrently sourced (= 0). (In the hypothetical example in Figure 2, five of the ten IT activities are concurrently sourced.) To construct the summative index measure for concurrent sourcing, we then added the dummy scores for each IT activity. Since seven IT activities were retained in the final scale, the construct score for the concurrent sourcing can range from zero (no concurrent sourcing) to seven (all concurrently sourced). The theoretical maximum concurrency therefore occurs when the construct score for this summative index is seven, corresponding to concurrent sourcing of all seven IT activities that can potentially be concurrently sourced.<sup>6</sup>

Our scale items tapped into the percentage to which the following IT activities done by in-house IT staff vis-à-vis outside IT vendors in the preceding two fiscal years: software architecture development, high-level conceptual design, detailed design, coding, software testing, beta testing, systems integration, software maintenance, end-user support, and IT services provision. The measure spans the preceding two years, following Cassiman and Veugelers [13].

Interfirm IT capability complementarity was measured using four items that assessed the overall degree to which the outside IT vendors used by the firm were complementary in their IT knowledge, IT skills, IT expertise, and IT know-how compared to the firm’s in-house IT department. The scale was based on Rothaermel and Boeker [54] and adapted to an IT context.

For the four facilitating mechanisms, we used only as a starting point Bradach’s [11] definitions to generate an item pool in the IT context, which we then iteratively refined with our panel of thirteen experts. Modeling used five items to assess the extent to which the firm’s IT vendors adopted the client’s IT department’s internal standard operating procedures, work practices, software development and coding practices, and project management practices. Socialization used five items that assessed the extent to which the vendors used by the client were able to adapt to its culture, values, goals and priorities, business norms, and way of doing things. Therefore, socialization captures vendors’ learning to operate within the context of a client firm whereas modeling implies adopting IT work practices from the client firm. Ratcheting used five items to assess the extent to which the client used inhouse performance data regarding project schedules, milestones, timelines, budgets, and overall performance to set benchmarks for the vendors. Spillovers used four items that assessed the extent to which the client’s in-house IT staff and IT vendors over the course of their interactions learned new things from each other, acquired critical new skills from each other, improved their business processes, and enhanced their skills.

We adapted existing measures for IT performance [59, 65]. In-house IT performance used five items to assess the overall extent to which the work of the client firm’s in-house

IT unit over the preceding two years was well aligned with its needs, demands, priorities, goals, and expectations. An identical set of items anchored on the degree to which the work produced by IT vendors used by the firm in the preceding two-year period was used to assess outsourced IT performance, consistent with earlier studies (e.g. [52]).

The acceptable scale αs $( \ge . 8 9 )$ and eigenvalues (> 1.19) for all constructs provides the first assurance that the scales had high convergent validity. The loadings of all but one (.65) item on the corresponding theoretical constructs also exceeded the recommended .7 threshold in the exploratory factor analysis in Appendix B. ## Data Collection

We used a random sample of management information systems (MIS) directors in 1,000 firms drawn from Dun and Bradstreet’s executive directory as our sampling frame. We first sent prospective respondents a prenotification letter to solicit participation. We retained the 889 managers who could be contacted during the prenotification phase in the survey sample pool. Three mailings sent to these MIS directors generated 233 usable responses for a response rate of 26.2 percent (233 out of 889). We then also collected line function managers’ matched-pair assessments of IT performance to assess common methods bias. The response rate for the matchedpair respondent in this follow-up phase was 31.3 percent (N = 73 out of 233).

## Control Variables

The measures and sources of the control variables are shown in Appendix A. To account for rival explanations of the four mediators, we controlled for: (1) firm size (measured as the log of 2009 sales; raw sales yielded consistent results), (2) the firm’s prior concurrent ITsourcing experience measured in years, (3) industry effects using the firms’ three-digit North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) code, (4) the relative project size of projects involving outside vendors vis-à-vis those completed entirely in-house, (5) the client firm’s IT investment intensity [65], (6) anticipated shifts in the mix of inhouse and outsourced IT activities by adapting Parmigiani’s [49] concurrent IT sourcing longevity measure to an IT context, and (6) the extent to which outsourcing by the client firm was motivated by cost reduction (cost motivation) [4, 26]. The significant controls —shown in bold in Table 5—suggest that a client firm’s use of modeling and socialization as facilitating mechanisms is enhanced by prior concurrent IT sourcing experience; cost motivation enhances modeling and ratcheting; larger client firms are more likely to use ratcheting; and client firms that expect longevity in concurrent IT sourcing are likely to use ratcheting. The controls explain between 7.3 percent and 15.4 percent of the variance in the facilitating mechanisms. To account for rival explanations of in-house and outsourced IT performance, we controlled for: (1) IT demand volatility, which varies by industry, (2) a client firm’s use of concurrent IT sourcing to access IT skills that were internally lacking (skill motivation) [45], (3) and the number of vendors used by the client firm for each outsourced project on average (vendor density). We considered but subsequently dropped employee count, relative IT department size, and relational capital with vendors as controls. The significant controls (shown in bold in Table 4) suggest that clients that use concurrent IT sourcing to access internally lacking IT skills have better outsourced IT performance.

## Analysis and Results

Before testing the hypotheses, we must econometrically test for endogeneity of concurrent IT sourcing. The rationale is that if IT managers deliberately choose a level of concurrent sourcing based on ex ante consideration of its performance consequences, the endogeneity of this choice must explicitly be accounted for in the analyses. We used Garen’s [24] two-step econometric procedure to test endogeneity, as summarized in Appendix C. It failed to reveal endogeneity in concurrent IT sourcing, suggesting that it was appropriate to estimate the model using a regression model. We used seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) models instead of ordinary least squares (OLS) because we expect the errors of individual regression equations to be correlated $[ 7 4 ] . ^ { 8 }$ In such situations, OLS estimates are consistent but not efficient. SUR estimation yields more efficient estimates by using the correlation in errors across equations. For example, the equation disturbances for in-house and outsourced performance might be contemporaneously correlated due to omitted factors that influence both dependent variables. As Table 2 shows, in-house and outsourced performance are positively though not causally correlated at $0 . 3 4 ~ ( p <$ .05). We used one-tailed tests because the hypotheses are unidirectional.

We used two hierarchical SUR models to test the hypotheses, the first predicting the two IT performance variables and the second predicting the four facilitating mechanisms. The first stepwise model used in-house and outsourced IT performance as the dependent variables (see Table 4). We first added the control variables (Step 4.1), followed by the two predictors (Step 4.2), and finally the four facilitating mechanisms (Step 4.3, which tests H1 through H4).

The second model used the four facilitating mechanisms as the dependent variables (see Table 5). We added in a stepwise fashion the control variables, two predictors, and then their interaction term that tests H5 through H8. The interaction between concurrent IT sourcing and IT capability complementarity hypothesized in H5–H8 does not require the main effects to be significant, only their interaction effect [30, p. 48].

## Robustness Tests

We conducted robustness checks for: (1) alternative coding schemes for concurrent IT sourcing, (2) alternative estimation methods and model identification, and (3) common methods bias.

Robustness Tests for Different Coding Schemes for Concurrent IT Sourcing

We performed four tests to assess robustness using alternative coding schemes for concurrent IT sourcing: (1) A more stringent cutoff,<sup>9</sup> (2) depth of concurrent sourcing,<sup>10</sup> (3) explicit empirical distinction between outsourcing and insourcing as the two nonconcurrent sourcing modes,<sup>11</sup> and (4) bias from concurrent sourcing rejecting firms.<sup>12</sup>

Robustness Checks using Alternative Estimation Methods and Model Identification

a. 2SLS, SUR, and general linear modeling (GLM) reanalysis. Three alternative estimation methods yielded consistent results: (1) 2SLS and (2) variations of our SUR model for identification purposes,<sup>13</sup> and (3) GLM.

b. Model identification using the Bassman [8] test and Hansen J-test.<sup>14</sup>

Tests for Common Methods Bias and Agreement among Respondents from the Same Firm

Four sets of tests revealed no evidence of common methods bias: (a) Harman’s onefactor test,<sup>15</sup> (b) the marker variable test,<sup>16</sup> (c) triangulation using interrater agreement between IT and line function managers for the matched-pair subset of the data,<sup>17</sup> and (d) an assessment of differences between firms that did and did not provide matched-pair responses.<sup>18</sup>

## Limitations

Our results should be interpreted cognizant of four limitations. First, being an initial exploratory study of concurrent IT sourcing, our model has modest explanatory power. It explains only 12.4–20.2 percent variance in the facilitating mechanisms and 20–43.2 percent of the variance in the IT performance variables. Second, our newly developed ratcheting measure was unable to capture the entire theoretical breadth of the construct. Third, outsourced IT performance is the client’s perception rather than an objective measure. Fourth, caution should be exercised in broadly generalizing the results, even though the industry effects control variable was nonsignificant. Splitting the sample into manufacturing and service firms using their NAICS codes did not show significant differences in our performance model. However, it is unclear how readily the results will generalize from our sample of mid-sized firms to smaller or larger firms. Using the relationship of firm size with the mediators and predictors to empirically assess limits to the generalizability of our results, we found no evidence that larger firms systematically differ in their use of the four facilitating mechanisms, although larger firms are lesser likely to use concurrent IT sourcing (β = –.34, t-value = –2.93; p < .01).

## Discussion and Implications

The growing simultaneous use of both insourcing and outsourcing by firms for the same IT activities—a previously unstudied phenomenon we call concurrent IT sourcing—was the impetus for this study. Although stories abound in the trade press (e.g. [45, 53, 62]), this phenomenon has not yet attracted theoretical scrutiny in part due to its newness and in part because IT outsourcing studies have focused on explaining IT outsourcing decisions and performance (e.g. [2, 9, 61, 63]). We examined how and when concurrent IT sourcing enhances a client firm’s in-house as well as outsourced IT performance.

We zoomed out to the IT function level to first theoretically delineate the concurrent IT sourcing phenomenon. We then developed two sets of ideas. First, concurrent IT sourcing enhances performance only when vendors’ IT capabilities complement the client’s. Second, their mutually reinforcing interplay enhances both in-house and outsourced IT performance for the client but through distinctive pairs of pathways. These pathways are interfirm learning-related mechanisms; we argued that opportunities for interfirm learning to occur are greater when vendor’s IT capabilities complement those of the client’s in-house IT unit. Results from 233 firms provide considerable support for the proposed ideas.

## Contributions and Theoretical Implications

## Contribution 1: Where and When Concurrent IT Sourcing Pays Off

Our first contribution is about where and when concurrent IT sourcing—introduced in this paper—enhances a client firm’s IT performance. We found that it simultaneously enhances both in-house and outsourced IT performance but only when the client’s in-house IT capabilities complement those of its vendors.

For the IT outsourcing literature, the theoretical implications of this finding are primarily for the minuscule stream detecting inklings that IT insourcing complements IT outsourcing (e.g. [16, 26, 41]), rather than the larger stream explaining IT outsourcing choices and reversals (e.g. [2, 9, 63, 71]). That internal IT investment increases payoffs from IT outsourcing in reducing non-IT operating costs [26, p. 322] and that raising internal IT investment bolsters the payoffs from IT business process outsourcing [41] hint at the value of simultaneously doing both. But, unlike Han and Mithas [26], who focused only on hardware and software, we studied the IT portfolio spanning the entire gamut of a client’s IT activities. Our results imply that IT capability complementarity provides the context under which concurrent IT sourcing enhances IT performance. One is useless without the other but their combination is powerful. Insourcing thus does not automatically enhance outsourcing performance as others have speculated [11, 26], but it can under this condition. Without IT capability complementarity, the client incurs the costs of concurrently sourcing the same IT activities without realizing commensurate benefits. Concurrent IT sourcing can be an antidote for the erosion of clients’ in-house IT capabilities due to outsourcing and instead initiate a virtuous cycle that strengthens them.

For the IT capabilities literature that focuses on a firm’s internal IT capabilities (e.g. [3, 10, 70]), this result implies that a client’s IT capability differences vis-à-vis the vendors to which it outsources may be valuable for fully exploiting its own IT capabilities. For the broader non-IS concurrent sourcing literature, they are a first step toward understanding the understudied conditions under which concurrent sourcing pays off or complementarities between insourcing and outsourcing can be realized [13]. Our richer firm-level, portfolio-spanning conceptualization of the phenomenon is a step forward from its predominantly microlevel treatment (e.g. [29]). Unlike non-IS studies of the phenomenon that have historically aggregated both in-house and outsourced performance into overall performance (e.g. [11, 29, 49]), our separation here lends itself to a more granular understanding of where concurrent sourcing impacts are manifested.

## Contribution 2: Facilitating Mechanisms

Our second contribution is about the mechanisms through which the mutually reinforcing interaction between concurrent IT sourcing and IT capability complementarity influences outsourced and in-house IT performance (the “how”). We expected that the former is enhanced by modeling and socialization of vendors, and the latter by ratcheting and knowledge spillovers. Modeling and socialization by reducing the difficulty and the need for verifying vendors’ work quality, and ratcheting and spillovers by legitimizing benchmarking in-house IT performance and through interfirm learning. We found support for three of the four; the support was strong given the substantial jump in explanatory power of 15.9–37 percent in Step 4.3 in Table 4.

To the IT outsourcing literature, these results contribute new insights into the mechanisms through which the previously suspected complementarities between insourcing and outsourcing shape IT performance. This was explicitly noted as a gap by Han and Mithas [26, p. 327]. Especially noteworthy is the role of interfirm— as opposed to intrafirm—knowledge spillovers [60], which enhance outsourced IT performance and not just the client’s in-house IT performance as found by Ko, Kirsch, and King [37]. As a bidirectional mechanism, knowledge spillovers allow IT vendors to better tailor their work by learning idiosyncratic nuances of the client’s internal operations. This insight is absent in prior IT outsourcing studies, which focus primarily on the other direction (vendor to client) of knowledge spillovers [16, 41]. To the IT capabilities literature theoretically wanting for explanatory mechanisms linking IT capabilities to IT performance (e.g. [3, 10]), the four pathways in our model contribute precisely that.

For the broader non-IS concurrent sourcing literature, the primary implication stems from what we did not find: Evidence for ratcheting as a performance-enhancing mechanism. That literature’s longstanding premise is that fostering competition between in-house and outsourced operations is the central mechanism through which concurrent sourcing enhances client firm performance [11, 12, 49, 69]. We failed to find evidence for this. One interpretation for this is that the client’s IT department is often the principal in IT outsourcing but the ratcheting effect might occur only between competing units. A second interpretation is that interfirm ratcheting might be less feasible in outsourcing of complex IT activities, in contrast to simple, specifiable blue-collar operations (e.g., fast food or sheet metal parts) exclusively studied in prior non-IS studies. Not one of the empirical studies in the reference disciplines building on Bradach’s theory has examined the facilitating mechanisms [29, 49, 51], which continue to be treated as a blackbox. Our substantive reconceptualization using Bradach as only a starting point is a first step away from such oversimplification of a complex phenomenon.

## Implications for Practice

The key implication for practice is that when a firm’s in-house capabilities complement its IT vendors’, IT managers should shift their thinking from outsourcing or insourcing to outsourcing and insourcing. This can improve IT performance of both in-house and outsourced IT activities. Second, managers must become more cognizant of how their project-by-project sourcing decisions fit with their firm’s portfolio of IT activities.

## Directions for Future Research

These findings raise three questions for future IT sourcing research by expanding the pervasive make-or-buy logic to include a make-and-buy possibility. First, our nomological network can be expanded with other contingencies that affect concurrent IT sourcing benefits, as well as other consequences such as IT ambidexterity (i.e., simultaneously being efficient and adaptive). Examples of contingencies include whether the client outsources primarily to access expertise or to reduce costs, interfirm asymmetries in IT capability maturity (e.g., capability maturity model levels), formal control mechanisms used, and interfirm power asymmetry.

Further, does concurrent IT sourcing in one period influence IT sourcing choices in subsequent periods? Second, a richer multidimensional conceptualization of firmlevel IT capabilities that include IT infrastructure assets and cultural elements of firm capabilities in Bharadwaj [10] and Aral and Weill [3] is appropriate. Finally, IS scholars have a distinctive advantage as many subtleties unobservable in the bluecollar context of prior studies are more observable in IT sourcing. These include multisourcing (partitioning of outsourced IT work among multiple vendors) [4], tapered integration (where an IT project is passed on to a vendor after some stages are completed in-house), and parallel sourcing (the same IT activities are outsourced in parallel to multiple vendors) [27]. Their theoretical synthesis is uncharted territory.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, the industry-agnostic prevalence of concurrent IT sourcing eschews IT insourcing and outsourcing as mutually exclusive choices. In one of the earliest studies to delineate and theorize this phenomenon, we showed that concurrent IT sourcing matters only in the presence of complementarity between client and vendors’ IT capabilities. The conceptual analogy is concurrent IT sourcing as a catalyst for otherwise-inert interfirm IT capability differences to enhance the client’s in-house and outsourced IT performance through distinctive pathways. More broadly, this study is an initial step toward understanding how firms can keep their in-house IT strategically nimble without forsaking the scale advantages of outside IT vendors.

## Appendix A: Measures

The respondents were asked to consider all major IT activities completed in their organization in the past two fiscal years (2007 and 2008) in responding to the survey [following 13]. All multi-item scales used seven-point measures. (\* indicates dropped items.) All scales used anchors ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree unless otherwise indicated.

Concurrent IT sourcing was assessed as a summative index using seven items that tapped into the percentage of the following IT activities that were done by outside IT vendors rather than in-house in the preceding two fiscal years: (1) software architecture development, (2) high-level conceptual design, (3) detailed design, (4) coding, (5) software testing, (6) beta testing, (7) systems integration\*, (8) software maintenance, (9) end-user support\*, and (10) IT services provision\*. The scale anchors were 0 percent (completely in-house), 1–10 percent, 11–36 percent, 37–63 percent, 64–89 percent, 90–99 percent, and 100 percent (completely outsourced). Each item was dummy coded if it was concurrently sourced; these dummy-coded activities were then added to create a summative index measuring concurrent IT sourcing.

IT capability complementarity used four items that assessed the degree to which the outside IT vendors’ used by the firm were complementary in their: (1) IT knowledge, (2) IT skills, (3) IT expertise, and (4) IT know-how compared to the firm’s internal IT department. The anchors were 1: completely overlapping, 4: somewhat overlapping, and 7: complementary. Adapted from Rothaermel and Boeker [54].

Modeling used five items that assessed the overall extent to which the firm’s IT vendors adopted the firm’s IT department’s internal practices in: (1) standard operating procedures, (2) work practices, (3) software development practices, (4) software coding practices, and (5) project management practices.

Socialization used five items that assessed the extent to which the IT vendors used by the firm were able to adapt to its: (1) culture, (2) values, (3) goals and priorities, (4) business norms, and (5) “way of doing things.”

Ratcheting used five items that assessed the extent to which the firm used its internal IT department’s performance to set benchmarks for IT vendors’: (1) project schedules, (2) project milestones, (3) timelines for deliverables, (4) project budgets, and (5) overall performance.

Spillovers used four items that assessed the extent to which over the course of their interactions, the firm’s internal IT staff and outside IT vendors: (1) learned new things from each other, (2) acquired critical new skills from each other, (3) improved their respective business processes, and (4) enhanced their respective skills.

In-house IT performance used five items that assessed the extent to which, overall, the work of the firm’s internal IT department was well aligned with the firm’s: (1) needs, (2) demands, (3) priorities, (4) goals, and (5) expectations.

Outsourced IT performance used five items that assessed the extent to which, overall, the work produced by its IT vendors was well aligned with the firm’s: (1) needs, (2) demands, (3) priorities, (4) goals, and (5) expectations.

Industry effects were controlled using a three-digit NAICS code (the successor of the Standard Industrial Classification [SIC] code).

IT demand volatility used five items that assessed the degree to which changes in the following were predictable in the preceding two fiscal years: (1) hardware needs, (2) software needs, (3) required IT skills, (4) overall IT needs, and (5) overall business environment. The anchors were 1: very predicable; 4: somewhat predicable; and 7: very unpredictable. The scale was adapted from [50].

Firm size is the log of 2009 annual sales of the client firm. Raw sales also yielded consistent results. See [39].

Concurrent IT sourcing experience was measured as the number of years that the firm had used a mix of internal IT staff and outside IT vendors for software development projects, adapting Leiblein and Miller’s [39] measure.

Cost motivation was measured using three items that assessed the overall degree to which the firm used outside IT vendors in the preceding two fiscal years primarily to: (1) lower its IT development costs, (2) lower its IT implementation costs, and (3) lower its IT support costs. Scale based on Dussauge, Garrette, and Mitchell [21].

Skills motivation was measured using three items that assessed the overall degree to which the firm used outside IT vendors in the preceding two fiscal years primarily to: (1) develop systems with which it had little prior experience, (2) access IT skills not readily available internally, and (3) fill gaps in its internal IT expertise. The scale was based on [21].

IT investment intensity was measured following Dewan, Michael, and Min [20] as the percentage of annual revenue spent by the firm on IT relative to its top three competitors. The anchors were 1: much smaller; 4: about the same; and 7: much larger.

Relative outsourced project size was measured using four items that compared projects involving outside IT vendors in the preceding two fiscal years, on average, to a typical project completed entirely in-house in terms of: (1) person-months of development work, (2) project duration, (3) dollar budget, and (4) project size. The anchors were 1: much smaller, 4: about the same, and 7: much larger. The measure was adapted from Eccles [22].

Concurrent IT sourcing longevity was measured as the extent to which the firm planned to significantly change its current mix of internal and vendor-sourced work in the next two years, following Parmigiani [49].

Vendor density was measured as the number of IT vendors used for a typical IT project in the past two fiscal years.

## Appendix C: Econometric Tests for Endogeneity of Concurrent IT Sourcing

Concurrent IT sourcing might be endogenous, that is, deliberately chosen based on the client’s expectations of what sourcing mix will produce better IT performance. If so, it would bias tests of H5 through H8. We econometrically tested for endogeneity in concurrent IT sourcing using Garen’s [24] two-step procedure to account for this. We used a reduced form model to estimate endogeneity-correcting η for concurrent IT sourcing (Step 1), which we then included in the subsequent model (Step 2). Given no prior empirical research on concurrent IT sourcing, our choice of instruments is guided solely by conceptual logic. We used industry effects, cost motivation, IT investment intensity, relative outsourced project size, and concurrent IT sourcing longevity as instruments for concurrent IT sourcing. We complemented this analysis with econometric diagnostic tests for instrument sufficiency.

The results for Step 1 (Table C1) show that cost motivations and IT investment intensity have a significant relationship with concurrent IT sourcing. Step 2 (Table C2) predicts the four facilitating mechanisms using concurrent sourcing and its Garen η estimated in Step 1. The nonsignificant values of $\boldsymbol \eta _ { \mathrm { c o n c u r r e n t ~ s o u r c i n g } }$ for all four facilitating mechanisms in Table C2 indicates the absence of endogeneity. Adding the Garen η to the model produced results for H5–H8 identical to Table 5, confirming their robustness. We triangulated these results with the Durbin–Wu– Hausman (DWH) endogeneity test. The DWH $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic was nonsignificant for modeling (.11; p =.74), socialization $( 1 . 6 4 ; p = . 2 0 )$ , ratcheting $( . 2 1 ; p = . 6 4 )$ , and spillover $( 1 . 5 7 ; p = . 2 1 )$ , indicating that concurrent IT sourcing is exogenous in our model.

Instrument adequacy using Sargan and Anderson–Rubin [1] tests. The Anderson– Rubin [1] test’s null is that the excluded instruments are uncorrelated with the error term; its significance indicates insufficiency of the set of instruments used. Anderson–Rubin $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was nonsignificant for modeling (5.29; $p = . 3 8 )$ , socialization $( 5 . 8 0 , p = . 3 3 )$ , ratcheting $( 9 . 8 6 , p = . 0 8 )$ , and spillovers $( 7 . 2 8 ; p = . 2 1 )$ , suggesting that the set of instruments used is valid and sufficient. A Sargan test $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 6 . 1 3 , p =$ .19 for modeling; $\chi ^ { 2 } = 3 . 0 4 , p = . 5 5$ for socialization; $\chi ^ { 2 } = 7 . 2 4 , p = . 1 2$ for ratcheting; $\chi ^ { 2 } = 4 . 2 4 , p = . 3 7$ for spillover) also confirmed the adequacy of the instruments used in the model. Further, as desirable, none of the instruments had significant correlations with in-house or outsourced IT performance. Collectively, these analyses show that endogeneity of concurrent IT sourcing is not a concern in the model.
