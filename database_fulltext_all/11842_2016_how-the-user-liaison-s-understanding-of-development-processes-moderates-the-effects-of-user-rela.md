# How the user liaison’s understanding of development processes moderates the effects of user-related and project management risks on IT project performance

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 19 February 2013 Received in revised form 8 September 2015 Accepted 15 September 2015 Available online 25 September 2015

Keywords: Information technology Project management User knowledge Risk management

## A B S T R A C T

Managing risks in information technology projects is a prime concern for both researchers and practitioners. However, little is known on how key project-based knowledge, such as the user liaison’s understanding of development processes (ULUDP), affects the relationship between risk and performance. Using quantitative data collected from 63 completed projects, ULUDP has been empirically proven to weaken the negative effects of user-related and project management risks on product performance. Although the effect of the project management risk on performance remains negative, this effect decreases significantly with high levels of ULUDP, whereas user-related risk affects performance insignificantly in such situations.

## 1. Introduction

The successful management of information technology (IT) projects remains a major challenge. The Standish Group reported that 18% of IT projects in 2013 were canceled or delivered but never used, and 43% of the projects during that period were challenged with respect to functions, budget, and schedule. Only 39% of the projects were delivered within budget, on time, and with the required functions and features [1]. These data suggest that previous IT projects exhibited unsatisfactory performance.

Two separate research streams have emerged in the past few years to improve IT project performance. One research stream is the risk-based view, which states that risks negatively affect IT project performance, and that management of critical risks, such as those associated with users and project management [2–4], enhances project performance. The other research stream is the knowledge-based view, which argues that project-based knowledge is a valuable resource that significantly contributes to IT project success and that effective management of knowledge increases performance [5–7]. However, these two streams are rarely integrated in existing research, and at least two gaps have been identified in previous studies.

First, several studies pay close attention to the identification, evaluation, and mitigation of risks [3,8,9], whereas the influence of risks on performance in IT projects lacks intensive understanding. The effects of various risk types on performance differ significantly [10]. Previous research has indicated that some types of risks significantly and negatively affect performance, whereas other types insignificantly affect performance [4,11]. Furthermore, the findings on the relationship between the same risks and performance in IT projects are contradictory based on prior literature. For instance, some researchers have determined that user and requirement risks affect performance directly and negatively [12], but other researchers provide empirical evidence that the effects of these two risks on performance are insignificant [13]. Similar situations also occur with respect to the effect of project management risk [4,14]. Thus, the way in which key risks, such as user-related risk (i.e., requirement uncertainty and uncooperative behavior of users) and project management risk (i.e., poor management of the development process and team), affect IT projects performance requires further examination.

Second, project-based knowledge, which promotes problem solving [15], can occasionally show an effect of risk. Although risks exert significant uncertainty on the process and outcomes of a project, project-based knowledge facilitates stable development plans and expected outcomes because it is a combination of process and product knowledge related to a project [16,17]. Thus, project-based knowledge may decrease the negative effect of different types of risks. However, this knowledge is often associated with technical and methodological solutions [5], but the social aspect of projects is neglected. Although the effect of methodology-related risks (e.g., project management risk) on performance may decrease with high levels of project-based knowledge, the influence of such knowledge on the relationship between social-related risks (e.g., user-related risk) and performance may be limited. Therefore, exploring how key project-based knowledge alters the relationship between different aspects of risks and performance is essential.

Surprisingly, previous research has made limited systematic attempts to investigate the collective effect of risk and knowledge. With very few exceptions (e.g., [18,19]), previous studies have focused mainly on the individual effect of risk or knowledge. Advocates of risk stream have empirically determined that a multitude of risks negatively affect IT project performance [4,10]. Previous research has also shown that IT projects can benefit from project-based knowledge, such as process and experiential knowledge [5,6]. However, few studies have discussed how the risk-to-performance relationship is influenced by such knowledge. The significance of this gap has been highlighted in a recent study, which reveals that high levels of a type of projectbased knowledge (i.e., the knowledge that developers possess to complete tasks) weaken the negative effect of user risk (primarily user involvement risk) on project performance [19]. Understanding this issue is crucial for managers because such an understanding allows them to manage project risks effectively and minimize the investment of unnecessary resources in the presence of high levels of knowledge. However, even the few studies that focus on both knowledge and risk provide contradictory findings. Some researchers argue that knowledge decreases the negative effect of risk on performance [19], whereas in other research, knowledge insignificantly affects the risk–performance relationship [18], thereby leaving this issue unclear with respect to the collective effects of knowledge and risk on performance. Moreover, knowledge from the user side has never been integrated into the risk–performance relationship. Therefore, this study investigates how key projectbased knowledge, such as the user liaison’s understanding of development processes (ULUDP) or the extent to which the user liaison (UL) understands the actions and behaviors required to convert inputs to outputs during the development process of an IT project, moderates the relationships of user-related and project management risks with product performance.

This study focuses on the input of ULs because ULs are responsible for overseeing projects to ensure project delivery with sufficient business value [20]. As user representatives, ULs provide oversight for projects from user perspective [20] and oversee projects to promote common goals and ensure that outputs are delivered with the intended functions [21]. ULs can also manage risks in IT projects [22]. They act as conduits between a business unit and technical staff, and their input can significantly affect project success [13]. Inadequate examination of this stakeholder can raise substantial practical issues because a risk that insignificantly affects performance for other stakeholders may exert a significant and negative effect on performance for ULs.

This study focuses on ULUDP and its interactions with risks because ULUDP represents critical project-based knowledge that compensates for the bounded rationality of users, and ULs with high levels of ULUDP exhibit an intensive understanding of and reaction to development team activities to enhance success levels [23]. In addition, ULUDP affects the resources that should be used in a project [24]. ULs with high ULUDP are able to understand development processes and technical issues [25]; and can efficiently coordinate and monitor the activities, work procedures, and outputs of the development team [23]. Given its significance, ULUDP is investigated in this study. Therefore, the following two research questions are addressed:

(1) How do user-related and project management risks affect IT project performance?

(2) How does ULUDP alter the relationships of user-related and project management risks with regard to IT project performance?

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: first, the theoretical background related to risk and knowledge is introduced, and the relevant literature is reviewed. Second, the research model and hypotheses are presented. Third, the research methodology is described, and the hypotheses are empirically tested based on the survey data obtained from 63 IT projects. Next, the results of the study are presented and discussed. Finally, the theoretical and practical implications of the findings are presented.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. IT project risk

Risk management is broadly accepted as an effective approach to improve IT project performance [3,4]. According to the literature [8,26], risk is conceptualized as a condition that seriously threatens the success of an IT project. Previous studies have identified a large number of risk factors and categorized them as various dimensions [10,26]. For example, Keil et al. presented a framework that divided risks into four categories, namely, environment, customer mandate, execution, and scope and requirements [27]. Wallace et al. identified over 40 risk factors and categorized them into six risk dimensions, namely, organizational environment, complexity, requirement, user, planning and control, and team [10]. Among the risks that were developed in previous studies, two types of risks are broadly acknowledged as critical: user-related risk and project management risk [28]. These two types of risks dominate the top ten risk factors identified by managers in several Delphi studies [26,28]. The rich literature on user participation and project management also suggests that managing users, teams, and development processes is critical to IT project success [22,29]. Thus, this study focuses on these two types of risks.

According to existing studies [30,31], user-related risk is the requirement uncertainty and uncooperative behavior associated with users. Such a risk comprises two dimensions, namely, requirement and involvement. Requirement risk refers to the uncertainty that surrounds user requirements, whereas involvement risk refers to lack of user involvement during system development [32]. These two risk dimensions reflect social aspects of risk. In contrast to user-related risk, project management risk focuses on the methodological aspect of risk. Such risk is defined as the uncertainty associated with managing key project processes and the development team that executes project tasks [10]. Two dimensions of project management risk include planning and control and team. Planning and control risk refers to poor planning of and control over development process, whereas team risk refers to risk with respect to project team members that can reinforce outcome uncertainty [4,32]. User-related and project management risks are conceptualized as second-order constructs. In this way, the shared and unique variance of the sub-dimensions of these risks can be relevant and user-related, and project management risks may not have opposing effects on performance.

## 2.2. Risk and performance in IT projects

Previous studies on IT project risk have primarily focused on five domains: (1) development of framework and process for managing risks [33]; (2) identification of risk factors [8,26]; (3) development of methods and techniques for assessing risk [9,34]; (4) exploration of risk mitigation strategies to reduce and avoid project risks [2,31]; and (5) evaluation of risk effect on performance [35,36]. Although the first four domains were intensively investigated, the fifth domain remains poorly understood. Additional empirical evidence on the relationships between different types of risk and performance is needed because contradictory findings have been presented with regard to the effect of risk on performance. Consistent with the general understanding in risk literature, this study asserts that each type of risk per se negatively and significantly influences IT project performance. Managers must effectively develop and implement risk management strategies to accomplish project goals and meet the expectations of users.

## 2.3. Knowledge and risk

Previous studies indicate that changing project-based knowledge results in varied skill performance of team members, management support, task difficulties, and technical considerations [37,38]. Such situations alter the influence of risk for project development because both the business and technical environments of a project change [39]. Thus, we posit that project-based knowledge can moderate the relationship between risk and performance.

Although knowledge and risk are clearly correlated, previous studies on the influence of risks are not well integrated with the research on project-based knowledge. A literature review shows that, except for some instances (e.g., [18,19,40]), previous studies have focused on the influence of risk or knowledge in isolation, but limited systematic attempts have been made to investigate their collective effects. This observation is possible because research streams of risk and knowledge have evolved separately within the information systems (IS) literature. A sampling of studies in the areas of risk and knowledge is presented in Table 1. Not all articles on risk or knowledge are provided. However, the list emphasizes the critical references related to these two streams and indicates that relatively few studies have meaningfully integrated knowledge and risk.

Table 1 indicates that the majority of studies focus on risks (i.e., numbers 1–10) or knowledge (i.e., numbers 11–14) in isolation, and only a few studies (i.e., numbers 15–17) have attempted to examine both risk- and knowledge-related constructs. Moreover, these few studies present at least three research gaps. First, two of the three studies are qualitative studies, and only one study investigates risk and knowledge quantitatively. Therefore, further empirical evidence is required to examine this issue. Second, most of these studies (e.g., [14,18]) examine knowledge and risk only at a general level but fail to determine specific knowledge or investigate knowledge and risk at a granular level; different degrees of knowledge may have different effects on the relationship between risk and performance. Third, existing research presents contradictory findings on the joint effect of knowledge and risk; despite the general understanding that knowledge can weaken the negative effect of risk on performance [19], the interactive effect of environmental risk and knowledge on performance is insignificant [18]. Therefore, limited insight has been obtained from these studies with regard to the collective effect of knowledge and risk, and an immense gap exists with regard to the effects of user-related and project management risks with high levels of key knowledge (e.g., ULUDP).

These gaps are related to practical problems because certain risks that negatively affect performance may influence performance insignificantly in a particular knowledge context. An intensive understanding of these research deficiencies enables ULs, project leaders, and other stakeholders to avoid a superfluous input of cost and resources and excessive pessimism about project outcomes. Compared with previous studies, researchers are currently attempting to provide empirical evidence on the joint effects of specific project-based knowledge (i.e., ULUDP) and two critical risks (i.e., user-related risk and project management risk) on IT project performance. In theory, this study takes a further step in references 1–14 (Table 1) by integrating a risk-based and knowledge-based view. The present study also surpasses references 15 and 16 (Table 1) by investigating this issue quantitatively but not qualitatively. Different from reference 17 in Table 1, the knowledge that this study focuses on are associated with users but not the development team. Moreover, two different types of risks are investigated in this study instead of only one risk.

## 2.4. ULUDP and the relationship between risk and performance

The current research examines a critical type of project-based knowledge (which comprises process and product knowledge – namely, ULUDP, which moderates the relationship between risk and performance. ULUDP is the extent to which the UL understands the actions and behaviors required to convert inputs to outputs during the development process of an IT project [20]. ULUDP is a part of process knowledge (i.e., know-how) that contributes to the provision of products and helps with the realization of project objectives. ULUDP is also a type of user knowledge that is directly associated with the development process of an IT project [23]. With this knowledge, ULs can help development teams identify accurate user requirements and obtain a solid understanding of the organizational context that the system supports [45]. This study focuses on ULUDP for three reasons. First, the existing research has widely acknowledged ULUDP as crucial. ULUDP is associated with risk mitigation and performance enhancement [23], and the rich literature on user participation indicates that effectively managing user knowledge is significant to IT project success [46]. Second, previous studies suggest that ULUDP serves as a moderator, thereby indirectly affecting performance [20,24]. For example, Kirsch et al. determine that ULUDP interacts with various modes of control to affect IT project performance [20]. Third, ULs are in a good position to evaluate ULUDP.

In this study, ULUDP is deemed as a pure moderator; that is, it does not directly influence performance in the presence of risks. In particular, high levels of ULUDP weaken the relationship between risk and performance. This information may sound intuitive because such knowledge is a mix of experience and expert insight and represents a valuable component of project resources [6], thereby lessening the negative effect of risks. Although several studies indicate the negative relationships of user-related and project management risks with performance [14], other studies determine that such relationships are insignificant [13], thus implying that other factors may influence the effect of risk. Meanwhile, previous research has argued that ULUDP assists in accurately estimating required resources, thereby reducing the negative effect of certain risks (e.g., project management risk) on performance [24]. Based on existing studies, the relationship between risks and performance is posited to be negatively moderated by ULUDP. This conceptualization of ULUDP as a moderator is consistent with the findings of Hung et al., who theorize that another type of project-based knowledge (i.e., knowledge associated with tasks) negatively moderates the relationship between risk and performance [19].

## 2.5. Product performance as a dependent variable

Product performance is the dependent variable of this study. This variable is the overall evaluation of a product created by a development process [32] which captures the quality and functionality of a system and reveals a critical dimension of success that should be achieved by a project team [4]. Therefore, it can appropriately reflect project outcomes. Furthermore, product performance can be fairly perceived and evaluated by ULs [47].

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 1 shows the research model. User-related risk and project management risk negatively affect product performance in IT projects. However, the relationships of user-related and project management risks with the product performance of IT projects are negatively moderated by ULUDP.

Risk is regarded as a serious threat that impairs project performance. Various types of risks are negatively associated with IT project performance [10,19]. Liu and Wang further provide empirical evidence indicating that user-related and project management risks have negative effects on performance in either in-house and outsourced IT projects [4]. Therefore, we posit that both user-related risk and project management risk negatively influence IT project performance. We first discuss the influence of user-related risk.

User-related risk reflects the requirement uncertainty and uncooperative behavior that are associated with users, which directly impede successful delivery of project outcomes. Lack of user involvement results in the insufficient articulation of business and operational needs, thereby increasing the difficulty of meeting user expectations [19]. Users with negative attitudes or significant resistance to change may also have inaccurate or misleading demands, which hinder the accomplishment of project goals [10]. Changing and unclear requirements generate ambiguous goals and conflicting scopes of projects, thereby leading to unreliable project outcomes [4]. High user-related risk results in the loss of the significant resources provided by ULs [8]. Thus, the intended functions may not be fully realized with limited resources.

H1. User-related risk is negatively associated with the product per formance of IT projects.

Projects that are poorly managed may not meet user needs and provide high-quality products [10]. The damage caused by risks related to project management to the performance of IT projects is very serious [28]. Project management risk reflects poor management of development processes and team. Poor planning and control of development processes is unlikely to generate reliable project outcomes [48]. Ineffective project management methodology also results in unsystematic deliveries [8]. Moreover, team members with insufficient skills and expertise cannot accomplish project tasks well, thereby leading to low quality outcomes [29].

H2. Project management risk is negatively associated with the product performance of IT projects.

Although few studies explicitly discuss the moderating effect of ULUDP on the relationship between risk and performance, the literature on user participation suggests that user knowledge or input moderates the effect of risk on project performance. For instance, Hsu et al. determine that the negative influence of requirement uncertainty (i.e., a sub-dimension of user-related risk) on software project responsiveness decreases when levels of user formal knowledge and review are high [49]. The effect of requirement determination on project performance is also affected by user knowledge [25]. Although requirement determination is not a userrelated risk, it reflects the opposite side of requirement risk. Thus, the influence of requirement risk on project performance is expected to be moderated by user knowledge, such as ULUDP. Moreover, the IS risk literature suggests that a high level of knowledge weakens the negative effect of user risk on project performance [19]. Based on these findings, the negative effect of user-related risk on product performance is asserted to be weakened by ULUDP.

High levels of ULUDP enable the UL to share common values and norms with the development team [20], which greatly reduces the effect of the requirement uncertainty on performance because changes around requirements decrease [49]. High ULUDP also helps minimize conflicts between users and developers because ULs with high ULUDP possess a good understanding of project plans, measures, and activities that are eventually taken by a development team [50]. Even if users with resistance or negative attitudes toward the project are involved in a project, the minimization of conflict can prevent negative influence of such user involvement risks. ULs and developers can also communicate effectively with each other because ULs with high ULUDP understand technical issues and processes well [24]. Despite insufficient user involvement, communication effectiveness can compensate for inadequate user participation, thereby reducing the negative effect of userrelated risk. Moreover, ULs with high ULUDP can provide pertinent feedback about requirements [23], which neutralize the negative influence of user requirement risk on product performance. High ULUDP also enhances the capability and skills of ULs to perform their jobs [37], thus reducing the negative effect of inadequate user participation on performance because they can effectively complete their work with limited participation. With high ULUDP, ULs become aware of collecting and dealing with key information in their everyday jobs to assist developers in managing requirements and business needs, thereby leading to high product performance [22]. Such ULs can also clearly identify their roles and responsibilities and appropriately use their own knowledge to address issues associated with users, hence greatly reducing the negative effect of user-related risk on the product performance of projects.

H3. The negative effect of user-related risk on the perceived product performance of IT projects is moderated by ULUDP, such that the negative effect decreases when the ULUDP level increases.

Prior studies have indicated that user knowledge of IS development interacts with project management practices to influence project performance [15]. Tiwana and Keil also determine that client knowledge significantly affects the relationship between two project management activities (i.e., process and outcome control) and performance in the context of project outsourcing [24]. Based on these findings, the relationship between the risk associated with project management and product performance of IT projects is also posited to be moderated by ULUDP.

Project management risk entails poor planning and control and team management, thus resulting in poor performance. However, ULs with high ULUDP can provide substantial support for the project by obtaining the required resources [24], thereby effectively reducing the negative effect of project management risk on performance. For example, they can arrange additional meetings for negotiation between users and the development team. They can also design a realistic project schedule, thus avoiding the outcome of poor planning. Furthermore, users and the members of the development team are more likely to exchange necessary and valuable information and gain rapid and coordinated access to the specialized expertise of one another if ULs gain a deeper understanding of the development process [21]. As a result, the development team members are able to effectively manage the project and understand how to meet user expectations. In addition, ULUDP is also regarded as an ability type of ULs [20] that can compensate for the expertise risk of the project team because such ULs can encourage correct behavior among development team members. In such circumstances, the negative effect of team risk on performance decreases. This study of capability helps to develop remedial measures and solutions when poor planning and control influence the performance of IT projects [38]. ULs with high ULUDP also efficiently coordinate and monitor the activities, work procedures, and outputs of the development team [23], thereby preventing product performance from being significantly influenced by project management risk.

H4. The negative effect of project management risk on the perceived product performance of IT projects is moderated by ULUDP, such that the negative effect decreases when the ULUDP level increases.

In the research model, the effects of the following control variables on performance were investigated: project duration, cost, strategic orientation, and sourcing arrangement. Project duration and cost indicate the size of a project and may affect product performance [4]. Strategic orientation, which refers to the strategic importance of the project, is categorized into strategic, managerial, and operational levels. Projects with different levels of strategic importance may result in varied levels of performance. The sourcing arrangement indicates whether a project is outsourced or conducted in-house. In this study, the sourcing arrangement is used as a control to capture the effects of sourcing variations on performance.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

An instrument was developed and a survey was conducted to obtain quantitative data to test the hypotheses. A list of 300 representative firms in China was obtained with the help of the office of information industry in a Chinese provincial government division. These firms were selected because of their active engagement in IT projects. The senior IS managers of these firms were contacted. Those who agreed to take part in the study were given survey forms. They were requested to select appropriate projects that their respective firms were engaged in with the condition that the identified project is completed within one year to avoid recall issues in responding to the survey. The senior IS executives were allowed to distribute the survey to a UL of the selected IT project. Convenience sampling was adopted in this study.

Three hundred email- and paper-based questionnaires were sent to the senior IS managers. They were then contacted to guarantee that the questionnaires were distributed to the appropriate respondents. Communication with the respondents was also initiated to verify that they were comfortable responding to the survey questions. The respondents were requested to retrospectively evaluate the risks, performance, and ULUDP of their respective projects. After six months, 63 useable questionnaires (13 email questionnaires and 50 paper-based questionnaires) were returned, thus providing a response rate of 21%. The ULs reported an average of 7.3 years’ experience in IT projects (standard deviation = 6.0). ## 4.3. Data validation

Mann–Whitney tests were conducted to check for differences in the responses to the emailed and paper-based questionnaires. The result of the tests on IT experience, project duration, cost, strategic orientation, and sourcing arrangement $( p = 0 . 9 8 , p = 0 . 0 9 , p = 0 . 4 5 $ $p = 0 . 5 6 ,$ , and $p = 0 . 8 7$ , respectively) showed that no method bias existed.

Non-response bias was checked to ensure external validity. Responses in the early and late stages were subsequently compared. T-tests on the means of requirement risk, user risk, planning and control risk, team risk, ULUDP, and product performance $\left( p = 0 . 3 6 , \ p = 0 . 8 6 , \ p = 0 . 6 3 , \ p = 0 . 9 3 , \ p = 0 . 1 5 , \right.$ , and p = 0.27, respectively) indicated no significant differences between the late and early responses. Thus, non-response bias insignificantly influenced the sample.

Finally, common method bias was examined with the analysis of marker variables [51]. The marker variables with the lowest and second lowest correlations were identified during the administration of the survey. The average correlation between each marker variable and study variables was then computed. The correlations corrected by introducing these four marker variables revealed very small decreases in correlation. In addition, the significance level of all the correlations did not change. Therefore, common method bias insignificantly threatened the sample.

## 6. Discussion

Table 5 summarizes the results of the hypothesis testing. H1, H2, H3, and H4 are all supported.

As hypothesized, this study empirically determines that both user-related and project management risks negatively affect the product performance of IT projects (H1 and H2). This result empirically supports the common understanding in IS risk literature that risks per se are negatively correlated with performance [4,19]. This finding is also consistent with the previous observation of ULs [28], in which the risks related to project management skills and capabilities are significant to project performance. Thus, the effective management of these two risk types is essential to facilitating project success.

Another significant finding from our study is the moderating influence of ULUDP on the relationship of user-related and project management risks with the product performance of IT projects (H3 and H4). Our result indicates that user-related and project management risks with higher levels of ULUDP yield higher performance. Although user-related and project management risks negatively influence product performance, high levels of ULUDP weaken the negative effect of such risks and thus enhance performance. Furthermore, the negative effects of user-related and project management risks on product performance decrease significantly when ULUDP is high. These results support the forgoing arguments with the claim that knowledge can be integrated into risk management to promote excellent project performance [40]. The findings of this study provide solid empirical evidence that key project-based knowledge, particularly ULUDP, decrease the negative effects of user-related and project management risks on project performance. Thus, improving the levels of critical project-based knowledge (e.g., ULUDP) is an effective way to reduce the negative impact of the risks. The findings of this study advance the work of Hung et al. [19], which only examines the interaction between knowledge and user risk and fail to combine risk and knowledge-based views theoretically.

Although project duration, cost, strategic orientation, and sourcing arrangement insignificantly influence the product performance of IT projects, these project characteristics may influence the linkage between risk and performance. For instance, the effect of user-related risk on performance may be stronger for outsourced projects than internal projects because users and development team are present across organizations [36]. The inter-organizational relationship increases the difficulty of users to participate extensively in projects. However, the influence of project management risk on performance is likely to be stronger for internal projects than for outsourced projects because the development team in an outsourced project is professional and has rich project management experience [24]. Risk may influence performance to a greater degree in projects with long duration and high cost than projects with short duration and low cost because the former type of project is more complex than the latter, which increases the uncertainty of projects. Therefore, investigating the influence of risk on performance among different types of projects (e.g., large and small, strategic and non-strategic, internal and outsourced projects) can be an interesting endeavor in future research.

## 7. Implications

## 7.1. Implications for theory

This study introduces pioneering theoretical insights by showing that project-based knowledge moderates the effects of user-related and project management risks on product performance. While previous studies tend to investigate risk and knowledge separately, this study is a very early attempt at combining risk with knowledge and investigating their collective effects on product performance. Therefore, our findings are of theoretical significance in this domain in terms of providing insight into the mechanism of how specific knowledge moderates the effects of risks on product performance. In particular, ULUDP is found to weaken the negative effect of user-related and project management risks on product performance. The theoretical implications of this study are four-fold.

First, this study contributes to risk management literature by investigating the influences of various types of risks on IT project performance. We empirically determine that both user-related and project management risks per se negatively influence the product performance of IT projects. This result is in accordance with previous arguments and observations that the effects of various dimensions of risk on performance are negative and significant [12]. Thus, the successful accomplishment of project outcomes is affected by both the social (e.g., user-related risk) and methodological (e.g., project management risk) aspects of risk. Although each type of risk negatively influences performance, the effects of user-related and project management risks on performance may differ. For instance, the influence of project management risk is deemed more significant than that of user-related risk from the UL perspective [28]. Therefore, managers can prioritize mitigating the most damageable risks.

Second, the findings of this study reveal that different types of risks exhibit varying influences in the presence of high levels of project-based knowledge. In particular, the effect of user-related risk on the product performance of IT projects is insignificant with high levels of ULUDP, whereas project management risk still significantly and negatively affects performance (though less significantly) when high ULUDP is present (Table 4, Model 3). This evidence can address the inconsistency of risks being significantly associated with project performance in some studies but insignificantly affecting performance in others [4,13]. Our findings suggest that these contradictory observations may be the result of the different ULUDP levels in the project. Risks significantly affect performance when ULUDP is low. However, risks may insignificantly influence performance when ULUDP is high. Moreover, risk management should take advantage of key project-based knowledge (e.g., ULUDP). Such knowledge is also a type of valuable project resource that effectively reduces the influence of risks.

Third, this study presents a new perspective that integrates knowledge and risk for knowledge researchers. Prior research associated knowledge with performance without considering risk. Just as ULUDP moderates the relationship between risk and performance, we can reasonably assume that such project-based knowledge can moderate the relationship between other factors (e.g., control) and performance [24]. Thus, knowledge researchers should reconsider simplistic models that merely associate knowledge with performance and think more deeply about the moderating function of knowledge.

Fourth, this study contributes to the extant literature by addressing the existing gap in previous research where knowledge is considered to weaken the negative effect of risk on performance [19], whereas the moderating effect of knowledge on the risk– performance relationship is found to be insignificant in other studies [18]. Our results provide solid evidence that key projectbased knowledge (e.g., ULUDP) lowers the negative influences of various types of risks on the performance of IT projects. This study also advances the previous research [19], which fails to investigate the moderating role of user knowledge. Our findings highlight that user knowledge, such as ULUDP, can also weaken the negative effects of risks on performance. Therefore, managers cannot ignore the effect of user knowledge and should learn to utilize such knowledge to mitigate critical risks in IT projects.

## 7.2. Implications for practice

Several managerial implications are raised on the basis of our findings. First, given that both user-related and project management risks negatively affect the product performance of IT projects, managers should develop appropriate mitigation strategies to manage these risks. Project managers should develop a trusting and close relationship with the users [8]. They should also attempt to understand the goals, values, and norms of the UL to promote common interests and user involvement [59]. To identify requirements clearly, the development team can use effective development approaches, such as prototyping, to manage the requirements of users better. In addition, project managers and ULs should work together to develop a systematic and effective project plan and control mechanism. A sophisticated education system should also be established to ensure that team members possess adequate skills and expertise to complete project tasks.

Second, managers should recognize the benefit that ULUDP can introduce to the project and learn how to enhance such knowledge because ULUDP weakens the negative effect of user-related and project management risks on IT project performance. Project managers should encourage development team members to communicate with users proactively or provide detailed documents on the steps and procedures of system development. They can also provide technical training to users, encourage them to participate in development, and even invite ULs to work with developers to gain an intensive understanding of development processes. In addition, ULs should proactively obtain technical knowledge and accumulate expertise on development processes so that they can shoulder the responsibilities associated with managing IT projects. ULs can develop a knowledge list that includes the key technical points that they need to know from the development team. They can also arrange regular meetings with the development team to address development issues collectively and obtain a clear picture of the process.

Finally, managers should understand how to manage different types of risks in IT projects effectively when high ULUDP is present. Our results indicate that project management risk negatively and significantly influences performance, whereas the effect of userrelated risk on performance is low with high ULUDP. Therefore, managers should prioritize mitigating project management risk in such situations. Meanwhile, when high ULUDP is present, they should avoid investing too many resources in managing risks associated with user involvement and requirements. This is particularly important in managing large IT projects, such as ERP projects, because the resources are very limited in these projects and they should be allotted to the management practices of the most significant risks.

## 8. Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations. First, similar to other studies associated with project management (e.g., [13,20]), the results of the study were obtained from a limited sample size because of the difficulty in obtaining a large sample size at the project level. Second, the use of a single respondent for each project may raise common method issues and social desirability concerns. A pairwise design, where one key informant responds to independent variables and another informant responds to the dependent variable in the same project, can significantly prevent common method bias and improve data quality. For instance, the project manager (i.e., project leader) can assess the variables of risks, whereas the UL in the same project can evaluate the variables of ULUDP and performance. Objective scales can also be employed to measure the dependent variable so that the influence of a singleinformant sample can be significantly reduced. For example, future research can use the product defect rate – which reflects product reliability – to measure product performance. Future studies can also record the system application response time to indicate product performance. Third, we failed to collect data regarding the requirements engineering technique for each project. The requirements engineering technique may influence the effect of risks (especially user-related risk) on the performance of IT projects because they can resolve the complexity of software development [60]. Some requirement engineering techniques, such as goaloriented requirements engineering technique, can simplify the explanation and justification of requirements and address conflicts from manifold viewpoints of different stakeholders [61]. In such circumstances, the effect of user-related risk on performance may decrease. Therefore, future research should examine whether similar results can be obtained from those projects with effective requirements engineering techniques. Fourth, the perceptions of risk can be changed by culture [26]. Thus, further research should be conducted to determine whether the results can be generalized for other countries. Fifth, different stakeholders may have varied project objectives and focus on different aspects of success. Although product performance is determined as the dependent variable in this study, the results are likely to vary with different outcome measures, such as process performance and team performance.

This study also provides a few directions for further exploration. First, the moderating influences of other knowledge types on the relationship between risk and performance should be examined. Given that ULUDP primarily reflects the process knowledge of the UL, investigating how the product knowledge of ULs alters the risk–performance relationship may be an interesting endeavor. Second, future research can introduce additional types of risks (e.g., environmental risk) into the model and examine whether key knowledge moderates the relationship between a larger profile of risk and performance. Third, examinations of how key project characteristics influence the correlation between risk and performance are interesting. For instance, the negative effect of risk on performance may be stronger for strategic projects than nonstrategic projects because high levels of complexity and uncertainty are involved in strategic projects. Therefore, the influence of risk on performance may vary among different types of projects. Fourth, future research can investigate the role of the requirements engineering technique on the risk-performance correlation and examine if risks continue to negatively influence performance in projects with requirement specification techniques. A comparison between the influence of risk on projects with effective requirement specification techniques and those without these techniques is an interesting research direction to pursue. Fifth, the positive contribution of ULUDP to IT projects gives rise to the need to understand how to manage (i.e., create, integrate, and leverage) such knowledge in future studies. Finally, given that different stakeholders have varied perceptions on risks, an examination of the joint effect of knowledge and risk from the perspectives of other key stakeholders (e.g., senior executives and project managers) would be desirable.

## 9. Conclusions

This study represents one of only a handful of studies that have sought to integrate knowledge and risk-based views by examining the relationship among ULUDP, risk, and performance. The study clearly shows that user-related and project management risks negatively influence the product performance of IT projects. This finding implies that different types of risks can decrease project performance. Another significant contribution of the study is the moderating role of key project-based knowledge (i.e., ULUDP) on the relationship between risk and performance. Our results indicate that ULUDP weakens the negative effects of user-related and project management risks on product performance, thereby implying that key project-based knowledge can be integrated into risk management to reduce the influence of risks. Our findings also highlight the different influences of various types of risks on performance in the presence of high ULUDP. With high ULUDP levels, the effect of project management risk on performance decreases significantly but is still negative, whereas user-related risk affects performance insignificantly. Therefore, managers should prioritize mitigating the risks that are more influential involving high ULUDP.
