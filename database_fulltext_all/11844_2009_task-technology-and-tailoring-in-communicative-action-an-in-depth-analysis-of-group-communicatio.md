## a b s t r a c t

Communication is fundamental to team work and it occur increasingly often in a technology-mediated environment. Understanding how communication evolves in such environments is essential to ensuring that the right technology is provided for whatever task a group needs to accomplish, even if it is not precisely known how the technology is going to be used. With the growing body of research on different tools for supporting computer-mediated groups, we still have much to learn about how communication evolves, where potential breakdowns occur, and how groups tailor technology during the communication process. This paper addresses these important issues by providing an indepth analysis of two-person dyads that communicated over a web-based set of collaboration tools during a 6-week project. We analyze communication through the lens of communicative action theory, showing how social action unfolds during communication and how groups challenge and resolve validity claims in different task-technology environments. This study contributes by exploring group processes, developing and applying group communication analysis tools, and enhancing theorizing on technology tailoring - 2008 Elsevier Ltd. ## 1. Introduction

The interplay of individuals with communication technology remains a fundamentally important area of study and, quite naturally, different perspectives exist. One perspective argues that individuals continually acquire knowledge for action in a dynamic, changing environment and they alter their actions, language, and technology in response to that dynamism (Goldkuhl, 2006). This view treats people as active players who are tailoring technology in the changing context of use, reflecting how a technology might be used in a new context, and then acting on that reflection (Germonprez, Hovorka, & Collopy, 2007; MacLean, Carter, Lovstrand, & Moran, 1990; Romme, 2003). The focus on dynamism and active involvement is quite appropriate in today’s environment, where boundaries across technologies continue to blur. For example, the popular concept of ubiquitous computing embodies the provision of seamless access to a variety of services for different tasks, and outcomes emerge in continuing process of on-going, contextualized interaction (Weiser, 1993).

For these reasons, it is more important than ever to understand the ‘‘black box” of the process b which technology is used (Te’eni, 2001). However, the starting point often focuses on matching tech nology to the task being performed in a deterministic manner. This prescriptive approach treats the user as a recipient of a defined task and technology, rather than an active player who is capable of modifying technology in the context of use. Theories of task-technology fit provide an important starting point, but a more intimate knowledge of communication processes is required before we can full understand users in a dynamic context.

We approach this challenge through the theory of communicative action (Habermas, 1976), which provides a set of constructs that allow a detailed analysis of technology-in-use via group communication. Communicative action theory posits that people strive to improve their communication and the environment through which they communicate. While task-technology fit theories prescribe the best initial environment for a given task, communicative action theory gives us the constructs we need to examin how that environment is actually used and tailored in a dynamic way. We devised a study to explore these relationships by combining the nominally prescriptive theory of task-technology fit with the concept of technology tailoring in the context of communicative action theory. The key research question is: How do task-technology fit profiles impact the process by which people communicate and strive to shape thei communication environment? This study contributes by analyzing group communication through th lens of the theory of communicative action (TCA), in the context of technology tailoring and task-technology fit (TTF). The research model is a process model that treats communication as dynamic and user as active players who tailor technology during the use process. Each theory provides parts that combine to create new possibilities of understanding (Gabriel, 2002). TCA provides the concepts and methods by which to examine communication in depth. TTF provides fit profiles to define a starting set of technolog capabilities. Technology tailoring links the concepts in a dynamic process of use

Section 2 provides the theoretical framework of task-technology fit, technology tailoring, and communicative action theory. Section 3 details the research method and the coding scheme for analyzing group dialogue. Section 4 presents the results and discussion, and Section 5 concludes the paper with implications for research and practice.

## 2. Theoretical framework

Communication has both a deterministic nature, in that it can reinforce existing social norms and practices, and an emergent nature, where it can change social structures. Through communication, individuals are able to enact meaning and change in their environment, a process that is constrained by the context within which people function. That context is partly determined by the technology that is used to support communication, including the fit of that technology with the task that groups undertake. The relationship of communication to task-technology fit is a natural one, in that different profiles of fit can be used to examine the nature of communication in different environments. Users, however, are not passive in this situation; instead, they are active participants in on-going use of technology for group communication. Technology tailoring occurs as users adapt and modify availabl tools in the context of use

Fig. 1 shows the conceptual model for this research. Profiles of task-technology fit provide a start ing point for supporting a particular group task with relevant technology tools. Technology tailoring occurs as group members use and adapt the tools provided to them during on-going group commu nication. Feedback arrows between group communication and technology tailoring indicate the iter ative nature of this process. Our main focus is on the group communication process, which we examine in terms of communicative action theory (Habermas, 1976, 1987). Group communication occurs as a series of communication acts, each of which consists of a sequence of different types of social actions, various validity claims on those social actions, challenges to the validity claims, and either resolution of the claims or a breakdown of communication. Communication plays a central role, not to prove or disprove a specific theory, but rather to understand the micro-level pro cesses by which the phenomena of interest occur. A communication process ultimately results in group outcomes, but outcomes are outside the scope and purpose of the current study and we do not show them in the model. Each of the concepts in Fig. 1 is discussed further in the sections that follow.

## 2.1. Task-technology fit

Significant research on the relationship of tasks with technology has resulted in theories such as media richness (Daft & Lengel, 1984), channel expansion (Carlson & Zmud, 1999), media synchronization (Dennis, Valacich, & Speier, 1998), task-technology fit (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Zigurs & Buckland, 1998), and the fit-appropriation model (Dennis, Wixom, & Vandenberg, 2001). Of these different theories, we chose as our starting point the task-technology fit theory of Zigurs and Buckland (1998) because it was developed in the specific context of group collaboration

Task-technology fit (TTF) theory defines a typology of group tasks that is based on characteristics that relate to complexity (Campbell, 1988; Zigurs & Buckland, 1998). Five different types of tasks are defined, on well-accepted dimensions that describe variations in underlying task complexity. TTF then characterizes different types of technology for group collaboration in terms of three fundamental dimensions: communication support, process structure, and information processing support. A given technology will provide more or less support for each of these three dimensions, depending on the specific tools provided. For example, the ability to develop and enforce an agenda provides support for the process structure dimension. TTF then prescribes ‘‘profiles of fit” that are combinations of appropriate levels of technology support on each of the three dimensions, matched to each type of task.

The ultimate premise of TTF is that proper fit profiles will result in more positive group outcomes than improper fit profiles, including task performance, member satisfaction, and continuing commit ment to the group. However, these outcomes are achieved through the group process, of which communication is an essential component. Hence, using TTF profiles to study the interplay of technol ogy and group communication provides a starting point and a theoretical foundation. It is not our in tent in the current study to examine all possible combinations of task types with technology dimensions, but rather to use the concept of fit profiles as a trigger for technology tailoring and group communication.

## 2.2. Technology tailoring

The concept of technology tailoring has roots in information systems and computer science and is informed through the early work of Winograd and Flores (1986) and the more recent work of Dourish (2001). Tailoring is the activity of modifying a computer application during the context of use, as opposed to during initial design and development, and it includes the manner in which features or tools are used as well as choices among available features or tools (Germonprez et al., 2007). Tailoring im plies that users are capable of interacting with their technology in reflective, constructive, and meaningful ways (Dourish, 2001). Research on technology tailoring has focused primarily on objective aspects of the problem, e.g., studying how users tailor by combining components of an application, or specifying guidelines for designing tailorable technologies. Little attention has been given to communication-level nuances of what tailoring in use might entail.

Tailoring in use can occur in various ways, similar to the action research classification by Chiasson Germonprez, and Mathiassen (in press). Tailoring can be planned, where modification of the technology-in-use is deliberate and modifications are maintained between time periods of use (Germonpre et al., 2007). Tailoring can be emergent, where modification of the technology-in-use surfaces during use and changes are maintained between time periods (Dourish, 2001). Finally, tailoring can be improvisational, where modification of the technology-in-use surfaces during use and the changes are not maintained between time periods (McGann, 2004; Nachmanovitch, 1991). The three tailoring types account for the action of users as they recognize and repeat useful patterns during tailorin in use.

Task-technology fit profiles have been shown to produce different outcomes (Zigurs, Buckland, Connolly, & Wilson, 1999), but the process by which the outcomes are produced has not been addressed directly. We propose that technology tailoring sits at the intersection of task-technology fit with group communication via the active role of users as they seek to improve their communication environments. Group communication is the vehicle for examining this relationship. Rather than test ing a direct effects or predictive theory, we examine a constellation of relationships, combining the profile approach of task-technology fit with the dynamic nature of tailoring in use during on-going communication. The following section describes in detail the theoretical foundation of group communication in the context of our model.

## 2.3. Social actions

Fig. 1 shows the group communication process as social action, which is comprised of validity claims, communication acts, and a potential challenge that ends in either resolution or breakdown These concepts come from the theory of communicative action (Habermas, 1976), which holds that communication is rational in nature and is constructed to achieve, sustain, and review consensus, a consensus that rests on an individual recognition of disputable claims of validity. Communicative rationality is the use of communication to produce mutual understanding between individuals. Each entry into rational, goal-oriented communication where individuals purposively construct intention is termed a social action (Habermas, 1987; Te’eni, 2001). Social actions are of thre types:

\- Communicative Action occurs when both communication partners see each other as intelligent actors and they intend to achieve mutual understanding between them

\- Instrumental Action occurs when a communication sender views the receiver as an obedient recipient of the communication and is telling the other to take action.

\- Strategic Action occurs when a communication sender attempts to influence a receiver through a communication act.

Communicative action, the first type of social action, is focused on the creation of coordinated behaviors and mutual understanding between communication partners. As individuals interact through communicative action, common definitions of their context are constantly produced and used as information is processed, understanding is pursued, and knowledge is distributed between communication partners (Habermas, 1976). Group members are expected to be conscious actors that rationalize the opportunity to accomplish goals constrained by the task or technology.

## 2.4. Validity claims and challenges

Once a communication act has been initiated via social action, validity claims are implicitly or explicitly raised and then subsequently accepted or challenged. A validity claim is defined as the contention by the sender of a communication act of the comprehensibility, truth, normative rightness, o sincerity of the communication. Accepting a validity claim means that a listener (receiver) has ac cepted the sender’s communication act in the context in which it was presented (Goldkuhl, 2000). Challenging a validity claim means that a listener questions the comprehensibility. truth. normative rightness, or sincerity of the validity claim. Whatever the reason for a challenge, individuals strive for resolution and, if a challenged claim cannot be resolved, a communication breakdown occurs (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997).

In his original writings, Habermas (1976, 1984, 1987) identified three validity claims: truth, normative rightness, and sincerity. Habermas states that communication is based on language as a medium of understanding and, in this understanding, communication participants can raise validity claims that can be accepted or challenged (Habermas, 1984; Goldkuhl, 2000). In addition to Habermas’ three validity claims, a fourth validity claim related to language as a medium of communication is comprehensibility. If the communication is incomprehensible, then accepting or challenging the validity claims of truth, normative rightness, or sincerity is impossible (Goldkuhl, 2000).

In the current research, the original four validity claims are expanded to seven in order to provide a richer and more detailed examination of the validity claim concept (Goldkuhl, 2000) and to frame our research within prior information systems research (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). ## 3. Research method

The research consisted of a single longitudinal study of two-person, student groups working on a 6-week-long class project on electronic privacy in healthcare. Twenty-seven dyads participated in th study, with each member coming from one of two geographically-distributed universities. The projec consisted of three steps or phases, each of which was a decision task that involved accessing and discussing information on a specific topic, making a decision on the issue, and producing a report on the decision. Dvads were provided with a custom designed. integrated communication system which they could use synchronously or asynchronously in any way they felt might be useful. Dyads were asked to communicate exclusively through the system and use the information that i provided. Half of the dyads were provided with proper task-technology fit and the other half with improper fit, consistent with task-technology fit profiles for a decision type of task. Group dialogue was collected and coded according to the components of communicative action as specified in the research model.

## 3.1. Project dyads and dyad formation

Subjects were drawn from four different courses. Three courses were upper-level (Masters) infor mation systems (IS) courses, where students were expected to have advanced knowledge of information systems. The fourth course was a lower-level (undergraduate) IS course, where students were expected to have limited knowledge of information systems. A student from the upper-level IS course was randomly paired with a student from the lower-level IS course, forming zero-history, geographically-dispersed dyads. The use of zero-history groups helped to limit the possibility of members communicating with each other outside of the technology provided as well as limit the experience that one member had with the other. Students from the upper-level courses played the role of a Healthcare Inc. employee, and students from the lower-level course played the role of an E-Neo Consulting employee. The different roles were implemented to generate a realistic project setting, a unique perspec tive for each dyad member, and a need for communication to share differing ideas and opinions. Dyad responsibility was created through requiring project deliverables that relied on ideas, concepts, and opinions of both members. Commitment to the dyad was created by providing members a unique opportunity to work in a virtual environment, using new communication technology, and discussing a new topic as part of a class project (Fisher & Ellis, 1980).

All members were assumed to have an interest in technology and the role it played in both learning and business settings, given that group members were all IS majors and thus likely to have an interest in technology-aided projects. Students were expected to be able to use and tailor all components of the system including the help page, the information board, the message board, the chat room, and the file upload utility. Finally, students were assumed to be able to react and communicate within their context and enact changes that can lead to a resolution of communication breakdowns.

## 3.2. Project topic and task

The overall topic of the project was electronic privacy in the context of the healthcare industry. The project had three time periods or phases, each of which lasted 2 weeks and required the participants to complete a decision task that involved processing information and making a decision. In the first phase, the topic was the nature of electronic privacy and the types of electronic privacy tools currently available to provide security of sensitive information. In the second phase, dyads dealt with the relationship of electronic privacy to the healthcare industry with respect to governmental regulation. In the final phase, dyads decided on how governmental regulation of electronic privacy supports and contradicts best practice policies of organizations.

The deliverable for each phase was a two-page report on the specific issue to be decided in that phase. Participants were instructed to read the informational materials provided, discuss the issues with their partner, come to a decision about their response, and then write the report. Each phase was independent and did not rely on decisions from a prior phase. Each group worked on a single phase at a time. The decision task in each phase was designed to generate communication between group members, maintain a high level of interest from the participants so as to promote the exchange of ideas and information, and be of a sufficiently high level of complexity that it could not be completed in a minimal number of communication events (Carlson & Zmud, 1999). The task was designed according to TTF theory which defines a decision task as a task in which the focus is on processing information and crafting a solution that best satisfies multiple and potentially conflicting outcomes (Campbell, 1988).

## 3.3. Project setting and technology

## 3.3.1. Overview of communication system

The setting within which all members functioned was a virtual project team. Virtual project teams are defined as geographically dispersed teams, working with computer groupware tools to accomplish specific tasks (Chidambaram & Zigurs, 2001). The geographic dispersion was across two time zones and 550 miles. The system through which the groups communicated was custom designed for the study as an Integrated Web Communication System (IWCS). Groups were given a single interface to a set of three tools: a message board system, a chat room, and a file sharing tool. While the individual components may have been known to group members, the collection of these components into an integrated communication system was new and unique. Appendix A shows the home page for the IWCS, the entry point for each of the tools, and the help screen.

One reason for custom designing the IWCS was so that we could provide the type of technology elements defined in task-technology fit theory. In addition, a custom design gave us greater flexibility in configuring the technology, more rapid response to problems with the IWCS, and a proper fitting of the technology to the task. The IWCS was hyperlink driven and designed to be easy to use. Moreover, the system was available from any online computer. Training on the system was not provided since it was designed based on recognizable components and conventions that included point and click navigation, hyperlink guides, and consistent formatting between pages. A help page on the use of IWCS and a technical support email link were provided. Emails regarding technica problems or difficulties with the system were sent to the administrator of the system (the first author).

## 3.3.2. Implementation of task-technology fit

Task-technology fit theory identifies three dimensions of technology and specifies a high or low presence of each dimension for proper task-technology fit for specific task types (Zigurs & Buckland, 1998). Proper task-technology fit for a decision task, which was the type of task used in this study, entails low communication support, high process structure, and high information processing support. We chose the process structure dimension as the specific dimension for setting up contrasts in proper and improper fit profiles. Task-technology fit theory does not specify gradients of fit, thus differences in a single dimension can result in differences in fit. We also wanted to avoid overly structuring a group member’s ability to communicate in a flexible way, since communication was a key component of the research. The information processing dimension was important to keep consistent across groups since the study was part of a learning experience in a university curriculum and all students needed access to appropriate information tools to support learning in the course.

Two variations on the process structure dimension were provided. The dyads with a ‘‘proper fit” profile were provided with a detailed project agenda. The dyads with an ‘‘improper fit” profile were not provided with any agenda or instructions on how to go about completing the task. Half of th dyads were randomly assigned to proper fit profiles and the other half to improper fit profiles. Dyads were unaware of their task-technology fit profile assignment and were notified individually of their assignments via email.

## 3.3.3. Potential for technology tailoring

The IWCS can be considered a tailorable technology because its design was based on principles for tailorable technology (Germonprez et al., 2007). Specifically, seven design principles were implemented in the IWCS. The technology: (1) provided support for variable tasks and problems; (2) used recognizable components from existing technologies such as windows, web pages, and hyperlinks; (3) supported use patterns from existing technologies through point-and-click navigation, standardized message exchange, and file uploading conventions; (4) supported symbolic representations for work such as agendas or open ended exploration; (5) provided a choice of tools within the system; (6) allowed groups to design by learning as group interaction evolved; and (7) provided support for functional requirements of the users.

Approximately 1700 communication acts were identified and analyzed across the 27 dyads that participated in the study. Each communication act was coded on the categories shown in Table 3. The analysis consisted of two levels of examination that together provide an interpretation of ob served events (Habermas, 1976). At each level, the raters asked themselves questions to determine a challenge of a validity claim and a subsequent resolution. The two levels were:

1. Was it a communication act? Did the communication act contribute to the task at hand? Within which social action did it occur?

2. Did the communication act result in a challenge of a validity claim? Which challenged validity claim required resolution? Was resolution provided?

Training on the coding occurred in two phases. First, during a pilot study, two raters were introduced to reading group communication to identify the aforementioned activities. Following the pilot study, the same raters coded the communication for the current study. Raters met a total of four times. In the first meeting, the researcher described the coding process, introduced terms used, discussed group tasks, and identified potential areas of difficulty. (Appendix B shows the coding instructions.) In the final three meetings, raters compared and contrasted their coding of three groups, using reflection, debate, and discussion to resolve differences. Subsequent inter-rater agreement on communica tion act type, social actions, challenged validity claims, resolutions, and validity claim types was .85, .91, .92, .93, and .89, respectively. The two levels of understanding used by the raters represented a best attempt at consistently identifying and coding the communication. The following sections provide examples to illustrate the training approach, rater disagreements, and resolution of those disagreements.

## 4. Results and discussion

The results are presented with respect to the research model, focusing first on TTF and tailoring, followed by the analysis of group communication. We provide descriptive analyses and illustrations of group dialogue to show the interplay of task-technology fit and technology tailoring with the following aspects of communication:

\- Types of social actions

\- Extent of validity claim challenges

\- Types of validity claims challenged

\- Resolution of communication challenges

## 4.1. TTF profiles and technology tailoring

Task-technology fit profiles appear to play a role in technology tailoring. The discovery of a relationship between task-technology fit and tailoring advances both theories in a useful way by showing their interplay in group communication. It may be going too far to suggest that group members try to achieve fit during the communication process in the specific way that is prescribed in TTF theory. Improper fit groups did not structure an agenda to provide a proper TTF profile for a decision task. Instead, they tailored the technology based on the overarching task and on phases of the task as they encountered them over the course of the 6-week project. It seems that both fixed and subjective views of task and technology are appropriate at the intersection of the task, technology, and tailoring domains. We can prescribe task-technology fit profiles with the intention that the profiles will be tai lored in the context of use and we can expect that different fit profiles will affect tailoring in different ways.

Our data indicate that tailoring in the two fit profiles was different. Although we cannot say with certainty why these differences occurred, clear possibilities include varied context (Mark & Poltrock, 2004) or the shaping of the virtual reality (Madsen, 1989; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001), or the treatment of recognizable parts of the technology (Gargarian, 1993). Fig. 3 illustrates the variability in the ways that the technology was tailored by showing three different dyads and the communication tools that each dyad used during a specific window of the 6-week project. In the figure, time is not represented on a uniform scale; for example, a string of chat room communication all occurred within a single day. Instead, the point is to show variation in how the technology was tailored, not the pace at which tailoring occurred.

Fig. 4 shows that the overall use of the three types of communication tools did not vary across task technology fit profiles. The extent of use of the message board, chat, and file share tools were approximately consistent across proper and improper fit groups.

This finding extends the concept of tailoring by illustrating that tailoring can occur in the interaction of language and technology, not just on the technology itself, as earlier theory on technology tailoring might imply. Users may tailor at the communication level even though they are using two similar tech nologies. This examination of tailoring via group process illustrates tailoring as a richer concept. ## 4.2. Types of social actions

The data was coded for the existence of the three types of social actions described earlier, namely communicative, strategic, and instrumental. Of the 1753 total communication acts, 4.6% were identi fied as not being relevant to the project and therefore were not coded (first level of understanding). Of the total coded acts across all groups, communicative action occurred 95.4% of the time, indicating that group members were most often concerned with gaining a mutual understanding between each other and about the project. Strategic action occurred only 3.9% of the time, and instrumental action occurred only 0.7% of the time. The dominance of one type of social action is not unusual (Habermas, 1976; Habermas, 1984; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; McCarthy, 1978). Given the dominance of com municative action throughout all groups, it is not surprising that we found little difference in fit pro files with respect to social actions. Proper fit groups entered communicative, strategic, and instrumental actions at rates of 96.8%, 2.7%, and 0.5%, respectively, while improper fit groups entered at rates of 93.6%, 5.4%, and 1.0%.

## 4.4. Tailoring and types of validity claim challenged

A challenge can occur on one of seven types of validity claims: contextuality, efficiency, authority, clarity, completeness, truthfulness, or sincerity. We examined the percentage of each type of validit claim challenge in proper and improper fit groups and found that they differed across fit profiles, most noticeably with the two validity claims of efficiency and completeness $( p < . 0 1$ for efficiency and completeness) (Fig. 6). Efficiency was challenged more often in proper fit groups and completeness was challenged more often in improper fit groups.

Improper fit groups, in using the synchronous communication tool for topic discussions, generated short incomplete communication acts through the rapid-fire environment of that tool. We believe these communication acts carried high importance as they were being used for task completion. This pattern differed from proper fit groups who used the chat room at considerably lower rates for topic discussions and were less dependent on communication acts being completed. These short communication acts resulted in completeness being challenged and the acts were later completed to provide comprehensibility to the communication.

Proper fit groups relied more heavily on the message board for topic discussions than did improper fit groups. Greater value was then placed on the message board to carry the conversation in the production of final outcomes. If group members did not respond immediately or directly to prior message board postings, the efficiency of dyad members was called into question. It appears that in both prope and improper fit groups, the challenge of validity claims followed the communication tool that was most often used for topic communication acts due to the importance of topic communication acts for producing required outcomes.

## 4.5. Resolution of communication challenges

We examined the relation of task-technology fit profiles and tailoring with the resolution of communication challenges. As with the generation of communication challenges, no differences were found in how resolutions were achieved within the different social actions or across task-technology fit profiles. Since the communication volume is likely an undermining factor when exploring communication processes, we considered the ratio of resolutions per challenge. The average number of res olutions per communication challenge shows that improper fit groups were marginally better in their ability to resolve communication challenges in T1, showing higher rates over the final two periods. Improper fit groups supported a higher number of resolutions per challenge across the three time periods (Fig. $7 : p < . 0 1$ for T2 and T3).

We believe the differences in resolution rates are based on the type of validity claim that was chal lenged. In the case of improper fit groups, a completeness validity claim challenge was easier to resolve based on the immediacy of feedback provided in the chat room and the active engagement of both dyad members using the same communication tool at the same time. Likewise, the proper fit groups were left with large time lags between challenges to efficiency validity, which impacted thei ability to resolve the challenge given that the challenge was no longer relevant or was not even rec ognized by the offending member.

## 4.6. Summary of key findings on research questions

TTF profiles had an impact on group communication in how people tailored and used the technology for communication and task accomplishment. Varied fit profiles and technology tailoring were associated with different paths in how the dyads engaged in communication and accomplished their tasks. These findings tell us that: (1) TTF profiles affect the process component of information systems tailoring and use; (2) tailoring can occur at the intersection of technology and communication, not just on technology alone; and (3) subtle changes in the design and subsequent tailoring of technology can affect the choices groups make to communicate and accomplish tasks. ## 5. Implications and conclusions

First, we found that TTF and technology tailoring were manifested in how communication tools were being used and the types of tailoring in use, thus extending the work on the design of tech nology tailoring (Germonprez et al., 2007) by investigating tailoring in use. This finding aligns with the concept of localization of technology modification (McGann, 2004), where variation in tailoring is based on contextual factors. It reinforces the idea that tailoring can occur without users physically modifying the system’s architecture via deleting or moving components. Instead, tailoring happens at the intersection of technology with process, as users communicate through available tools.

Second, task-technology fit and tailoring affected communication. We saw that within the commu nicative action type of social action (which represented the great majority of communication ac types), communication acts were generally focused on process structure and technology use. The dominance of communicative action types in this study may be a function of the circumstances. Group members may have accepted the frameworks and norms that had already been created by the researcher and not engaged in discussion about them. The lack of history in the groups also may have led to a desire to not ‘‘rock the boat” and cause tension in the dyad. This would then dictate discussion to facilitate coordination and cooperation (Tan, 1994).

Beyond the social actions, fit groups challenged completeness claims at a lower rate and efficiency claims at a higher rate than improper fit groups. This difference is interesting in its relationship to the communication tool used for the challenges. Two-thirds of the completeness challenges in impro per fit groups occurred on topic communication acts in synchronous communication. Synchronous communication occurred in rapid bursts of short text segments and the brief and rapid nature ot the ‘‘conversation” required frequent claims on completeness, at least in the way these groups used the tool. The finding of a higher rate of efficiency claims in fit groups also seemed to be related to tech nology use, in that fit groups used the asynchronous tool more often for topic-related communication acts. This finding indicates a questioning of the efficiency of the meeting and communication processes managed by the groups, possibly caused by the time lag between communication acts in the asynchronous tool. We found differences in the resolution rate of challenges (completeness resolved more often than efficiency), which we believe to be a function of the types of validity claims that were challenged and the tool within which they were challenged.

Third, we provided an illustration of how the theory of communicative action can be applied in an information systems context and we developed a coding method for detailed examination of commu nication in a technology-mediated environment. The method provides a consistent procedure for pars ing dialogue into discrete and identifiable components, as well as identifying the relative or absolute frequency of occurrence of dialogue components, relationships among components, and context of occurrence of all components. The method entails a relatively simple and short training period, produces high inter-rater reliability, and greatly reduces the time needed for handling large amounts of dialogue. Patterns and relationships can be readily revealed through simple graphical and descrip tive statistical techniques.

The coding system also provides a means of revisiting group dialogue for a deeper understand ing. Our unexpected discovery of specific ways in which validity claims were challenged is one example. Although the same patterns applied in both groups, we observed slight technology varia tions. Specifically, unintentionally-generated self-referential challenges occurred more often in asyn chronous than in synchronous communication. These findings are consistent with Te’eni (2001) and the role of communication strategies in complex communication. The opportunity to read and reflect while using asynchronous tools may explain the higher occurrence of this type of challenge. We suggest this is a benefit of asynchronous tools, provided that the challenge can later be resolved effectively.

This study makes several contributions to practice. We began with the argument that communication is the essence of organizations, and technology is part and parcel of that communication. Managers often fail to think explicitly about strategies for electronic communication. Instead, choices for electronic communication are often made by default and people use the ‘‘lowest common denominator”

or most familiar tools, regardless of what they are trying to communicate. It takes time to train employees in electronic communication and the training often consists of ‘‘how to” lessons for using specific tools, rather than strategic lessons for knowing how and why to use the tools. Our results suggest that there is much that can be done for developing strategic awareness about the effects of technology on communication and the extent to which different aspects of communication impact the process.

This study also illustrates the nature of tailoring in use. As designers of technology, we need to embrace the idea that systems can be built without knowing how they will be used. Users have become adaptable and opportunistic and they will gravitate toward technology that suits their needs for dif ferent contexts. This calls for a period of tailoring where users are free to modify technology in th context of use. Some modifications may become institutionalized while others may fall by the way side. In any case, we must be conscious as designers that some of our systems are being used in completely unexpected ways. We have argued that not all systems need to be designed initially with the purpose of explaining error during use. Instead, systems can be built with the full intention of being tailored. While tailoring may lead to application of the information system in such a way as to reduc errors, an initial state can be built without knowing how the technology will be used. Outcomes then become value-free and researchers can focus on understanding process, not explaining outcomebased error.

Certain limitations apply to this research. The use of student subjects is always open to criticism but their engagement in the task and its relevance to them mitigate this criticism. Participants in this study represent the new generation of technology users who are likely to be adept at tailoring, given that they have grown up with blogging, cell phones, and social networking. These are the very tools that are being used to create new and unique systems based on the context of individual users. A second limitation is that we examined only one fit profile – that of a decision task – and it may be that other fit profiles have different implications for tailoring and communication. Finally, the use of two-person groups limits generalizability to larger groups, which suggests potential for future investigation.

Directions for future research range from research implications of technology tailoring to practical implications. We did not address design characteristics of tailorable technology and how they play out in TTF and group communication. We built a technology that was evidenced as tailorable, supporting the idea that tailoring can occur without the presence of all design principles. However, there is ample opportunity to examine whether a threshold of design support is necessary for tailoring in use to occur, as well as to test the importance of specific design principles.

We also do not propose that the presence of an agenda is the only factor that affects tailoring. Dourish (2001) suggests that meaning is embedded in technology. Technology tailoring can be affected through the discovery of how the technology functions, what the technology is similar to, and how well the technology supports group understanding. More work is necessary to identify specific factors and their relationships surrounding tailoring in use.

Finally, we need to examine varied contexts and impact on tailoring in use, the institutionalization of tailored technologies, and the large scale tailoring that occurs among multiple technologies. An emerging area of research centers on service architecture as an inherently tailorable technology. Service architectures allow users to define how and when information is delivered. A tension ha emerged with the possibilities of user tailoring of service architectures conflicting with the realitie of building and supporting these architectures.

Research needs to identify how a balance is struck on this issue and practical findings are critica for guiding organizations as they struggle to make progress in this domain

This study illustrates the rich complexity that exists at the intersection of task, technology, tailoring, and group communication. The next steps are to focus the investigation on the obvious contrasts, in terms of task, technology, and context, and determine how those contrasts alter our explanation of task and technology fit with tailoring and communication. Likewise, determining the variables associated with tailoring can help us better explain the relationships between fit, tailoring, and communication. This study illuminates one part of the larger issue of how we design. tailor. and communicate through technology and how we understand the factors that affect this process

## Definitions

Process structure communication: Communication that is concerned with task coordination and tim ing, work distribution, and statement regarding completed work.

Topic communication: Communication that is concerned with the central task topic.

Social communication: Communication that is not concerned with process structure or topic. Instead the communication centers on humor, the benefits/drawbacks of the current technology, and greetings.

Communication breakdown: Communication acts that are false, incomplete, insincere, or unwarranted (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997).

Resolution of Distorted Communication: Communication acts that accept and act upon the raised validity claims to provide clearness in the communication.

Communication act: A communication act ends at the speaker’s function change or when another person speaks (Poole, 2001).

## Validity Claims

(1) Contextuality – appropriateness of the action.

(2) Efficiency – ability of the action to achieve desired ends

(3) Authority – authority of the communication partner.

(4) Clarity – clarity of the communication act and associated jargon.

(5) Completeness – completeness of the communication act.

(6) Truthfulness – truthfulness of the communication act.

(7) Sincerity – sincerity of the communication partner.

## Social Actions:

(1) Instrumental – When a communication partner views the other partner as an information receptacle, rather than as an actor. Often, instrumental action can be thought of as an individua giving orders to another individual.

(2) Communicative – To obtain a mutual understanding between communication partners. This type of action is different from instrumental action in that it treats both members of the dialogue as actors, and not receptacles of information.

(3) Strategic – When one actor attempts to influence another through communication.
