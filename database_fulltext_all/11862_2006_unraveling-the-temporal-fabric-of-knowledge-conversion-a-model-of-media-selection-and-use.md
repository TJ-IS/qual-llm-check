## Abstract

We draw from and extend Nonaka's (1994) theory of knowledge creation to develop a model of media selection and use in the knowledge conversion (KC) process. KC is a process wherein an individual is affected by the experiences of another. The outcomes of KC—transferred and transformed knowledge—hinge on the development of understanding. The KC process is enabled via various communicative and noncommunicative media. Because the KC process occurs over time, it possesses a temporal fabric or structure. We explore the practical realities of KC as a dynamic, time- and experience-dependent process. We consider how the temporal fabric of KC creates an evolving reciprocal relationship among perceived media utility, selection, and use of media, as well as switching and/or combining media. We propose and discuss two key factors as determinants of perceived media utility use in the KC process: (1) the temporal behavior of individuals engaged in the KC process and (2) individual and joint experience-based factors. We also discuss the role of contextual factors as antecedents. Finally, we offer and illustrate two primary temporal structures for KC media selection and use: (1) monophasic, wherein KC participants use a single medium at a time, and (2) polyphasic, wherein KC participants deploy multiple media simultaneously. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for the design of KC-enabling systems and directions for future research.

Keywords: Knowledge management, knowledge conversion, individual temporal behavior, media utility, monophasic and polyphasic temporal structures

## Introduction

Knowledge management has gained attention over the last decade (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Grover and Davenport 2001; Huber 2001; Schultz and Leidner 2002). The shift from advances in technologies designed to move inputs and products to moving information and knowledge has altered the nature of organizations and the basis of competition (Barkema et al. 2002; Massey et al. 2002). Within the domain of knowledge management, researchers and practitioners have considered a broad array of theoretical questions, strategic issues, and technical approaches, including knowledge creation, capturing best practices, building intranets and corporate libraries, measuring intellectual capital, installing groupware, enacting cultural change, and fostering collaboration. Our interest lies within the realm of knowledge creation. Specifically, we explore how knowledge conversion (KC) is accomplished through continuous recycling and creative use of a firm's shared knowledge and experiences. KC involves both exchange of existing knowledge and potential creation of new knowledge.

We draw from and extend Nonaka's (1994) theory of knowledge creation to develop a model of media selection and use in the KC process. Nonaka proposes four modes of KC that can be characterized as transference (i.e., tacit to tacit, explicit to explicit) and transformation (i.e., tacit to explicit, explicit to tacit) of knowledge (see also Leonard and Sensiper 1998). In our model development, we explore KC as a dynamic, time- and experience-dependent process. Because the KC process occurs over time, it possesses a temporal fabric or structure—a compounding of time and activity (Benabou 1999; McInerney 2002; Reder and Schwab 1989). Individuals act and make choices within this temporal fabric. Thus, KC must be considered as a sequence of activities and events that ultimately lead to KC outcomes (i.e., transference or transformation of knowledge). We incorporate time as a central concept to explain the KC process and the behavior of individuals engaged in it.

Our objective is to characterize the KC process and its relationship to media selection and use. KC is characterized by episodes of uninterrupted activity that lead to understanding, punctuated by events that disrupt progress (i.e., divergence from understanding). Media employed in the KC process may be communicative or noncommunicative and may or may not involve technology. We propose that the KC process creates a dynamic and evolving reciprocal relationship among perceptions of media utility and media selection and use. We define the elements of the KC process and articulate its temporal nature. We then turn our attention to the link between perceived media utility and media selection and use. Media capacity theories provide prescriptive guidelines regarding the fit between media and KC requirements. The social nature of KC and its evolution over time, however, are not explicitly incorporated in media capacity theories. In this light, we focus on dynamic, social-temporal theories to address the implications that various experiences may have on perceived media utility. We propose and discuss two key factors as drivers of perceived media utility: (1) the temporal behavior of individuals engaged in the KC process, and (2) individual and joint experience-based factors. We also discuss the role of contextual factors as antecedents.

Finally, we recognize that contemporary media capacity and social dynamic theories implicitly focus on the selection and use of only one medium at a time. These theories do not explicitly take into account networks and computing technologies, many of which allow KC participants to select from and use multiple media (Rennecker and Godwin 2003; Turner and Tinsley 2002). We extend past research by proposing two temporal structures for media selection and use: (1) monophasic, wherein participants use a single medium at a time, and (2) polyphasic, wherein participants deploy multiple media simultaneously. Because organizations must commit to a particular technical structure based on some criteria, we consider how our model of media selection and use in the KC process may be related to the design of enabling systems. Based on a deeper understanding of the KC process, we contend that no one “right” approach enables it. Rather, design should reflect the ways participants engage in the KC process, individually or collectively. We conclude with a discussion of future research directions.

## Knowledge Conversion

Knowledge conversion (KC) is the process by which individuals are affected by the experience of another (Argote et al. 2000; Nonaka 1994). Experience may be embodied in the mind of another (i.e., tacit knowledge) or a knowledge artifact (i.e., explicit knowledge) (Argote 1999; Nonaka 1994; Nonaka and Konna 1998). Knowledge artifacts (documents, diagrams, procedures) are intermediary mechanisms used to capture and provide experience to others. The success of KC hinges on both developing understanding (i.e., cognition) and the ability to use knowledge (i.e., behavioral change) in some local context (Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs 1986; Fiol and Lyles 1985; Huber 1991). The type of knowledge (tacit/explicit) involved in the exchange is the primary determinant of the KC mode (Nonaka 1994). Importantly, social processes drive the transfer and transformation of knowledge.

For several reasons, development of understanding through KC can be difficult to achieve. First, individuals may not be adept at communicating their knowledge to others (Szulanski 1996, 2000), either directly or through knowledge artifacts. Second, an individual may not possess the absorptive capacity necessary to acquire and integrate new knowledge. Absorptive capacity is a function of existing cognitive structures or prior related knowledge that enables individuals to recognize the value of new knowledge, assimilate it, and apply it (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Third, individuals may not possess a shared interpretive context—shared meanings or experiences—that allows the communication needed for KC to take place (Weick 1990; Zack 1993; Zack and McKenney 1995). Shared interpretive context manifests through blending of KC participants' absorptive capacity. Participants are mutually dependent because each impacts the other's communication and interpretation processes. Both absorptive capacity and shared interpretive context allow participants individually and collectively to converge toward understanding.

## Knowledge Conversion Forms and Enabling Systems

Direct KC may also be complemented by using knowledge artifacts.

Systems enable KC. In Figure 1, we categorize enabling systems as (1) communication and discourse systems and (2) knowledge repository systems. Both types encompass various media (electronic and nonelectronic). Communication and discourse systems include face-to-face interaction and technology-based options like telephone, video-conferencing, instant messenger, e-mail, groupware, and voice-mail (Robey et al. 2000). Knowledge repository systems facilitate access to knowledge artifacts, for example, structured data, diagrams, text-based documents, models, and images (Daft and Lengel 1984; Stein and Zwass 1995). Knowledge artifacts make structured, explicit knowledge available to an organization and its members. Knowledge artifacts may be made available via electronic systems that include data warehouses, best-practice databases, intranets or internets, and portals (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Robey et al. 2000; Stein and Zwass 1995). Electronic repository systems provide the infrastructure for storing, accessing, and using knowledge artifacts, even from remote locations.

Indirect KC achieved via solitary (noncommunicative) work involves use of knowledge artifacts as intermediary mechanisms between the original source(s) and a recipient. For example, an engineer (the source) may produce a product design document (a knowledge artifact) and store it as a shared document for access and use by others (recipients). Whether receivers can use knowl edge artifacts is a function of their absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Thus, the explicit nature of knowledge artifacts is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for understanding and KC to occur.

Direct KC achieved via communicative interaction is enabled by communication and discourse systems that facilitate development of shared understanding between participants (Robey et al. 2000; Zack

1993). It involves communicative activity between participants with a goal of enhancing understanding. Direct KC will be affected by participants' absorptive capacity and shared interpretive context. Some situations will involve only human-to-human interaction; others will call for simultaneous use of knowledge artifacts. For example, an engineer (source) may engage in communicative activities with another engineer (recipient), perhaps using technology, as they simultaneously examine a design document (knowledge artifact) to facilitate understanding and KC.

The above discussion highlights that both communication and repository systems facilitate the KC process. An informing relationship exists between the two. Knowledge workers retrieve and use knowledge artifacts for solitary work and/or communicative purposes to transfer and transform knowledge to or from others. Communication and discourse systems facilitate exchange of human-embodied knowledge. Knowledge artifacts subject knowledge to scrutiny and test, increase understanding, and potentially create new or revised knowledge artifacts (Robey et al. 2000).

## Knowledge Conversion Process

KC is a dynamic process entailing a complex drive toward understanding. All four modes of KC proposed by Nonaka (1994) require that a receiver understands the knowledge content that a source intended to send. As the process unfolds, advancements in understanding invoke use of data, information, and/or knowledge (Nonaka and Konna 1998). The specific mode of KC affects how the process unfolds (e.g., ease or difficulty, degree of communicative versus noncommunicative activity). In practice, however, it does not matter whether the knowledge to be transferred or transformed is tacit or explicit. Rather, what matters is recipients' understanding and how the process unfolds. Because the KC process occurs over time, we must consider its temporal fabric or structure. We contend that the underlying process for all modes of KC consists of periods of uninterrupted progress and repair events (Figure 2a).

At times, the KC process proceeds with uninterrupted progress. The absorptive capacity of individuals and the shared interpretive context between individuals are sufficient for advancements in understanding (Figure 2b). At other times, however, deeper explanations and clarification of some aspect of knowledge are needed. Interruptions to progress reflect divergence from understanding. They may be a result of deficiencies in individual absorptive capacity and the shared interpretive context (Zack 1993). For example, participants may not understand some aspect of a knowledge artifact, or they may have insufficient background to render communicative messages meaningful. Zack suggests that divergence in understanding calls for repair or clarifications and elaborations. We distinguish two types of repair: parallel and serial.

Parallel repair (Figure 2c) is a satisficing condition wherein participants conclude they may continue KC with repair occurring off-line (i.e., clarification can be added at a later time). Conversely, serial repair arises when participants perceive that further KC is unattainable without immediate clarification (Figure 2d). We might describe parallel repair as a bump in the road, whereas serial repair is a roadblock to progress. As shown in Figure 2, the degree of repair may vary as a function of absorptive capacity and shared interpretive context. If absorptive capacity and shared interpretive context are strong, repair efforts may be simple to accomplish. Conversely, if they are weak, repair will be more complex because context must be found or developed (Trevino et al. 1987; Zack 1993). The repair concept is also related to the desire to bring closure to a task or problem (Straub and Karahanna 1998). The need for serial repair may stem from a sense of time urgency and a desire for immediate closure (Conte et al. 1995). Conversely, parallel repair is less urgent; immediate closure is not required. If serial repair is not achieved in the timeframe desired, repair could degrade to a protracted state with multiple activities and messaging (Figure 2e).

The process of uninterrupted progress and repair events applies to both direct and indirect forms of KC. At times, due to its temporal fabric, the KC process may involve solitary work. At other times, it may involve communicative activities. Progress and repair may or may not call for communicative activities or use of communicative media. For example, individuals may be working with a knowledge artifact (e.g., a report) when they experience a repair event (e.g., they require clarification about something in the report). They may choose to access another knowledge artifact (e.g., another report) in a repository system to facilitate deeper understanding. Alternatively, they may choose to contact an individual and ask a question of clarification. In sum, the temporal structure of the KC process may be disjointed over time, participants, forms, and media.

## A Model of Media Selection and Use in the Knowledge Conversion Process

The link between the KC process and media has only recently been explored. We infer from past research that different media may be more (or less) appropriate for different types of repair (Straub and Karahanna 1998; Zack 1993). Following Zack (1993) and Daft and Lengel (1984), we define the term media broadly to include face-to-face interaction, telephone, written memos, documents, and computer-based alternatives. KC often requires a mixture of media because it occurs over time in a solitary and/or communicative fashion and involves dynamic sequences of progress and repair events.

Figure 3 presents a research model of media selection and use in the KC process. It proposes that perceived media utility drives media selection and use. Prior research theorizing about media choice emphasizes that individuals choose media that best fit task requirements (Daft and Lengel 1984; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Short et al. 1976). While we draw from this perspective, we contend that the temporal nature of the KC process suggests other antecedent factors are critical to participants' perceptions of media utility. Specifically, we explore the roles of individual temporal behavior, experience-based factors, and contextual factors as drivers of perceived media utility. As part of our research model, we develop the notion of the temporal structure of media selection and use for the KC process. Because it is a dynamic process, we expect media selection and use to be fitted reciprocally to the temporal structure of the KC process. As Figure 3 shows, the KC process is differentiated from ordinary communication and interactions by the four modes of KC (Nonaka 1994). These underpin the selection and use of media intended to advance understanding. The outcomes of the KC process are transferred and transformed knowledge.

In the following sections, we articulate a set of research propositions (noted in Figure 3). We first define perceived media utility and discuss the three focal antecedents: contextual factors, individual temporal behavior, and experience-based factors. We then discuss the temporal structure of the KC process and explore how it is related reciprocally to media selection and use.

## Perceived Media Utility

Perceptions of media utility derive from media characteristics such as richness, social presence, and interactivity (Daft and Lengel 1984; Zack 1993). Media richness, social presence, and interactivity are highly developed constructs that are significantly interrelated (Fulk and Boyd 1991; Webster and Trevino 1995; Zack 1993).

Richness blends four factors: (1) immediacy of feedback, (2) use of multiple cues (verbal and nonverbal), (3) language variety, and (4) ability to personalize messages (Daft et al. 1987). Rich media have high levels of each factor; lean media have low levels of each factor, Richness indicates the capacity to convey cues and facilitate understanding in a timely manner. For example, face-to-face interaction is high on all factors, whereas artifacts (e.g., a written document) used in a solitary fashion are low on all factors.

Social presence describes the degree to which a medium allows participants to feel psychologically close or present (Fulk and Boyd 1991; Short et al. 1976). Use of multiple, nonverbal channels and cues, as well as continuous feedback, conveys social presence. Furthermore, high social presence conveys social influence and other symbolic content and social context cues. Media low in social presence filter out those cues.

Interactivity is characterized by (1) simultaneity and continuity of communication, (2) use of multiple, nonverbal cues, (3) spontaneity of involvement, (4) ability to interrupt or preempt, (5) mutuality of constructing and coordinating the process, and (6) patterns of turn taking (Zack 1993). Understanding how and why individuals develop media perceptions is an important component of understanding media selection and use (Carlson and Zmud 1999).

Consistent with past research, we expect that perceived media utility drives the selection and use of specific media in the KC process (see Carlson and Zmud 1999; Daft and Lengel 1984; Markus 1994; Walther 1992; Zack 1993). Stated formally,

## Contextual Factors

We consider three critical contextual factors in the KC process: spatial-temporal proximity of KC participants, availability of a recipient, and media accessibility. Spatial-temporal proximity refers to the geographic dispersion of participants. Thus, it will make some media more attractive than others (Reinsch and Beswick 1990; Rice and Aydin 1991; Trevino et al. 1987). Recipient availability refers to the extent to which recipients of a message are perceived to be immediately available to receive a communiqué (Straub and Karahanna 1998). For example, availability may affect whether individuals perceive a synchronous medium, such as face-to-face interaction or telephone, to have higher utility than an asynchronous medium, such as e-mail (Reinsch and Beswick 1990). Physical accessibility of media also affects perceived utility, which in turn encourages or discourages selection and use (Culnan 1985). Individually, and in concert, contextual factors lead individuals to perceive the utility of certain media to be functionally equivalent (Reder 1993). As examples, the telephone may be considered functionally equivalent to face-to-face interaction when KC partners are not colocated, or e-mail may be considered functionally equivalent to telephone when KC partners are unavailable. ## Individual Temporal Behavior

Because the KC process possesses a temporal structure, individual perceptions of and use of time are likely to affect subsequent choices and actions (Waller et al. 2001) including responses to repair events and perceptions of media utility. Thus, we consider temporal beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors of individuals engaged in the KC process. Hall (1959) introduced the construct of polychronicity to describe individual temporal behavior. Polychronicity consists of three dimensions: (1) time-use preference, (2) contextuality of communication, and (3) time tangibility (Palmer and Schoorman 1999).

Time-use preference is the extent to which individuals engage in multiple tasks simultaneously. Bluedorn et al. (1992) describe a continuum of time-use preference from engagement in one activity at a time to engagement in multiple activities simultaneously. If individuals prefer to engage in one activity at a time, they are monochronic. Task-oriented, monochronic individuals tend to consider interpersonal communication as secondary. They are accustomed to short-term relationships (Benabou 1999; Bluedorn et al. 1992). Conversely, if individuals prefer to engage in multiple activities simultaneously, they are polychronic. Polychronic individuals tend to place more emphasis on relationships than tasks. They develop long-term relationships with others (Benabou 1999). Relative to polychronic individuals, monochronic individuals have a greater desire for immediate closure. Thus, they will prefer media that allow immediate feedback (generally characterized as richer and higher in social presence and interactivity).

Contextuality refers to the “location” of meaning in a communicative message (Hall and Hall 1990; Palmer and Schoorman 1999). In low-context communication, most meaning is contained in the content of the message. In high-context communication, most is embedded in the information that surrounds an event (Hall and Hall 1990). With most meaning contained in the message itself, low-context communicators rely less on (and tend to be less skilled at) nonverbal or paralinguistic communication (Palmer and Schoorman 1999). Because monochronic individuals tend to be low-context communicators, they may also perceive some media (generally characterized as leaner, lower in social presence and interactivity) as a better match to their style of communication (Massey et al. 2001).

Time tangibility reflects the notion that multiple conceptualizations of time exist. When time is considered tangible, it is conceived as a commodity (Hall 1959; Hall and Hall 1990; McGrath and Rotchford 1983). When time is considered intangible, activities are typically event-based. Time is viewed as a “backdrop against which events unfold” (Palmer and Schoorman 1999). Monochronic individuals tend to view time as a tangible resource (i.e., something to be saved or wasted). They often hold negative views about interruptions, such as KC repair events, considering them disruptive. Similarly, doing things simultaneously, or using multiple media simultaneously, may be perceived as chaos (Cotte and Ratneshwar 1999). Conversely, polychronic individuals tend to view time as an intangible resource that is not directly controllable. Activities are timed by events rather than a clock. Such individuals will treat unplanned interruptions as equal to planned activities (Bluedorn et al. 1992) and perceive doing things simultaneously as positive. Monochronic individuals are task-oriented and view time as tangible. Relative to polychronic individuals, they may perceive serial repair events as more time-urgent. Thus, they may have a preference for media that provides immediate feedback.

Previous research on media selection and use has not considered the temporal predispositions of individuals engaged in the KC process. We contend that individual temporal behavior is an important driver of perceptions of media in the KC process. The three dimensions of individual temporal behavior may have countervailing effects in terms of specific media preferences. Thus, we expect individual temporal behavior to produce differential perceptions of media utility in the KC process. Stated formally,

## Individual and Joint Experience Characteristics

Media capacity theories assume that media differ in objective characteristics (i.e., richness, social presence, interactivity) that are invariant despite user and context of use. For the most part, the media capacity perspective is static and not viewed within a sequence of interactions (Saunders and Jones 1990; Walther 1992). Attempting to match media to Nonaka's four KC modes would be a technologically deterministic view that ignores the dynamic nature of the KC process (Markus 1994). Dynamic media theories emphasize the social and temporal aspects of communication. The dynamic media perspective assumes that properties of interaction environments are not necessarily invariant over time and experiences. Thus, a time- and experience-based view of the process is necessary. Because KC is a social-temporal process, we contend that media perceptions must be viewed dynamically.

Social-temporal perspectives emphasize the collective character of media use and the social construction of media characteristics (Fulk 1993; Fulk et al. 1987). Over time, individuals develop beliefs about the usefulness of a medium as they gain experience with it (Markus 1994; Walther 1992). As absorptive capacity and shared interpretive context evolve and expand, perceptions of media utility may change. Thus, beliefs about appropriateness are not static (Fulk 1993; Markus 1994; Trevino et al. 1987; Walther 1992; Zack 1993).

Among social-temporal theories that consider the implications for media perceptions and use are adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis and Poole 1994), social influence theory (Fulk et al. 1990), social information processing theory (Fulk et al. 1987; Walther 1992), time-interaction-performance (TIP) theory (McGrath 1991), and channel expansion theory (Carlson and Zmud 1999). All emphasize that past or current individual and joint experiences with media affect perceptions of media utility. We draw heavily from channel expansion theory as it most closely matches the temporal structure of the KC process.

Channel expansion theory suggests that perceived media utility evolves as participants gain experience with topics, each other, organizational context, and media (Carlson and Zmud 1999). As participants learn and develop a better understanding of how to work together, these experiences may lead participants to perceive an expansion in utility that may, in turn, affect future media selection and use (Burke and Chidambaram 1999; Carlson and Zmud 1999; McGrath 1991; Walther 1992). An experience-based factor that has not been considered explicitly in past research concerns the meaning of time and time-related behaviors. Individuals must learn and accept partner-created meanings of time and behavior (Cotte and Ratneswhar 1999), particularly when the KC process involves communicative activity and participants hold disparate views about time. As participants create a pattern of pacing and time use through the KC process, the meanings of time and time-related behaviors become socially constructed.

A medium's perceived utility is related to its capacity to facilitate repair (Zack 1993). In practice, cycles of uninterrupted KC progress and repair interact with acquisition of experiences to affect perceptions of utility. Therefore, we expect that perceived media utility evolves not only with the acquisition of experience, but it also changes as the KC process unfolds. For example, if the need for serial repair arises, continuing with a medium that does not enable the required repair may result in stress or frustration on the part of the KC partners. Switching to another medium that better fits the specific situation should facilitate repair and enable continued progress in KC. Conversely, switching to an inappropriate medium could lead to protracted repair and inhibit KC progress. When parallel repair is required, use of a medium that is a better match for serial repair may be distracting and could create cognitive overload (Brodt et al. 2002). We expect that individual and joint experiences lead to improved understanding of the appropriateness of specific media for a specific KC need (progress or repair) (Reder 1993; Reder and Schwab 1989). While channel expansion theory lends an important temporal perspective to understanding media perceptions in the KC process, it has two limitations: (1) it does not address how expansion progresses over time or the limits of expansion, and (2) it assumes use of only one medium at a time. We will explore the implications of the second limitation in a later section. Using Figure 4, we first discuss two competing views of the nature of expansion for a single medium.

Figure 4a suggests that perceived media utility progresses linearly with the acquisition of more experience. Although unlikely in practice, a linear relationship appears to be the underlying assumption of channel expansion theory. For example, past empirical research has examined the use of e-mail and how individual or joint experiences expanded the perceptions of the capacity of e-mail for rich exchange (Carlson and Zmud 1999). We expect that the empirical findings in support of channel expansion are due partially to the fact that the students participating in experiments were at a point early on the learning curve in terms of experience with the task, their partners, the organizational context, and the technology.

In organizational settings, KC participants may have a long history with each other, the topic/task, the organizational context, and a particular medium for KC (e.g., e-mail) (Zack and McKenney 1995). Therefore, as shown in Figure 4b, we propose that perceived media utility is related to accumulated experience in a curvilinear fashion. Specifically, perceived media utility expands through the process of KC progress and repair. The overall expansion increases at a decreasing rate until a maximum is reached. At some point, diminishing returns to expansion arise because no single medium has infinite utility with respect to richness, social presence, and interactivity (for all possible situations and at all points in time during the KC process). ## Media Selection and Use

We expect media selection and use to be fitted reciprocally to the temporal structure of the KC process itself. The temporal structure of the KC process consists of a complex pattern of periods of progress interrupted by repair events. Repair events reflect divergence in understanding (as determined by the absorptive capacity of an individual or shared interpretive context between individuals), characterized by some sense of time urgency and desire for closure. We propose two primary temporal structures for KC media selection and use: (1) monophasic, wherein participants switch between or alternate use of a single medium at a time, and (2) polyphasic, wherein participants deploy multiple media simultaneously to affect progress and repair.

Monophasic and polyphasic structures are strategic responses to the confluence of factors in Figure 3. Initial and subsequent media selections, switching media, adding or discarding media, or using multiple media simultaneously are components of a KC strategy. Specific media offer tactical resources for implementing a KC strategy. In the following subsections, we describe the two types of temporal structure in more detail.

## Monophasic Temporal Structure

A monophasic temporal structure for KC is characterized by media switching with one medium used at a time (Figure 5a). Individuals may be working alone or with KC partners. Here, the initial medium is either drawn from repository systems or from communication and discourse systems. As understanding increases and other experiences are gained over time (e.g., with a partner), perceptions of media utility increase. At some point, a serial repair event occurs. A decision must be made about whether the current medium is appropriate to accomplish repair. If inappropriate, perceived utility may decrease. If use of the inappropriate medium is continued and closure is not attained, a protracted repair state may result (Figure 5a) (Straub and Karahanna 1998).

![](/api/attachments/T88SKHT9/fulltext/images/282279bea1439414ed88f57642a1a8c986410c0ca35425e7902316d4d0830705.jpg)

As shown, participants (individually or collectively) may switch to other media perceived to be more appropriate for repair. For example, an individual working in a solitary fashion may select another medium from a repository (e.g., access another knowledge artifact) or seek assistance from another individual by switching to a communicative medium (e.g., face-to-face interaction, e-mail). Conversion partners may initiate individually or decide collectively to switch (e.g., e-mail to telephone, telephone to face-to-face interaction). If the switch is inappropriate, protracted repair may result. Participants may experience stress or frustration (Reder 1993; Straub and Karahanna 1998). If the alternative medium is appropriate and serial repair occurs, perceived utility may increase. Notice, however, that a point of diminishing returns with the repair media occurs (Figure 5a). Specifically, continuing in a medium beyond repair may create cognitive overload or knowledge complexity (i.e., “too much, too soon”) in the KC process (Grise and Gallupe 1999/2000; Brodt et al. 2002). As a result, a switch back to the initial medium may be warranted for continued KC progress.

As shown in Figure 5b, concurrent with the accrual of experiences over time, and as long as KC progress continues and serial repair is successful, each medium involved in monophasic KC will continue to be perceived by participants as increasing in utility to some maximum. Figure 5b offers a more-detailed specification of our earlier discussion of Figure 4b and proposition P5 concerning the maximum of perceived media utility. Stated formally,

P5a: In a monophasic temporal KC structure, the experience-derived perceived utility for each medium expands in a curvilinear fashion to a maximum in the knowledge conversion process.

## Polyphasic Temporal Structure

A polyphasic temporal structure for KC is characterized by the simultaneous use of multiple media, with specific media added and discarded over time. We will briefly describe Figure 6 and then turn to the implications of a polyphasic structure.

In Phase A, the KC process begins with use of one medium (monophasic), which could be communicative or noncommunicative. Progress continues as experiences are gained (along with an increase in the perceived media utility) until a serial repair event occurs. In Phase B, while the individual or KC partners continue using the initial medium, a second medium is added to address repair. For example, KC partners may have been using a communicative medium (e.g., face-to-face or telephone) to discuss a topic such as a product design. If meeting face-to-face (first medium), one participant might create a paper-and-pencil sketch (second medium) to facilitate the discussion. Alternatively, they might access a knowledge artifact (second medium) from a repository (e.g., an existing diagram). Conversely, if a telephone (first medium) is used, a shared electronic space (second medium) may be added to draw the sketch (e.g., a whiteboard). The collective use of two media provides utility; neither alone is sufficient. In Phase B, participants continue to use two media simultaneously, even beyond the initial serial repair event. We posit that both media will expand and contract in perceived utility as the KC process unfolds, with both increasing in overall utility. In Phase C, a third medium is added as neither of the first two used in Phase B are perceived to possess sufficient utility to address a serial repair event. For example, KC partners might view another knowledge artifact (e.g., a marketing plan concerning the product) via a shared electronic application to advance understanding. Relative to the needs of the KC process, Phases D and E continue with media added or discarded. As before, protracted states of repair may result if, at any time, any particular medium or concurrent media are inappropriate to achieve serial repair.

In a polyphasic structure, conversion participants can select, combine, and/or switch between multiple media at any point in time. The media can be simultaneously asynchronous and synchronous. Participants can be colocated and/or dispersed. As described in P5a for a monophasic temporal KC structure, each medium reaches a maximum of experience-derived perceived utility. Conversely, in a polyphasic structure, we propose that media used in concert jointly expand to a maximum of perceived utility. Furthermore, we propose that a polyphasic structure will accelerate the process of gaining various experiences (e.g., topic, partners, media, and time). Stated formally,

P5b: In a polyphasic temporal knowledge conversion structure, experience-derived perceived media utility expands in a curvilinear fashion to a maximum in the knowledge conversion process.

P6: A polyphasic temporal structure will accelerate the acquisition of various individual and joint experiences in the knowledge conversion process.

We also expect that the temporal behavior of individuals affects the preferred temporal structure of KC media selection and use—monophasic or polyphasic. The time use and time tangibility preferences of monochronic individuals create a sense of urgency for closure and doing one thing at a time. Thus, monochronic individuals prefer and tend to adopt a monophasic structure. Conversely, polychronic individuals may tend toward and be comfortable with a polyphasic structure. Circumstances may require monochronic individuals to use a polyphasic strategy or polychronic individuals to use a monophasic strategy. Individual temporal behavior reflects an overall tendency toward one temporal structure for KC media selection and use versus another. Moreover, when tensions exist between participants' meaning of time and time-related behavior, a KC strategy must be negotiated such that harmony can be achieved (Benabou 1999; Cotte and Ratneshwar 1999). Within any temporal strategy, the tactical choice of media is best understood as a search for "mediational structures" needed to accomplish KC progress and repair (Reder 1993). Stated formally,

P7: Individual temporal behavior affects the decision to switch between or combine media in the knowledge conversion process because (1) polychronic individuals prefer a polyphasic temporal structure, and (2) monochronic individuals prefer a monophasic temporal structure.

## Implications for System Design

As a practice, knowledge management (KM) addresses the issues of creating, capturing, exchanging, and applying knowledge-based assets. While information technology drove much of the initial interest in KM, academics and practitioners are now aware that not all knowledge can be made explicit, formalized, and stored in repositories. Moreover, even explicit knowledge artifacts may not be understood if individuals cannot integrate new knowledge into existing cognitive structures. Thus, efforts to recontextualize abstract and explicit knowledge often require communication and discourse systems that offer access to others and facilitate collaboration (Ackerman et al. 2003).

As computing and networking technology evolve, KC-enabling systems will not be able to constrain participants to a particular device, place, or time. The first step toward successful KC and leveraging computing and networking technologies is to base design on an understanding of the nature of KC. System designers need to understand the temporal fabric and complexity of the KC process and the people engaged in the process. Our research model highlights key interrelationships to take into consideration.

Given the dynamic and complex structure of the KC process, we conclude that no “right” approach for enabling KC exists. Attempts to match KC activities to specific media characteristics in a technologically deterministic manner negate the temporal nature the KC process. The design of KC-enabling systems should reflect the way in which KC participants, individually or collectively, carry out the KC process. Some individuals, professions, and KC activities (e.g., reviewing a knowledge artifact) are more suited to solitary work. Others are more suited to communicative work. Some individuals prefer to work with multiple media and perceive interruptions or repair events as normal. Others prefer to work with one medium at a time, strive to minimize interruptions and distractions, and perceive simultaneous multi-media use as chaos. In a comprehensive and integrative fashion, KM systems should support a fluid mix of individual and collective work styles. Unfortunately, the work environment that many people face entails a combination of tools and technologies that are isolated and incompatible. While tools may be useful for specific needs, collectively they result in disjointed user experiences because each has its own interfaces and formats (Straub and Karahanna 1998). KC participants may opt for the least-common denominator (e.g., e-mail), irrespective of whether it is appropriate. The KC process hinges on the ability of participants to efficiently and effectively interact with knowledge artifacts and with each other.

KM system design objectives should focus on reducing the overhead and coordination costs associated with accessing and using knowledge artifacts and moving between individual and collective work (Ishii et al. 1993). Aggregation technologies (e.g., web-based portals) provide one point of entry to access knowledge artifacts and communication and discourse systems. Unified interfaces offer convenience and also opportunities for customization. Single-point entry systems should support and enable synchronous integration (i.e., seamless media switching and multimedia use). Seamlessness is defined as mitigating or “eliminating unnecessary obstructing perceptual seams” (Ishii et al. 1993). KC-enabling systems must enable knowledge artifact search and retrieval and access to human experts via various media (Greenberg and Roseman 2003). In addition to portals, synchronous integration is available with technologies like MSN Messenger® or Groove® which, in addition to enabling instant messaging, allow users to start audio conversations or video-conferencing sessions, send files, share applications and whiteboards, and send messages to mobile devices.

Communicative media will fall short if exchange participants cannot find human experts or determine their availability. For example, applications like ActiveNet® (Kaihla 2004) attempt to understand who knows what by constantly monitoring and mining employee profiles, e-mail, reports, and other documents. When a human expert is needed, ActiveNet® suggests synergistic matches. A complementary capability would be a system that integrates presence awareness, thus making it possible to locate and identify a computing device (desktop, handheld) as soon as an individual connects to the network (Rennecker and Godwin 2003). Users could manage presence by controlling the conditions under which they are detectable. Presence technology is expected to be part of third-generation wireless networks and deployable across many devices (cellphones, PDAs, and pagers).

In addition to minimizing overhead and coordination costs via synchronous integration, designers should strive to maximize knowledge transposition across media (i.e., asynchronous integration over time). For example, knowledge acquired via a repair event (serial or parallel) using one medium needs to be transposed for use in another medium as the process continues. A history of a repair conversation conducted in an instant messenger tool could be captured electronically. Subsequently it could be used as a knowledge artifact in solitary work (e.g., review and reflection) or discourse (e.g., e-mail exchange) between KC participants.

Finally, knowledge or knowledge artifacts derived or used in one setting may not be relevant in another context. As such, designers must consider translation capabilities so that conversion participants can easily leverage knowledge across contexts. For example, Virtual Mentor (Massey et al. 2002) is a KM tool to support the front-end of new product development processes. It was designed to support knowledge workers in their local language and functional language translations of knowledge so that it could be used by other knowledge workers. Specifically, the tool translates knowledge from the language of engineers to the language of managerial decision makers. Many of today's tools and technologies are not sufficiently robust to support this type of dynamic translation within or across contexts.

Pragmatically, conceptualizing the KC process and implementing KC-enabling systems requires an assessment of fit with the existing technical and organizational infrastructure. KC participants work within the constraints of organizational design. Firms do not operate with a clean slate; rather, operational constraints are real and binding. These include long-standing investments in alternative technical systems, organizational and human resource policies, and operating procedures. Legacy investments in infrastructure impose an implicitly embedded KC discipline. The question then becomes: Is this implicit (and often unintentional) discipline maximizing the firm's return on investment in knowledge-based resources? The answer may be that collections of incompatible and redundant tools and technologies should be reconsidered in light of comprehensive, synergistic, and integrative approaches to enabling KC. Only through a deeper understanding of the KC process and what motivates media selection and use can we come to understand the value of existing and emerging technologies.

## A Research Agenda

Our analysis of the KC process raises many research questions. Multiple studies are needed to understand the complex phenomena associated with KC. Our model of media selection and use in the KC process can be tested using positivist and interpretivist research approaches (Lee 1991). A positivist approach would begin by substituting specific empirical indicators into the propositions to form testable hypotheses. Because our propositions are broad “truth” statements about our research model (Wheeler 2002), hypotheses will have to focus on one or two constructs at a time. Hypotheses could be tested in single or multiple snapshot views (Markus and Robey 1988). For example, Proposition 3 states that individual temporal behavior affects perceived media utility in the KC process. To specify a testable hypothesis, the specific dimension of individual temporal behavior that is of interest would need to be specified. For example, a study may include measures of time-use preference, contextuality of communication, and/or time tangibility. Also, the specific dimension of perceived media utility that is of interest would need to be specified. A study might include measures of richness, social presence, or interactivity (see Carlson and Zmud 1999). A sample hypothesis may be: Relative to polychronic individuals, monochronic individuals have stronger preferences for media that offer immediate feedback.

Similarly, the main point of Proposition 5 is that perceived media utility expands in a curvilinear fashion to a maximum. Here, subsets of P5 related to the links between various experiences (e.g., topic, partner, time) and perceived media utility could be articulated as hypotheses. For example, a study could involve the use of one medium (e.g., e-mail) and explore whether perceptions of a medium's utility expands asymptotically as experiences are gained. Because perceptions of a maximum of utility evolve over time, multiple snapshots of experiences and perceptions will be needed. Contextual conditions must be reported carefully. The study would include measures of experiences and perceived media utility (see Carlson and Zmud 1999; Dennis and Kinney 1998). A sample hypothesis might include: As KC partners gain experience with each other over time, a medium reaches a maximum of perceived utility. One way to examine this hypothesis would be to test the (curvi)linearity of perceived media utility over time. One might postulate a curvilinear decreasing function for some period of time with an identifiable inflection point as perceived media utility reaches its maximum. Another study might compare the rates of growth (or decay) in perceived utility, the points of inflection in the curve, and/or the maximum points. Further detailed hypotheses can be derived from the propositions we have offered.

Whereas a positivist approach begins with theory and constructs, an interpretivist approach provides understanding of the complexities of the KC phenomena by examining the process theory tenets of our proposed research model (Wheeler 2002). Interpretivist data collection entails detailed, longitudinal case studies so data collection and analysis can occur simultaneously. Because knowledge is most relevant in a specific use context, cases could focus on specific organizational business processes (e.g., new product development). Analysis within and between cases could look for common patterns, events, and sequences of events that advance understanding (Lee 1991; Paré and Elam 1997).

Because time plays a key role in our model, systematically varying time dimensions provides multiple options for any research design. One tactic would be to follow a single business process over time as different KC partners, topics, and media are engaged. Measurements could assess perceptions and the event history that shapes the KC process. A second tactic would be to hold time constant to capture perceptions as a snapshot. A third tactic would combine the two by conducting longitudinal studies with snapshot measurements taken at predefined intervals.

The proposed model of media selection and use in the KC process has two important boundary conditions. First, our discussion of the KC process assumes a dyadic KC relationship. In practice, the KC process involves complex temporal, spatial, and interactional overlaps, usually contending for limited resources (Reder 1993). In work settings, KC often involves multiple participants over time. For example, two knowledge workers engaged in a communicative activity may recognize the need for repair and solicit another knowledge worker's input to solve a problem. The first two participants might check the third person's availability and involve him/her in the communicative activity (e.g., via teleconference or through an instant messenger application) (Rennecker and Godwin 2003). This example shows that knowledge work involves a complex network of relationships and intricate interactional patterns. Regular interactions occur with individuals both inside and outside of one's focal group/task (Benabou 1999; Dewhirst 1971; Reder 1993). Future research could explore the effects of relational complexity on the KC process and media selection and use.

Second, our discussion of the KC process and media selection and use did not consider potential interaction or transference effects across KC media. Monophasic and polyphasic structures have potentially significant implications for theories concerning KC media selection and use and the design of enabling systems. Positive (negative) perceptions of media utility may affect subsequent perceptions of another media's utility as experiences are transferred forward, suggesting the presence of a halo effect. A halo effect is the influence on an individual's attitude or beliefs toward a particular action as generalized from other actions (Balzar and Sulsky 1992; Murphy et al. 1993). For example, if the KC form is communicative and involves alternating between face-to-face interaction and e-mail, the knowledge acquired (about the topic or partner, for instance) via face-to-face interaction should positively affect subsequent perceptions of e-mail's utility with the same partner and/or topic. Moreover, halo effects may be even stronger in polyphasic KC as participants capitalize on the synergies found in the simultaneous use of multiple media. The characteristics of any one medium (richness, social presence, interactivity) may not possess the needed utility; rather, it is the collective synergy of multiple media that provide utility. Future research is needed to assess the nature of interaction and transference effects for specific combinations of media used in the KC process.

## Conclusion

Management and deployment of knowledge-based resources are complex, strategic decisions. In this paper, we explored the temporal aspects of knowledge conversion, a process intended to increase the understanding of an individual and mutual understanding between individuals. Advancements in understanding will occur over time in dynamic, complex, and iterative cycles leading to transference (i.e., tacit to tacit, explicit to explicit) and transformation (i.e., tacit to explicit, explicit to tacit) of knowledge (Leonard and Sensiper 1998; Nonaka 1994). Our paper is a direct extension of Nonaka's theory of knowledge creation to the practical level of knowledge work. Specifically, we offered a temporal view of KC as a process consisting of periods of uninterrupted process punctuated by repair events leading to transferred and transformed knowledge.

Merely establishing that time matters or that the KC process possesses a temporal fabric, however, is not sufficient to advance understanding. Our objective was to gain a deeper grasp of the temporal structure of KC and to understand how this structure relates to media selection and use. The contribution of our research is a model positing that media selection and use in the KC process is a function of perceived media utility, which in turn is predicated on a complex entangling of individual temporal behaviors, evolving individual and joint experiences, and contextual factors. We also extend past research by considering the evolving expansion and contraction of media perceptions and proposing that expansion is asymptotic and iterative. Furthermore, we define the temporal structure of KC media selection and use as either monophasic or polyphasic, and we conclude that it is reciprocally fitted to the temporal structure of the KC process.

Finally, KC is disjointed across time, participants, forms, and an array of enabling media. Designers of systems intended to enable KC must take into account the variability of the KC process and the styles of those engaged in it. Knowledge will be manageable only insofar as the temporal fabric of the KC process is embraced and fostered. Otherwise, practitioners and researchers risk either underutilizing the potential of advanced computing and networking technologies or overestimating their utility.
