# A critical perspective of engagement in online health communities

Inkyoung Hur<sup>a</sup>, Karlene C. Cousins<sup>b</sup> and Bernd Carsten Stahl<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems and Cybersecurity, College of Engineering and Computing, Nova Southeastern University, Davie, FL, USA; <sup>b</sup>Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, Florida International University, Miami, FL, USA; <sup>c</sup>Faculty of Technology, School of Computer Science and Informatics, De Montfort University, Leicester, UK

## ABSTRACT

Many individuals go to online health communities to obtain emotional and information support. This study employs critical discourse analysis (CDA) to examine the discourse in <sup>fi</sup>ve online childhood immunization forums to identify online practices related to engagement and emancipation. We identify four online engagement discourse types in online health communities: cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and political. Consistent with our critical perspective, we identify drivers and obstacles to emancipation. The obstacles to emancipation include the adverse consequences of building online knowledge and collective intelligence, shifts in patient–physician relationships, and “Big” institutional power. Members’ suggestions for empowering community members include encouraging members to conduct their own research on childhood immunization and to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between facts and opinions. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that sensemaking is one way to emancipate online health community members, while highlighting the challenges of using online communities to promote emancipation.

## 1. Introduction

Personalized care approaches focus on empowering persons to make health care decisions. The underlying principle is that individuals control how their health care progresses (Sepucha, Fowler, & Mulley, 2004) rather than depending on health care professionals to decide for them. Though a shift toward patient-centred care is emerging, the individual’s role in health care decisions is still weak and people still encounter di<sup>fi</sup>culties in making medical decisions (Bernabeo & Holmboe, 2013). Further, individuals are often passive and compliant while receiving medical treatments (Bernabeo & Holmboe, 2013), because they believe their knowledge and skills are not comparable to those of health care providers. Thus, a major challenge lies in engaging people and empowering them to make more decisions about their health care.

Health care providers recognize that individuals have the right and responsibility to make their own health care decisions (Anderson & Funnell, 2005; Funnell & Anderson, 2003), and that in order for individuals to actively participate in and control their own health care, providers should o<sup>f</sup>er them relevant health information, including the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of health care treatment options. One such approach involves implementing online health information systems.

In recent years, researchers have examined health care technologies that could enhance individuals engagement in their health care, including patient portals, personal health records (PHRs), and mobile health (mHealth) applications (Tang, Lorenzi, Harle, Zhou, & Chen, 2016). These information systems often fall short in engaging individuals, and there is scant evidence that their use results in increased engagement or improved patient-centred care delivery (Tang et al., 2016). Further, engagement in online health communities is rarely examined. To address these de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, we investigate engagement in online health communities empirically using a critical theoretical approach.

An appropriate setting in which to critically examine the role of information systems (IS) in engagement is online health communities focused on childhood immunization. Childhood immunization is a controversial program, shrouded in uncertainty about its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks. Individuals may be reluctant to vaccinate their children due to the fear of side e<sup>f</sup>ects. Society, in response, raises public safety concerns, because non-vaccination often leads to the spreading of fatal diseases. Moreover, some people believe that big institutions try to control individuals childhood immunization decision-making by hiding or exaggerating information. Due to the lack of valid information, parents are hesitant to make childhood immunization decisions and to take responsibility for their decisions’ consequences (Cataldi, Di Caro, & Schifanella, 2010). Organizations such as pharmaceutical companies often implement online health communities to connect directly to their customers, by providing them with tools, resources or buying opportunities. Often, parents turn to these online health communities to obtain and share information to assist them in making childhood immunization decisions. These online health communities produce intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to members, such as information and emotional support (Van Uden-Kraan, Drossaert, Taal, Seydel, & van de Laar, 2009; Zhang, O’Carroll Bantum, Owen, Bakken, & Elhadad, 2017). However, it is not clear how members’ engagement and participation in online health communities might empower individuals to make health care decisions (Demiris, 2006).

Thus our research question is: how does engagement unfold in online health communities and what are the e<sup>f</sup>ects of such engagement? Accordingly, this study has two research objectives. The <sup>fi</sup>rst research objective is to understand how engagement emerges as a social practice from written discourse in online health communities from a critical perspective, which includes an investigation of hidden power relationships. Our second objective is to provide insights into potential ways to help online health community members become more empowered with regards to their own health care and that of their family members. We sought to achieve this emancipatory objective by identifying obstacles to and drivers of participants’ emancipation through critical discourse analysis (CDA) (Fairclough, 2013). CDA can inform the reproduction of public discourses via online technology as well as the societal implications of such discourses and identify possible solutions, yet researchers rarely use CDA for such purposes (Cukier, Ngwenyama, Bauer, & Middleton, 2009). As critical researchers, we use CDA to initiate positive change to overcome the disempowered status that online discourse promulgates in order for individuals to become emancipated.

The contribution of the paper to current discourse is thus theoretical as well as practical. We contribute to the rapidly growing discourse concerning online engagement in health care and beyond which tends to focus on online engagement’s bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We demonstrate based on the well-established basis of critical theory, that online engagement can have disempowering as well as emancipatory e<sup>f</sup>ects. Furthermore, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to the IS critical research literature and provide insights in an unexplored area, thus responding to Rowe’s (2018) call for more philosophically informed critical IS research (Rowe, 2018). The paper contains a methodological contribution and shows the value of Fairclough’s CDA when applied to IS. In addition to these theoretical and methodological insights, the paper positions itself in the critical tradition by aiming to provide practical support to promote emancipation. While our contribution to the respondents’ emancipation is modest, we show that our intervention did help some respondents move towards achieving their potential.

In order to achieve these contributions, we begin with a review of the relevant literature, starting with engagement in online technologies and then moving on to IS critical theory. This provides the basis for the introduction of Fairclough’s CDA as the method employed in this study. We describe the two phases of data collection we undertook, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and results. The data analysis leads to the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of drivers and obstacles to emancipation and sensemaking’s role in emancipation. We close with a selfre<sup>fl</sup>ective and critical discussion that highlights the achievements but also the limitations of our approach.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Engagement in online health communities

Engagement is one of the success factors of social network technology use (Thielst, 2010), and provides a powerful explanation for sustainable technology use (Barrett, Oborn, & Orlikowski, 2016; Rich, Lepine, & Crawford, 2010). As summarized in Appendix 1, researchers from many academic disciplines have empirically examined the relationships between engagement and its consequences in various technology use contexts such as education, politics, customer service and health care. Despite these emergent streams of research, the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of engagement is fragmented (Brodie, Hollebeek, Juric, & Ilic, 2011) and consequences vary according to the technology use context.

In the health care industry, patient engagement involves changing patient behaviours to promote wellness and preventative health care. Engaged patients are proactive, willing, and active participants in their own health care; participate in social health care activities; and interact with health care providers. Patient engagement e<sup>f</sup>orts can be as simple as marketing campaigns for public health and as advanced as wearable health and behavior monitors. Patient engagement has a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on health outcomes (Laurance et al., 2014) and the quality of health care service (Roseman, Osborne-Stafsnes, Amy, Boslaugh, & Slate-Miller, 2013).

Consumer (or customer) engagement dominates the engagement research literature (Heinonen, 2018) and exhibits similar properties to engagement in online health communities. Prior research has scrutinized the concept of consumer engagement with a focus on conceptual formulations (for a literature review see Brodie et al., 2011; Dessart, Veloutsou, & Morgan-Thomas, 2015). Consumer engagement represents the individual’s speci<sup>fi</sup>c interactive experiences with the organization’s o<sup>f</sup>erings, activities and brands and other users of the community (Brodie, Ilic, Juric, & Hollebeek, 2013; Vivek, Beatty, & Morgan, 2012). Engagement may take the form of a multidimensional process, consisting of cognitive, emotional, and behavioural aspects (Brodie et al., 2011; Patterson, Yu, & de Ruyter, 2006). The cognitive aspect suggests that a person’s mental state is accompanied by active, sustained, complex, cognitive processing, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting their intellectual absorption in the activity (Brodie et al., 2011). By contrast, the emotional aspect re<sup>fl</sup>ects the emotional bonding or impact that occurs during the activity (Brodie et al., 2011). The behavioural aspect indicates the amount of attention, concentration, e<sup>f</sup>ort, persistence, and verbal participation the person shows during the activity (Brodie et al., 2011). Political engagement is also salient in online communities and re<sup>fl</sup>ects political participation and political knowledge (Conroy, Feezell, & Guerrero, 2012), focusing on activities such as how governments set policy and regulations. Based on the foregoing literature, we adopt the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the various types of engagement in Table 1.

Building on the work of Brodie et al. (2011), marketing researchers uncovered evidence of the cognitive, emotional, and behavioural factors that positively and negatively determine consumer engagement in online brands (Brodie et al., 2013; Dessart et al., 2015) and interest communities (Heinonen, 2018). Researchers have also examined the nature of political engagement through social media sites that facilitate news dispersion (Gil de Zúñiga et al., 2012; Xenos, Vromen, & Loader, 2014), political donations (Bimber, 2001) and civic participation (Campbell & Kwak, 2010; Valenzuela, Park, & Kee, 2009).

As shown in Appendix 1, from a methodologica standpoint, most studies on online engagement are positivist and studies that employ a critical approach to understanding online engagement are rare. For example, Rambe (2012) employed CDA to Facebook conversations to expose the relational power between educator and learner and the learning challenges that occur. Overall, researchers have primarily embraced a positivist perspective to develop theoretical perspectives of the factors driving online engagement in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts with assumptions that online engagement has positive e<sup>f</sup>ects. This raises the question as to whether engagement in online health communities produces e<sup>f</sup>ects similar to engagement in other contexts such as learning, building relationships and political action. In particular, prior research has not examined the negative consequences of engagement when it occurs in online health communities via technology or provided any solutions, or examined the relational qualities of the power-based structures that exist in online communities. Therefore, we need more knowledge about what types of engagement occur in online health communities and how they unfold. We also need to expand our knowledge of online engagement beyond the dominant positivist perspective that prevails. Critical research o<sup>f</sup>ers such an alternative perspective. As we will argue below, critical research allows building on a wide range of approaches, including the work on engagement.

Online health communities provide one of the most exciting research areas for IS researchers in the health care community (Zhang, Bantum, Owen, Bakken, & Elhadad, 2017). Though prior studies show that online health community members give informational and emotional support to others (Deetjen & Powell, 2016), there is room for researchers to study technology’s role in engagement, such as the technologies used in online health communities, more extensively. However, rather than taking a conventional view of online engagement, this study takes a critical view of the dynamics around engagement in online health communities and investigates the opportunities for online health community members emancipation. Patients making use of online communities are often subject to serious illnesses or conditions such as cancer, heart failure, dementia, etc., thus increasing their vulnerability. This raises research ethics issues when investigating them. More importantly, it can skew their perception and thought processes, making online engagement’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence on emancipation more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess. We, therefore, chose to concentrate on a group that is similar to patients in that it is worried about health and health care, but is not immediately facing a debilitating illness, namely the group of parents who are in charge of making immunization choices for their children. These children are typically healthy but subject to their parents’ decisions about health care interventions, which may a<sup>f</sup>ect individual children and entire cohorts. Understanding online engagement’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence on this group helps us understand the emancipatory potential of such IS more broadly.

## 2.2. The childhood vaccination debate

Contagious diseases such as measles, mumps, rubella (MMR) and whooping cough pose serious public health threats, especially in highly populated urban areas. Childhood vaccination has been e<sup>f</sup>ective in nearly eradicating these diseases in the Western hemisphere (Rabinowitz, Latella, Stern, & Jost, 2016) and is an important aspect of childhood preventive health, worldwide (Sporton & Francis, 2001). However, vaccination policies in countries around the world are diverse (Walkinshaw, 2011). While some countries focus on educating the population about vaccination’s bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and leaving them to make the choice, others o<sup>f</sup>er <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives to vaccinate or mandate vaccinations (Walkinshaw, 2011). However, in recent years, vaccination has declined in many regions of the world such as the US partly due to Andrew Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld’s article in The Lancet in 1998 concerning a possible link between the MMR vaccine and autism. Although the research was discredited and the article retracted, doubts linger about vaccinations’ potential harms. This has caused several opposing camps to emerge in defence of and in opposition to childhood immunization. Vaccination’s opponents believe that children’s immune systems can deal with most infections naturally, and are concerned about the risk of side e<sup>f</sup>ects such as seizures, paralysis and death. These anti-vaxxers contend that research shows that vaccines may trigger autism, diabetes and attention de<sup>fi</sup>cit hyper activity disorder. Thus, many anti-vaxxers believe that exposure to the disease itself would often be preferable to vaccination. Vaccination’s proponents say that vaccines are safe. These pro-vaxxers believe that children are susceptible to serious vaccine-preventable diseases and support vaccination. They contend that vaccination prevents illnesses such as smallpox, polio and whooping cough saving millions of children’s lives and adverse reactions are rare. In addition, selective vaxxers support the use of some but not all vaccines so as to minimize any potential risks.

In the US where babies.com (one of our research sites) is located, vaccinations are mandatory for children entering public school, even though religious and philosophical exemptions are possible. Some US states allow the public school system to suspend children who are not vaccinated. Many paediatricians will not treat unvaccinated children. And some legal experts believe that parents who do not vaccinate their children should be subject to prosecution if their unvaccinated children infect and harm other children.

In Canada, where mothers.com (our second research site) is located, only three provinces require vaccination of di<sup>f</sup>erent types; Ontario, New Brunswick and Manitoba. In these three provinces, parents may apply for exemptions. However, in the event of an outbreak, schools will prevent unvaccinated children from entering.

Prior research shows that parents actively seek to make an informed decision based on an assessment of the risks and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of immunization (Sporton & Francis, 2001). Many parents reported that they did not perceive health care providers as sources of balanced information (Sporton & Francis, 2001). Therefore, it is important to provide parents with easy access to accurate information concerning the pros and cons of treatments. Often, parents turn to online health communities to gather information, engage in discussions and make informed decisions about childhood immunization. These real or perceived power di<sup>f</sup>erences were the starting point for our use of critical theory as the theoretical basis of this study.

## 2.3. Critical theory in information systems research

Our interest in the emancipation of the online health community users led us to adopt a critical theoretical position. The IS literature often describes the critical approach as a “paradigm” and contrasts it with the two more widely used paradigms of positivism and interpretivism (Chua, 1986; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). Critical theory is not a monolithic theoretical position but consists of a set of interlinked principles (Klecuń, 2004). We can apply various criteria to determine whether a piece of research is critical (Basden, 2002, 2011; Klein, 2009). One frequently recurring aspect of critical research is its emancipatory aim (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011). This implies that critical research is not exclusively descriptive but aims to “bring about real change in the human condition” (Ngwenyama, 1991, p. 276). Or, as Cecez-Kecmanovic (2005, p. 19) put it, “critical IS researchers produce knowledge with the aim of revealing and explaining how IS are (mis)used to enhance control, domination and oppression, and thereby to inform and inspire transformative social practices that realize the liberating and emancipatory potential of information systems.”

There are further characteristics that de<sup>fi</sup>ne and shape critical research, such as a non-performative position (Alcadipani & Hassard, 2010), an explicit value position (Deetz, 1996), a non-objectivist ontology that links to a corresponding epistemology (Alvesson & Willmott, 2003) and a commitment to critically re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the research itself (Richardson & Robinson, 2007). We consider these characteristics to be relevant and return to some of them in the later discussion, but for this paper’s purpose assume that their main importance is their contribution to critical research’s emancipatory aim.

Emancipation means that “more people can achieve their potential to a greater degree” (Klein & Huynh, 2004, p. 163). This raises the question of the researcher’s role in the emancipatory process. Emancipation cannot mean that the researcher provides their respondents a pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned solution. Instead, the research process provides a stimulus to help actors within society and organizations to develop their own future in accordance with their own values (Broadbent & Laughlin, 1997). This position implies that researchers are not objective and detached observers but play an active part in the research process and outcomes. Thus, one key activity of critical research is to identify obstacles to emancipation and allow research participants to understand these and overcome them. Such obstacles can be con<sup>fl</sup>icts (Waring, 2004) but also particular views of the world or pseudo-naturalized constraints (Hirschheim, Klein, & Lyytinen, 1995) which contain and perpetuate ideologies (Freeden, 2003; Hawkes, 2003) through hegemonic means (Stahl, Doherty, Shaw, & Janicke, 2014).

This very brief introduction to IS critical research should su<sup>fi</sup>ce to demonstrate its value to our research. Information technology’s use for organizational and social interaction can have emancipating consequences by allowing individuals and groups to better understand their own position and develop means to achieve their legitimate objectives. At the same time, there are numerous examples of IS being used to reify power relationships, remove them from critical scrutiny and perpetuate inequalities and power di<sup>f</sup>erences. In this study, we are interested in understanding the way in which online interaction can have emancipatory or disempowering e<sup>f</sup>ects with regards to the contentious topic of children’s vaccination. Undertaking such a critical study can be done in di<sup>f</sup>erent ways, which leads us to the discussion of our chosen methodology.

## 2.4. Framework for critical discourse analysis

There is no standard and recognized way of doing critical research, no universally accepted “critical methodology” (Brooke, 2002; Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011). Critical research employs any methodology that allows it to further its emancipatory aims. In practice, one can observe two groups of methodologies, both of which further critical research’s emancipatory aim in di<sup>f</sup>erent ways: action-oriented and language-oriented methodologies. The action-oriented methodologies see critical researchers engaging directly in the context of their research. These methodologies tend to be based on variations of participatory action research (Argyris & Schön, 1989; Whyte, 1991). The other set of methodologies focuses on the use of language as a central means of promoting or inhibiting emancipation. They are based on the recognition of the importance of language in shaping the social world and aim to expose obstacles to emancipation such as ideology and hegemony. Such work can take the form of ideology critique (Schultze & Leidner, 2002), deconstruction, investigation of metaphors and others. The most advanced set of methods in this tradition fall under the heading of critical discourse analysis (CDA), which we have adopted as our methodology.

CDA begins with a normative critique of discourse, assessing it against norms such as speaking the truth, speaking sincerely and justly (Fairclough, 2017). It then moves to an explanation of the social reality that leads to such discourse and towards action to change those features of social reality that need changing. CDA reveals how power relations and inequality expressed through language unfold. It is a relational form of research in which the focus is not on entities or individuals but on social relations (Fairclough, 2013). CDA presents a unique opportunity to examine how social structures, such as power, domination, identity, inequality, and communication distortions, manifest through language, thus revealing emancipatory possibilities. We employ CDA to examine these social issues in online health forums and to reveal the emancipatory possibilities (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011; Howcroft & Trauth, 2005; Myers & Klein, 2011). We are interested in the ways community members use language and technology to discursively construct online practices. Thus, the focus of our analysis is on what people do with their language and their technology.

There are several CDA approaches. Prior IS research studies using CDA relied on the perspectives of Foucault and Habermas (Cukier, Bauer, & Middleton, 2004; Cukier et al., 2009; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Stahl, 2004; Stahl, Tremblay, & LeRouge, 2011).

The Foucauldian perspective views discourse as the history of knowledge and practice and how knowledge and practice become solidi<sup>fi</sup>ed and normalized (Wall, Stahl, & Salam, 2015). Power is a central element in the Foucauldian perspective. This perspective focuses on emancipating those who are dominated by hegemonic powers such as those present in social groups or the power of one country to dominate the other. Foucauldian CDA’s purpose is to identify power and challenge it. To do so, Foucauldian CDA examines the communicative utterance and its context by assessing power relationships between actors and the structures and processes that guide behaviour (Kelly, 1994; Stahl, 2008; Wall et al., 2015). Foucauldian CDA also focuses heavily on the history of discourse, which may span centuries (Foucault, 2002).

The Habermasian perspective views discourse as actors’ speech in a communicative exchange (Stahl, 2008; Wall et al., 2015). Focusing on ideal speech situations (McGrath, 2005), the goal is to identify departures from the ideal speech situation, the nature of power structures that lead to marginalized or oppressed groups, and solutions for the hegemony. In determining the ideal speech situation, Habermasian CDA assesses violations of four validity claims: the discourse’s comprehensibility, truthfulness, and legitimacy and the speaker or author’s sincerity (Wall et al., 2015).

This paper does not o<sup>f</sup>er the space to discuss the complex relationship between Habermas and Foucault (Ashenden & Owen, 1999; Kelly, 1994). Su<sup>fi</sup>ce it to say, though they are both recognized as key sources of the IS critical research tradition, neither of them produced a methodology in the sense of an elaborate description of steps to be taken to address a research question. We, therefore, adopted Fairclough’s CDA methodology, which is a form of explanatory critique (Fairclough, 2013) and which includes aspects of both Habermas’ and Foucault’s positions. Norman Fairclough and his colleagues (Fairclough, 1992, 2005; Fairclough & Wodak, 1997) developed CDA as a response to earlier analytical approaches that he felt either focused too narrowly on the micro-linguistic aspects of discourse while neglecting its more macro-social aspects or vice versa.

## 2.5. Principles of Fairclough’s CDA

Fairclough’s CDA methodology facilitates a dialectical-relational analysis of social practices in order to identify obstacles to emancipation and ways to overcome them (Fairclough, 1995, 2001, 2013). We interpret these obstacles to emancipation as social wrongs. Each social practice includes several social elements including activities, subjects and their social relations, instruments, objects, values, and discourse (or semiosis). These elements are dialectically related in that they are not discrete, separate elements. Each element internalizes the others without being reducible to them. For example, in knowledge economies and societies, the phenomenon “knowledge driven” amounts to “discourse driven” as knowledge is generated and circulated as discourses (Chiapello & Fairclough, 2002). Thus, the process through which the discourses become operationalized in knowledge economics and societies is an example of the nature of the dialectics of discourse. In this dialectical relationship, elements of social practices are socially constituted by discourse but also socially shape discourse. Thus, CDA explores the tension between these two sides of language use; the socially shaped and the socially constitutive. The nature of this relationship varies between institutions and organizations and according to time and place. This requires CDA to be integrated within frameworks for transdisciplinary research that combines elements from di<sup>f</sup>erent disciplines (Fairclough, 2013). In bringing disciplines and theories together to address research issues, the result is the theoretical and methodological development of each of them.

Fairclough’s CDA method gives a step-by-step approach in order to identify possibilities to emancipate actors and resolve the ways actors deal with obstacles. It makes use of the key terms de<sup>fi</sup>ned below.

Discourse is an extended stretch of speech or writing and may include language and other forms of semiosis or signi<sup>fi</sup>cation such as body language or visual images (Chiapello & Fairclough, 2002). Discourse is a particular way of representing certain parts or aspects of a version of a social practice plus ideas about it and attitudes to it. According to Fairclough (2013), discourse is implicated in social activity in three ways: discourses (pluralized), genres, and styles. These three elements are also CDA’s key dimensions and serve as analytical categories.

Discourses are socially constructed knowledges of some aspect of reality (Van Leeuwen, 2005). Thus, there can be di<sup>f</sup>erent discourses, di<sup>f</sup>erent ways of making sense of the same aspect of reality which serve di<sup>f</sup>erent interests. These discourses can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with di<sup>f</sup>erent positions or perspectives of di<sup>f</sup>erent groups of social actors.

Genre concerns the how of communication (Van Leeuwen, 2005). The term “genre” is generally used to mean a type of text. It is the use of language associated with and constituting part of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c social practice such as interviewing people or advertising goods. Genres typically have three types of characteristics; form, content and function (Van Leeuwen, 2005). For example, in literature and <sup>fi</sup>lm, we may characterize genres such as the western and the fairytale based on their content. Genre can also be viewed as the use of language in a particular way. The genre of magazine advertisement is de<sup>fi</sup>ned on the basis of its function, advertising as well as its medium, the magazine. Genres realize speci<sup>fi</sup>c power relations between the communicating parties. The advice genre, for example, is one of the ways in which society enacts and perpetuates the relation between the helpless and ignorant layperson and the resourceful and knowledgeable professional expert (Van Leeuwen, 2005). Thus, the advice genre can be seen as one way in which power is exercised through everyday routines, habits and communicative practices.

Styles are identities or ways of being in their semiotic aspect – for example, the styles of business managers or political leaders (Fairclough, 2013). Style is the identity that the text’s author projects in the text. For example, being a particular sort of person, statements of claims about the case at hand, or value claims about what is good and desirable.

Fairclough’s CDA allows us to examine networks of social practices. Networked social practices constitute a social order. For example, the social order of education in a particular society at a particular time. The discourse/semiotic aspect of a social order is an order of discourse. An order of discourse is a particular combination of di<sup>f</sup>erent discourses, genres, and styles that are articulated together in a distinctive way (Fairclough, 2013).

Fairclough (2013) suggests a methodology for applying a dialectical-relational version of CDA. He cautions that though we can identify stages or steps in the methodology, these stages should not be interpreted in a mechanical way as it may not always make practical sense to proceed from one to the next. These steps, as a form of explanatory critique, can be articulated as four stages (Page 235):

Stage 1: Focus upon a social wrong (i.e., obstacle to emancipation) in its semiotic aspect

Stage 2: Identify obstacles to addressing the social wrong

Stage 3: Consider whether the social order “needs” the social wrong

Stage 4: Identify possible ways past the obstacles

As mentioned previously, IS Researchers have rarely employed Fairclough’s CDA framework. Most authors in other disciplines (e.g., Rambe, 2012; Roode, Speight, Pollock, & Webber, 2004; Thompson, 2004) relied on Fairclough’s earlier frameworks for analyzing discourse related to social and cultural change (Fairclough, 1995). In this earlier work, Foucault and Blanchot (1989) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three levels of relational discourses as: (1) social conditions of production and interpretation, that is, factors in society that lead to the production of texts and how these factors a<sup>f</sup>ect interpretation; (2) the process of production and interpretation, that is, how texts have been produced and how this a<sup>f</sup>ects interpretation; and (3) the product of the <sup>fi</sup>rst two stages, the text. Corresponding to these levels of discourse, Foucault and Blanchot (1989) prescribes three stages of CDA as description, interpretation and explanation. IS researchers used these early frameworks (Fairclough, 1995, 2001) to develop a characterization of power in discourses related to IS. IS researchers have further applied these frameworks to develop speci<sup>fi</sup>c genres and discursive types (Ng’ambi, 2008; Rambe, 2012; Roode et al., 2004; Thompson, 2004). These text genres and discursive types are outcomes of the process of production and interpretation of the text. Using a deductive approach, researchers applied these speci<sup>fi</sup>c genres uniformly across these research studies to examine issues of power and domination in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts. Further, the researchers employed an iterative analysis (moving from text to social action) of CDA, that is description, interpretation, and explanation (Foucault & Blanchot, 1989), to unravel the social practices embodied in the text.

For example, prior research has focused on developing a critical perspective of the power structure underlying online technology use in education revealing the tensions between students’ perceptions of the in<sup>fl</sup>exibility of traditional teaching methods and students’ demands for <sup>fl</sup>exible learning (Ng’ambi, 2008). An examination of the nature of collaborative engagement through social networks, such as Facebook in an academic setting, revealed learner challenges and associated power structures (educator–learners and learner-peer) (Rambe, 2012). In the country development context, the focus has been on unravelling the con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of power underlying the discourse of government o<sup>fi</sup>cials and development agencies to disclose barriers to development through information and communication technology (Thompson, 2004), including criticisms of technocratic approaches and advocacy for a more sociocentric perspective (Roode et al., 2004).

Together, these approaches set out to reveal and to criticize the hidden connections between properties of texts, social processes, ideologies and power relations. However, most of the approaches which employ Fairclough’s CDA to IS are primarily descriptive, and do not employ the rich transdisciplinary theoretical approach that Fairclough (2013) advocates, which would, for instance, combine critical theory with theoretical perspectives grounded in the IS literature. The systematic, deductive approach mostly employed which applies the speci<sup>fi</sup>c text genres and discourse types to discourses about IS, while valuable, also limits the capability to develop new theoretical insights.

IS researchers have not used Fairclough’s CDA to understand how online discourse informs the relations, challenges and hegemonic structures prevalent in online health communities or to question the underlying assumptions of these social practices and structures. Understanding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online health communities on power relations is crucial to meaningful engagement in online health communities, because online communities are not value-free.

## 3. Data collection and analysis

In our CDA approach, we used both critical hermeneutic analysis and content analysis techniques (Cukier et al., 2009). Cukier et al. (2009) recommended this strategy to overcome the challenges the analysis of the vast amount of data presents and to increase the rigour of the critical analysis of large data sets. We collected and analyzed the data in two phases. In Phase 1, we focused on the analysis of engagement discourse within online health communities. In Phase 2, we focused on the discourse within interviews carried out with the online health community members to examine whether they were of aware of the drivers and obstacles to emancipation.

## 3.1. Phase 1 – analysis of online health community content

In Phase 1, we collected and analyzed the contents of the <sup>fi</sup>ve online forums within two online communities to identify the nature of engagement discourse and drivers and obstacles to emancipation. We analyzed the data collected using a content analysis approach. ## 4. Data collection

To de<sup>fi</sup>ne the corpus of data to be analyzed (Cukier et al., 2009), we sought to identify appropriate online communities relevant to the context of our research and illustrative of the communicative exchange and relationships among the communities’ participants. Further, we sought to use multiple data sources so as to ensure the corroboration of the evidence. We applied a convenience-sampling approach by using the Google search engine to identify online health communities and discussion forums within these communities that focused on childhood immunization. The most relevant discussion forums we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed were located in two web portals for online communities created for parents, which we gave the pseudonyms babies.com and mothers.com.<sup>1</sup> The pseudonyms were carefully chosen to closely represent the online communities’ missions. Most members in these two portals are mothers with children or mothers who are expecting a baby. Overall, the online community members discussed the pros and cons of immunization options, shared their experiences and feelings, and gave advice to members who asked for help. Between these two portals, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve online forums which enabled us to carry out a cross-case analysis and to corroborate the evidence from multiple sources.

Using NVivo’s NCapture feature, we uploaded members’ posts and comments on each post for the period from January 1 2011, to July 31 2014, from each of the <sup>fi</sup>ve forums into NVivo 11 for analysis. Because some forums contained as many as 8,000 posts, we decided to further reduce the corpus of data to focus on a shorter time period of January 2014 to July 2014.

## 4.1. Data analysis

To carry out the analysis, we applied content analysis and coding procedures. The objective of the content analysis is to identify empirical observations pertaining to the three interconnected but analytically separable aspects of genre, style and discourse (Chiapello & Fairclough, 2002) within the social order of online engagement. That is, what sort of activity is this a part of, what sort of interaction characterized by what sort of social relations (genre)? What sort of authorial identity is constituted here (style)? What sort of representations do we <sup>fi</sup>nd here of online engagement (discourses)? The analysis also focused on uncovering the drivers and obstacles to emancipation occurring through discourse in the online forums with a focus on emergent power structures.

As shown in Figure 1, coding for the content analysis was a two-staged procedure consisting of top-down than bottom-up coding. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in top-down coding involved running a frequency query to identify keywords which could be grouped to represent each type of engagement discourse, namely: cognitive, emotional, behavioural and political engagement. The commonly used words in the result of frequency query allowed us to identify political engagement which we did not expect before starting the analysis.

As shown in Table 3, we created a list of search words based on the results of the frequency query and supplemented by relevant terms from the literature. We then executed a search on each relevant keyword to identify relevant text for open coding. Using word-stemming algorithms available in NVivo 11, we included all word stems and synonyms in the keyword search.

Seeing that we were interested in understanding the discourse in context, we decided that at least the paragraph surrounding the key search term and the corresponding responses to those posts were necessary as the context for understanding how the term was used. This was also a data-reduction strategy to reduce the amount of text for analysis so as focus on the most relevant content in the online forums. This data reduction strategy reduced the total number of codes to be analyzed to 1,130.

Once we completed top-down coding, we began the inductive part of the analysis or bottom-up coding by reading the content in each open code and developing axial codes to present salient themes for each genre, style and discourse we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Genres are diverse ways of acting. For instance, genres belonging to the emotional engagement discourse included empathizing with others, appreciating advice, and creating emotional bonds of friendship. Styles were represented through the constitutions of authors’ identities, or ways of being, as they participated in the discourse. For instance, one dominant authorial identity was that of the supporter. The supporters’ identities were represented through their authorship of the text and constituted through the genres of discourse, which included empathizing with others. Thus, our analysis allowed us to look critically at how engagement emerged in terms of the three analytically separable aspects of genre, style, and discourse (Chiapello & Fairclough, 2002).

4.2. Phase 2 – analysis of community members’ email interviews

## 5. Data collection

In Phase 2, we focused on community members emancipation. The focus of the study was to determine if members were aware of the drivers and obstacles to emancipation we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in Phase 1 of the study and to lead them to think of ways to empower themselves to overcome these obstacles to emancipation. We, therefore, conducted 30 semistructured email interviews with community members from both babies.com and mothers.com to see if they were aware of the obstacles to emancipation occurring in online health communities and what they believed could be done about these obstacles. Before interviewing these community members, we noti<sup>fi</sup>ed the online communities’ administrators that we were conducting a research study within the online communities. By way of a post in each online forum, we invited community members to participate in the study. Those members who participated in the semi-structured interviews gave their informed consent to participate in the study. We rewarded these interviewees with a Starbucks gift card.

We administered email interviews using the Qualtrics online survey tool. We asked questions about how participating in the online health community empowered members to make immunization choices and asked for suggestions on what community members should do in the online community in order to empower themselves. We also asked subjects to identify the risks of participating in the online health community and what they did to deal with those risks. The full interview protocol is available in Appendix 2.

## 6. Data analysis

Using NVivo 11, we analyzed the results of the 30 email interviews. Based on an inductive approach, we developed open codes to identify the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of participating in the online community, obstacles to emancipation members perceived and their suggestions on how to overcome the obstacles to emancipation.

As shown in Table 5 we grouped the open codes into axial codes representing strategies to overcome the obstacles to emancipation. By using this approach, we were able to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the analysis in Phase 1, develop a theoretical explanation of the social orders of engagement and the obstacles to emancipation that occurred, the identities of participants, the power structures that emerged, and emancipation strategies.

As members took part in debates, they developed the styles of opponents or advocates of di<sup>f</sup>erent viewpoints.

All you who vaccinate are great. It’s your choice. \~ I chose to read, read, read, read, read, and read some more on this topic. I have educated myself on both sides of the debate and make an informed decision for my children. (Forum A, Member 2)

Some members <sup>fl</sup>ocked to the online communities to obtained balanced viewpoints so that they could understand both sides of the debate. As members read the discourse, they developed styles as information assimilators. Members also developed styles as information disseminators as they shared information about the health care process and the latest updates on certain topics of interest.

The cognitive engagement discourse re<sup>fl</sup>ected persons’ high levels of health knowledge through their use of academic terms, in-depth medical information, and references to research journals. This resulted in the emergence of power structures within the communities. Members asserted themselves as experts by using professional terms for medical diseases or medications, which gave them a powerful position within the communities. Some members emphasized their use of a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c approach by using user names (e.g., science mom) to indicate that they are experts. Community members showed respect for these experts through the discourse. Thus, these linguistic articulations created a power structure among community members. The language moulded and shaped power positions by facilitating and constraining social practice associated with ways of discussing information and ways of interpreting texts.

Within the support forums, members often shared the same opinion. To establish forum identity, the forum names indicated members’ preferred vaccination stance such as anti-vaccine, pro-vaccine, and selective-vaccine. However, members often expressed disagreement with opposing views by using aggressive and sardonic language. Moderators sometimes censored content that violated forum guidelines including these opposing views. Thus, moderators shaped the forum’s identity by controlling the content of the discourse as well as clarifying the community’s purpose and rules. Censor was a common style for moderators within the communities. The genre of censorship primarily represents cognitive engagement, but also includes aspects of emotional engagement.

I have removed the last two posts because they certainly invite discussion of mandatory vaccinations, which is not a welcome topic on any of the Vax forums. (Forum E, Member 3)

In contrast, the debating community members were more inclusive and allowed posts representing di<sup>f</sup>erent viewpoints. Thus, in the debating community, two styles were common: advocate or opponent.

The online discussion boards played a critical role in knowledge creation, transfer, storage, organization, and access, resulting in the development of collective intelligence within the online communities (Lévy, 1997). Community members often used the community’s collective intelligence to <sup>fi</sup>nd better answers to their questions. However, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to separate fact from opinion. Poor information quality threatened the credibility of the community’s collective intelligence. For example, in Forum C, a member challenged the accuracy of a doctor’s statement about the number of deaths related to chicken pox in the US.

And let’s not forget the FACT this Dr. LIED to push a vaccine for a disease that even the VACCINE INSERT describes as “mostly benign.” Dr’s statement (lie) \~ that there had been 100 deaths among healthy kids with chicken pox last year.” I would DEFINITELY request that your Dr provide the reference(s) for that statement!!!!!! Total Number of Deaths from Chicken Pox: 48 (1999). (Forum C, Member 4)

However, the report Member 4 referenced only showed the statistics from Florida. Further reports veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that the average number of deaths from chicken pox in the 1990s in the US was 100, thus adding credibility to the doctor’s statement. Thus, relying on the community’s collective intelligence is risky, as it may be biased or inaccurate.

Also, sifting through the tremendous volume of information in the communities challenged members as they tried to rely on the community’s collective intelligence to conduct research.

We have tried to do our research, but are overwhelmed with the information on both sides. (Forum E, Member 5)

The community’s collective intelligence is a powerful information source which became legitimized as members recommended each other as trusted resources, expressed advice and caution on how to deal with medical professionals. However, voluminous information that re<sup>fl</sup>ects various viewpoints may overwhelm community members, and poor information quality may threaten sound decisionmaking and the credibility of the community’s collective intelligence.

## 7.4. Emotional engagement

Emotional engagement re<sup>fl</sup>ects the emotional bonding or impact that occurred as members participated in the discourse in the community (Brodie et al., 2011). Members engaged in emotional engagement through their willingness to share personal stories and o<sup>f</sup>er emotional support through text. Genres of emotional engagement included expressing worry and concern, appreciating other posters, empathizing with others and forming and sustaining friendships. When community members enacted these genres, it created emotional ties among them. The online communities provided emotional support to participants as they discussed personal and challenging problems with others who shared similar health conditions and concerns. However, the online communities also provided a venue where disagreement was evident. As members participated in the discourse, several styles emerged, including supporter, dependent, adversary, and friend.

Texts expressing worry and fear were common expressions of emotional engagement discourse genres. For example, in Forum C, Member 6, in a post titled “Whooping Cough Support Please,” requested encouragement because doctors had diagnosed her four young, unvaccinated children with whooping cough.

I have a 9 year old, 5yr, 3yr and 4 month old. All have never had one vaccination. Well yesterday they were all diagnosed with whooping cough and now I am worried. I am mostly worried about the fact that my in-laws have never supported me and our choice not to vaccinate. Now they are all freaking out. I have a little 3 month old niece who was exposed before the children were diagnosed and I am really worried about her and my family’s reaction to that as well. \~ Please does anyone have encouragement for me? (Forum C, Member 6)

As conveyed by her expression of worry and request for encouragement, Member 6 had a dependent authorial style. In response, several supporters provided emotional support through the text and by using the material features of the web portals such as emoticons of hearts or tears, pass-a-note, reply, and hugs to convey emotional support and empathize with other members. As they enacted these practices, members displayed the style of supporters.

For instance, the following post titled “Deciding on vax is really stressful” represents a dependent’s dilemma in deciding whether to vaccinate her child or not. This post had six hugs from supporters who empathized with the dependent.

Everything I read is biased. \~ Everyone I talk to contradicts the opposing side. My husband and I can’t agree on it. \~ I want to do what’s best for my child. \~ I’ve done so much research and I’m usually going more on the pro-vax side, but then I’ll read an opposing article, and it has me all terrified to think about it. \~Who is right in these articles? \~ How can I come to a decision and have something both my husband and I agree on. I don’t want anyone jumping down my throat for anything, I just want to know views, experiences, etc. I am really at a loss here. I have no idea what to decide upon. (Forum A, Member 7)

In general, members recognized the presence of mothers who shared the same di<sup>fi</sup>culties, concerns, and emotions and tried to support them emotionally. The discourse in the various communities re<sup>fl</sup>ects the bonds of sympathy between the members. Another prevalent style throughout the communities was a friend. Through emotional discourses, members could recognize others’ attitudes, opinions, experiences, and even personalities from the language they used. Members could also identify others like them. Online friendships were possible because the material features of the online technology provided a space to meet new people and gave visibility to members thoughts and personalities, allowing community members to become friends and develop their relationships. Some community members developed online friendships over several years. These longterm relationships resulted in stronger emotional engagement.

However, the discourse genre of expressing worry and fear may create fearmongering. Member 5 in Forum E created a post titled, “I am a 58 year old woman who contracted polio because my mother didn’t vaccinate me as a child.” The post contained two pictures of the paralyzed member, illustrating in a graphical way the negative consequences of nonvaccination. This post had a high number of views, drawing mass attention. Responses to the post re<sup>fl</sup>ected both sympathy and fear of non-vaccination.

## 7.5. Behavioural engagement

Behavioural engagement entails how much attention, concentration, e<sup>f</sup>ort, persistence, and verbal participation the person shows during the engagement (Brodie et al., 2011). The discourse genres that demonstrate the energy, e<sup>f</sup>ort, and time members spend participating in childhood immunization activities constitute behavioural engagement. Members developed styles as participants as they engaged in the behavioural engagement discourse.

In the analysis, we focused on members’ discourse about their own behaviour as they took actions relating to their childhood immunization needs. Members’ most prevalent example of behavioural engagement discourse was their interaction with health care providers in a face-to-face setting. The behavioural engagement discourse described the nature of physician–patient relationships. For example, community members posted information about health care providers’ interactions, such as their degree of support and receptiveness to the member’s vaccination stance and the advice and the information the providers gave during the visit. For instance, Member 8 compared the approaches of two di<sup>f</sup>erent doctors: a pro-vaxxer and a selective vaxxer. The di<sup>f</sup>erences in the doctors’ approaches impacted Member 8’s immunization decisions.

My husband and I have vaxed our previous 3 children b/c [because] we didn’t realize we had an option, our doctors said it was mandatory and were very intimidating so, being young and not well researched in the area we gave in. Now we have 10 years and more wisdom/research to pull from and vaxing is super questionable to us. \~ today \~ took DS (3 month old baby) for a wellness check \~ to the ped [pediatrician] who is very selective/non vax friendly. She did ask us to consider this one vax DTaP though. (Forum E, Member 8).

The relationships with health care providers depended on the caregivers’ and health care providers’ childhood vaccination stances and di<sup>f</sup>ered among anti-vaxxers, selective vaxxers, and pro-vaxxers. Members had di<sup>f</sup>erent viewpoints about health care providers and experiences interacting with doctors, resulting in members’ posting arguments that either bashed doctors or rebutted these arguments in the doctors’ defence.

The behavioural discourse of anti-vaxxers focused on members’ trust (or lack thereof) in health care providers. Anti-vaxxers’ discourse mostly supported a patient-centred decision-making model. In some instances, anti-vaxxers’ low level of trust in health care providers accompanied this belief.

I just couldn’t trust him with my child’s medical care if he can’t even get his facts straight & used scare tactics & exaggeration to try to persuade me. Plus he didn’t know what was in the vaxes & was shocked when I told him some of the ingredients. (Forum B, Member 9)

The above quote shows an anti-vaxxer’s strong distrust of doctors and an objection to the paternalistic approach of the physician and the exaggeration and scare tactics used. She used her o<sup>fl</sup>ine personal experience during the doctor’s visit to justify her lack of faith in the doctor. Stating her opinion in a public place crystallizes her distrust of doctors, and at the same time, it o<sup>f</sup>ers others room to think about doctors’ trustworthiness as a whole.

In the selective vaxxers’ support community, a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the behavioural engagement discourse is dedicated to <sup>fi</sup>nding the right health care professional who will support the parent’s vaccination stance, act as a counsellor, and respect the parent’s preferences and medical decisions.

I need help finding a doctor that is friendly towards us wanting to selectively vaccinate our children. (Forum E, Member 10)

On the other hand, pro-vaxxers primarily supported the paternalistic physician-patient model and suggested ways to communicate e<sup>f</sup>ectively with health care providers. In support of the paternalistic physician-patient model, Member 11 made recommendations to community members to discuss their concerns with a doctor and rely on the doctor to help them make their decisions.

If you have an open-minded pediatrician, call the o<sup>fi</sup>ce and ask to meet with him/her to discuss your fears. \~ Sometimes you have to ask for help. Say you are scared. (Forum A, Member 11)

While some members had relationships with doctors who supported their vaccination stance, some participants like Member 8 had experiences with doctors who did not provide them with balanced information and even with doctors who tried to convince them to vaccinate children. In order to overcome the obstacles of limited information access and physician paternalism, parents often visit online health communities to search for balanced information.

Thus, the behavioural engagement discourse revealed a power shift in parent–provider relationships from parents’ dependence on medical professionals to dependence on the community’s collective intelligence.

## 7.6. Political engagement

Political engagement is the degree to which people learn about and become involved in political activity, <sup>fi</sup>nd common causes, and become involved in public life (Baumgartner & Morris, 2009; Gil de Zúñiga et al., 2012). The political engagement discourse suggests that online community members are actively involved in political issues surrounding childhood immunization. The genres include reporting on policy, regulatory and legislative changes, sharing reports on members’ own activism, and calling other members to action. As such, members developed styles as political activists as they engaged in the political engagement discourse.

Members often reported policy, regulatory, and legislative changes on childhood immunization and discussed the possible consequences with community members. For example, in several states, parents wishing to enroll their children in public school could apply for immunization exemptions for their children for religious reasons. A member in Forum B shared a link to a news report that New Mexico was considering a bill to terminate the religious exemptions policy in public schools. The post resulted in many members’ sharing their activism results and calling members to action to object to the changes. The following quotation illustrates how this political engagement discourse motivated people to engage in political grassroots movements.

I just learned that a bill to severely limit the religious exemptions for vaccinations has been introduced in the NM [New Mexico] house. Currently NM residents can get medical exemptions, religious exemptions based on letters from members of clergy, or religious exemptions based on their personal religious beliefs. This bill, HB 522, would eliminate the last option. Fellow New Mexicans, what are we going to do about this? I’m writing my state reps and I’ll look into starting a petition. The bill is scheduled for the House Health committee on March 7th, perhaps we should pay a visit, as well. (Forum C, Member 12)

In response, other members heeded the call to action and reported on the result.

Hi, did you attend? I was there and spoke in defense of our right to choose. (Forum C, Member 13)

Members 12 and 13 shared updates on health care policy and established the possibility of political activism to overcome restrictions on religious exemptions for vaccinations.

Calls to action also included discourse about possible avenues for legal action. For example, one member reported on her intention to appeal a religious exemption denial and solicited community members opinions on possible class-action lawsuits.

I just got a rejection of my religious exemption for vaccination in the mail. \~ I am shocked that this was not accepted and now I have to appeal. Does anyone have any advice on what I should state in the appeal? Also I am wondering if there are any lawsuits brewing? This seems ripe for a class action lawsuit. (Forum B, Member 14)

The online forum’s visible and persistent features enable the visualization of members’ political stances and interests and connect members with similar interests, even though opinions may vary. Because the content, once created, can be reused and reanalyzed over time, these political ideas may persist within the community, resulting in groups which congregate around the ideas to critique or agree with them, hence causing tighter relationships among members. The persistence of information within the communities has the capability to build the political power of the community.

One central idea in the political engagement discourse is community members’ perception of the power of “Big” institutions, such as hospitals, pharmacies, and medical academic institutions. Community members believed that these “Big” institutions had the power to manipulate information and downplay childhood immunizations’ consequences. The term “Big” is treated as a proper noun (with a capital letter) and is used throughout the forums to describe this power display.

You think that all doctors, scientists, researchers are lying to protect “Big”? (Forum C, Member 15)

While some members propagated the possibility of a conspiracy, other members read, veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, and contested alleged industry rumours and lies through the political engagement discourse. In response to the posts on the “Big” conspiracy to deceive the public about childhood immunization, some people expressed their agreement or disagreement about it. Groups congregated around these ideas, tightening the relationships among community members and strengthening the community’s political power to either <sup>fi</sup>ght against “Big” institutions or to support them.

Some members recognized the inequality between low-powered groups such as parents and their children and high-powered groups such as the medical and pharmaceutical communities and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

The medical, pharmaceutical and FDA community, that our world is driven and run by a group of sick, money hungry, greedy, egotistical, powerful « \_s.” … Please, please understand these people are not looking out for you, for me, for our children. They simply don’t care. Yes, there are TONS of people in these <sup>fi</sup>elds who do care and do want to make a di<sup>f</sup>erence, but they are not the policy makers, they are not the deep pockets, and most of all, they are not the power. Again, I would rather deal with nature than harming my child for rich, sick bastards. (Forum C, Member 16)

Overall, our analysis shows that cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and political engagement discourses emerge when individuals use an online health community technology. These types of engagement discourses varied, depending on the individual’s identities and vaccination choices, discussion topics, and power di<sup>f</sup>erentials within the online communities. Drivers and obstacles to emancipation emerged through these engagement discourses.

## 7.7. Emancipating community members

An important step in critical research is the research subjects’ emancipation. Researchers can help facilitate emancipation by asking the research subjects to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on their own experiences and to make suggestions for their own emancipation. In this section, we report on our analysis of email interviews with 30 community members, whom we asked to engage in retrospection of their own discursive practices within the online communities and to make suggestions of ways in which members could become empowered. In the context of online health communities, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne empowerment as a person’s ability to make independent, informed decisions about his or her own health care. Empowerment is one aspect of emancipation, the self-directed ability of the individual to overcome sti<sup>fl</sup>ing power relationships. Empowerment thus forms part of the critical agenda of promoting emancipation.

Members were aware that they experienced empowerment when the community provided information to make informed choices and prepared them to ask their physicians questions that would make their appointments more productive. However, members often experienced information overload. Interviewees spoke of fearmongering within the communities, because members’ personal stories often included negative experiences and recommendations that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced parent’s decisions.

As shown in Table 5, interviewees suggested four main strategies that members should employ to overcome these challenges. First, to overcome information overload and become more empowered, members should conduct their own research to make sense of the childhood immunization discourse and to shape sound health care decision-making.

Second, the recommended research tactics included seeking balanced and unbiased information to support health care decision-making. Third, because of poor information quality in the online health communities, interviewees recommended that members should attempt to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between fact and opinion and critically think about the veracity and reliability of the discourse about childhood immunization. Relying on scienti<sup>fi</sup>c articles and reliable information will help members make sense of the discourse and alleviate fearmongering. Fourth, members should try to identify trustworthy advisors and health care professionals and discuss their concerns with them.

In accordance with our critical perspective, we also re<sup>fl</sup>ect on whether participation in online health communities has an emancipatory result and the role of sensemaking in participants emancipation.

## 8.2. Community members’ sensemaking and emancipation

Members contemplated their emancipation when we asked them to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the pros and cons of their participation in the online community. When asked to re<sup>fl</sup>ect in this way, members became aware of the good and the bad in the online health community. Though members had positive experiences, they recognized that the consequences of online engagement were not ideal. First, they proposed that members seek empowerment by conducting research about childhood immunization, determining information sources’ veracity and reliability, and evaluating the trustworthiness of health care providers and other advisors. Second, members should di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between facts and opinions about childhood immunization and rely on scienti<sup>fi</sup>c information, such as journal papers and published reports, to aid in their decision-making. These suggestions indicate that members were aware of the cognitive gaps they faced and the need to <sup>fi</sup>ll these gaps.

We interpret the interviewees’ retrospection to mean that participants are engaging in sensemaking processes (Dervin, 1983; Weick, Sutcli<sup>f</sup>e, & Obstfeld, 2005). We contend, from a critical perspective that sensemaking is an important aspect of emancipation. By making sense of a situation, an individual gains agency, an ability to understand the context and options. They can re<sup>fl</sup>ect on and reject existing power relationships and be truly autonomous. Sensemaking is thus not just part of individual cognition but a necessary condition of emancipation. Individual and collective sensemaking occurs when groups of people use social technologies such as those used in the online health communities (Heverin & Zach, 2012; Oh, Eom, & Rao, 2015). In the context of online health communities, individuals engage in sensemaking in order to <sup>fi</sup>gure out what to do next and to deal with anxiety and fear. Consequently, individual sensemaking may help members become empowered to make their own immunization decisions. Collective sensemaking may enable members to converge around viewpoints and motivate them to rise up and take collective action toward childhood immunization.

Sensemaking involves active information seeking, o<sup>f</sup>ering, and sharing and provides insight into the meaning individuals and organizations ascribe to events through the di<sup>f</sup>erent orders of engagement discourse. Sensemaking allows meanings to materialize (drivers and obstacles to emancipation) that shape identity (styles), action (genres) and power relations.

Through sensemaking, power is expressed in acts that shape what people accept, take for granted, and reject (Weick et al., 2005). Sensemaking occurred as participants established social relations through the discourse, sought information and brought their identities and experiences to bear as they engaged in discourses on the topic, even becoming friends. However, we observed that sensemaking of the dis courses is power infused. For instance, power di<sup>f</sup>erentials exist between government agencies that se and enforce immunization regulations, physicians who may be primarily paternalistic in their approach, pharmaceutical companies with their own agendas, expert community members and those seeking information and support. Members’ identity, knowledge experience and the resulting power di<sup>f</sup>erentials constructed through the discourse all in<sup>fl</sup>uence how individuals make sense of childhood immunization and make their own health care decisions. The various discourses with statements of facts, opinions, support and opposition to vaccinations are not simply approaches to determining the truth, but are part of an ongoing negotiation among various power-infused knowledge positions .<sup>2</sup> For instance, powerful players in the community such as the experts gave cues abou their authority through user names such as “science mom” and challenged or con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the accuracy or plausibility of the discourse with scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research being the gold standard. To further establish their legitimacy, experts would often cite the academic literature. Other community members helped to legitimize the collective intelligence of the community by assimilating, sharing, challenging and commenting on posts. Members through the political engagement discourse also called others to action to advance immunization policy. When given the opportunity for retrospection on the obstacles and drivers to emancipation in the online health communities, members were able to articulate the emancipatory possibilities and some members highlighted the need for retrospection by pointing out the need to <sup>fi</sup>gure things out.

Figure 2 represents the ways in which online engagement discourse can serve as the environment in which sensemaking can occur and how this a<sup>f</sup>ects the emancipation of the individual discourse participant.

Overall, sensemaking of the engagement discourses in the online health communities is dialectical-relational. The engagement discourses shape the obstacles to and drivers of emancipation and vice versa. The <sup>fl</sup>ow of power through interactions among the many social groups and identities help to shape the nature of the cognitive, emotional, behavioural and political engagement discourses, which in turn shaped sensemaking. In turn, sensemaking shaped the <sup>fl</sup>ow of power and engagement discourses within the communities.

## 9. Conclusion

As the discussion has shown, the answer to our research question, how does engagement unfold in online health communities and what are the efects of such engagement, is complex. By exploring engagement types, we have used a methodological approach that showed the richness of online interactions. The critical angle has shown that the engagement can have contradictory e<sup>f</sup>ects. It can be emancipating but it can also be disempowering. By focusing on the concept of sensemaking, we have shown some of the mechanisms through which emancipation can be promoted or limited. As a critical study, the paper goes beyond a simple response to the research question and re<sup>fl</sup>ects on our own work, which is a hallmark of critical research. We then highlight the paper’s contributions and point to next steps and further work.

## 9.1. Re<sup>fl</sup>ection

One characteristic of critical research is that it aims to explicitly incorporate re<sup>fl</sup>exivity. This goes beyond the customary elaboration of research limitations and involves the exploration of the researchers’ ontological and epistemological assumptions and their role in the study (Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2001). One initial point of re<sup>fl</sup>ection has to do with our ability to assess the emancipatory e<sup>f</sup>ect of the study itself. We used netnography techniques such as email interviews to collect members’ re<sup>fl</sup>ections on emancipation. Our methodology constrained the ability to collect richer data from our online subjects because of the di<sup>fi</sup>culties in asking follow-up questions through email to probe more deeply into subjects perspectives on emancipation.

By choosing the study’s focus to be participant’s online interaction within the online forums, we have rendered invisible large parts of the social power structures that support these technologies. The portals were owned by large multinational corporations that have interests in vaccination and see online participants as potential consumers. However, these companies were not explicitly part of the analysis. An interesting observation is that these companies did not visibly interfere with the discussion, but left them largely to the participants. This does not imply that their presence did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the discussions in some way that may be worth investigating further. We pointed to the issue of censorship, for example. Censorship is based on individual actions of people who companies gave the responsibility for discussions. Technology that the hosting organizations control also facilitated censorship in the discussion groups. These organizations’ commercial interests mean that they want to attract users and avoid alienating them, which, in turn, means that controversial topics are o<sup>f</sup> limits.

Another unaddressed key question was the role of truth in the discourses. The topic of childhood vaccination is highly contentious and we do not take a position on whether all views are equally acceptable. We argue that a better informed decision by the parents can be seen as a sign of emancipation. We do not discuss whether this means that all decisions that are based on higher levels of information are equally acceptable. Truth is a key concept of critical research. Many positions in the philosophy of science would agree that truth emerges from the interplay of di<sup>f</sup>erent and often con<sup>fl</sup>icting ideas. Social media can add a diversity of views and thus strengthen truth claims, but they can similarly be used to exclude dissenting voices and thereby serve as an “echo chamber” or, worse, give rise to conspiracy theories, which limits robust truth claims. In our empirical work, we found evidence for both of these e<sup>f</sup>ects. This raises follow up questions about power and emancipation that are important but not well re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the online discourses. One of them is the question of the consequences of decisions for others. An individual decision on immunization may have little e<sup>f</sup>ect, but in an aggregate form, they do. If enough individuals opt out of immunization, then herd immunity gets lost, which can lead to the infection of others who would otherwise be protected. This side-e<sup>f</sup>ect can have serious consequences and is part of online discussions. However, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to compare these e<sup>f</sup>ects in terms of emancipatory outcomes.

A further question is that of intrinsic power structures in parental relationships. Critical research tends to problematize paternalistic power relationships but in the case of childhood vaccination these are inescapably the norm. Parents need to make these decisions for their children. However, the decisions outcomes may not be clear for decades and the children may evaluate the emancipatory potential very di<sup>f</sup>erently once they reach an age where they can re<sup>fl</sup>ect on them. The regulatory environment which mandates vaccination in many jurisdictions re<sup>fl</sup>ects these issues. This implies that individual parental views are subservient to the greater good of society and the next generation. This may be seen, as an imposition of alien values onto parents, which shows the complexity of the situation and demonstrates the di<sup>fi</sup>culty that we as the authors faced when being confronted with the di<sup>f</sup>erent value choices of the participants.

These questions point to a larger problem for critical research. If emancipation is the aim of critica research, as we have assumed in this paper, then a key question is who is to be emancipated and how this is to be evaluated and ascertained. In our study, we have highlighted the problem of a paternalistic relationship between parent and doctor, which is often seen as detrimental to emancipation. However, by focusing on immunization decisions, we have complicated this relationship, which now includes the health care provider, the parent and the child. If emancipation is the ability to reach one’s potential, then there are likely to be a trade-o<sup>f</sup> between di<sup>f</sup>erent levels and units of emancipation. The empowerment of one parent to go against the medical orthodoxy can lead to the disempowerment of their own child and of others who are exposed to diseases. Thus, individual immunization decisions can have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant unintended social consequences.

## 9.2. Contribution

This paper is well located in the critical tradition. It corresponds with Myers and Klein (2011) critical research principles. By drawing on Fairclough’s work we employ core concepts from critical social theory. Our value position is that emancipation is desirable and we have revealed established practices in online health discourses. We have contributed to the potential of individual emancipation, which may likely translate into societal improvements.

To highlight our research contribution in more detail, we distinguish between methodological, theoretical and practical contributions. First, this study provides a novel methodological contribution to IS research. Drawing on Fairclough’s CDA methodology and building on prior engagement research, we present a holistic explanation of the four types of engagement discourse in online health communities: cognitive, emotional, behavioural and political engagement. The CDA advanced our understanding of the drivers and obstacles to emancipation that occur when engagement discourse occurs online. The CDA analysis also illuminated the di<sup>f</sup>erent “styles” or authorial identities that members assumed as they participated in the online discourse and, subsequently, the power structures that emerged.

Second, this study contributes to the engagement literature that reports on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of engagement and factors associated with engagement in health care with empirical evidence. Patient engagement is found through actions to monitor their health conditions of patients who have chronic diseases. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>es the engagement of the growing population of healthy people who consider preventive care health care. Those who get preventive health care services may show more cognitive engagement. Interestingly, this study found political engagement, possibly due to the controversial characteristics of the subject. This study also reveals both positive and negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of engagement in the use of online health communities. Prior literature focused primarily on the positive aspects, such as the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ect on health outcomes (Laurance et al., 2014) and quality of health care service (Roseman et al., 2013). The online health care community is a necessary system to empower those who consider treatments or preventive care, but it can also disempower them. For example, health information accumulated in the online community is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an enabler to empower members, at the same time incorrect information spread in the community disempower them. Emotional support is one of the main activities that occur in the online health community, but fear mongering through emotional engagement discourse is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an obstacle. The engagement in online discussion groups can help participants develop a better and more sophisticated understanding of their options and make more informed decisions. Such drivers and obstacles identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to complement the small but growing literature on engagement in health care. We also complement the sensemaking literature by showing that to overcome these obstacles, a dialectical-relational process of sensemaking occurs. Engagement in the online health communities involves sensemaking and in turn, sensemaking shapes online engagement, hegemonic structures and the potential for emancipation.

Third, consistent with our emancipatory purpose, we initiated the community members’ emancipation by leading them to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the engagement discourses’ consequences. These participants’ recommendations for members’ emancipation con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from phase 1’s content analysis and supplemented our understanding of engagement and emancipation in the health care context. The recommendations are practical contributions. Not only online community system designers but also its members can get insights on how the systems are designed and how their contents are utilized to increase parent’s empowerment to make their own health care decisions.

## 9.3. Next steps

We demonstrated that online engagement in healthrelated discussions, notably in the area of vaccination, can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from taking a critical angle. We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed obstacles and drivers of emancipation and shown how sensemaking modes can contribute to individuals’ emancipation.

We can extend the study in numerous ways. One way would be to use a di<sup>f</sup>erent methodology. Beyond Fairclough’s approach, there are other ways of conducting CDA that could be applied to the data we collected. In addition, there are other critical methodologies that do not rely on discourse analysis, such as participative action research, which could create di<sup>f</sup>erent insights.

In this study, we have used a rather generic theoretical approach which was informed by Habermas and Foucault’s positions, but which relied heavily on the theoretical implications of Fairclough’s methodology. In light of the <sup>fi</sup>nding that truth remains contested and is a key determinant of emancipatory practice and its evaluation, one could employ Foucault’s concept of regime of truths to better understand how these regimes are established and stabilized. A Marxist perspective, on the other hand, could focus on the socio-economic environment’s role and on the impact of the capitalist organization of online forums on the possibility of emancipation. One could, moreover, move beyond the vaccination debate and look at health discussion sites more broadly to understand the role of these IS in facilitating or limiting emancipation.

Finally, our study has highlighted a key issue of critical research, namely the nature of emancipation and the problem of observing and evaluating it. This is not a new issue in critical research, but our study has highlighted the complexity of the relationship between individual and collective emancipation. A related but separate issue is the tension between self-described and self-perceived emancipation of the individual and the determination of emancipation from an external perspective. These are not questions to which we expect to <sup>fi</sup>nd simple answers, but they need to be considered in future critical research.

While there are numerous ways of extending the study, we do believe that we have succeeded in showing the relevance of the critical approach when understanding online health communities. By providing empirical insights, methodological developments and theoretical ideas, we believe we have made an important contribution to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Appendix A2 Email Interview Protocol

(1) What are the risks of participating in this online health community? What have you done to deal with these risks?

(2) What are your recommendations to online community members on how to handle these risks?

(3) Has your participation in the online health community changed your relationship with your physician? If so, how? Why do you think this happened?

(4) How does participating in the online health care community empower you to make immunization choices?

(5) Please make recommendations to online community members on how they should participate in the community in order to empower themselves to make decisions about immunization.

(6) Please share some of your favourite moments in this online community.
