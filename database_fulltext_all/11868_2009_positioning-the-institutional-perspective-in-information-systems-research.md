## Abstract

In recent years, a number of studies have adopted institutional theory as a perspective for examining Information Systems (IS)/Information Technology (IT)-related phenomena such as IT innovation, IS development and implementation, and IT adoption and use. The objective of this paper is to take stock of how institutional theory is being used in IS research. To this end, it first proposes a conceptual framework to encapsulate the main concepts of institutional theory. Second, it synthesizes the findings of 53 articles that adopted an institutional perspective to study IS/IT phenomena. Finally, it identifies conceptual and methodological issues that researchers need to address when adopting an institutional perspective.

## Introduction

T <sup>he</sup> <sup>idea</sup> <sup>that</sup> <sup>managerial</sup> <sup>decisions</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>actions</sup> <sup>are</sup>aimed at maximizing efficiency and effectiveness is a aimed at maximizing efficiency and effectiveness is a commonly held assumption in IS research (Avgerou, 2000; Tingling and Parent, 2002; Teo et al., 2003). Indeed, most IS studies adopt the view that managers behave as efficiency-seeking decision makers, and that they assess the costs and benefits of various decision alternatives before selecting those that will maximize efficiency and effectiveness. Some researchers have adopted an alternative perspective, under which one cannot explain everything that happens in organizations by considering only the rational actions of managers, but rather one must find a means for taking into account the ‘irrationalities’ arising within the institutional context that surrounds organizational actors (Avgerou, 2000; Orlikowski and Barley, 2001; Teo et al., 2003). This is the institutional perspective, under which actors ‘accept and follow social norms unquestioningly, without any real reflection’ (Tolbert and Zucker, 1996: 176) and seek legitimacy more than efficiency.

In an IS context, institutional analysis has been said to have the potential to help researchers understand ‘how institutions influence the design, use, and consequences of technologies, either within or across organizations’ (Orlikowski and Barley, 2001: 153). In recent years, a number of studies have adopted an institutionalist perspective to examine IS/IT-related phenomena such as IT innovation, IS development and implementation, and IT adoption and use. The objective of this paper is to take stock of this research, by first summarizing what we have learned from these studies, then by assessing how closely IT researchers have followed the precepts of institutional theory, and, finally, by suggesting avenues through which IT research can adopt an institutional perspective.

The paper first presents the foundations of institutionalism and proposes a conceptual framework of institutional effects. It then synthesizes the contributions of IT studies that have adopted an institutional perspective and examined such effects. Finally, it identifies some conceptual and methodological issues that researchers need to address when applying institutional theory, and suggests avenues for future research.

## Institutional effects and institutionalization

In the institutional perspective, ‘organizations are suspended in a web of values, norms, beliefs, and taken-forgranted assumptions’ (Barley and Tolbert, 1997: 93) that guide and constrain their actions over time. These values, norms, beliefs and assumptions arise from the existence of institutions. An institution is a social structure that gives organizations or individuals lines of action or orientations, yet at the same time controls and constrains them (Scott,

2008). Institutions then ‘represent constraints on the options that individuals and collectives are likely to exercise, albeit constraints that are open to modification over time’ (Barley and Tolbert, 1997: 94). Contracts, the formal organization, insurance, and the corporation are commonly acknowledged as institutions (Jepperson, 1991).

The central underlying assumption of institutional theory is that organizations and organizational actors seek to gain legitimacy in their environments in order to be accepted and thus ensure their long-term survival (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). Legitimacy is often considered the core concept in institutional theory (Barley, 2008: 506). Institutionalists use the term ‘institutional field’ when referring to the environment within which legitimacy must be gained, repaired, or maintained (Suchman, 1995). At the organizational level, it is defined as ‘those organizations that, in the aggregate, constitute a recognized area of institutional life: key suppliers, resource and product consumers, regulatory agencies, and other organizations that produce similar services or products’ (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983: 148). Germane to the notion of legitimacy are the concepts of status and reputation that have recently received much attention (Deephouse and Suchman, 2008).

Within its institutional field, a given organization can be seen as connected to or similar to other organizations (Scott, 2008). Connectedness refers to the existence of exchange relations or communications between organizations (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Similarity, which is also labeled structural equivalence, refers to organizations that have the same position in the field structure and thus ‘are proximate to the extent that they have the same pattern of relations with occupants of other positions’ (Burt, 1987: 1291). Depending on the context, the focal organization may then be more influenced by the behaviour of similar organizations or by those with which it has contacts (Scott, 2008).

Two kinds of processes are examined in institution theory: institutional effects (Jepperson, 1991) and institutionalization (Devereaux Jennings and Greenwood,

2003). Institutional effects pertain to processes in which institutions affect other institutions, organizations or organizational entities (Jepperson, 1991). In such processes, an institution is taken as the independent variable. The process of institutionalization, on the other hand, refers to the stages in the formation of an institution (Devereaux Jennings and Greenwood, 2003). In such processes, the institution is the object of analysis. Each of these processes will now be examined in turn.

## Institutional effects

Figure 1 presents a conceptual framework depicting institutional effects. Institutions exert three kinds of institutional pressures on organizations and organizational actors: coercive, normative and mimetic pressures (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Coercive pressures arise from the legal environment of the organization and through the existence of standards, which can be imposed by structures on which the focal organization is dependent (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Normative pressures are brought about by professionalization: inter-organizational networks, similar educational backgrounds and mimetic behaviours in a profession (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Mimetic pressures often appear at times of uncertainty, when firms will tend to model themselves on other organizations in their fields that are perceived to be more legitimate or successful (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Organizational models can be diffused through employee migration or by consulting firms. In order to maintain the normative characteristics endorsed in their institutional field, organizations tend toward isomorphism: that is, a homogeneity of structures observed in several fields (for instance, hospitals and universities are organizations that have very similar structures). Indeed, ‘organizations that share the same environment tend to take on similar forms as efficiency-seeking organizations seek the optimal ‘‘fit’’ with their environment’ (Boxenbaum and Jonsson, 2008: 78).

Figure 1 A conceptual framework of institutional effects.

Devereaux Jennings and Greenwood (2003) suggest that the notion of institutional pressures is akin to the concept of institutional pillars proposed by Scott (1995, 2001, 2008). According to Scott, there are three institutional pillars – regulative (coercive), normative and cultural-cognitive – representing the analytical components of institutions. These pillars operate in combination, yet through distinct mechanisms. In the regulative pillar, coercion (tests of strength and fear of sanctions) explains how institutions constrain and regularize the behaviour of actors. The normative pillar is based on agents’ social obligations, which are observable through values and norms. The cultural-cognitive pillar is characterized by imitation: in a context of uncertainty, organizations will tend to copy other organizations they consider to be leaders or models. In summary, coercive, mimetic and normative pressures are control mechanisms exerted by regulative, cognitive and normative structures on organizations in order to constrain their behaviour (Haggerty and Golden, 2002).

Institutional pressures can be exerted upon organizations or sub-organizations (see Figure 1). Fligstein (1985) studied how institutional pressures (mostly mimetic) exerted on organizations since the beginning of the 20th century drove them to adopt what is called a multidivisional structure (M-form). An example of the sub-organization level of study is given by Shepsle and Weingast (1982), who examined political committees. They show that majority rule does not, on its own, explain the constitution of political committees. Instead, a complex interplay of institutional pressures exerted on political committees explains their formation and composition. Finally, at the individual level, institutional pressures have been shown to influence managers’ mental models of competition (Daniels et al., 2002).

Under institutional pressures, organizations will implement strategies in order to gain, maintain or repair their legitimacy (Suchman, 1995). As shown in Figure 1, acquiescence is one of a number of strategies for managing legitimacy. It has been suggested that organizations may adopt other strategies in response to institutional pressures (Oliver, 1991; Suchman, 1995). Oliver, taking an agentic perspective (Boxenbaum and Jonsson, 2008), suggests that firms do not always comply, and may engage in some form of defensive action. She formulates a typology of strategic responses that organizations enact as a result of institutional pressures toward conformity. The strategies proposed by Oliver are: acquiescence, compromise, avoidance, defiance and manipulation. These strategies are exerted through tactics. The examples of tactics given here are those provided by Oliver (1991). The strategy of acquiescence, which is an organization’s conscious intent to conform for self-serving reasons, is expressed through the tactics of habit, imitation and compliance (Oliver, 1991). Organizations are using a habit tactic when they follow invisible, taken-for-granted norms. Imitation occurs when organizations imitate institutional models. Compliance means obeying the laws and accepting norms. When organizations use compromise strategies, they promote their own interests through tactics such as balancing, pacifying and bargaining (Oliver, 1991). Balancing occurs when an organization combines the expectations of multiple constituents. Pacifying is a tactic that consists in placating and accommodating several institutional influences. The last compromise tactic, bargaining, implies negotiating with institutional stakeholders. The third strategy, avoidance, is an attempt to circumvent the need to conform to an external pressure (Oliver, 1991). Avoidance tactics include concealing (disguising nonconformity), buffering (when an organization loosens institutional attachments) and escaping (changing goals, activities or domain, for instance). Defiance is the rejection of institutional norms (Oliver, 1991). Defiance tactics include dismissing, challenging and attacking. The tactic of dismissing may imply ignoring norms and explicit values. Organizations may also challenge existing rules and requirements, or even attack the sources of institutional pressures. Lastly, manipulation is the purposeful and opportunistic application of the tactics of co-opting, influencing, or controlling an institutional pressure (Oliver, 1991). Co-opting means importing new influential constituents. Influence consists in shaping values and criteria. Finally, when organizations use controlling tactics, they try to dominate institutional constituents and processes.

While Oliver states that acquiescence ‘is more likely to occur when the degree of legitimacy attainable from conformity is high’ (1991: 159), it has been argued the strategies she proposes ‘clearly fall along a continuum of strategies for gaining legitimacy’ (Suchman, 1995). The five general strategies proposed by Oliver help explain how an organization will, under institutional pressures, manage its legitimacy in its field. Likewise, sub-organizations, under institutional pressures, may use the same strategies and tactics to manage their legitimacy in the organiza tion. Interestingly, the response to institutional pressures may come not only from a single organization, but from multiple organizations, which articulate a concerted response that has ‘the potential to shape the nature of the demands and even to redefine the rules and logics operating within the field’ (Scott, 2001: 176).

Other, non-agentic, studies also suggest that organizations may respond heterogeneously to institutional pressures, for different reasons: they ‘link organizational responses to environmental contingencies, demonstrating how time, space, and local competition introduce variation in organizational response to isomorphic pressures’ (Boxenbaum and Jonsson, 2008).

## Institutionalization

As illustrated in Figure 2, the institutionalization process begins following jolts in the institutional field that affect existing institutions (Greenwood et al., 2002). These disruptions can be social, technological or legal in nature. The existing consensus in the field is then questioned. New ideas emerge, and the possibility of change now exists; this is the innovation step. New structures, said to be preinstitutionalized, appear in response to existing problems. At this point in time the innovations remain very local, since problems are perceived as specific. During the crucial and central step of theorization, structures are explained, specified and justified. Abstract categories are developed, and cause and effect relationships are elaborated. This theorization exercise brings legitimacy to the new structures. New ideas are aligned with existing norms.

Figure 2 Institutionalization process (based on: Tolbert and Zucker, 1996; Greenwood et al., 2002; Devereaux Jennings and Greenwood, 2003).

Structures, now legitimized, are diffused. A general consensus on their pragmatic value prevails. Structures are said to be fully institutionalized when they are considered taken for granted. Then the institutionalized structure can survive over long periods of time (Greenwood et al., 2002). This institutionalization process may be followed by a deinstitutionalization process (Zucker, 1987). In short, ‘the process of institutionalization is a cycle – institutions emerge, diffuse, change, die, and are replaced by new institutions’ (Haunschild and Chandler, 2008: 630). Figure 2 represents this process. While earlier conceptualizations of the institutional process ‘highlighted the overwhelming, deterministic power of the institutional order over anyone working over it’ (Zilber, 2008: 157), recent studies have begun to view the institutional process as political, where the key features are the power held by various actors and the politics between them. These studies ‘picked up the emphasis and showed how power and power relations are articulated through meaning’ (Zilber, 2008: 157).

## Methodology

A series of IS studies that adopted the institutional perspective were selected from management and IT publication outlets. We used three criteria to select the journals. First, because our main audience is IS, we included the list of eight journals suggested by the Association of Information Systems (AIS) IS Scholars basket (European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Information Systems Research, Journal of AIS, Journal of MIS, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology). Second, from the AIS MIS journal ranking list, we selected journals relevant to our topic and audience (Information Technology & People, Information and Management, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions of Engineering Management, Scandinavian Journal of IS, Information and Organization, Syste\`mes d’Information et Management, Journal of Global Information Technology Management). Third, we included management journals that publish IS studies (Academy of

Management Journal, Organization Science, Organization Studies, Administrative Science Quarterly, Journal of Management Studies, Organization). We ensured that our selection of journals was aligned with the North-American and European IS research communities (Robey et al., 2008). This led to searches in 20 publication outlets (full text when available, abstract otherwise). As suggested by Webster and Watson (2002), this set of top publications was then completed by other publication outlets that were identified by ‘going backward’: that is, reviewing citations from the articles previously identified. This resulted in a set of 53 documents (listed in Table 1) in which institutional theory played a central role.

The documents were first sorted according to the process on which they focused: either institutional effects or institutionalization. A third group of studies emerged through this process: those on the interaction between IT and institutions. Papers of a more general nature were not included in our sample but were identified and used in our discussion.

The articles were first classified and coded separately by each co-author. The results were compared and discrepancies resolved. Each article was then coded along the dimensions of our conceptual frameworks (see Figures 1 and 2) and the sub-process(es) of the innovation process. Several frameworks have been used to describe the process of IT innovation (Kwon and Zmud, 1987; Cooper and Zmud, 1990; Swanson and Ramiller, 2004). Among these, the framework proposed by Swanson and Ramiller (2004) is particularly well suited to the present study because it is based on an institutional perspective. Indeed, it acknowledges that IT innovation takes place both at the organizational level and at the level of the institutional field. For Swanson and Ramiller (2004), organizational innovation comprises four component processes: comprehension, adoption, implementation and assimilation. In the adoption phase, organizational know-why is central; the business value and the challenge of the prospective change are weighed before the firm decides to implement the innovation. ## Institutional effects and institutionalization in IS research

As reported in Table 1, the object of study of each of the 53 documents in our database falls into one of three broad themes. Note that the three themes are not mutually exclusive, since two articles report on studies that covered two themes (Klein and Hirschheim, 1989; Avgerou, 2000). The first theme is that of institutional effects. The 36 papers falling under this theme report on studies that examined the impact of institutional pressures on the diffusion of IT innovations, be they software applications such as ERPs (Benders et al., 2006), web-based applications (Lewis et al., 2003) and electronic-trading systems (Khalifa and Davison, 2006), or management practices such as outsourcing (Miranda and Kim, 2006) and software project management practices (Gopal et al., 2005). Institutional effects were examined within all four phases of the process of IT innovation. The second theme is that of the institutionalization process. Of the 10 articles on this theme, four examined the institutionalization of software applications such as CRM systems – for example, Wang and Swanson (2008) – and telemedicine systems – for example, Miscione (2007) – while six focused on the institutionalization of management practices, such as security standards (Backhouse et al., 2006) and application service provision (Currie, 2004). A third theme emerged from the analysis of the 53 documents, that of the interaction between the IT artefact and existing institutions. The nine articles under this theme typically studied how an IT interacts with existing institutions and the consequences of this interaction.

We now turn to each of the three themes and synthesize what researchers found.

## Theme 1 - institutional effects in IT innovation

The studies related to institutional effects in IT innovation are presented in Table 2, which indicates the innovation process phase and the level of analysis associated with each study.

Thirty-six articles – 31 of which are empirical studies – examined the effect of institutional pressures during one or several of the four phases of the innovation process. Although the vast majority of the 36 articles address the organizational level of analysis, a small number focus on other levels, including nation, industry, group and individual. We examined each article in terms of the elements identified in the conceptual framework of institutional effects (Figure 1): entities from which pressures arise, entities on which pressures are exerted, entities’ responses to such pressures, legitimation strategy, level of analysis and study conclusions. The results of this analysis have been summarized for each of the four phases of the process of IT innovation: comprehension (Table 3), adoption (Table 4), implementation (Table 5) and assimilation (Table 6).

## (a) Components of the conceptual framework

Entities from which pressures arise: The studies vary considerably with respect to choice of entities exerting institutional pressures. Government and regulatory agencies, customers, a parent firm and dominant firms are seen as the key sources of coercive pressures. Industry, business trade associations, training schools, professional associations, and normative structures are depicted as the main sources of normative pressures. Finally, peers, competitors (especially successful competitors) and best practices are seen as exerting mimetic pressures.

Entities on which pressures are exerted: Most of the studies that address this theme examine the effect of institutional pressures on organizations. This is true for all four phases: comprehension, adoption, implementation and assimilation. A few studies, however, are interested in the effect of institutional pressures on other types of entities: groups (project teams) and individuals (managers) (Tingling and Parent, 2002; Lewis et al., 2003).

Legitimation strategy: The vast majority of the studies on institutional effects addressed the issue of whether or not entities on which institutional pressures are exerted indeed comply, and most observed acquiescence through a compliance tactic as the response to institutional pressures. The results of these studies provide support for the original hypothesis of acquiescence to institutional pressures.

A handful of studies attempted to broaden the understanding of institutional effects in an IS setting. Ayres (2003), for instance, examined the ceremonial and faithful versions of acquiescence. Other researchers analysed situations that involved responses other than acquiescence. For example, Standing et al. (2009) found that government agencies respond to coercive pressures from the central government with either acquiescence, defiance, avoidance or compromise, and Noir and Walsham (2007) report on the ceremonial use of IT as a means of buffering (an avoidance tactic) in order to ‘ward off genuine evaluation and inspection’ (p. 323).

(b) Institutional effects during the process of IT innovation Comprehension (see Table 3): During the comprehension phase, ‘through the sensemaking efforts of its members, the firm engages the organizing vision in substantive terms and ponders the signals about its importance embedded in the broader community’s reaction to it’ (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004: 557). The comprehension phase precedes the decision to adopt. The intention to adopt is thus part of the comprehension phase.

Three studies are particularly informative on the relative effect of institutional pressures during the comprehension phase of the innovation process, as they examined the distinct effect of each of the three types of pressures. Teo et al. (2003) studied the decision to adopt EDI, and found that the strongest pressure was the normative pressure represented by the extent of adoption within the institutional field (by customers and suppliers), followed by coercive pressure from a parent organization, dominant customers and dominant suppliers, and, finally, the mimetic pressures originating in adoption by competitors and the success of such adoptions. Using the measures of institutional pressures developed by Teo et al. (2003), Son and Benbasat (2007) found that all mimetic and normative pressures had a significant effect on firms’ intentions to adopt B2B electronic marketplaces. Studying small and medium enterprises’ decisions to adopt electronic trading systems (ETS), Khalifa and Davison (2006) found that coercive pressures from dominant customers were the strongest driver – among institutional pressures – of the intention to adopt ETS, followed by mimetic pressures – competitors – and normative pressures operationalized as employee beliefs.

Of particular interest among the studies that focused on a subset of institutional pressures is that of Standing et al. (2009), which examined not only acquiescence to coercive pressures but also non-conformity in the institutional environment. Their findings suggest that defiance as a strategic response can be exerted when the parties are loosely connected. Interesting insights are provided by Tingling and Parent’s (2002) experiment on the effect of mimetic pressures on managers’ decisions to adopt IT. This study confirmed the strength of mimetism in influencing management decisions regarding IT, in that the participating decision maker mimicked other firms even when the choice was contrary to extensive evaluations by their own staff.

Adoption (see Table 4): In the adoption phase, the participants in the organization deliberate about ‘knowwhy’ issues related to the adopted innovation (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004).

Two case studies examined the effect of all three types of institutional pressures during this phase of the innovation process. In a multiple-case study of the adoption of interorganizational business process standards, Bala and Venkatesh (2007) compared how dominant and nondominant firms complied with institutional pressures. They found that dominant firms were influenced by normative pressures and non-dominant firms were affected by all three types of pressures. Benders et al. (2006) report on the implementation of an ERP, where they found that mimetic (influence of competitors), normative (industry norms) and coercive (important clients and headquarters) pressures influence decision makers in their software selection process.

Two studies focused on a subset of the institutional pressures to study adoption at a national level. In their study of the adoption of IT in Chile, Silva and Figueroa (2002) examine how institutional interventions as defined by King et al. (1994) can affect the adoption of IT in developing countries. They found that governmental interventions (coercive pressures) such as subsidies and barrier reduction, investments in education, and the implementation of facilitating regulations and norms can exercise some influence over the adoption of IT within a nation. Swan et al. (1999, 2000) found different patterns in the adoption of computer-aided production management across countries that were explained by the influence of different national professional associations (normative forces).

Implementation (see Table 5): Swanson and Ramiller (2004) define implementation of an IT innovation as bringing the innovation to productive life for its users.

Some authors have argued that all three types of pressures are effective during the implementation phase. In a discussion of social control in IS development, Nicolaou (Nicolaou, 1999) posits that organizations are expected to ‘conform to social constraints to develop IS consistent with expectations in order to demonstrate legitimacy in operational and managerial decision making (Nicolaou, 1999: 133). In similar fashion, Gosain (2004) proposes that organizations from highly regulated domains will tend to have enterprise information systems (EIS) with similar mandated configurations, and that the tendency will be stronger when they are dependent upon organizations that expect compliance for resource allocation and legitimacy. Regarding the implementation process, Gosain (2004) proposes that organizations will tend to use EIS configurations that reflect the current vision held by the IS profession, particularly when they are embedded in mature networks diffusing specialized practices. Organizations will also tend to mimic the EIS configurations used by large and supposedly successful organizations, especially when they are confronted with uncertainty and goal ambiguity when making configuration-related decisions (Gosain, 2004). This in turn could lead the organization structure to isomorphism (Nicolaou, 1999): that is, there is a risk that organizations will share similarities in their organizational structure if configurable technologies are configured in the same way. Swan et al. (1999, 2000) describe the role of these professional circles, which they call ‘central agencies’, that promote best practices (in the case of implementations of Computer Aided Production Management (CAPM)). The production and design of these CAPM technologies are socially shaped through an institutional carrier: interorganizational networks (Swan et al., 2000). Swan et al. (2000) also studied mimetic pressures stemming from professional associations. They propose that many practitioners join professional associations to benefit from the latest ‘best practices’ in their domain. By copying best practices, organizations mimic other organizations. Interestingly, professional associations’ influence on the diffusion of an IT innovation was found to differ in the UK and Sweden, where they had less influence (Swan et al., 1999). Mimetic pressures, arising from an institution of the same type, may then differ significantly for the same technology in different countries (Swan et al., 1999) due to the differen roles played by professional associations in culturally distinct jurisdictions.

Assimilation (see Table 6): Swanson and Ramiller (2004) define assimilation as the phase in which the IT innovation begins to be absorbed into the worklife of the firm and demonstrate its usefulness.

In this context, top management was portrayed as exercising normative pressures on organizational actors (Chatterjee et al., 2002). Top management is considered an institution, since it provides managers of departments and business units with institutional norms and values by offering them visions and guidelines that reassure them about the legitimacy of their assimilation action (Purvis et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002). Top management is said to exert normative pressures through three carriers: (1) championship, which was found to have a significant and major impact on the effective assimilation of an innovation in a firm (Chatterjee et al., 2002); (2) the strategic investment rationale (more specifically, an authority system), through which ‘by stating explicit investment criteria, senior management can influence the prevailing structures of legitimation and domination’ (Chatterjee et al., 2002: 71); and (3) the extent of coordination. Top management and local management commitments were also studied by Lewis et al. (2003) as institutional factors in the assimilation (use) by faculty members of the Internet in their teaching activities. The results suggest that top management commitment has a positive significant influ ence on beliefs about the technology, while local management commitment has a positive significant influence on attitudes.

Assimilation of outsourcing as a practice was also studied with the institutional lens. Coercive pressures were found to play a role in their assimilation. In the US banking industry, federal regulators set countless rules and regulations and regularly conduct audits in order to ensure that the assets of bank customers are secure (Ang and Cummings, 1997). Federal regulators thus exert coercive pressures. The carriers of such pressures appear to be laws (a symbolic system) and protocols/standards/operating procedures (routines). Not all types of organizations react to coercive pressures in the same way. While all banks (large or small) were found to comply with the State’s influence, technological uncertainty was found to reinforce conformity in large banks (Ang and Cummings, 1997). These authors also studied the influence of business and professional circles’ normative pressures on IT outsourcing decisions, and found that different organizations react differently. Large banks and small ones with enough financial capacity to resist were found to be able to temper these pressures.

In a study of IS outsourcing in US municipal governments, Miranda and Kim (2006) found that coercive pressures (i.e., regulatory structures such as opposition from elected officials, employees and department heads), normative pressures (i.e., normative structures such as council managers and council-mayor forms) and mimetic pressures (i.e., cognitive structures, such as, under conditions of uncertainty, investigating other governments’ IS decisions or attending conferences or professional meetings on IS management) mitigated the logic of transaction cost economics (TCE): ‘the institutional context appears more important in moderating the effects of human frailty conditions (i.e., opportunism and bounded rationality) and of frequency, than of fundamental situational condi tions (i.e., asset specificity and uncertainty)’ (Miranda and Kim, 2006: 746).

In their comparison of dominant and non-dominant firms, Bala and Venkatesh (2007) found that dominant firms with general deployment of interorganizational business process standards were influenced by normative pressures, while non-dominant firms were influenced by all three types of institutional pressures. In situations where the standards had not been assimilated by the firms, they found that no institutional pressures had been exerted on the organizations. While Son and Benbasat (2007) found that mimetic and normative pressures influenced the intention to adopt B2B electronic marketplaces, they did not find any relationship between institutional pressures and extent of assimilation, once a firm had decided to adopt the technology.

## Theme 2 - institutionalization and IS research

Ten studies describing the institutionalization process of a practice or system were identified. As illustrated in the figures represented in Table 7, most of these studies are at the industry level. Three of them describe an institutionalization process in its entirety (Klein and Hirschheim, 1989; Avgerou, 2000; Wang and Swanson, 2007) and two studies concentrate on one part of the process (Currie, 2004; Backhouse et al., 2006). Two studies describe the interrelationship between two institutionalization processes (Cousins and Robey, 2005; Miscione, 2007). Finally, four studies focus on the role of an organizing vision in the process (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997; Ramiller and Swanson, 2003; Currie, 2004; Mark, 2007; Wang and Swanson, 2008).

The studies that described IT institutionalization processes adopted quite different approaches. First, the objects of institutionalization were quite different: standards (Backhouse et al., 2006), IS development practices (Klein and Hirschheim, 1989), a specific IT innovation (Currie, 2004; Wang and Swanson, 2007), and IT innovation in general (Avgerou, 2000). Avgerou (2000) described the institutionalization of an IS system in an organization, whereas Klein and Hirschheim (1989) concentrated on the forces at play throughout the legitimation process, and Wang and Swanson (2007) analysed the role of institutional entrepreneurs throughout the institutionalization process of an IT innovation (professional service automation). Interestingly, Currie’s (2004) study of an application service provision suggests that full institutionalization could never be attained because the organizing vision was insufficiently developed at an early stage in the institutionalization process.

Two institutional processes sometimes interrelate. Cousins and Robey (2005) examined the structure and business models of electronic metal exchanges by studying the exchanges’ responses to competing pressures for efficiency and legitimacy from 1995 to 2003. Here, the institutions under study are not organizations, but rather the institutional context: the technical, social and cultural demands of the environment on the organizations. Miscione (2007) studied the institutionalization of telemedicine and the interplay between different institutions – local medicine vs telemedicine-enhanced practices.

The notion of an organizing vision was first introduced by Swanson and Ramiller (1997). They suggest that, by creating and using an organizing vision for an IS innovation in the early stages of its diffusion, a community may ensure its later adoption. Organizing visions facilitate three key activities in the process of innovation: interpretation, legitimization and mobilization. Organizing visions are typically produced through the community discourse. Ramiller and Swanson (2003) also explored the responses of IS executives to organizing visions, and suggest that IS executives are specifically interested in organizing visions’ interpretability, plausibility, importance and discontinuity. The notion of organizing vision was also used by Wang and Swanson to explain the diffusion of CRM (Wang and Swanson, 2008).

## Theme 3 - interaction between IT and institutions

Two conclusions can be drawn from these studies. At the beginning of the process, some forces seem to oppose each other (Cousins and Robey, 2005; Miscione, 2007). For these practices to be institutionalized, there needs to be an organizing vision that is developed very early and that is present throughout the process. In the case of the study by Cousins and Robey (2005), we may hypothesize that institutionalization did not occur, because there was no organizing vision. This was the case in Currie’s study (2004).

A third group of studies emerged from our analysis of the IS institutional literature: nine studies that focus on the interaction between IT and an institution. This stream of research originated in the idea that a system will encounter great implementation difficulties when it runs counter to existing institutions, whereas it will be greatly facilitated when the IT implementation is congruent with existing institutions (Christiaanse and Huigen, 1997). Indeed, in the context of healthcare, it has been shown that a lack of alignment between institutional arrangements and a medical innovation emerged as a barrier to further adoption of the innovation (Cho and Mathiassen, 2007). For instance, when the medical innovation was not aligned with relevant laws and regulations, was not receiving attention and funding from state governments and, being the first of its kind, lacked standards, further institutionalization and diffusion of the innovation was impeded by a lack of major efforts in early stages and by a lack of potentially influential stakeholders.

The nature of possible misalignments (between package functionality and organizational requirements) were studied in the specific case of ERP software (Sia and Soh, 2007). Sia and Soh derive four types of misalignments of varying degrees of severity (they combine imposed or voluntary acquired institutional structures and deep or surface ontological structures) that predict when a package should be customized to fit the organizational constraints vs when the organization should change to fit the software. Indeed, imposed-deep misalignments were found to be resolved through customization; imposed-surface and voluntary-deep misalignments could be resolved through organizational adaptation; and voluntary-surface misalignment can be resolved through organizational adaptation.

Other studies see IS as consequential from the point of view of institutional reform (Avgerou, 2000; Mangan and Kelly, 2009). Avgerou (2000) shows that there can be two interrelated institutionalization processes at stake simultaneously: the gradual development of IT and the efforts to achieve a new organizational structure. None of these two institutionalization processes can be subsumed within the other: ‘when the objective organizational change is of ambiguous validity, both planned and improvised efforts to harness IT for organizational change are likely to be erratic. Yet, IT innovation tends to intensify rather than being held accountable for organization transformation results (Avgerou, 2000: 259). In their study, Mangan and Kelly (2009) argue that the IT implementation project inadvertently ended up playing a major role in processes of institutional change because the change process implied such engagement (in terms of scope and intensity) that key tensions finally crystallized and triggered some major interventions. Organizational change is therefore truly embedded in the system being implemented. Projects can be a meeting place where, through processes of standardization and scaling, formal institutions and informal constraints end up being institutionalized in work practices through the IT implementation (Mekonnen and Sahay, 2008).

Contradictions may, however, arise when an institution is embedded in a system (Currie and Guah, 2007). Currie and Guah studied the highly institutionalized organizational field of healthcare and showed that it is infused with conflicting institutional logics that both fuel and inhibit the adoption of IT. A gap between the institutional logics embedded in a system and the institutional logics of the receiving organization will work against system diffusion.

Finally, a department (conceptualized as an institution) has been shown to be a potential barrier to IT implementation (Barca and Cordella, 2006): habits may act as institutionalized barriers when the organization attempts to introduce change. The introduction of new arrangements may be impeded when organizational components (in this instance, a department) are seen as institutions and are taken for granted.

## Issues and avenues for research

Our synthesis reveals some important points about institutional research in IT. The IT phenomena under study are varied and relate to most of the topics of interest in the IT literature: aspects of IT innovation, adoption, implementation and assimilation are covered, for a broad array of technologies. The articles present a wide variety of sources of institutional pressures, including governments, trading partners, employees, professional associations and cognitive structures. All three types of institutional pressures are present in practically every step reviewed, and several research designs are employed. However, the synthesis also reveals some unresolved issues.

(a) Which units of analysis would help IT institutionalists make new contributions?: While studies at the organizational level of analysis constitute the majority of the studies surveyed, the field level has remained practically unexplored until quite recently. Several recent studies related to standards and standardization were carried out at the field level or employed elements from the field level (Backhouse et al., 2006; Nickerson and Zur Muehlen, 2006). According to Lyytinen and King (2006), IS standardization is generating more and more interest as a research topic in IS, and we should see a growing research agenda on this subject emerge in the next few years. Also, the influence of industry on IS activities may not be receiving enough attention (Chiasson and Davidson, 2005; Nickerson and Zur Muehlen, 2006): most studies examined by Chiasson and Davidson in a literature review of top-tier IS journals did not even mention the industry in which the study was conducted. Finally, there also appears to be room for IT contributions at intermediate levels of analysis (organizational sub-systems) such as groups, departments and processes. Among the studies surveyed, these levels were practically non-existent until quite recently.

(b) There is a need for broader and clearer definitions of institutions in IT: It is not always clear if the institutions under study may truly be considered institutions in terms of institutional theory. Many studies, and especially those classified in the invention/innovation steps, consider only large powerful organizations to be institutions. From the evidence in our literature review, institutions may be more than that. Some studies used other types of institutions. For instance, top management support and top management championship are presented as institutions exerting normative pressures on individuals in the organization (Purvis et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Lewis et $a l . ,$ 2003). This conceptualization is interesting, but these studies provided no actual demonstration that they are indeed institutions. For example, in order to show that they are institutions, one might suggest that they follow an institutionalization process (Tolbert and Zucker, 1996; Greenwood et al., 2002).

(c) All pressures are not institutional pressures: Competitive pressures could be easily confused with institutional pressures. The studies reviewed in the comprehension/ adoption/implementation steps that relied on DiMaggio and Powell (1983) very often conceptualized coercive, normative and mimetic forces. It is not clear, however, that these conceptualizations are rigorous. For instance, by measuring pressures from competitors, we may be taking into account competitive pressures rather than exclusively institutional pressures.

(d) History: Most of the studies reviewed examined IT phenomena using cross-sectional methodologies. Although these studies provide additional support for the key propositions of institutional theory, they do not capture the essence of the theory, where the process of institution creation plays a key role. Recently, however, efforts have been made to conduct more longitudinal and historical designs (Currie, 2004; Cousins and Robey, 2005; Backhouse et al., 2006; Nickerson and Zur Muehlen, 2006; Wang and Swanson, 2007). Again, several studies of IS standardization used an historical approach. In these studies, IS researchers bring new contexts and problems that go beyond the traditional economic and sociological analyses of standard creation and diffusion in the industry (Lyytinen and King, 2006). Longitudinal studies are particularly relevant to the study of institutional forces, since, as we showed in our description of the extant literature, pressures may differ from one phase of the implementation process to another, and these pressures may not have the same effects (in terms of the legitimation strategies used).

(e) Legitimating strategies enacted: Institutionalists have developed a complete range of legitimating strategies that have not yet been taken into account in IT. Oliver (1991) identified a vast array of such strategies (acquiescence, compromise, avoidance, defiance and manipulation) and tactics, and even today they are under-utilized. Indeed, very few of the 53 articles reviewed studied strategies other than acquiescence. IT implementation and adoption are research areas where strategies such as compromise, manipulation and avoidance very likely exist. Interestingly, the same type of comment was recently made in relation to the use of the concept of legitimation in Organizational Institutionalism. As stated by Deephouse and Suchman (2008: 69) ‘More than a decade after Suchman’s 1995 review, we still find that most treatments cover only a limited aspect of this complex but crucial subject. A more adequate formulation would contain careful, widely-accepted definitions, would examine more aspects of the concept, and would incorporate both strategic and institutional views’. This could be an avenue for IT institutionalists to make a contribution that would benefit organization theory as a whole.

(f) Institutional determinism and the political view of the institutionalization process: Zilber (2008) recently observed that, in the study of organizational institutionalism as a whole, while earlier conceptualizations of the institutional process focused on the ‘overwhelming deterministic power of the institutional order on anything working over it’ (p. 257), recent studies have been slowly shifting toward a political view of the institutional process that includes key components such as power, politics and various actors. A parallel pattern is also occurring in IT studies of institutional phenomena, where power and politics are key, classic phenomena that future research could examine through an institutional lens.

(g) New trends of research suggested by the latest publications: Institutional theory has been used in IT for many years. The first studies in this review date back to the late 1980s and early 1990s. However, it is only in the last few years that the IT literature seems to have truly taken stock of institutionalism and produced a prolific number of studies. A few comments can be made about these publications. While most have traditionally focused on the identification and measurement of the institutional pressures that lead to IT adoption and implementation, several studies, concentrated in the last few years, have examined the institutionalization process as a whole (Avgerou, 2000; Ramiller and Swanson, 2003; Currie, 2004; Backhouse et al., 2006; Miscione, 2007; Wang and Swanson, 2007, 2008), and a new set of studies has been identified that focuses on interactions between IT and institutions (Avgerou, 2000; Barca and Cordella, 2006; Cho and Mathiassen, 2007; Currie and Guah, 2007; Sia and Soh, 2007; Mekonnen and Sahay, 2008; Mangan and Kelly, 2009). Many of the new publications were published in Europe or were written by European scholars. This vein of IT institutionalism is particularly rich, because it is based on interesting and original conceptualizations of the phenomena under study (Swan et al., 1999, 2000; Avgerou, 2000, 2002; Currie, 2004; Cho and Mathiassen, 2007; Currie and Guah, 2007).

## Conclusion

We undertook this study to better understand how institutional theory has helped explain IS phenomena and to determine the most promising research avenues in terms of contributing to the advancement of knowledge. To this end, we first reviewed the key concepts and precepts of institutional theory and proposed a conceptual framework of institutional effects. We conducted a search of the IS literature and identified 53 studies identifying institutional theory as a foundation. We analysed each article to extract the essence of its contribution and synthesized the key research findings. Finally, reflecting upon our analysis, we identified some conceptual and methodological issues and proposed research avenues.

The selected articles fell under three main themes: institutional effects (36 articles), the process of institutionalization (10 articles) and the interaction between an IT and one or several institutions (9 articles). Two articles pertain to two themes, for a total of 53 articles.

Our detailed analysis of the 36 studies of institutional effects reveals that the influence of institutional pressures has been studied at each step of the innovation process, and that all three types of institutional pressures are at play during all four phases, yet with different levels of strength and significance. Although these studies contribute to the advancement of knowledge by providing additional support for the key propositions of institutional theory, we contend that there are avenues of research that may yield richer additional knowledge. One such avenue would be to adopt a longitudinal approach and compare institutional effects over the course of an IT innovation process. Indeed, some authors have examined those effects over more than one phase, and their results strongly suggest that this is a promising path (Bala and Venkatesh, 2007; Son and Benbasat, 2007; Standing et al., 2009). A second avenue is to investigate situations where the targets of institutional pressures enact responses other than the acquiescence strategy. The findings of Ayres (2003), Standing et al. (2009) and Noir and Walsham (2007) suggest that there is much to learn in this area. The 10 studies that analysed the institutionalization process of IT practices or IT artefacts reveal the potential richness of research that adopts a historical approach. They also suggest that the notion of an organizing vision, put forth by IS researchers (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997), is instrumental to explaining the creation – or lack – of institutions. Continuing to explore the role of the organizing vision represents another promising avenue. Finally, a number of recent studies have focused on the interaction between an IT artefact – be it an application or a project to develop or implement an application – and existing institutions. These studies have shown how this perspective helps explain the emergence of conflicts as well as the transformation of institutions. This avenue holds promise as another way to advance our understanding of IT implementations in organizations.

Overall, our analysis reveals that institutional IS research has borne much fruit. It also suggests that many avenues of research remain to be explored if we are to fully exploit the richness of institutional theory.

## About the authors

Muriel Mignerat is currently an Associate Professor at the Telfer School of Management, University of Ottawa, Canada. Her current research interests are focused on project management practices applied to information systems and on the use of information technologies in mega-events. Dr. Mignerat uses an institutional approach to study these phenomena.

Suzanne Rivard is HEC Montreal Endowed Chair in Strategic Management of Information Technology, and is a Fellow of the Canadian Royal Society. Dr. Rivard’s research interests are in the areas of enterprise systems implementation, outsourcing of information systems services, user resistance to information technology implementation, and software project management. Her work has been published in such journals as Communications of the ACM, Data Base, Information and Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, and others.
