# Adopting organizational virtualization in B2B firms: An empirical study in Singapore<sup>§</sup>

Chunhui Liu <sup>a,</sup>\*, Choon-Ling Sia <sup>b</sup>, Kwok-Kee Wei <sup>b</sup>

Keywords: Virtual organizing Organizational structure Intent formation modeling e-Commerce Transaction cost theory Institutional theory Capability-based theory

## A B S T R A C T

We empirically tested a parsimonious model that incorporates cost-benefit analysis/transaction cost theory, institutional theory, and organizational capability-based theory to predict organizational adoption of IT-facilitated virtualization in an Asian context. Virtualization implies the use of IT and communication technology by organizations in managing their interactions and key business operations with stakeholders, such as customers, suppliers and employees. Our research extends existing literature by using organizational structure adoption theories. The study should provide insights into the salient adoption issues of Asian firms. A survey was conducted of B2B organizations in Singapore. Results indicated that an increase in the level of net perceived benefits, external influences, and organizational capabilities had a positive effect on organizational decision makers’ intention to use virtualization. In particular, external influences were found to be the most important antecedents of intention to adopt virtualization in B2B organizations in Singapore.

## 1. Introduction

Virtual organizing (VO) or virtualization has been considered as a new paradigm for building competitive advantage [31]. It can be viewed as an organizational design practice that results in flattened organizational structures, larger spans of control, and extensive geographically dispersed work. VO uses IT in organizations to expand their business by improved interaction with stakeholders. To provide flexibility, many organizations have combined core competencies to respond better to specific customer need and preferences. Specific advantages of VOs have been stated to be increased productivity, adaptability, flexibility, agility, and strategic competitiveness [24].

Khalil and Wang [16] noted that VO is increasingly being recognized as an integral part of e-commerce. U.S. Commerce Department reported that the US e-commerce sales sector grew 19% in 2007 to \$136.4 billion. The Bitkom industry association <sub>reported that the volume of Germany online sales reached</sub> s<sub>438bn</sub> in 2006, 90% of this is from B2B sales; it also predicted that the <sub>volume of online sales would reach</sub> s<sub>781bn by 2010, more than</sub> 80% of which would be sales between businesses [28].

Despite many predictions of the fast and broad acceptance of VO and successful cases like Dell Computers, Cisco, Boeing, Coopers & Lybrand, etc., it was not as widely adopted as expected. It has been argued that the present stage of e-commerce involves a much slower embedding of the virtual in the physical [34]. This is particularly true in Asia (ex-Japan) where VO adoption has not been widely reported [33]. Past studies conducted in the West have shown that factors important to organizations that had adopted virtual firm structures included cost reduction [4], IT [19], and trust [11].

However, it is not clear whether such considerations are as important to organizations in Asia. For instance, recent work involving Asian organizations have shown that external institutional forces sometimes influence the adoption decision more than internal organizational considerations [17,23]. This might be due to the collectivistic tendencies of Asian businesses. It would thus be useful to identify the factors that influence organizations decisions to virtualize in the Asian context. Furthermore, a study of external contingency factors affecting VO adoption in firms is important because firms that are open are more influenced by and respond to external environments [15]. An understanding of virtualization could therefore be achieved by examining both internal and external factors that lead to the intent to be a VO, as a precondition to actual becoming one.

## 2. Conceptual foundations

## 2.1. Innovation adoption intent formation

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [1] posited that the immediate antecedent of a given behavior is the intent to perform it and that it can be predicted by knowing the person’s beliefs towards the behavior: attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control.

## 2.2. Innovative organizational structure adoption theories

Many theories have been developed to explain why one type of organizational structure is adopted over others. Three of the most prominent perspectives are cost-benefit analysis or transaction cost theory, institutional theory, and an organizational-capabilitybased theory of governance structure. Each of these emphasizes a specific aspect of adoption.

## 2.2.1. Cost-benefit analysis and transaction cost theory

Cost-benefit analysis is used to determine which alternative has the greatest net benefits less cost. Transaction cost theory posits that organizations decide on a specific governance structure based on its ability to economize on the cost of doing business. It has emerged as a predominant economics-based explanation of the choice of internal organizational structures. Not surprisingly, reduced cost has been found to be a major factor in producing positive attitudes towards the adoption of virtual work environment by managers.

Transaction costs can be determined by determining: asset specificity, uncertainty, and frequency of occurrence. Asset specificity is the extent to which durable investments are used to support particular transactions. Unspecialized goods or services cost less since they are easily available on the market and redeployable. Uncertainty is the extent to which organizations are faced with unforeseen actions; these increase the need for ways to enforce contracts between transactional parties while increasing the need for greater flexibility and speed in coordinating responses to unforeseen problems or opportunities. Frequency is the volume of transactions in a given time. Within the context of VO, a good fit between the organizational structure and transaction load should reduce transaction costs.

## 2.2.2. Institutional theory

This focuses on legitimacy of new organizational structures, regardless of productivity or efficiency. Organizations are subject to external influences that cause them to be similar to others in their environment. Legitimacy allows them to secure resources and support for survival. External influences may include institutional pressures and promotion. Novel organizational structures are more likely to be adopted when they are adopted by other organizations [14].

Institutional pressures arise from three major sources. Coercive pressures are exerted by organizations upon whom they are dependent for scarce and important resources. Mimetic pressures are those which make an organization imitate others when the organizational technologies are poorly understood, goals are ambiguous, or the environment is uncertain. Normative pressures result primarily from professionalization; this is the collective effort of members of an occupation in defining the conditions and methods of work, thus establishing a cognitive base and legitimacy for occupational autonomy.

Support from professional, trade, business, and other organizations may contribute to isomorphism. The effort may involve dissemination of information through communication channels, marketing campaigns, consulting services that share knowledge and experiences, etc. Direct and frequent communications are likely to result in similar thinking and behavior.

Institutional theory could contribute to a better understanding of management’s motivations towards virtualization. Environmental factors like technology advancement, growing prominence of e-commerce, and global economy could contribute to more adoption of VOs. For example, it was found that adoption of interorganizational linkages, critical to the virtualization of B2B firms, could be best explained by institutional pressures [27]. Further, institutional theory models the influence of subjective norm on the adoption of an organizational structure.

## 2.2.3. Organizational-capability-based theory

The organizational capabilities also play a role in shaping its structure. This theory could provide an alternative, complementary explanation to cost-benefit theory in understanding governance decisions. It argues that the capabilities of an organization define its boundaries and structures. Organizational capabilities behave both as a source of competitive advantage and as a constraint of structural change. This helps explain why seemingly similar activities may be organized differently in different organizations.

Novel structures are adopted when they help the organization utilize its capabilities to realize value. Major capabilities affecting organizational structures include IT [9], trust [18], innovation [8], and risk assessment [2]. IT is believed to be a necessary but not sufficient, enabler of structural change within organizations. Trust can be viewed as an organizational resource that influences the extent to which behavioral uncertainty affects organizational design characteristics. Strategic advantages arising from the purposeful pursuit of innovative activities should also be considered in selecting organizational structures. Organizations need to be able to innovate over time. Risk assessment also affects an organization’s ability to adopt a new structure. Organizational structure innovations can be rejected simply because the risk assessment of the innovation is too high [25]. VOs, like other innovations, hinge on implementing capabilities accessible to potential adopters. Organizational capabilities needed for VO adoption include IT and trust. Facilitating capabilities emphasized by organizational-capability theory are also important in shaping behavioral control beliefs.

## 2.2.4. Value of multiple theoretical perspectives

Testing a research model that incorporates economics-based, institutional, and organizational capabilities theories make it possible to determine the relative impact of each factor in influencing the adoption decision.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Research model

Drawing on TPB and Chwelos et al.’s model, we proposed a research model (see Fig. 1). In this, the adoption intent for virtualizing an organization is assumed to be affected by three major factors: net perceived benefits, external influences, and organizational capabilities.

## 3.2. Research hypotheses

## 3.2.1. Net perceived benefits and VO adoption intent

Net perceived benefits are beliefs about the net benefits less cost of the virtualizing organization. They can have a positive influence on intent formation. Economic theorists believe that existing organizations are primarily the product of a series of organizational innovations that have had the effect of realizing the major net perceived benefits of the organizational structures. Based on a costbenefit framework, greater net benefits should lead to favorable evaluation of the adoption decision. Moreover, empirical studies have shown that net perceived benefits of virtualization may lead to the intent to virtualize an organization. Thus,

H1. Net perceived benefits will have positive effect on intent to become virtual.

Based on transaction cost theory [32], three major antecedents may contribute to the net perceived benefits of virtualizing an organization: they are asset specificity, uncertainty management, and frequency facilitation. Asset specificity is the extent to which large fixed investments are specialized to particular transactions. A reduction in site, physical asset, and human asset specificities could enhance organizational efficiency. For instance, lowering operating costs as a result of savings in office space, rentals, site maintenance, etc. when transactions are carried out in a virtual environment rather than physical storefronts, when less warehousing costs are incurred due to better information sharing between trading partners, and when employees need not work in a traditional office. A reduction in physical asset specificity could lead to savings through reduced investments in warehouses, etc. Further, VO emphasis on capturing and leveraging intellectual expertise and knowledge through the use of advanced technologies could reduce the cost of knowledge specificity and thus increase organizational performance [22]. For example, Xerox and British Petroleum utilized advanced database and satellite video conferencing technologies to disseminate critical expertise to locations requiring time-sensitive responses, thereby increasing organizational efficiency and effectiveness. Therefore,

H1a. Asset specificity will have negative effect on net perceived benefits.

Effective uncertainty management based on appropriate information processing may help to reduce costs by aiding the organization by detecting and responding to unforeseen problems or opportunities. Through the use of decentralized structures and advanced information and communication technologies, organizations could acquire and process quality information gathered at source, and thus increasing the information processing capacity of an organization. Thus, a major benefit of virtualization would lie in higher productivity and reduced costs due to better management of uncertainty.

H1b. Uncertainty management will have a positive effect on net perceived benefits.

Frequency facilitation shows how well the volume of transactions is achieved. The frequency of transactions affects transaction costs. Thus, VO structures specially designed to facilitate more effective handling of the transaction workload should lead to greater organizational efficiency. Decision makers may perceive higher organizational performance if they find that virtualization helps them process needed current and anticipated future transactional frequencies.

H1c. Frequency facilitation will have positive effect on net perceived benefits.

## 3.2.2. External influences and VO adoption intent

External influences have been found to affect intent and actual behavior in adopting multidivisional organizational forms, corporate acquisition [13], corporate diversifications, and corporate innovations. Since VOs involve the redesign of organizational structures, external influences should also play an important role in intent formation.

H2. External influences will have positive effect on intent to become virtual.

External influences may arise from two sources: institutional pressures and institutional promotion. Institutional pressures are coercive, mimetic, and normative while institutional promotion involves the activities and assistance offered by professional, trade, business, and other organizations. Both may influence an organizations’ behavior.

Subsidiaries are commonly subject to standardized reporting mechanisms used by their parent. Torres [30] noted that professionalization helped to define acceptable structures and eliminate potential variations within the organization. According to Scott [21], inducements play an important role in influencing organizational behavior. Encouragement by external agencies also helps enhance the legitimacy of certain practices, and thus promote their dissemination.

H2a. Institutional pressures will have positive effect on external influences.

H2b. Institutional promotion will have positive effect on external influences.

## 3.2.3. Organizational capabilities and VO adoption intent

Organizational capabilities are critical for VO adoption: inadequate capabilities create barriers or constraints. Purposive innovation and risk assessment may also influence virtualization intent because minimal perceived risk and greater innovation improve organizational redesign.

H3. Organizational capabilities will have positive effect on inten to virtualize.

The potential for IT to reduce the need for spatial and temporal proximity has suggested that IT is a critical facilitating factor of VOs. Sufficient IT investment funds and/or adequate IT infrastructure are thus critical to the success of VOs. Extensive use of IT is a constitutional element that differentiates a VO from others. Therefore, organizations with more IT resources should be more capable of becoming virtual.

H3a. IT resources will have positive effect on perceived capability to virtualize.

Trust can be viewed as social capital. It is especially important for a VO, as much of the work is done through electronic communications with little physical supervision. Thus, a VO needs more trust than control and stronger trust should be associated with greater perceived capability to virtualize an organization.

H3b. Trust will have positive effect on perceived capability to virtualize.

Strong purposive innovation may also enable virtualization. Purposive innovation is the extent to which organizations are designed to innovate. To succeed, organizations need to engage in innovation, even in the absence of short-term benefits. Since VO adoption necessitates a new organizational structure, it constitutes an innovation. Innovative organizations would be more likely to possess the necessary mindset, skills, technical expertise, and technologies to adopt VO. Thus,

H3c. Purposive innovation will have positive effect on perceived capability to virtualize.

Finally, risk assessment may also influence an organization’s ability to become virtual. Higher risk assessment of virtualizing demands more resources in carrying out a change. Virtualizing involves risk: irrecoverable investments in technologies; changes to work styles, reward systems, and control and coordination systems; opportunistic behaviors from their alliance partners; and opportunistic behaviors from workers under conditions of less physical supervision. If the risk of virtualizing the organization is high, the organization may seem to have less ability to become virtual because of the constraints placed on organizational resources. Thus, it is likely that lower risk assessment may lead to higher perception of organization’s capabilities to become virtual.

H3d. Risk assessment will have negative effect on perceived capability to virtualize.

## 4. Research method

A survey was used in testing our model. Given that virtualization is especially helpful for B2B organizations [6], we sampled B2B organizations listed in Kompass 2000 for Company Information in Singapore by selecting them by using a table of random numbers generated by SPSS 9.0 for Windows. A parcel enclosing a cover letter, a definition of virtualization, a copy of the questionnaire, and a prepaid reply envelope was sent to the Chief Executives of 1000 organizations. The respondents were chief executives, because strategic decisions resulting in organizational structure changes are typically made by top executives [10]. Where available, the items for the questionnaires were drawn from previous research; otherwise, new items (Appendix A) were developed based on a review of the literature see (Table 1). Items were measured on a 7- point Likert-type scale.

Content validity of all items was carefully assessed. First, the items were examined by two independent scholars with expertise in measurement theory, organizational structure adoption, and intent formation theories. Minor changes were made, based on the experts’ suggestions, to avoid double-barreled questions. Second, the revised items were sorted using Moore and Benbasat’s [20] sorting procedure. The constructs labeled by the judges in round one and round three revealed that they labeled constructs very similarly and this provided evidence of content validity of the constructs. Inter-judge reliability was found to be strong using Cohen’s Kappa scores for all constructs above Moore and Benbasat’s criterion of 0.65 and placement ratios greater than Gellert’s criterion of 0.85 [7].

Of the 1000 questionnaires distributed, 168 were returned by the postal service because they could not be delivered. A check with the business telephone directory confirmed that 131 organizations had changed address. The final 37 could not be located. Additional packages were sent to the 131 revised addresses. Follow-up telephone calls were then made every 2 weeks over a period of 4 months starting 1 month after the survey was originally mailed to increase the response rate, confirm whether respondents were aware of the survey, clarify questions, and ask for answers if questions had been inadvertently ignored. 78 new packages were sent to respondents who stated that they misplaced theirs.

246 usable responses were finally received. 43 of them were from organizations that had already become virtual. Since the purpose of the research was to test a model of intent formation, these responses were dropped. The remaining set of 203 responses was used to test the causal model. A usable response rate of 20% was reasonable compared to similar studies [12,26]. Table 2 shows their demographics.

Based on their own assessment, 75% of the respondents stated that they were familiar with the concept of VO and 26% of these had had some working experience with VO. Thus, respondents were well exposed to the concept. Data from the 93 late respondents who sent their material after follow-up calls were compared with that from 110 earlier respondents. Since the data were not normally distributed, a Mann–Whitney test was conducted on their intent to become virtual to test for response bias. No significant differences were found between the two groups. Thus, the random sampling could be considered successful and the possibility of response bias was unlikely to be a critical concern.

## 5.2.1. Measurement model

Reflective indicators are measures that are affected by the same underlying latent variable. A change in a reflective indicator may imply a similar directional change for the other reflective indicators of the same variable. Therefore, reflective indicators should have both convergent and discriminant validity. Formative indicators are measures that form or cause the creation or change in a latent variable. A change in a formative indicator may not necessarily imply a similar directional change for the other formative indicators of the same variable. The strength of the constructs modeled with formative indicators can be assessed by the significance of the item weights and t-statistics of the indicators.

Four constructs (net perceived benefits, external influences, organizational capabilities, and intent to virtualize) were measured with reflective indicators. The item loadings, t-statistics, composite reliability, and average variance extracted are presented in Table 3. All four constructs displayed strongly positive loadings and high levels of statistical significance. Because all of the loadings were of approximately the same magnitude, it was not possible to assess the relative importance of individual items in determining the overall level of each. With composite reliability above 0.7 and average variance extracted above 0.5, all four constructs had convergent validity and were reliable as shown by Cronbach’s alphas above 0.7.

Discriminant validity of these reflective constructs was assessed (see Table 4). The correlation between any two constructs must be statistically lower than the square root of the average variance extracted from indicators within a construct in order for the constructs to have discriminant validity. There was therefore evidence of discriminant validity for the four reflective constructs.

Nine constructs (asset specificity, uncertainty management, frequency facilitation, institutional pressures, institutional promotion, risk assessment, IT support, purposive innovation, and trust) were measured with formative indicators. The item weights of the first three constructs displayed the importance of their impact on net perceived benefits. The item weights of institutional pressures and promotion showed their importance external influences. The item weights of IT support, trust, purposive innovation, and risk assessment can show their importance on organizational capabilities. The sub-construct weights and t-statistics for the formative constructs are presented in Table 5. The weights are all found to be significant at the $p < 0 . 0 1$ level.

The tests provided evidence of acceptable measurement quality for both reflective and formative constructs in our research.

Approximately 59% of the variance in intent to virtualize was due to these three constructs $\left( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 9 \right)$ . Asset specificity was negatively related to net perceived benefits at the 1% significance level, while uncertainty management and frequency facilitation were positively related to net perceived benefits at the 1% level. Both institutional pressures and institutional promotion were positively related to external influences at the 1% significance level. IT support, trust, and purposive innovation were also positively related to organizational capabilities at the 1% significance level, while risk assessment was negatively related to organizational capabilities at the 1% significance level.

The standardized path coefficients range from 0.14 to 0.62, with two out of three of these paths exceeding the suggested ideal standard of significance at 0.30. $M ^ { 2 }$ for the hypothesized model was 0.51 exceeding the criterion of 0.50. Such results provided evidence for a good fit of the overall model.

The beta values can be interpreted directly because standardized data were used for the analysis. Hence, a one standard deviation increase in net perceived benefits, external influences, or organizational capabilities resulted in a 0.24, 0.53, or 0.40 standard deviation increase in intent to virtualize, respectively. External influence has the greatest impact on intent to adopt [5].

## 6. Discussion and concluding comments

## 6.1. Limitations

Our research had several major limitations. First, the use of PLS did not allow us to explore bi-directional effects. In addition, the collection of cross-sectional data at one setting limited the validity of causality. However, the foundation of our model was based on theories that clearly identified the direction of causal relationships between the variables, which helped to increase and verify the internal validity of our findings. The use of perceptual measures could also raise concerns about the possibility of common-method bias. To alleviate this concern, the Harmon’s one-factor test was conducted. No general factor was found to explain most of the variance. Thus common method bias may not be a major concern. Further, in the questionnaire, several items were reverse-scaled (e.g. AS1–AS5), which also helped to alleviate concerns about common-method bias. Nonetheless, we admit that this resulted in a limitation on the generalizability of our findings. Second, this research was conducted in the context of B2B organizations in an Asian country. Generalizing to a different cultural, political, or economic context should be made with caution.

Third, the 20% effective response rate for our survey was not high though reasonable. Fourth, the use of sole respondents within an organization limits the validity of the findings. Nevertheless, most of the organizations were small and medium enterprises in Singapore with CEOs or Managing Directors, who made up the respondents. Fifth, there was a possibility that respondents had ‘‘social desirability’’ bias—overestimating their intention to become virtual. Sixth, our research model explained 59% of the variance in intention. In follow-up calls, additional factors were suggested by respondents (including environmental uncertainty, trading partner readiness, and few known local successes).

## 6.2. Findings

We found that intent to virtualize an organization was influenced by net perceived benefits, external influences, and organizational capabilities, providing strong support for our model.

## 6.3. Implications

## 6.3.1. Practical implications

Our findings support efforts in promoting virtualization. Our results show that institutional forces, pressures from partners and similar organizations and promotional initiatives have a big impact on enhancing intentions. Many organizations in Singapore look to institutions such as the government for strategic adoption of new technology.

Institutions in Asia seeking to encourage virtualisation [29] could introduce measures to promote the benefits, provide incentives, and reduce the risk of adoption.

Our findings also have implications for potential adopters. First, they could assess their organizational capabilities before deciding to virtualize. Second, they may save the costs of explorative and pre-adoption trials by understanding how competitors or similar organizations respond to virtualization. They may also seek information from industry, trade, and professional bodies about the relevance of virtualization to their own organizations.

## 6.3.2. Research implications

Our research also has academic implications. First, it provides empirical evidence of the applicability of TRA in studying organizational decision-making. The work promotes understanding of strategic decision-making in organizations.

Second, the model incorporates organizational factors, suggested to influence virtualization, into a parsimonious model which provides a systematic way of understanding virtualization intent by examining its key antecedents.

Finally, this study contributes to a better understanding of virtual organizing adoption through three theoretical lenses and showed that external factors influence virtualization adoption. We also highlighted the role of purposive innovation and risk assessment in enabling organizations to virtualize.

## 7. Conclusion

The globalization of organizations facilitated by the advent of telecommunications and Internet technologies has promoted virtual organizing of firms. We empirically examined major antecedents to an organizations’ intent to virtualize, based on several theories. Our investigation of VO adoption provided an important step towards a better understanding of the new ITenabled business model. Implications may help both institutions and potential adopters. This may also provide useful insights into innovation adoption considerations that are of greater significance than others to Asian firms. Our model and constructs may also provide guidance on adoption of new organizational structures.

## A.1. Net perceived benefits

Please rate the importance of achieving each of the following benefits of virtual organizations in terms of your organization’s decision whether or not to become virtual.

 PB1 Increased Productivity.

 PB2 Reduced Overhead Cost.

 PB3 Reduced Communication Cost.

## A.2. External influences

 EI1 My organization has sensed external pressures to become virtual.

 EI2 My organization has received external encouragement to virtualize.

 EI3 The value of virtualizing has been introduced to my organization by other organizations.

 EI4 My organization may receive external reward by using VO.

## A.3. Organizational capabilities

 OC1 My organization is able to become virtual.

 OC2 It is possible for my organization to become virtual.

 OC3 Becoming virtual is within my organization’s capabilities.

## A.4. Intent to become virtual

 INT1 My organization is seriously contemplating to become virtual soon.

 INT2 It is critical for my organization to become virtual soon.

 INT3 My organization intends to become virtual in () less than 4 months; () 4–8 months; () 8–12 months; () 12–16 months; () 16– 20 months; () 20–24 months; () no immediate plans.

 INT4 My organization is at an advanced stage in planning to be virtual.

## A.5. Asset specificity

 AS1 VO can lead to savings in our cost of space (e.g. office, site building).\*

 AS2 VO can lead to savings in my organization’s warehousing costs.\*

 AS3 With VO, my organization can hire needed expertise at a lower cost.\*

 AS4 With VO, my organization may suffer less cost resulting from employee turnover.

 AS5 With VO, my organization’s critical knowledge need not reside in specific people.\*

## A.6. Uncertainty management

 UM1 VO allows my organization to handle political uncertainty more effectively.

 UM2 VO allows my organization to handle demand uncertainty more effectively.

 UM3 VO allows my organization to handle supply uncertainty more effectively.

 UM4 VO allows my organization to handle uncertainty from competition more effectively.

 UM5 VO allows my organization to handle uncertainty resulted from fast technology innovation and technology obsolescence more effectively.

## A.7. Frequency facilitation

 FF1 VO allows my organization to handle our current transaction load more effectively.

 FF2 VO allows my organization to handle our expected transaction load in the near future more effectively.

 FF3 VO allows my organization to handle our expected transaction load in the long run more effectively.

## A.8. Institutional pressures

 PRE1 Our parent company, if applicable, plans to adopt VO.

 PRE2 Our major suppliers have adopted VO with their other customers.

 PRE3 Our major customers have adopted VO with their other suppliers.

 PRE4 Our major competitors have adopted VO.

 PRE5 VO is considered legitimate in view of regulatory bodies overseeing our business.

 PRE6 VO is considered a professional way of organizing in my industry.

## A.9. Institutional promotion

 PRO1 Professional bodies actively introduce virtualization’s value to organizations like mine.

 PRO2 Trade associations important to us encourage virtualizing organizations with rewards.

 PRO3 Our business community provides assistance to organizations that want to become virtual.

## A.10. IT support

 IT1 My organization has sufficient resources to invest in IT facilitating VO adoption.

 IT2 Our existing electronic communication and information system are able to support VO adoption.

 IT3 IT staff in my organization has the skills and experiences to handle implementing VO in my organization.

## A.11. Trust

 TR1 Our employees can be trusted to work diligently even if they may conduct their work virtually.

 TR2 Our suppliers, if applicable, can be trustworthy alliance partners in our possible virtual cooperation

 TR3 Our customers, if applicable, can be trustworthy alliance partners in our possible virtual cooperation.

 TR4 We trust that all the component entities can cooperate trustworthily once the virtual organizing is used.

## A.12. Purposive innovation

 PI1 Our management supports the adoption of beneficial innovations like virtualization.

 PI2 Our management rewards the discovery and/or introductory of beneficial innovations like virtualization.

 PI3 Our employees are used to learning and adapting to innovations like virtualization.

## A.13. Risk assessment

 RA1 With VO, my organization may have difficulty recovering the investment in technology.

 RA2 With VO, my organization may suffer low performance from administrative changes.

 RA3 With VO, my organization may suffer low performance from negative morale caused by the necessary changes.

 RA4 With VO, my organization may suffer low performance as a result of changes in our reward system.

 RA5 With VO, my organization may suffer loss from irresponsible behaviors from employees working virtually.

 RA6 With VO, my organization may suffer loss from opportunistic behaviors from our alliance partners.
