# What in<sup>fl</sup>uences ERP beliefs — Logical evaluation or imitation?

Vincent S. Lai <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Connie K.W. Liu <sup>a</sup>, Fujun Lai <sup>b</sup>, Jian Wang <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Business Administration, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, Hong Kong

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 14 August 2009 Received in revised form 7 January 2010 Accepted 2 August 2010 Available online 6 August 2010

Keywords: Technology adoption Imitation Enterprise resource planning (ERP) Diffusion of innovation theory

## a b s t r a c t

Various models have been proposed to explain information technology (IT) adoption behavior. However, these models are based primarily on logical deliberation. In reality, it is impossible to obtain perfect information for a logical evaluation of new or emerging IT. In this situation, sometimes the “best alternative” is imitation. This study proposed an integrated model (based on diffusion of innovation and imitation models) that examines the effect of these two opposing forces on the beliefs of enterprise resource planning (ERP). This study also explored how these relationships were affected by the temporal effect of adoption, as well as the extent of implementation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that imitative forces, along with logical evaluations, are shown to have a consistent direct effect and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indirect effect on beliefs. Our study also indicates that ERP adoption time and extent have different effects on imitation and logical evaluation behaviors. Hence, imitative forces play a crucial role in the decision-making process, which opens up a new avenue for research into technology adoption.

## 1. Introduction

In the last two decades, various well-tested approaches, including the technology adoption model [14], diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory [56], theory of reasoned action [17,30], theory of planned behavior (TPB) [4], and the Triandis model [64], have been applied to explain information technology (IT) adoption behavior. Many researchers (see, e.g., [10,42]) have attempted to expand and/or modify the original models to make them more theoretically complete. However, by nature of their assumption – that all adoption processes are systematically conducted and follow a rational path – these models still focus primarily on logical deliberation.

In reality, we believe that there are two opposing forces in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the beliefs of an individual or organization when IT adoption decisions are being made: logical evaluation and imitative forces. Belief is the psychological state in which an individual holds a proposition or premise to be true. A belief about an IT is the subjective psychological state regarding the potential of that IT [36]. Extant explanations of why users behave in particular ways toward ITs have tended to focus predominantly on beliefs as the driver [2,4], thus con<sup>fi</sup>rming the importance of belief in understanding IT behaviors. In the last two decades, belief has been an underlying theme among many popular models in the information system (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld even though these models diverge widely in their objectives and focuses. However, research into belief (see, e.g., [3,5]) has focused only on the explanation of the logical causation and formation of belief, leaving the non-logical factors unexplored. Moreover, belief from an individual perspective has been investigated whereas few studies have addressed IT adoption initiated at a committee level. Therefore, research is needed to explore belief formation from diverse perspectives, with evaluation of both logical and imitation forces in the same setting at the group or committee level.

Of the ITs adopted at the organizational level, enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems have been widely researched and adopted. An ERP system is a business support system that maintains in a single database the data needed for a variety of business functions, such as manufacturing, supply chain, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, project, human resource, and customer relationship management. In the last decade, companies are increasingly utilizing ERP systems because these systems are believed to be effective in reducing costs and increasing pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins [57]. In practice, however, assimilation of an ERP system is complex, and ERP success is even harder to achieve [68]. Often, a steering committee, which includes representatives from many departments and functional groups, will be formed to gain a better understanding of the constraints and requirements of the company in adopting an ERP system. The beliefs of these committee members about the system then affect their subsequent psychological states and adoption behavior, as theorized in TRA, TPB, TAM, DOI, and other adoption models. Hence, the forces that shape such beliefs, which can be manipulated through various interventions [66], must be investigated.

The purpose of this study is to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap by examining how logical and imitation forces impact the beliefs of ERP steering committee members and studying the interaction of these two types of forces on ERP beliefs. This study will also explore how these relationships are affected by the temporal effect of adoption, as well as the extent of ERP implementation. In this study, DOI is chosen as a reference model for logical ERP adoption forces because of its maturity and theoretical completeness, along with its capacity to investigate organizational ERP systems. The imitation model [24] is chosen as a reference model for illogical adoption forces because of its comprehensive classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of imitation modes.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Imitation theories

Organizational theorists have frequently investigated the phenomena of inter-organizational imitation of business practices and structures, using several theories of organizational action [23]. Theories of organizational learning, for example, argued that organizations imitate other organizations so that they could adapt to the changing environment for survival and competitiveness. Learning also allows imitators to enjoy second-mover's advantages, particularly in cost savings and risk avoidance associated with experimentations [34,35]. Theories of institutionalization, however, argued that imitation is one of the processes through which organizations change over time to resemble other organizations that face the same set of environmental conditions [16,25]. These theories identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three basic mechanisms through which isomorphism occurs: coercive mechanism that stems from political in<sup>fl</sup>uence and the need for legitimacy, mimetic isomorphism resulting from standard responses to uncertainty, and normative isomorphism associated with professionalism [16,28]. March [41] called the mimetic isomorphism an obligatory action, which suggests that organizations mimic actions of other organizations without thinking when such actions were endorsed by a critical number of organizations. Evidence of interorganization mimetic behaviors have already been con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in a wide range of institutionalism investigations (such as [18] and [62]).

Drawing on institutional and learning theories, Haunschild and Miner [24] distinguish three distinct modes of inter-organizational imitation — frequency based, trait based, and outcome based. In frequency-based imitation, if there are enough social actors carrying out a particular action, then this action will be taken for granted and other social actors will take similar action [41]. Trait-based imitation can be seen as a more selective form of imitation [39]. Organizations often identify themselves with other organizations that they view as more legitimate or successful, because this is what they are all striving to achieve. In doing so, the former mimic the course of action of the latter based on the belief that actions taken by successful organizations will be more likely to yield positive outcomes. Outcome-based imitation is based primarily on the perceived consequences of practices; therefore, organizations will tend to adopt the practices of other organizations that yield positive or successful outcomes.

In the past decade, researchers in IS discipline had devoted more effort to assess the role of mimetic pressures in the adoption and diffusion of ITs. Tan and Teo [60], for example, investigated <sup>fi</sup>nancial EDI technology and found imitation to be critical to its adoption intention. Similarly, Ang and Cummings [6] found peer practices to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on outsourcing decisions in banking industry. Along the same line of research, Son and Benbasat [59] have validated two types of mimetic pressures – the extent of adoption by competitors and the perceived success of competitor adopters – to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on the adoption intention of B2B electronic marketplace. Liang et al. [36] also found imitation pressures to have direct effect on top management belief in the assimilation of enterprise systems.

## 2.2. Diffusion of innovation theory

DOI research generally focuses on evaluating the relationship between innovation characteristics and success in adoption and diffusion, and adopts either an adoption or a diffusion perspective. The adoption perspective evaluates the organizational characteristics that create a culture receptive to innovation and change [33], whereas the diffusion perspective investigates the innovation characteristics that lead to the widespread acceptance of IT [52]. DOI research has been conducted in many different disciplines, including sociology, economics, marketing, management, operations management, and information systems. Much of the research conducted has been related to innovations adopted by individuals. Research of DOI at the organizational level is still limited, probably because of the methodological complexity in identifying the decision-making unit and devising measures for data collection.

The success of innovation adoption and diffusion, to a great extent, is dependent upon the characteristics of the innovation as perceived by the organization [15,61]. Consequently, many DOI studies in the past few decades have investigated the effect of innovation characteristics on its adoption and diffusion. This line of research indicates that a large number of innovation characteristics are critical to the adoption and diffusion of various types of innovation [56], including compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, cost, communicability, divisibility, pro<sup>fi</sup>tability, social approval, trialability, and observability. Of these characteristics, compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity were consistently found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant [63].

In this study, DOI represents the technical evaluation of an ERP system in a systematic and logical manner. Committee members go through the evaluation process together, analyzing the features, functions, capacities, costs, and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the ERP system intended for adoption. Sometimes the evaluation process is assisted by ERP vendors, who may provide statistics and experience on ERP adoption, usage, and implementation. Such information affects the beliefs of ERP committee members resulting in a more informed scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and logical evaluation process.

## 3. Research model

The three research models examined in this study are the imitation, DOI, and proposed integrated model (shown in Fig. 1). In the imitation part of our model, we have included Haunschild and Miner's [24] three forms of imitative behavior – frequency based, trait based, and outcome based – to attempt to evaluate their effects on the beliefs of ERP steering committee members. In the DOI section, only three variables – relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility – were considered for testing because they were found to be the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors in Tornatzky and Klein's [63] meta analysis and are widely accepted. In the integrated model, we believe that imitative adoption behavior will have a direct effect on logical DOI behavior in the ERP evaluation process because of bounded rationality. These two types of behavior will subsequently impact the beliefs of ERP steering committee members. To control for the effects of <sup>fi</sup>rm size, <sup>fi</sup>rm age, ownership type, and industry type, which might affect ERP steering committee beliefs, we used these four variables as control variables.

In this study, ERP has been chosen as the technology for investigation because it is an expensive technology that affects the entire organization [57,68], which leads potential adopters to evaluate the technology thoroughly, including following industry leaders, to minimize adoption risks. Furthermore, committee belief was chosen as the dependent variable instead of the belief of an individual decision maker because ERP systems are often expensive and require the involvement of the entire company, thus, the evaluation of which cannot be left to a certain individual, but to include representatives from multi departments and functional groups. The forming of such committee will allow a better understanding of the constraints and requirements of the company, which is also a common practice across different industries. The observation of this phenomenon prompted our investigation on the beliefs of ERP systems, not at an individual level, but looking at it from the point of view of a committee.

## 4. Research hypotheses

## 4.1. The imitation model

The massive introduction of ERP systems into organizations is bound to have critical implications for those that are still considering or have not adopted one. The 67% adoption rate of such systems among mid- and large-sized organizations [36] enhances the legitimacy of the ERP practice and suggests the technical value of ERP, which leads to greater consideration of the adoption of these systems by others. The increase in the frequency of ERP system adoption directly creates positive externality and changes the competitive landscape. Consequently, many companies are “forced” to adopt an ERP system because their major clients have, whereas others adopt such a system because many of their rivals in the same industry have already done so. In many cases, companies are afraid that without the technology, they will lose their competitive advantage, or that they will be deemed old fashioned or complacent if they do not follow the current trend and risk losing support from their stakeholders. One way to overcome this risk is to imitate their industry leaders for an optimal solution. This optimization technique is analogical to the stochastic swarm theory that describes the behavior of a <sup>fl</sup>ock of birds or the sociological behavior of a group of people [65]. This swarm theory is a population-based optimization technique, which is modeled on swarm intelligence, with random solutions called particles (or particle swarms). When a particle discovers a promising solution to an optimization task, all the other particles will move closer to it, exploring this candidate solution more thoroughly for a possible optimal solution. In the process, all particles communicate their solutions to each other and adjust their own solutions, thus producing an optimal solution with reduced risks and increased cognitive consistency among the large number of members that make up the particle swarm.

Hence, the frequency of ERP system adoption may serve as a valid proxy/indicator of the technical value of ERP, which in turn could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the beliefs of top management favorably, leading to the subsequent adoption of an ERP system. These phenomena have been described in the bandwagon theory [1] and validated in studies such as those of [18] and [47]. Therefore, we postulate the following.

Hypothesis 1. The perceived number of organizations using a particular ERP system has a positive impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

Many ERP initiatives fail because organizations do not understand the resources and commitment required for ERP system implementation and plunge in simply because other big players already have ERP systems [58,68]. Despite these failures, management still believes that imitating large and successful organizations will reduce the chance of ERP failure. Liang et al. [36] argue that top management succumbs to pressure, imitating their successful peers or competitors to maintain the legitimacy of their ERP adoption decisions and avoid any potential loss of face. Thus, ERP steering committee members mediate the impact of trait-based imitative forces on ERP adoption. In other words, they serve as gatekeepers and represent those individuals with expertise of the technology as a result of their special interests, talents, and position in the organization [29]. They act as a bridge and bring important ERP technological advances from the outside world into the organization by evaluating the ERP practices of leading organizations and their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and form favorable or unfavorable beliefs that are translated into actions for adoption decision making and implementation. They also gather and process external information in order to make effective ERP decisions. In order to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the role of a gatekeeper, they may selectively solicit ERP practices from a subset of other organizations, usually large and successful, and translate them into terms that are meaningful and useful to their internal ERP decisions. Hence, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 2. The perceived traits (large and successful) of organizations using a particular ERP system have a positive impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

ERP systems have emerged as complete business software systems that, ideally, facilitate technical and business organizational integration without geographical restrictions [53]. In practice, however, ERP system implementation is complex and ERP success is even harder to achieve [68]. Therefore, potential ERP adopters need to analyze the adoption outcomes of successful ERP adopters and evaluate these outcomes in their own context. ERP vendors often publicize the positive outcome of their customers as a marketing strategy to change perceived ERP values, especially given the low success rate of ERP. Copying organizations based on their achieved ERP outcomes gives a second-mover advantage of unexpected or unsought unique bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, including the accrual of an external referent of prestige. These unanticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, along with the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of lower adoption risks and costs, could favorably shape the beliefs of members of management about an ERP system, leading to its eventual adoption. Therefore, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. The perceived outcomes (bene<sup>fi</sup>ts achieved) of organizations using a particular ERP system have a positive impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

## 4.2. The DOI model

DOI research <sup>fi</sup>ndings show a strong correlation between an innovation's relative advantage and a user's attitude toward its use. Chau and Lai [12] and Liao and Cheung [38], for example, have empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that relative advantage has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant direct effect on attitude toward the use of Internet banking. Ramamurthy et al. [52] has also validated that competitive advantage has a positive impact on organizational commitment in data warehouse adoption. Regarding intention to adopt and actual system use, Tan and Teo [60] also provide evidence to support the criticality of relative advantage to intention to use and eventual use of an innovative IT. It is obvious that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of an ERP system supersede most of its competing systems, which in turn could favorably in<sup>fl</sup>uence the beliefs of steering committee members about possible ERP adoption. Hence:

Hypothesis 4. The perceived relative advantage of an ERP system has a positive impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

The incompatibility of an ERP system with existing values, past experience, and needs has a negative effect on its adoption and diffusion. An incompatible innovation inhibits further innovation use and implementation because of adoption resistance and implementation complexity. Prior DOI research (e.g., [44] and [63]) has shown that practical compatibility and value compatibility are both considered essential in adoption decision making, and that incompatibility of either can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negatively affect the innovation's use and adopters' attitude. Chau and Hu [11] also <sup>fi</sup>nd that when users are entrenched in a particular working habit, it is unlikely that they will accept a technology that is perceived to be incompatible with their practices. Subsequently, it is likely that the incompatibility of an ERP system will negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the beliefs of steering committee members, which will lead them to evaluate the system negatively.

Hypothesis 5. The perceived compatibility of an ERP system has a positive impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

ERP systems are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand, use, and implement [13,21,26,31], although they exhibit higher performance across a wide variety of <sup>fi</sup>nancial metrics [46]. In practice, ERP system implementation has a low success rate of 10% [68], and the use of such complex information systems has continued to challenge their adopters [31]. The association among learning, complexity, and IT adoption has also been validated in prior TAM and DOI investigations. These studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the positive effects of perceived ease of use [10,14,42] and negative effects of perceived complexity [32,56,63] on a user's attitude toward, intention to use, and eventual adoption of a technology. Hence, we postulate the following.

Hypothesis 6. The perceived complexity of an ERP system has a negative impact on the belief of the steering committee in the ERP adoption process.

## 4.3. Towards the integration

In the context of ERP assimilation, we believe that ERP imitation could arbitrate the outcome of ERP evaluation. If the force of ERP imitation is high, organizations would adjust their ERP evaluation, either strengthened or weakened, to align with the belief of their referent group, which in our research is composed of high-status, large, successful organizations. On this basis, the referent group's ERP practices would probably be more reliable and successful, thus reducing the <sup>fi</sup>rstmover risks, experimentation costs, and learning curve associated with decisions derived from ERP evaluation. However, when the desire for imitation is weak, it becomes less disturbing for an organization to stick to their rational decision choice, thus reducing the impact of ERP imitation on ERP evaluation. Hence, we proposed:

Hypothesis 7. Frequency-, trait-, and outcome-based imitative forces have a direct impact on the evaluation of an ERP system's relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility.

## 4.4. Control variables

To fully account for the differences among organizations, this research also included four control variables that had potential impact on ERP adoption as suggested by prior literature. These controls include organization size, age, ownership type, and industry type.

## 5. Methodology

## 5.1. Instrument development and pre-test

This study used a number of measures to ensure the validity and reliability of the instrument. First, whenever possible, previously validated questions were used. Second, the questionnaire was pretested by two business professors with expertise in survey research and eight IT professionals with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ERP experience. Third, following Mullen's [45] and House and Singh's [27] two-step method, the questionnaire, which was originally in English, was translated into Chinese as most IT executives in China were not pro<sup>fi</sup>cient in English language. The translated Chinese questionnaire was then translated back into English, which was then compared with the original English version by two native Chinese IS professors from the School of Business to check the translation. The feedback from this phase of instrument development resulted in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant re<sup>fi</sup>nement of the survey, which improved its content validity. Fourth, a <sup>fi</sup>eld study of 52 graduate MIS students with work experience and ERP knowledge was conducted to assess the reliability and construct validity of the resulting scales. The feedback from the pilot test was used to improve the readability and quality of the questions in the instrument and the format of the questionnaire, resulting in the addition, removal, and rephrasing of several items.

## 5.2. Data collection

A total of thirty graduate MIS students from a major university in Beijing participated in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of this project, with the understanding that not all of them would be hired in the second stage as interviewers. These students were familiar with ERP concepts and had experience in conducting an academic survey. Nevertheless, they were provided with one day of training in ERP and interview techniques, together with the background of our project. They were also provided with reviews of the questionnaire items, and participated in mock interviews with trainers. To maximize the consistency and standardization of interviewing, the students were given a standardized script, including opening and closing remarks, and asked to read the questions exactly as they were worded in the questionnaire. At the end of the training, sixteen students were short-listed, based on their performance in the training period, to participate in our project as interviewers.

Samples of our research were randomly selected from a company database maintained by the Ministry of Commerce. This database has more than 10,000 registered companies, but with only about 1800 of them having ERP implemented. A total of 379 companies were randomly chosen from these ERP companies. These selected companies were contacted by the graduate students to obtain information on members serving on ERP steering committees. Wherever possible, the highest ranking member of the committee was contacted for an on-site interview to answer the questionnaire. Because of time and resource constraints, only one committee member from each company was interviewed. This person's perspective was considered to be representative of the committee's belief about the ERP system as committee members had reached a collective decision on ERP adoption after a long decision process.

We adopted measures to minimize the impact of interviewer error on the data collected. First, the <sup>fi</sup>rst completed survey instrument of each interviewer was carefully reviewed to assess the quality and reliability of data collection. Any issue identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this review process was discussed with the interviewer prior to resuming his or her interview duties. Second, <sup>fi</sup>fty-six surveys were randomly chosen from the completed surveys for validation. These respondents were contacted by the project supervisor, who randomly chose a few items from the questionnaire for the respondents to answer again. This validation process did not only ensure data reliability, but also ensured that the interviewees speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the responses had actually been interviewed. Although this measure was implemented on a small scale because it constituted a disturbance, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd much deviation between the <sup>fi</sup>rst and second responses of these respondents. We also did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any fault associated with the interviews.

## 5.3. Variable operationalization

All items of the research variables were rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The averaged aggregate scores of these surrogate factors provided the score for each research variable. The dependent variable, steering committee beliefs, was adapted from the measure of top management belief in Liang et al. [36]. This three-item scale was modi<sup>fi</sup>ed for our context to measure the committee's beliefs about the value of ERP in business activities.

Frequency-based imitation was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the adopter's perception of the number of times that adoption of ERP occurs, and was measured using a four-item scale. Respondents were asked to agree or disagree with statements related to their perception of the ERP adoption rate of their customers, suppliers, competitors, and industry. Trait-based imitation was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the adopter's perception of the positive distinguishing quality or characteristics of a company that had adopted an ERP system using the four-item scale developed by Haunschild and Miner [24] and Liang et al. [36]. The traits were large, leading, successful, and favorable perception by customers and suppliers. Outcome-based imitation was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the adopter's perception of the consequences of ERP adoption, and we developed a seven-item scale based on the most common advantages that would be expected to be gained by using an ERP system.

Relative advantage was measured by asking the respondents to evaluate the extent to which the proposed ERP system could meet their business operation expectations compared to their current system. These expectations were measured using an eight-item scale adapted from Liang et al. [37], Reimers [54], Xue et al. [67], and Zhang et al. [68], which evaluates the relative advantage of ERP in enhancing internal operations, streamlining business processes, and improving relationships. Compatibility was assessed using a three-item scale adapted from Karahanna et al. [30], Lai [32], and Liang et al. [36], in which an ERP system's compatibility with the respondent's existing software and hardware environment, business processes, values, and work style is rated. Complexity was assessed using a four-item re<sup>fl</sup>ective scale adapted from Lai [32] and Liang et al. [36], which measures the respondent's perception of his or her understanding and use of an ERP system.

Four control variables were used: <sup>fi</sup>rm size, industry type, <sup>fi</sup>rm age, and ownership type. Firm size was measured by the af<sup>fi</sup>liate's annual sales and number of employees; industry type was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as <sup>fi</sup>nance, manufacturing, IT, insurance, health, and so forth; <sup>fi</sup>rm age was the number of years of business operation since its establishment; and ownership type was categorized as state-owned, domestic private-owned, joint-venture, or solely foreign invested.

## 6. Data analysis and results

## 6.1. Profile of respondent

Two hundred and eight subjects completed the questionnaire, representing a response rate of 54.9%. Details of the responding companies are shown in Table 1. Of these respondents, 52 (25.0%) were state-owned, 59 (28.4%) were domestic private-owned, 28 (13.5%) were joint-venture, and 64 (30.8%) were foreign companies; 47.1% of these companies had less than 1000 employees, and 26% of them had more than 5000; and just slightly more than half had total <sup>fi</sup>xed assets of less than ¥300 million. More than half (51.9%) had been operating in China for less than 10 years, and nearly 20% had been operating in China for more than 20 years.

The interviewees were mostly top management, including chief executive of<sup>fi</sup>cers (13.5%), chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cers (20.2%), chief <sup>fi</sup>nancial of<sup>fi</sup>cers (18.8%), and chief operations of<sup>fi</sup>cers (9.6%). All these top executives accounted for 77.9% of our overall respondents. The remaining responses came from middle management, which include senior IT manager (9.6%), senior sales managers (5.8%), and senior <sup>fi</sup>nance managers (2.4%). The seniority of our respondents suggested the reliability and quality of data collected.

Following the suggestion of Fornell and Larcker [19], discriminant validity was tested by examining the average variance extracted and the average variance shared between a construct and its measures. As shown in Table 4, the correlations of all seven entries, squared values of which represent the shared variance among variables, were found to be consistently lower than the squared roots of the diagonal average variance extracted. This suggests that our measures are distinct and unidimensional, thereby con<sup>fi</sup>rming discriminant validity at the construct level.

Although common method variance (CMV) is not serious in IS as in other disciplines [40], CMV is an attention-needed issue for studies conducted in a Chinese context due to Chinese cultural factors [69]. Hence, we conducted a series of analyses to detect if CMV is present in our study in accordance with Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, and Podsakoff [50]. First, we conducted commonly used Harmon's single factor test, in which all items were included for an exploratory factor analysis (EFA). CMV is present if a single factor emerges from EFA or the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounts for most of the covariance [40,51]. Our analysis showed that neither of these two conditions holds. Second, we checked the partial correlation, in which the highest factor from an EFA was added into the model as another control factor on the dependent variable. CMV exists if this factor signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the variance explained of the dependent variable [48]. Last, we checked the correlation matrix. CMV is unlikely if there are no extremely high correlations of 0.90 or above [49]. The results of these three tests collectively ascertained that CMV did not seem to be problematic in our study.

## 7. Discussions

## 7.1. Comparison of the DOI and imitation model

As illustrated in Table 6, both the DOI and imitation model explained more than a third of the variance in steering committee beliefs. Although the imitation model had a slightly higher power in explaining the variance, the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of the two models suggest that they both have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and direct impact on beliefs. When the models are compared on the basis of valuable information provided, the imitation model could be seen to provide valuable intrinsic information on why people act in a particular way under a certain stimulus. In contrast, the strength of the DOI model is in the provision of speci<sup>fi</sup>c information about innovation characteristics, and in the provision of a construct to measure the performance of these characteristics. These models attempt to describe human behavior in different ways. It is not surprising to observe that although each model contributes to a certain extent in explaining the variance in beliefs, neither of them outperforms the other because it is hard to <sup>fi</sup>nd a situation in which a belief is formed purely on the basis of technical evaluation without being in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by environmental factors, or vice versa.

## 7.2. Integration of the DOI and imitation model

The integrated model explains more than 43% of the variance in steering committee beliefs, which is better than the 34% and 35% explained by the imitation and DOI models alone. All of the three DOI variables in the integrated model are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, showing that they have a strong direct effect on beliefs. Although frequency and trait imitation are only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.05 level, their direct positive effect is still indicative of a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on belief. As for the hypothesized imitation effect on DOI, only the impact of frequency imitation on relative advantage and trait imitation on compatibility were not supported.

The insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant correlation between frequency-based imitation and relative advantage suggests that an increase in the number of ERP users does not necessarily signify that the ERP system under evaluation is superior to the existing system it is going to supersede. An increase in the number of ERP users may only suggest that a company evaluating an ERP system could face increased pressure to adopt the system to gain legitimacy or recognition, Hence, the relationship between frequency-based imitation and relative advantage is not supported by our results. In addition, from a committee point of view, a big company using an ERP system is not a proof that the system is more compatible, but could be a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the vast amount of resources that a bigger and more successful company has to make the system compatible with its daily routines and activities. This rationale is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 7.3. Post-hoc analysis

## 7.3.1. Effects of adoption time

Early ERP adopters may invest more resources to investigate the suitability and effect of implementing an ERP system. Hence, we performed a time-based analysis of the point in time at which an ERP system was adopted to provide better empirical understanding of how ERP beliefs change over time during the adoption process. In this analysis, our respondents were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three adopter categories – early adopters, early majority, and late majority – which are the three middle categories of the original <sup>fi</sup>ve adopter categories proposed by Rogers [55]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst (pioneers) and last (laggards) categories were excluded from our categorization scheme because of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in differentiating the ‘extreme’ relative earliness or lateness of ERP adoption. Although our scheme may not precisely correspond with the adoption percentages recommended in Rogers' diffusion cycle, the use of ‘years of adoption’ to establish adoption categories provides a logical structure for evaluating the effects of DOI and imitative forces on belief over time.

As shown in Table 7, although the effects of the three DOI forces on belief are mostly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant among the three adopter categories, their magnitudes decrease steadily from early adopters to early majority to late majority. These results suggest that rigorous, technical ERP evaluation forces are most important in the shaping of the beliefs of early adopters, but that such forces are decreasingly in<sup>fl</sup>uential when ERP systems are adopted in the latter phases of the diffusion cycle. When imitative behaviors are evaluated over time, a reverse trend is observed. The beliefs of early adopters are found to be least in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by imitative forces, followed by early majority and late majority at an increasing rate. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide evidence that imitative forces have the strongest impact on beliefs when organizations adopt ERP

## 7.3.2. Effects of implementation extent

The relationship between imitative/DOI forces and ERP beliefs can also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the extent of ERP implementation. In this study, we captured implementation extent by examining the ERP modules that were adopted at the time of our survey. Following Ranganthan and Brown's [53] classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme, ERP implementation extent was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into four levels, based on the extent of the adoption of value-chain (material management, operations, sales and distribution, supply chain management, and customer services) and enterprise-support (<sup>fi</sup>nance, human resources, and data warehouse) modules. An organization is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a level-1 implementer if it has adopted only enterprise-support modules, a level-2 implementer if it has adopted one value-chain module (with or without enterprisesupport modules), a level-3 implementer if it has adopted two or three value-chain modules (with or without enterprise-support modules). and a level-4 implementer if it has adopted a full suite or selected suite of four value-chain modules). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme are presented in Table 8.

Most of our responding organizations (69.7%) are in the level-3 or level-4 category, meaning that they have adopted at least two valuechain modules, with or without enterprise-support modules. The results (see Table 8) clearly show the pattern of the impact of DOI and imitative forces on belief. In the DOI model, the impact is the lowest when the extent of ERP implementation is the smallest. However, the impact jumps sharply when one value-chain module is adopted. This impact increases steadily from level 2 to level 4, which suggests the increasing importance of logical evaluation as a company acquires more ERP experience and the impact of implementation becomes more critical. In the imitation model, however, a reverse trend is seen. When the implementation extent is the lowest, imitative forces are found to have the highest impact on belief. The magnitude of this impact weakens dramatically when one value-chain module is adopted. With the addition of more value-chain modules from level 2 to level 4, the impact steadily tapers off, though not dramatically, which suggests that ERP committees demonstrate gradually lower levels of imitative behavior as investment becomes critical.

## 8. Conclusions and implications

Both DOI and imitation models are found to affect the belief of the ERP steering committee. As discussed earlier, the selection of either model could depend entirely on a researcher's research objective and preference. Our integrated model is also found to be useful in explaining variances in the belief of ERP steering committee. Most of the independent and integrative paths proposed in the model were found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing, either directly or indirectly, the belief of steering committee for ERP adoption. As depicted in Table 5, almost all proposed relations are supported empirically. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, a number of implications for both researchers and practitioners can be drawn from the results of the study.

First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that imitation plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in affecting the beliefs of ERP steering committees. When organizations imitate successful practices based on the frequency of their adoption by high-status, successful organizations, differentiation among organizations may weaken and eventually they will become more homogeneous [24,43]. Homogenization means that organizations have chosen standardized best practices, which can be easily copied and reproduced by competitors, over uniqueness. The use of an ERP system also forces its adopters to modify their business processes to the best practices functionally delivered in the software, which also makes organizations more homogeneous [9]. Research is thus needed to investigate whether imitation and ERP adoption cause organizations to conform and become increasingly homogeneous over time. The loss of uniqueness could possibly contribute to the weakening of an organization's core competencies, which can be utilized to create value for the organization and are also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for its competitors to copy. Hence, it would also be interesting to explore the correlation between homogenization and core competencies in the context of ERP or enterprise systems.

Second, although the three types of imitative behavior are in<sup>fl</sup>uential in the logical evaluation of ERP adoption, their interaction processes are blurred and deserve deeper examination. Imitation can be a powerful engine for organizational transformation, but this force could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by external environmental and organizational factors. For example, an external environment characterized by uncertainty might increase the strength of the interactions because of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in making logical judgments due to very limited information. Organizations might depend more heavily on imitation and less on logical behavior when making an optimal cost-effective adoption decision. However, an organizational characteristic such as innovativeness might reduce the magnitude of the interactions, as innovative organizations are more likely to adopt novel solutions to solve problems and less likely to imitate the actions of others. Accordingly, the ERP adoption decisions of these organizations will likely be based largely on systematic evaluations and logical conclusions. Thus, research into the moderating effects of environment, competition, and innovativeness on the relationship between imitative/logical behaviors and ERP evaluation would be worthwhile.

Third, although we evaluated the impact of ERP implementation extent on the relationship between belief and behavior, we did not adopt a more sophisticated conceptualization of implementation extent. The three dimensions of breadth, volume, and diversity of ERP assimilation put forward by Liang et al. [36] constitute important steps in this direction. Similarly, the differentiation between early and late ERP adopters was based on the year of ERP adoption, which prohibited us from classifying organizations into the <sup>fi</sup>ner categories of pioneers, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards [55,56] and comparing the distinctiveness of their changes in belief over time. Future researchers could reference diffusion of innovation theory [55] to position the design of longitudinal investigations of belief to gain a better understanding of its temporal evolution.

Fourth, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research also have important practical implications for ERP beliefs and how these beliefs are affected by logical and imitation forces. The study has provided strong evidence that ERP belief is affected by imitation forces, along with the rational technical evaluation forces, with or without conscious knowledge of them. Imitating others is not necessarily a disadvantage; sometimes it can bring second-mover advantages and be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the company. However, practitioners need to be aware of the presence of these forces and the consequences they can bring, so that a suitable judgment can be made that leads to the maximization of returns for companies.

Fifth, a similar observation can be made about ERP promotion regarding ERP vendors and end users. In promoting their products, these vendors should focus on not only the technical merits of their systems but also on the successful application of these systems among leading organizations to validate the beliefs of their clients about ERP, which will make clients more likely to adopt such systems. End users, in contrast, seem to be more responsive to usage promotion based on technical values such as task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t [20] and perceived ease of use and usefulness [14]. However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide preliminary evidence that ERP beliefs are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by imitative forces, which can be exploited by practitioners to promote ERP among end users as a form of vicarious learning [8]. In this context, end users are encouraged to use an ERP system as doing so is the practice among large numbers of organizations, particularly those of higher status, with proven success. Thus, the use of an ERP system not only secures the legitimacy and performance of an organization, but it also helps in terms of work performance, personal credentials, and ego ful<sup>fi</sup>llment in being among industry leaders.

Finally, our research also provides initial evidence of two interesting effects of imitation and DOI on belief. First, an increase in ERP implementation extent diminishes the effect of imitation and increases the effect of DOI on belief. Second, DOI has more impact on belief when an ERP system is adopted at a relatively early stage, whereas imitation is more in<sup>fl</sup>uential in a latter stage. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have implications for vendors and consultants in that they could categorize potential clients based on their implementation extent and adoption stage and set up different marketing strategies for each sector. In-house IT professionals, however, should avoid jumping on the ERP bandwagon. One possible solution is to set up evaluation protocols and practices to avoid hasty decision making in any given set of circumstances. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that IT practitioners should be aware that a dilemma will always exist between the urge to imitate versus the need to evaluate. They must be very careful in striking a balance between the two in their search for the best solution for their organizations.

## 9. Limitations

Some limitations of this study are worth mentioning. First, our assessment of DOI–imitation integration is based on only one form of IT, that is, ERP systems. A more thorough evaluation of a wider range of IT would provide a better understanding of the effects of integrating these two models. Second, our study aimed to conduct interviews with top managers within ERP adoption committees. However, we were not able to solicit more than one respondent from each of the responding companies to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Although we believe that ERP adoption decisions are the result of the consensus of opinion of ERP steering committee members, and that the ERP beliefs of the members might be congruent, this supposition may be erroneous in a few of the cases. Hence, the multiple-response approach is more appropriate to explore organizational beliefs about ERP adoption.
