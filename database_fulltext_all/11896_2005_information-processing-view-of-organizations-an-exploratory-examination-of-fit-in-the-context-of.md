ABSTRACT: This study uses Galbraith’s information processing theory to examine the fit between information processing needs and information processing capability in an interorganizational supply chain context and to examine its effect on performance. Information processing needs are assessed based on various characteristics of the product and procurement environment and information processing capabilities are assessed by the level of information technology support for various activities in the procurement life cycle. A taxonomy of information processing needs and information processing capabilities is developed. The effect of the fit between information processing needs and capabilities on procurement performance is examined. The study collected data on 142 products through personal interviews and surveys, used cluster analytic techniques to develop taxonomies, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the fit between needs and capability, modeled as an interaction effect. The results reveal two clusters for information processing needs and three clusters for information processing capability. ANOVA results show that the interactive effect of information needs and capability has a significant effect on performance, supporting our fit theory.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: B2B, cluster analysis, information processing capability, information processing needs, information processing theory, interorganizational systems, strategic fit, supply chain.

SUPPLY CHAIN MANAGEMENT (SCM) has attracted significant attention in recent years, as it has become the next frontier for process reengineering through extensive information technology (IT) deployment. The successful deployment of new IT-enabled processes for reducing inefficiencies in internal operations has motivated firms to examine interorganizational business processes where there are significant operational inefficiencies. However, these processes span trading partners with different business objectives and stakeholders. Reengineering the IT-enabled interorganizational processes is quite complex, since the entire supply chain consisting of multiple trading partners with a wide range of communication and IT needs have to be considered. There needs to be a good fit between the technology capability and the requirements of the business process. The initial failures of business-to-business (B2B) electronic marketplaces serve as a reminder that force-fitting technology in incompatible situations will not result in successful outcomes. Hence, both practitioners and researchers are interested in understanding the linkage between technology capability, interorganizational relationships, and supply chain management requirements [12, 16, 27, 46, 63].

Studies on interorganizational systems (IOS) examine a variety of factors influencing IOS, including governance structure, interorganizational relationships, trust, power, dependence, competitive pressures, and other factors (for a review, see [14]). Most of these studies use the deterministic perspective of innovation adoption that new IOS technologies are inherently good for organizations. However, few studies explain IOS implementation from an organizational information processing view that emphasizes the notion of fit between a firm’s information processing needs and information processing capability [22]. Whereas most early scholarly work applied the information processing theory to internal organizational issues [33, 35], in recent years the theory has been extended to study interorganizational interactions [2]. The dramatic growth in information processing capability for interorganizational interactions provides a great opportunity for empirically testing this theory in the new environment. Further, the concept of fit, which is popular in strategy research [59], has been explored only to a limited extent in information systems (IS) research, primarily on alignment of IS strategic plan with business plan, and task–technology fit [35, 51]. Thus, empirically examining the interaction of information processing needs and information processing capability in the interorganizational domain is a significant contribution to this stream of research as well as to research on implementation of IS.

Although studies in other areas have attempted to develop taxonomies on interorganizational relationships [9], IS research mostly uses existing taxonomies without empirically validating them [13]. Most classificatory empirical studies fall into two broad categories—studies that focus on testing hypothesized classification schemes or typologies and studies that uncover natural taxonomies [2]. Typically, typologies formulated based on theoretical and conceptual foundations and past management information systems (MIS) research have focused on testing typologies. A common problem with typologies is that they may not be comprehensive, owing either to limited focus of the underlying theoretical foundations or to the assumptions that constrain the development of a comprehensive model. This study takes a novel approach to develop empirically taxonomies for information processing needs and information processing capability.

The primary objectives of this study are to:

• develop taxonomies of “information processing needs” based on various characteristics of the product and procurement environment, and of “information processing capabilities” based on the level of IT support for various activities in the procurement life cycle (PLC), and

• formulate and empirically test the information processing theory’s concept of fit between the information processing needs and capability.

## Theoretical Foundations

OUR RESEARCH IS BASED UPON GALBRAITH’S [22] information processing theory. In this section, we discuss this theory, the role of uncertainty, and the concept of fit.

## Information Processing Theory

One stream of research that is particularly relevant to our study is the information processing view of organizations. This theory identifies three important concepts— information processing needs, information processing capability, and the fit between the two to obtain optimal performance [22]. Organizations need quality information to cope with environmental uncertainty and improve their decision making [56]. Environmental uncertainty stems from the complexity of the environment and dynamism, or the frequency of changes to various environmental variables [20]. Typically, organizations have two strategies to cope with uncertainty and increased information needs: (1) develop buffers to reduce the effect of uncertainty, and (2) implement structural mechanisms and information processing capability to enhance the information flow and thereby reduce uncertainty. A classic example of the first strategy is building inventory buffers to reduce the effect of uncertainty in demand or supply; another example is adding extra safety buffers in product design due to uncertainty in product working conditions. An example of the second strategy is the redesign of business processes in organizations and implementation of integrated IS that improve information flow and reduce uncertainty within organizational subunits. A similar strategy is creating better information flow between organizations to address the uncertainties in the supply chain.

## Uncertainty

Uncertainty in interorganizational interactions is greater, since two organizations that have different business objectives and stakeholders are involved in a transaction. Quite often, the parameters for the transaction are suboptimal for one or both partners, prompting them to engage in opportunistic behavior to exploit the uncertainty (lack of information) to their benefit. Typically, transactions are made with imperfect information accompanied by varying levels of information asymmetry between the trading parties. In such an environment, research on organizational interdependence, power, and trust are important in understanding the sociopolitical aspects of the interaction [56, 59].

Given the complex relationships between multiple organizations, various strategies are developed to optimize relationship performance [4, 54]. Trust minimizes the risk and, thereby, uncertainty in interorganizational transactions [29]. Trust in the partner provides clear expectations of the actions of the partner and reduces the costs incurred in developing detailed contracts and monitoring compliance.

Uncertainty is also a central theme in the transaction cost economics (TCE) field, which focuses on identifying the appropriate governance structure for transactions that minimizes the total cost [61]. Uncertainty can arise through lack of information on the firm’s partners and their actions. Uncertainty causes higher transaction costs, prompting the participating firms to migrate toward a governance structure that minimizes the transaction cost. Firms develop long-term relationships with a few suppliers and make relationship-specific investments to minimize the uncertainty. Williamson [62] acknowledges the existence of various forms of hybrid governance structures between the two extremes of markets and hierarchies.

## The Fit Concept

The concept of fit is a core construct in the information processing view of the organization. Achieving a fit between the information processing needs and the information processing capability to attain optimal organizational performance has been a primary focus of organizational designers [18, 22, 57]. Daft and Lengel [18] argue that the amount and richness of information processing and communication media must match with the level of task uncertainty. They expound the contingency design theory, positing that the fit between information needs and information processing capability results in better unit performance. Few studies examine the notion of fit between information needs and capability, primarily at the task level [34, 45]. In the strategic IS literature, Chan et al. [10] suggest that the alignment between business strategic orientation and IS strategic orientation significantly affects both IS effectiveness and business performance. In the interorganizational context, the notion of fit was conceptualized and empirically tested, first, by Bensaou and Venkatraman [2]. However, no further studies expand on that concept, or examine fit in greater detail in the context of IOS research.

Fit, although intuitive from a theoretical perspective, is an elusive concept for empirical research. Although it is easy to theorize the concept, its operationalization and empirical testing with an appropriate statistical procedure is still a major issue. Galbraith and Nathanson comment, “Although the concept of fit is a useful one, it lacks the precise definition needed to test and recognize whether an organization has it or not” [23, p. 266]. Drazin and Van de Ven [19] identify three approaches to evaluation of fit in contingency research—selection, interaction, and systems approach. Venkatraman [59] provides an excellent overview of various forms of fit, statistical methods used for analysis, and the implicit assumptions made in the theoretical formulation and empirical analysis. His study primarily focuses on strategy research in which the concept of fit is extensively used in developing taxonomies of strategies [26, 41] to assess internal congruence [42] and to evaluate fit between strategy and structure [11]. Although conceptualized in the context of strategy research, it is applicable in other disciplines as well. Umanath [58] provides an excellent description of these concepts in the context of IS research.

Venkatraman [59] identifies six different perspectives of fit—moderation, mediation, matching, gestalts, profile deviation, and covariation. Whereas the first two— moderation and mediation—are traditional methods to test for causal effects and are extensively used in MIS research, the other methods are used infrequently. Umanath [58] identifies three broad categories of fit—congruence, contingency, and holistic. Whereas the first is a criterion-free specification based on bivariate examination of correlation, the second is an interaction-specification modeled as moderation or mediation effect, and the third is a systems specification that corresponds to the “gestalt” or “profile deviation” approach of Venkatraman [59] and is tested using pattern analysis. There is a high level of correspondence between Umanath’s and Venkatraman’s classification of fit.

## Research Model

WE USE THE INFORMATION PROCESSING VIEW of organization as the basic foundation for our research model shown in Figure 1. The research model illustrates the two main constructs of our study—information processing needs and information processing capability. Consistent with information processing theory and the definition of fit, as described earlier, we define the information processing needs, information processing capability, the fit between the two, and the effect of fit on performance. We develop taxonomies of information processing needs of an organization based on a variety of theoretically identified factors that contribute to uncertainty in the interactions. In line with the suggestions of Venkatraman [59] for developing taxonomies, we identify categories of products based on the internal coherence of a set of attributes that define the information processing needs of the product. Prior studies have used a similar approach to classifying interorganizational relationships [2, 9]. This approach examines feasible sets of internally consistent and equally effective configurations of multiple variables with minimal assumptions on the relationship between variables. Typically, an inductive technique such as cluster analysis is used. To build rigor into the analysis, based on the recommendations of Venkatraman [59], we (1) use formal statistical tools to test the number of clusters, (2) demonstrate cluster stability using cross validation of results, and (3) describe the clusters based on the theory that guided the selection of input variables.

Rather than using a single measure of information processing capability, we identify clusters of products with similar information processing capability based on the IT support for various procurement activities [38]. This conceptualization helps to develop clusters based on support from different types of technologies for the various/different activities in the PLC. Hence, this provides a more comprehensive assessment of information processing capability.

Fit is a complex construct that captures the interaction between needs and capabilities. As discussed by Venkatraman [59], there are various ways to conceptualize the fit between the two variables, but the choice should be driven by theoretical foundations and empirical measurement. In our study, we determined that “fit as matching” in Venkatraman’s [59] taxonomy is the best conceptualization of fit, which is consistent with prior theoretical foundations [22] and empirical assessment [2]. In this conceptualization “fit is a theoretically defined match between two related variables” [59, p. 430]. This conceptualization is also similar to the “interaction” approach to evaluation of fit suggested by Drazin and Van de Ven [19]. Although Venkatraman [59] conceptualizes this fit (as matching) as criterion-free—that is, specified without reference to a criterion variable—he agrees that, subsequently, its effect on a criterion variable could be examined. Venkatraman [59] posits that the interaction effect of the two variables will have a significant effect on the outcome variable of interest to the researcher. Since the two variables are categorical in our study, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) model is used to test the impact of interaction effects on procurement performance.

## Information Processing Needs

Information processing needs are defined as the communication requirements for interorganizational interactions in the context of a supply chain—specifically, product procurement. Information processing needs are captured by two forms of uncertainty, environmental and partnership uncertainty, reflective of the uncertainty in the product and procurement environment. Rather than directly measuring information processing needs on a set of dimensions, we adopt a holistic perspective of examining the internal coherence among a set of attributes that assesses the different uncertainties and, thereby, the information processing needs in an organization. Such an approach is used in prior studies [2]. This approach makes minimal assumptions on the relationships among the underlying variables. Since the context of the study is interorganizational interactions, we use Bensaou and Venkatraman’s [2] conceptualization of uncertainty in our model. They identify three categories of uncertainty—environmental uncertainty, partnership uncertainty, and task uncertainty. Environmental uncertainty arises due to general environmental conditions underlying the interorganizational business relations; partnership uncertainty emerges from one firm’s perceived uncertainty about its specific partner’s behavior; and task uncertainty arises from the characteristics of the task in various procurement activities, including task analyzability, task variety, and task interdependence. Task uncertainty was not included in this study for two reasons. First, our analysis conducted is at the product level, and since there are multiple tasks or procurement activities between two business partners for a single product and they could vary in their task characteristics for the same product, it would be difficult to develop a single measure as a representative activity for the product. For example, task characteristics for the search activity would be very different from those of the ordering activity. Furthermore, selection of one specific task may not provide a comprehensive assessment of uncertainty in that relationship. Second, our initial reservations were reinforced by the weak results reported in Bensaou and Venkatraman’s [2] study, where two of the three task uncertainty variables were found to be not significantly different between the different clusters of interorganizational relationships.

## Environmental Uncertainty

Researchers in organizational theory identify two major dimensions for uncertainty— complexity and dynamism [20, 42]. Whereas complexity captures the number of factors and their interactions relevant to decision making, dynamism captures the relative rate of change to those factors and the ability to predict those changes. In this study, we use product description complexity to represent the first dimension, and technology uncertainty, demand uncertainty, and supply uncertainty to capture the dimension of dynamism. We use the term uncertainty in all these variables, rather than dynamism, to be consistent with the terminology used in prior studies. In the procurement context, the changes in technology, product demand, and supply are the major factors that influence the information processing needs [1, 3, 30, 60]. We include a fifth variable, product criticality, to reflect the importance of the uncertainty (captured in the product dimensions) on the information processing needs.

Product description complexity contributes to uncertainty in the transaction [37]. A complex product without standard specifications requires greater interaction and information sharing with trading partners, more extensive search for suppliers, and joint action in the design and manufacturing process [44]. Firms prefer tight integration between the trading partners to facilitate easy information flow [9, 40]. The type and amount of information to be shared between trading partners is reflective of the product description complexity.

Technology uncertainty or (un)predictability significantly affects procurement activity, particularly the governance structure for the transaction [30, 60]. It refers to the inability to forecast accurately the technical or design requirements for the product. Frequent improvements in product functionalities, manufacturing process innovation, and changes in design of the components create significant information needs for the trading partners. Firms may need to exchange information more frequently with their trading partners, as well as to learn about new technology innovations and vendors’ capabilities.

Demand uncertainty reflects the changes in demand for the product being procured and the inability to accurately predict these fluctuations [60]. Buyers require timely information communication and tighter information linkages with their suppliers to communicate the frequent changes. Since the primary strategy for reducing demand uncertainty is the timely availability of relevant information, systems that provide near-real-time structured information to trading partners are preferred.

Supply uncertainty represents the dynamism in the supply market in terms of availability of supplies, stability of the suppliers, consistency in quality, and prices. The buyer has to match the market to the firm’s product needs. Significant supply market dynamism generates procurement risk for the buyer [9]. Under such conditions, firms prefer more information for decision making.

Product criticality is an important factor that determines the extent of monitoring required for the product and is therefore reflected in the information processing needs. It is likely that the firms share information more extensively with the suppliers of a critical product to minimize stock-outs that could lead to stoppage of operations. In a manufacturing firm, direct materials that are required for daily production are more likely to be closely monitored with daily or hourly updates from suppliers compared to some maintenance, repair, and operations (MRO) items. It influences information exchange and the relationship between partners [9].

## Partnership Uncertainty

A significant source of uncertainty for a firm is the uncertainty in the relationship with its trading partners, which can further aggravate environmental uncertainty. IOS research describes this uncertainty in terms of two types of transaction risk—operations risk and opportunism risk [15]. A firm faces operations risk due to the possibility of the partner’s underperforming in its contract, or opportunism risk of being locked up in a relationship with relationship-specific investments that do not have value outside that relationship. Bensaou and Venkatraman [2] identify three variables to capture the uncertainty in the partnership—mutual trust, firm’s asset specificity or relationshipspecific investment, and partner’s asset specificity. These three variables capture the two risk dimensions that create the uncertainty in the relationship. We use a similar conceptualization in our research model.

Investments by the firm and the supplier specific to the relationship for this product provide a strong signal to the other partner about their desire for long-term relationships [24]. A tight interorganizational relationship requires significant information exchange to support that relationship [49]. Researchers examining sociological factors identify trust as an important variable that affects interorganizational relationships [29]. Trust refers to confidence that the behavior of the other party conforms to one’s own expectations, and reduces the parties’ need to monitor each other [3]. Greater trust may also symbolize a shared set of sentiments of belongingness [55]. Thus, trust in the supplier reduces the perception of risk associated with opportunistic behavior [24], and therefore encourages greater information sharing between the two firms. On the other hand, firms with low levels of mutual trust restrict themselves to transmitting only those pieces of information that are operationally necessary [2]. Further, firms may make greater relationship-specific investments to cope with or reduce the uncertainty emerging from searching (for suppliers) and negotiating. In the presence of greater trust, reduced information exchange may be adequate due to lesser need for monitoring. However, as noted above, in line with extant research [2, 3, 24, 55], we consider investments specific to the product transactions and trust as sources of partnership uncertainty rather than coping strategies to reduce uncertainty. The level of partnership uncertainty for that product is represented in the information processing needs for the product.

## Information Processing Capability

Consistent with the study’s objective of understanding IOS usage, information processing capability is defined as the level of IT support for various activities of the PLC. We examine IT support at a more granular level of analysis than Bensaou and Venkatraman [2]. Conceptualizing various interorganizational interactions using the PLC and examining IT support using a range of technologies for each activity in PLC is a unique contribution of this study. Most studies in the past have examined the use of a single technology (e.g., electronic data interchange [EDI]) at the firm level. Developing categories of information processing capabilities based on the range of technology support for procurement activities provides a more accurate classification and is a better reflection of IOS support.

## Communications Technologies

Exchanges between buyers and sellers and therefore the technologies used can be classified into three broad categories—one-to-one, one-to-many, and many-to-many [32]. One-to-one (1:1), or dyadic, interactions were the predominant mode of interaction for many years. Most were noncomputer-based communication methods including face-to-face, paper, telephone, and fax technologies. Subsequently, EDI led to conversion of noncomputer to computer-based processes through direct communication between computers without manual intervention. Although EDI brought significant benefits due to automation of the communication of structured information, implementation was slow, because documents had to be standardized across industries and electronic capability made available on both ends of the dyad.

The change in the structure of communication from a dyadic to a one-to-many (1:m) interaction became possible with private exchanges that leveraged the potential of the Internet to provide Web-based access to many suppliers using a single interface. Qualified suppliers, using a customizable Web interface, access a variety of information on their supplies such as purchase orders, current order status, demand forecasts, delivery schedules, inventory status, shipping, vendor ratings, and so on. Additional facilities for e-mail, chat, and other real-time coordination capability are sometimes integrated in the interface. Although these exchanges are well suited for existing suppliers, they do not provide any support in the search and negotiation phase for identifying new suppliers, or evaluating and negotiating with different suppliers.

Collaborative public e-markets transform the communication structure between buyers and sellers by creating many-to-many (m:n) interfaces for the entire market to participate and thereby increase the visibility to all parties. In theory, their large number of participants provides liquidity to sellers, and choice and price competition to buyers. However, the large number of unknown participants raises the uncertainty and transaction risk. Further, they do not support all the activities of the PLC, as most of these marketplaces are designed for search and fast price negotiation through auction mechanisms, rather than for order coordination and management. Firms must develop their own electronic dyadic links for order coordination and management activities. Public e-markets can be facilitated through an independent intermediary or an industry-owned consortium. Industry-owned consortium-based e-markets strive to strike a balance between private and public e-markets by allowing the selection of a preferred supplier list to reduce the uncertainty and transaction risk, and better facilities for order coordination. The buyer-based industry consortia raise concerns among suppliers, as they worry about their becoming cartels for buyers that squeeze out all profits for them. In our study, we consider four technology categories— noncomputer, EDI, private exchanges, and public e-markets.

## Procurement Life Cycle

Although procurement is often treated as a single activity, it consists of multiple activities, as exemplified in the stages of PLC [36]. Typically, the PLC consists of five stages—search, negotiation and pricing or price discovery, ordering, order coordination, and payment. The search stage involves developing the product specifications and searching for potential suppliers; the negotiation and pricing or price discovery stage includes evaluating suppliers and negotiating prices; the order stage is the formal contracting process with the issue of a purchase order; the order coordination or management stage involves coordinating with the trading partner for delivery scheduling and shipping; and the last stage involves payment and order completion. Each stage has different communication needs. Although some stages may use unstructured communication that is difficult to automate, such as communicating customized product specifications or price negotiations, other stages involve straightforward, structured transaction communication, such as orders, shipments, invoices, and so on.

Research from practice suggests that firms choose a portfolio of technologies to suit their various procurement needs. For example, Starr et al. [53] illustrate the procurement portfolio of Dow Chemical, which clearly highlights the role of product transaction characteristics in determining the appropriate technology. They use six different options:

1. One-on-one (1:1)—set of direct connections for fulfillment (i.e., payment and order coordination) of strategic materials.

2. E-mart (m:n)—internal ordering tool with multivendor catalog for nonproduction goods.

3. Elemica (m:n)—consortium e-market for fulfillment (i.e., payment and order coordination) of production materials.

4. Trade-Ranger (m:n)—consortium e-market for sourcing, pricing, and fulfillment (i.e., payment and order coordination) of nonproduction goods.

5. ChemConnect (m:n)—independent e-market for commodity raw materials— sourcing and pricing.

6. SciQuest (m:n)—independent e-market for laboratory scientific supplies.

Starr et al. [53] further suggest that independent e-markets are better suited for lowrisk trading such as purchasing MRO and nonproduction goods, finding new trading partners, and spot trading on commodities. Private e-markets and one-to-one interactions are preferred for purchasing critical production materials that are complex and require deep collaboration with proprietary data sharing. Although industry-owned consortia may develop the capability for deep collaboration, firms are likely to be reluctant to use these facilities due to competitive compulsions, and the consortia may run the risk of losing transaction liquidity and thereby be of limited value to their participants.

Information processing capability is captured by the support of these technologies for various activities of the PLC. Organizations, depending on the product characteristics, may choose to use different technologies in the different PLC phases. Clusters of products with similar technology support indicate groups with similar information processing capability. It must be noted that our focus is on IOS technologies, and we are not considering other information processing capabilities that are complementary to the IOS technologies, as they may be invariant across multiple products within the same organization.

## Fit—Information Processing Needs and Capability

The research model posits, based on information processing theory, that the fit between the information processing needs and capability influences performance. Bensaou and Venkatraman [2] examine the fit between the two variables by evaluating the characteristics of the interorganizational interactions and information sharing among the six clusters (2×3) formed by the interaction of these two variables. Although they do not specifically test the effect of fit on performance, they indirectly test it as a test of external validity for cluster analysis by comparing the means of performance among the six clusters. In a recent study, Shah et al. [52] develop a 4×4 matrix of various categories of interorganizational relationships and IOS support. The interorganizational relationship categories range from arms-length to coordination. Although they depict governance structure, they describe the information needs to sustain the relationship. They posit that each of these categories requires different types of IOS to support procurement interactions. They suggest four types of IOS technologies, ranging from nonelectronic integration to high levels of electronic integration, and describe the characteristics of each. They further posit that organizations in the diagonal cells in their matrix represent the best fit between interorganizational relationship needs and IOS technology support for information, and therefore exhibit better procurement performance compared to organizations in off-diagonal cells. In a similar fashion, in this study, we examine whether there are differences in the outcome among the cells.

In summary, the research model examines the fit between information processing needs and information processing capability and the effect of the fit on procurement performance. Two forms of uncertainty, environmental and partnership uncertainty, reflect the “need” for information exchange. Environmental uncertainty is captured using product description complexity, technology uncertainty, demand uncertainty, supply uncertainty, and product criticality. Partnership uncertainty is captured using focal firm investment (into the relationship), supplier investment (into the relationship), and trust. Clusters of products with similar characteristics of uncertainty and, therefore, information needs are generated. Information processing “capability” is assessed as the support for procurement activities in PLC with four technologies— noncomputer, EDI, private Web, and public e-market. Clusters of information processing capability based on similar characteristics in technology support are formed. The fit between the two variables, modeled using the “fit as matching” perspective, is examined as the impact of the interaction effect between the two variables on procurement performance.

## Research Methodology

## Data Collection

SINCE THIS STUDY EXAMINES A COMPLEX INTERACTION of management practices and technology based on a comprehensive evaluation of procurement activities at the product level, data were collected through face-to-face interaction with persons responsible for sourcing and procuring products. To facilitate the gathering of detailed data, we augmented individual interviews with a standardized survey instrument (making use of measurement scales that are well grounded in theory). Hence, a semistructured interview process, combined with a survey instrument, was used to collect both qualitative and quantitative information. The survey instrument measured the variables using multiple indicator items derived from validated instruments used in prior studies. The interview process provided information on the organizational context, which sometimes may not be adequately reflected in a survey instrument. We believe the interviews helped us to understand the procurement decision-making process, allowed us to more deeply explore survey responses, and better informed our data analysis.

## Sample

Since product characteristics have a key role in influencing the transactional environment and therefore supply chain integration, we focused on manufacturing industries, which provided us with a wide variety of transactional environments. Initially, one researcher briefed the participants on the study objectives and the data collection method. Each participant was requested to select two different products among those that they were responsible for buying. After completing the survey, they participated in an open discussion with the researchers that lasted for one to two hours. Obtaining qualitative and quantitative information from multiple respondents in a firm provided rich information on their supply chain, product characteristics, supplier relationships, procurement philosophies, and their IT infrastructure. We must acknowledge that this method of data collection provides a richer and more accurate representation of the research context, but at a significant cost of interviewer time. We collected data on procurement practices for 142 products from 84 buyers/procurement managers representing 22 organizations, providing us a sample size of 142 for analysis. The sample characteristics are provided in Table 1.

The participating firms were in diverse manufacturing industries and were generally large firms with sales revenue ranging from \$300 million to \$17 billion. Most participants were senior buyers or purchase managers with significant procurement experience. The products included both direct materials (67 percent) and indirect materials (33 percent).

## Measurement

The eight constructs used to assess information needs were measured with multi-item indicators. Validated scales from prior studies were used in most cases. The Appendix lists the measurement indicators and the research support for operationalization. The indicators were appropriately phrased as statements and measured with a seven-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree). Information processing capability was assessed based on a dichotomous scale of the use of four technologies—noncomputer, EDI, private Web, and public e-market. The number of activities in which a particular technology is used provided a measure of use of that technology for that product. This is similar to the “breadth” measurement in EDI usage, where the number of transaction sets using EDI was assessed [39]. We expanded that notion to multiple technologies. The use of these technologies in 11 procurement activities that are representative of the five stages of PLC provided a measure of information processing capability. Each time a technology was used for a PLC activity, it received one point. The points were totaled across procurement activities for each of the four technologies to yield a score for information processing capability. Performance was assessed with six items measuring various dimensions of procurement performance.

The results of convergent validity testing indicate that all indicator items load on the theorized constructs, and the t-tests of all the loadings suggest they are significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The various fit indices in Table 3 (normed fit index [NFI], comparative fit index [CFI], and root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA]) are all satisfactory [5, 6]. Another test of convergent validity is that the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed the variance attributable to measurement error (i.e., $\mathrm { A V E } \geq 0 . 5 0 )$ ). The AVE values are listed along the diagonal in the correlation matrix, and they are all greater than the threshold value of 0.5.

Discriminant validity evaluates whether the indicator items load on the theorized construct and not on others. It was examined using Fornell and Larcker’s [21] recommendation that the square root of AVE for each construct should exceed all correlations between that and other constructs. This test is a stronger test of discriminant validity compared to the pairwise comparison of chi-square values of unconstrained and constrained CFA models [21]. Based on the square root of the diagonal elements in Table 2, we notice that this value is greater than the correlations between that and other constructs.

The reliability of the measurement scales was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha. All scales (except demand uncertainty) exhibited adequate reliability, above the normally accepted threshold of 0.7 [47], as shown in Table 2. Thus, we conclude that all the constructs exhibited adequate psychometric properties.

An important test for data collected from a single respondent is the test for common method bias. Harmon’s single-factor test was used [48]. If more than 25 percent of the variance is explained by a single factor, then we can suspect some common method bias. We performed a factor analysis loading all the 27 items measuring the eight constructs into a single factor. The results indicate that the factor explained about 25.9 percent of the variance, which is on the cutoff value. More than 13 items did not load on the factor, even at the 0.30 level. Given the close relationship between some of the variables (firm and supplier asset specificity), it can be expected that the items that measure these variables load together, if forced into a single-factor model, thereby explaining a larger variance. The average interitem correlation was 0.17, which is much lower than the correlation reported for the significant results. Hence, we can conclude that there was no significant common-methods bias.

Since, in our study, a single respondent completed multiple responses, it was necessary to ensure that the responses on different products were independent. A variety of measures were taken to ensure the independence of the responses. During our interview, we specifically gave instructions to our respondents to choose two products that were very different within their domain and supplied by different vendors. We even queried them on the products before they started filling out the survey. In order to ensure that they followed our instruction, paired t-tests of the study variables across the two responses from a single respondent were conducted. The results (in Table 4) indicate that the responses were dissimilar for all study variables constituting information processing needs, except “trust,” supporting the lack of response bias and our confidence that the respondents indeed chose dissimilar products.

## Results—Cluster Analysis

CLUSTER ANALYSIS WAS USED TO IDENTIFY products with similar information processing needs [25]. Cluster analysis classifies objects so that each is similar to others within the cluster, thereby exhibiting high internal (within-cluster) homogeneity and high external (between-cluster) heterogeneity [25, 31]. There are three major stages in the analysis—partitioning, interpretation, and validation.

## Partitioning

The partitioning stage requires making decisions on various parameters that determine the development of clusters, including (1) the identification of variables for grouping, (2) the algorithm to be used for placing similar objects into clusters, and (3) the number of clusters to be generated. Although the objective of cluster analysis is to understand the natural grouping of items, the entire exercise becomes atheoretical if we are unable to understand and explain the groupings. Hence, prior theory should drive the first decision of selection of variables, as in our study, where the research model drove the selection of variables. The second decision involves choosing between two broad categories of clustering algorithms—hierarchical and nonhierarchical or k-means cluster. The nonhierarchical method has become popular since it is less susceptible to outliers in the data, inclusion of inappropriate variables, and the distance measure. However, since it is sensitive to the initial seed, a critical factor is the selection of appropriate seeds for the initial cluster. Researchers recommend combining the two procedures by using the hierarchical method to establish the number of clusters and cluster centroids, and then using centroids as seeds in the nonhierarchical method [25]. We used a similar two-step approach in our study. Based on recommendations from prior researchers [8, 25, 43, 50], we used (1) standardized values for each variable, (2) squared Euclidean distance for similarity assessment, and (3) Ward’s minimum-variance method for cluster formation.

## Validation

The validation stage of cluster analysis ensures that the cluster solution developed is stable and generalizable across the sample population. Multiple cluster analysis with the same sample, but with different starting centroids, is suggested as a method to evaluate stability in small samples [25]. We performed two separate cluster analyses for the two contexts. The results indicate that the solutions are stable with almost the same number of data points in each cluster for the two cluster analyses. The significant gap in the mean values of the variables between the clusters for both the contexts, shown in Tables 5b and 6b, also provides support for the stability of membership in the clusters.

Another important aspect of validation in cluster analysis is profiling, where the characteristics of the cluster are profiled using well-understood variables, representative of the cluster, but not included in the cluster analysis. It can be considered as an assessment of the predictive validity of the clusters. Since the two clusters exhibit the information processing needs of different types of interorganizational relationships (interdependent and autonomous), we used an independent single-item measure assessing the level of strategic business relationship between the partners to determine whether the two clusters were significantly different. The mean values and the significant difference in the values between the two groups (interdependent = 5.03, autonomous = 3.75; $t = 5 . 1 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ indicate that the interdependent cluster has a higher level of business relationship compared to the autonomous cluster, providing validity to our clusters. The second cluster analysis identifies three clusters that vary in their level of IT support. Since one could logically argue that the groups would vary on the level of electronic information exchange with their partner, a single-item independent measure of level of electronic information exchange with the trading partner was compared between the three clusters. One-way ANOVA found that the variable was significantly different among the different groups (noncomputer = 3.83, mixed technology–EDI dominant = 5.20, Internet/Web = 5.06, F = 10.38, p < 0.0001; Bonferroni test of mean differences are significant for both mixed technology and Internet with noncomputer but not among themselves), attesting to the validity of our clusters.

## Fit Results and Discussion

Our research model posits that the “fit” between information processing needs and information processing capability has a positive effect on performance. As described earlier, we use the “fit as matching” perspective and operationalized fit as the interaction of the two variables, which is captured by the interaction effects (of the main factors) in ANOVA. Therefore, we expect the interaction effects to have a greater effect on performance compared to the main effects [59]. In order to control for organizational size, we used the natural logarithm of sales revenue (because of skewed values) as a covariate in the model. The mean sums of squares and the significance of the main and interaction effects for the performance variable are provided in Table 7. The R<sup>2</sup> for the model is 0.08.

The results indicate that although the interaction effect of the two variables has a significant effect on the overall performance, the main effects are not significant. This supports the notion of fit and our initial proposition that it is not the individual variables, but the fit between the two variables that results in better performance. Since we are using categorical variables, the results imply that performances in the bottom left and top right cells are going to be higher due to better fit than in the bottom right and top left cells of Table 8 [19]. The results are consistent with prior predictions by Shah et al. [52], and the empirical research of Bensaou and Venkatraman [2], who found similar patterns. Since we have a nonsymmetrical matrix, the middle row can be expected to have performances that are between those in the two diagonal cells, which is discussed later, along with description of the characteristics of the individual cells. The success of e-markets for MRO items, but not for direct production goods, is a real-world validation of the same concept [32, 53]. MRO-type items, which are more likely to be standardized (less complex), less critical, and with lower demand and supply uncertainty, are more suited to e-markets. Most managers interviewed are reluctant to risk procuring direct material that affects their daily production and final product quality from new suppliers participating in an e-market. Prequalification of suppliers, extensive discussion on the product requirements, frequent interactions, and long-term collaboration are the typical norms for procuring complex, critical products, suggesting a higher information processing need. A sophisticated Web-based technology that supports real-time information exchange for a wide range of activities provides the information processing capability to support these needs.

The cluster of products with similar information processing needs exhibit similar relationship characteristics; one cluster favors autonomous relationship (i.e., low asset specificity) and the other favors interdependent relationship (i.e., high asset specificity). The two-cluster solution is consistent with prior results that used similar variables for clustering [2], and is consistent with current theoretical conceptualization of interorganizational relationships. Although, statistically, a two-cluster solution was suggested, we did examine clusters beyond the two to identify other hybrid forms of relationship. The three-cluster and four-cluster solutions, although providing some meaningful grouping, were not very stable and more difficult to interpret. A larger sample size and a greater variety in transaction environments beyond the manufacturing industry may provide more information on hybrid relationships. The mean values for all variables, except trust, which defined the information needs of the two clusters were significantly different. Yet, in this study, there was little variation in trust among the data points, suggesting that either most firms had a high level of trust in their trading partner or there were measurement problems. Since we used a validated measure, we expect it to be the former reason. More research is required to understand the reasons for the lack of difference in trust values between the two clusters.

The cluster analysis of information processing capability revealed a three-cluster solution that clearly differed in the level of electronic support. Figure 3 shows some interesting patterns. In general, there is a greater tendency to use the Internet in the search phase, as shown by the higher mean values in the first two activities. The Internet/Web support cluster leverages the Internet for practically all PLC activities except payment and price negotiation. The mixed technology–EDI dominant support cluster focuses on EDI, but also attempts to leverage the potential of the Internet/Web for some of the other PLC activities, such as posting request for information (RFI)/ request for proposal (RFP) and exchanging engineering documents and specifications. However, the noncomputer-dominant support cluster still uses the traditional noncomputer interaction for all PLC activities, except, perhaps, the search phase. Typically, the activities in the search and negotiation phase are more unstructured than other activities. Although the gap between the clusters is not very high in the first two activities (i.e., the search phase) and for price negotiations, it widens quite significantly for the other activities, including ordering, documents exchange, delivery schedule, inventory, and shipping. This suggests that firms are more amenable to using electronic technologies, such as EDI, for communicating structured documents, and the Internet/Web for supporting semistructured communication.

## Emergent Taxonomy of Fit

The results emerging from the study can be further portrayed in the form of a 2×3 taxonomy/matrix as shown in Table 8. The taxonomy identifies six unique groups with properties that are quite revealing in a number of respects, as shown by the mean values of variables in each of the cells. This emergent taxonomy was again validated by conducting separate interviews with practitioners. Four senior procurement managers in three firms examined nine different products. Each interview lasted 75–90 minutes, and detailed notes were taken and analyzed later. We presented the 2×3 taxonomy describing the key characteristics of the six cells. The respondents were asked to identify specific products that they were responsible for procurement that would fit the cells and explain the information processing needs and IT support capabilities for that product. They did not encounter any difficulty in picking out products appropriate to the characteristics of the various cells in the taxonomy. Overall, they considered the taxonomy to be a good representation of various categories of products and IS support capabilities. The products chosen ranged from shrink film, fasteners, engine gaskets, R22-refrigerant, batteries, hand tools, silicone spray, office (MRO) supplies, and cable/wires, to variable-frequency drives, engine cylinder heads, and customized glass containers. The discussion of the characteristics of each cell along with some practical examples from interviews follows.

The products in the minimalist cell are characterized by low product description complexity and product criticality, and low levels of uncertainty in technology, demand, and supply market. There is relatively limited investment made by the firm or its partner in the transaction relationship, with autonomous relationship as the preferred choice. The characteristics of the cell suggest limited interorganizational information exchange needs. Products in this cell primarily use noncomputer technologies to support their minimal information processing and exchange needs. Procurement managers, however, use the Internet to support some of the activities in the search phase of the PLC. What is unique about this group is the low use of EDI and the high noncomputer use. The characteristics of this cell closely match with the first cell in Shah et al. [52]. A classic example of a product in this category, identified by one respondent, was a refrigerant, which is a standard product with limited suppliers that can be procured without major investments in technology.

The products in the electronic coordinators, or e-coordinators, cell represent slightly greater uncertainty, resulting in greater information processing needs. The products are a little more complex to describe, having greater levels of technology and demand uncertainty compared to the minimalists. However, the products have lower supply uncertainty, suggesting that the supply market is competitive with many alternative suppliers. Hence, for this product type, firms prefer a loosely coupled contractual relationship, but with greater interactions and information exchange to address the product uncertainty characteristics. A broader range of technologies is used to support various activities, suggesting greater information processing capability. Whereas the competitive supply market advocates a loose coupling approach and the use of the Web in the search phase for identifying suppliers and prices and collecting product information, the higher demand uncertainty requires closer monitoring and transfer of structured information such as demand forecasts, delivery schedules, and shipping, which are best handled with EDI technology. A classic example of a product with these characteristics could be one of the semispecialized products required for direct production, such as special fasteners in a manufacturing company. Based on our interviews, the respondent in one of the firms indicated that since there were many suppliers for the product, the Web enabled them to search for suppliers to find the best pricing. However, for their just-in-time (JIT) production, they would require that all their postordering coordination, such as delivery schedule, shipping, and so on, be completed with EDI due to frequency of transactions and the need for automation. This cell reflects the use of a variety of technologies that best match with the needs of different activities of the PLC.

Products in the electronic experimenter, or e-experimenter, cell share many product features with those in the minimalists. They have similar levels of description complexity and uncertainty in technology, demand, and supply market, but slightly lower product criticality. One of the interview respondents identified standardized batteries and bulbs as a representative product for this category. They have limited mutual investment in the supplier relationship and prefer loosely coupled relationships to take advantage of the markets for procurement. Hence, their information processing needs are comparable to those of minimalists. However, their use of technology differs. They actively experiment with technology and prefer to use the latest Internet-based technologies for supporting their activities. They extensively use the Web in the search phase of PLC. The standardized nature of the products, combined with relatively stable technology, make it highly suitable to use the Web to identify potential suppliers, evaluate product catalogs, request quotations, and participate in electronic markets.

The Web provides the flexibility to support both structured and semistructured information exchange and is, therefore, ideally suited for experimenters to try new technologies for information exchange. They leverage the Internet technologies for most of the order management activities, except bill payment. This cell is characterized by technology innovators who are constantly experimenting with new ways to use IT to support their procurement activities. For some (of these relatively standard) products, the information processing capability may be less optimal than for others. However, experimentation will help them to identify the optimal technology capabilities for the various products. During the experimentation period, they may get less value from the technology as reflected in the lower performance. A common product that we encountered in our interviews that closely matches these characteristics is office supply, which is low in supply and demand uncertainty and large in number of suppliers. In one company, they developed a Web interface for employees to directly order, and the requirements were aggregated and supplied by the vendor on a weekly/monthly basis. Their justification was that the large number of employees and the availability of Web development expertise justified their choice of technology, although it may look like overkill if considered in isolation. In a smaller firm, they used the telephone to call in their order, and their argument was that the volume and value did not justify developing a system to support this procurement. Clearly, the two responses, in the minimalist and e-experimenter cells using two different strategies for the same product, reveal that other factors such as IT infrastructure, organizational policies, volume/value of procurement, and so on, may influence organizational decision, which do not get directly reflected in information processing needs, but influence cost–benefit decisions in developing IT capabilities.

The products in the traditionalist cell are characterized by significant product complexity and product criticality. They also have higher levels of uncertainty compared to minimalists due to demand fluctuations, technology change, and supply market conditions. They have tightly coupled interorganizational relationships with their suppliers through mutual investments in the relationship. These procurement characteristics suggest a high level of information exchange needs between the partners. Traditional noncomputer technology is preferred over other technologies, but with limited use of the Web for some of the activities. The low usage of EDI is rather surprising. Although it would be hard to speculate all the reasons for preferring noncomputer technology, some of these could be the lack of significant in-house IS expertise to support electronic technologies, lack of IT sophistication at the supplier end to enable the use of EDI, lack of network infrastructure for communication, or simply, contrary to the technology imperative, the adequacy of noncomputer technology to support the information needs. The lack of fit between information processing need and capability is reflected in lower performance. In one of our field visits, we found a firm having many satellite suppliers within close proximity to their manufacturing plant, and extensively using phone and fax to coordinate the shipments. Some suppliers even had a representative stationed in the firm’s premises for coordination. Although noncomputer technologies, such as face-to-face and telephone, provide realtime rich communication, they are not cost-effective, and are likely to fail if participants are not available. In this case, the firm was constrained by availability of technology on the supplier end, and the respondents voiced the need to improve their capabilities. Another respondent in our validation interview commented that for their complex and specialized product (engine cylinder head), noncomputer interaction facilitates detailed discussions related to prequalification of vendors, customization of drawings, and discussions on process engineering, all critical for effective procurement. However, in order to improve performance, they are exploring newer Webbased technologies that automate some of the activities, such as communication of computer-aided design (CAD) drawings. However, suppliers are less enthusiastic. The CEO of one of their suppliers commented, “I have to hire a new person to look at e-mails every day . . . I don’t see any benefit.”

Electronic collaborators, or e-collaborators, exhibit similar product characteristics as traditionalists, except that they have tighter coupling with their suppliers through mutual investments. The tight partnership necessitates significant information exchange needs for interorganizational interactions. E-collaborators, unlike traditionalists, use electronic technologies to support their tight interorganizational relationship. They use EDI to support all their structured information flow. There is also limited use of the Web in the search phase of PLC for identifying potential suppliers. Due to high description complexity and probable necessity for customization, there is a need to exchange unstructured information on requirements and engineering documents, which are also partially facilitated through file transfer on the Web. Highly customized direct production products in manufacturing companies with JIT or large-volume operations characterize this cell. One study participant said that his firm used noncomputer (face-to-face) interaction in the initial stages due to the customized nature of the product and the need to educate the vendor on quality and process requirements. However, subsequent postorder communication of high-frequency structured information was supported by EDI.

Products in the electronic cooperators, or e-cooperators, cell have high levels of uncertainty arising from product description complexity, technology uncertainty, product criticality, and supply uncertainty. They prefer a tight interorganizational relationship with significant mutual investments by both parties. These features indicate that significant information exchange needs exist between the partners. Web- or Internetbased systems are the preferred technology choice. The limited use of noncomputer and EDI technology suggests a substitution effect of replacing these technologies with Web technology and interconnection standards. As one moves from collaboration to cooperation in interorganizational relationships, along with an increase in information flow, there is a greater need for supporting the flow of semistructured information such as product specifications, engineering documents, and so on. EDI, although efficient as a communications technology, is unsuitable for semistructured or unstructured information flow. Technology innovators may be more willing to try new technologies, such as a Web-based system, that provide the flexibility to support different types of information needs [38]. Further, the high product criticality of the products in this cell necessitates more real-time information flow, which is better served by a Web-based system rather than a batch-mode EDI system. One of the firms in our validation interviews described a Web-based configuration application that is accessible to preferred suppliers to get detailed specifications on a complex product critical to the firm’s daily operations. The cooperative environment between the two firms may result in many more activities beyond procurement, which may require a versatile and flexible technology platform such as the Web for interfacing with decision support or knowledge-based systems that support various joint decision-making activities.

## Conclusions

The availability of sophisticated IS and a ubiquitous network linking most businesses has created new opportunities for electronic integration between firms. However, the requirements of business processes in their supply chain and the interorganizational relationships influence the choice of communication technologies and implementation strategies. This study used Galbraith’s [22] information processing theory to examine the fit between information processing needs and information processing capability in an interorganizational supply chain context and to examine its effect on performance. A 2×3 taxonomy of information processing needs based on interorganizational transaction characteristics and information processing capability based on IT use in various activities of the PLC was developed. The study collected data on 142 products through personal interviews and surveys, primarily from firms in the manufacturing industry.

Cluster analysis was used to develop the taxonomies for information processing needs and information processing capability. The results revealed two clusters in information processing needs and three clusters in information processing capability. The fit between the two variables was examined using ANOVA, and the results showed that the interactive effect of needs and capability had a greater effect on performance than the main effects, supporting our fit theory. The mean values of the variables were examined in the two clusters of needs and three clusters of capability to better understand the characteristics of the various clusters.

This study makes both theoretical and methodological contributions to IS research. From a theoretical perspective, formulating and empirically testing the information processing theory’s concept of fit between the information processing needs and capability is a unique contribution to IS research, since the notion of “fit” has not been empirically examined extensively. Developing taxonomies of information processing needs and information processing capability using a rigorous approach to cluster analysis is a methodological contribution of this research and an extension of research on interorganizational relationships.

## Limitations

There are some limitations that need to be recognized. The data for the study were primarily collected from manufacturing firms in the midwest region, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results to other types of industries and geographic regions. A second limitation of the study is that it primarily focused on the procurement technology and did not consider other IT and organizational resources that complement the information processing capability of the organization. The third limitation was the sample size. A trade-off had to be made between obtaining accurate and rich information through structured interviews with a limited number of organizations and a large-scale survey of a wide range of organizations, with low response rates possibly due to complexity of the survey instrument and some doubts on accuracy of responses. We chose the former approach. We believe that we were fortunate to obtain detailed data on a slightly smaller sample, given the restrictions imposed by organizations in giving out data on business practices. Another limitation is the focus on the product level of analyses that precluded the study of task characteristics that may have a bearing on information needs. Future research could consider a design that complements product characteristics with task characteristics. Finally, future studies could extend the dyadic analysis to examine the fit between information needs and capability throughout the supply chain consisting of multiple networked nodes and its influence on the performance of the entire supply chain.

## Future Research

This study also identifies some exciting ideas for future research. Our taxonomy of information needs generated a two-cluster solution, which, although consistent with a prior study, does not seem to capture the wide range of hybrid relationships that are discussed in practice and predicted by TCE. This could perhaps be an artifact of our sampling, which focused only on the manufacturing industry. A more comprehensive sampling of different industries may provide a broader range of interorganizational relationships. Another avenue for future research would be to expand the range of activities to include strategic activities such joint product development, marketing promotion, and so on. An in-depth examination of these partnerships could reveal the evolution from information sharing to knowledge management that significantly enhances the value of the partnership and the most appropriate types of IT support to sustain such evolution and relationships. While our 2×3 taxonomy is interesting, it is ipso facto, based on the prevailing characteristics measured with interorganizational relationship and information processing technologies. An interesting direction for future research is to understand the dynamics of evolution. Our results suggest that such research is certainly warranted.

## Appendix. Measurement Indicators

ALL ITEMS WERE MEASURED USING a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Representative research support is cited within brackets.

## Description Complexity (DC) [37]

1. Amount of technical and other product information that needs to be provided for the supplier to understand our needs.

2. Familiarity of product’s specifications to most suppliers.

3. Extensive customization of the product to meet our requirements.

## Technology Uncertainty (TU) [1]

1. Frequent occurrence of major product innovations in the product.

2. Frequent changes to the product’s design/functionality.

3. Frequent changes made by the supplier to the product’s specifications.

## Demand Uncertainty (DU) [7]

1. There is significant uncertainty in our demand for this product.

2. Demand forecasts in terms of volume and timing are not accurate.

## Product Criticality (PC) [9]

1. Stock-out of this product will create major disruptions to operations.

2. This product is critical to our operations.

3. This product’s quality has a significant effect on the performance of the end product.

## Supply Uncertainty (SU) [9]

1. There is significant price variation among suppliers of this product.

2. There is considerable variation in quality/service among suppliers.

3. Over time, the price of this product fluctuates widely.

4. Over time, the availability of this product fluctuates widely.

## Firm Investment (FI) [3, 7, 30]

1. Our firm has provided tooling, warehouses, or other special equipment/facilities to this product supplier.

2. Our firm has modified product design to tailor it to supplier needs.

3. Our firm has made a major investment in time and effort to develop/learn business practices to meet supplier’s needs.

## Supplier Investment (SI) [3, 7, 30]

1. This supplier has reserved a portion of its manufacturing/distribution capacity to meet our demand.

2. This supplier has invested in information systems, tooling, warehouses, or other special equipment/facilities to meet our needs.

3. This supplier has recruited people with specific manufacturing/design skills and expertise to meet our requirements.

## Trust (TR) [24, 28]

1. We believe in honesty and accuracy of deadlines set by this supplier.

2. This supplier delivers on the promises made to us.

3. This supplier is honest in all business dealings.

4. This supplier is willing to share information with us.

5. This supplier honors all agreements with us.

6. This supplier exhibits consistency in business dealings with us.

## Performance (OB) [27, 46]

1. Reduced ordering costs.

2. Reduced order cycle time.

3. Improved inventory turnover.

4. Helped us get better prices on the procured products.

5. Improved coordination with the suppliers.

6. Provided better information for decision making.

4. Pricing and negotiation.

5. Purchase order.

6. Engineering documents.

7. Demand forecast.

8. Delivery schedule.
