# The IT artifact and its spirit: a nexus of human values, afordances, symbolic expressions, and IT features

Mustapha Cheikh-Ammar

Ivey Business School, Western University, London, Canada

## ABSTRACT

This article theorises the IT artifact using a new conceptualisation of IT Spirit that highlights the interrelationships between IT Features, Afordances, and Symbolic Expressions. Although these concepts have been discussed in the IS literature, researchers have mostly examined them independently. Drawing on the philosophical theories of Aristotle and Heidegger, this article discusses the “essence of technology” by conceptualising the IT artifact in terms of the intertwining relationships that exist between IT Features, their Symbolic Expressions, the Afordances connected to them at diferent levels of abstraction, and the Values that emerge from the enactment of their action possibilities within a user group. It is argued that these essential components of the IT artifact operate at variant levels of abstraction, instigate user sense-making, and subsequently form the overarching IT Spirit. The latter is presented as the incorporeal essence of a technology that shapes – and is itself shaped by – user sense-making and appropriation. With the emphasis that it gives to human values, the concept of IT Spirit ofers researchers a high-level unit of analysis to tackle the IT artifact and a systematic framework that facilitates the identification of its prominent action possibilities.

## 1. Introduction

IT artifacts are “bundles of material and cultural properties packaged in some socially recognisable form such as hardware and/or software” (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). They usually possess information processing capabilities that are put to use to perform individual, group, and/or organisational tasks (Nevo & Ein-Dor, 2009). However, not all IT artifacts are identical, and one and the same IT artifact can be completely diferent depending on the spatial, temporal, discursive, and social contexts in which it is embedded (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), and which shape what it essentially is. Given this complexity, conceptualising the IT artifact has been a long-standing issue in the IS field (Alter, 2015; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Weber, 2003; Wu & Saunders, 2003). Although there have been recurring calls to refocus IS research on the IT artifact and although it has been more than 15 years since Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) underscored the need for IT artifact theorising, the lack of attention to the IT artifact remains an unresolved issue in IS research (Akhlaghpour, Wu, Lapointe, & Pinsonneault, 2013).

Conceptualising the IT artifact may require focusing on IT features (Jasperson, Carter, & Zmud, 2005), but such an endeavour is hindered by their inherent fuzziness. Indeed, it is dificult to identity IT features since they can be continually ground into smaller entities (sub-features). This issue is often referred to as the Repeating Decomposition Problem (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; DeSanctis, Snyder, & Scott Poole, 1994; Grifith, 1999). Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) and its concept of Spirit provided some insightful starting points to overcome this challenge and to facilitate the investigation of IS use and impacts (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). The AST conceptualisation of Spirit, which was conceived as a high-level representation of intended values and goals, ofered a potentially useful tool to help differentiate between IT artifacts. Yet concerns were raised regarding the conceptualisation of Spirit as a property of technology since it implies that the intentions of designers are embedded in IT and exist independently of IT users (Markus & Silver, 2008). Because of its conceptual drawbacks and the limited number of studies engaging with it, Markus and Silver (2008) replaced Spirit by two new concepts, Symbolic Expressions (SE) and Functional Afordances, more than a decade after being introduced.

Although the initial conceptualisation of Spirit was considered problematic, benefits can be reaped from revisiting this concept because it provides a useful holistic view of an IT without treating Spirit as an embedded property of IT. Moreover, given that the concept of Spirit highlights human values as high-level end-goals, it provides a useful unit of analysis for studying IT artifacts that is easier to manoeuvre than the IT artifact’s numerous features and the multitude of action possibilities that they aford, particularly if Spirit is analysed in relation to a specific user group.

While SE, Spirit, and Afordances all have the potential to provide an interesting theoretical frame for investigating the impacts of IS design and for opening up meaningful research avenues, only the concept of Afordances has managed to attract significant interest from IS researchers. This may be because the concepts of SE and Spirit lack clarity, so that their theoretical utilisation is less compelling. All three of these concepts can be valuable to IS research, but before they can be utilised, they need to be clarified and systematically differentiated, and their interrelationships recognised and elaborated on. Here it will be argued that as a combination of values, IT Spirit is conceptually diferent from both SE and Afordances, but that the three concepts are interconnected and can coexist in the same theoretical frame. This conceptual coexistence is possible because the symbolisms projected by the materiality of an IT artifact and the action possibilities that it permits are likely to shape the values experienced in relation to it. In other words, SE, Afordances, and IT Spirit reside at variant levels of abstraction and are neither interchangeable nor substitutable, but they are interconnected.

To reach a comprehensive conceptualisation of the IT artifact, the present paper builds on the idea of the “Essence of technology” (what technology is) as discussed by Aristotle and Heidegger. The paper then argues that in addition to being shaped by instrumentality, the IT artifact also exists as what it is in and through value-laden human activities that are embedded in social contexts. This means that the IT artifact arises out of the intertwining relationships between IT features and the SE that they project, the functional afordances that they allow, and the values that emerge from their appropriation in a social context. The goal is to theorise these conceptual components as residing at diferent levels of abstraction and as emerging from IT features that are embedded in social contexts. This conceptualisation has the potential to provide a high-level unit of analysis for describing IT artifacts and explaining their impacts. Therefore, its potential contribution to IS research is twofold: (1) It ofers an approach to IT artifacts that is much more conceptually plausible than those focusing on features and the action possibilities that they allow; and (2) It ofers researchers a conceptual frame that makes it possible to identify focal IT afordances in specific contexts.

In what follows, the conceptual foundations are laid by briefly examining the IS literature on the concepts of

IT Features, SE, and Afordances. Next, the concept of IT Spirit is set in place through a discussion of the work of Aristotle and Heidegger on the ontological foundations of what a “thing” is. A new conceptualisation of the IT artifact is then presented, and the systematic interrelationships between its conceptual elements are explained. Finally, the theoretical and practical implications of this conceptualisation are discussed.

## 2. Conceptual foundations

## 2.1. IT features

IT features are the central components of an IT artifact shaping the variety of possible outcomes of its use (Jasperson et al., 2005). IT features may or may not be identifiable or easily interpreted by users who choose whether to adopt them or not. This choice can change during the life cycle of an IT, leading to variant organisational and/or individual outcomes at diferent points in time (Goodhue, 1995; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Grifith, 1999; Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994). As a result, feature use may increase or decrease as various features are resisted, abandoned, routinised, or extended (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006; Hartwick & Barki, 1994).

IT features often difer in their capabilities and in their efects on users. For example, some IT features are considered core features, whereas others are considered tangential (Grifith, 1999; Grifith & Northcraft, 1994). They also vary in their tangibility and can be placed on a continuum ranging from concrete to abstract (Grifith, 1999). Concrete features can be directly described and clearly identified, whereas abstract features are not easily specified. Although IT capabilities are often derived from IT features, the latter are always interrelated and bundled in a multitude of ways, so that often there are “features within features” leading to “contingencies within contingencies” (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994, p. 125). This means that it may be dificult to deduce their capabilities.

Although many researchers agree that adequate IT artifact conceptualisations should emerge from IT features (Jasperson et al., 2005; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), clearly identifying easily manoeuvrable sets of features that are theoretically driven and conceptually comprehensive remains dificult. IT artifacts have numerous interrelated and interbundled features, so that meaningfully selecting a feature (or a group of features) to be included in a research model is a complex problem (Grifith, 1999). It is not always easy to choose features that are theoretically and conceptually capable of representing an IT artifact (Faraj & Azad, 2012). The Repeating Decomposition Problem may be one of the reasons that researchers shy away from IT features and prefer to adopt concepts that are less concrete, but more manageable. One such concept is Symbolic Expressions.

## 2.2. Symbolic expressions

Markus and Silver (2008) define SE as “the communicative possibilities of a technical object for a specific user group”. SE is a relational concept that makes it possible to analyse the ways that the symbolism embedded in a technical object shapes IT use and impacts. Moreover, SE can be associated with subcomponents of a technical object (one of its features or a bundle of its features), so that it is possible for multiple SE to coexist within the boundaries of a given IS, sometimes without being aligned either with each other or with the initial intent of IT designers (Markus & Silver, 2008).

However, defining SE as the communicative possibilities of an IT is somewhat confusing because it brings into play a very wide theoretical domain that may include several distinct concepts (see MacKenzie, Podsakof, & Podsakof, 2011 on construct clarity). For instance, this definition of SE suggests that the symbolism of IT features has the capability to convey meanings connected to what a feature does (e.g., Grgecic, Holten, & Rosenkranz, 2015) as well as meanings connected to what a technology epitomises for users in terms of values (e.g., Goh, Gao, & Agarwal, 2011). From a conceptual standpoint, these are two distinct types of meanings. The first type has to do with the functionalities that features permit, whereas the second type has to do with the values made possible by the IT’s action potentialities. This is why delimiting the conceptual domain of SE can help clarify this concept and facilitate its use in future research.

2.2.1. Revisiting the concept of symbolic expressions The definition of SE as the communicative possibilities of a technical object is problematic because its theoretical domain covers a multitude of distinct concepts. However, one of the main motives for studying SE is the need to bridge the conceptual gap between technical objects and users’ interpretations (Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 622). SE provides additional sources of information (Bernhard, Recker, & Burton-Jones, 2013) that can con tribute to user sense-making and facilitate the interpretation of IT features. One of these important sources of information is closely related to the capability of features or artifacts to transmit “expressions about functionality” (Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 624). In other words, SE can help users uncover IS functionalities that can lead to goal-oriented interactions, and this is a significant aspect of their conceptual usefulness.

The initial conceptualisation of SE also included the values expressed by the IT artifact. However, unlike SE (i.e., communicative possibilities related to functionalities), values are often subject to users’ choices (e.g., Leonardi, Huysman, & Steinfield, 2013) and they often take shape in and through the enactment of the tasks permitted by the IT (e.g., Goh et al., 2011). Values and functionality-related communicative capabilities are conceptually distinct, so that merging them into one concept should be avoided. Moreover, there is no clear justification for replacing the term “values” by the term “SE”, since doing so would only add to the confusion that already surrounds these concepts. Therefore, in the present article, IT-promoted values are simply referred to as “values”, and the domain of SE is limited to the symbolism of IT features and their capability to convey meanings connected to what a feature does (Grgecic et al., 2015). Thus, SE is here defined as the possibilities of an IT artifact or of IT features to communicate impressions of functionality to a specific user group.

Under this definition, SE mostly consist of visual cues and symbols that help users identify the functionalities of features (Grgecic et al., 2015) and that facilitate the efort of users to understand what actions are possible with these features (Goh et al., 2011) given their own capabilities and goals. This way of facilitating understanding may be compared to what might happen if a primitive hunter saw a thin cylinder-shaped piece of wood with a sharp-edged point at one end lying on the ground. The form of this piece of wood might allow him to uncover possible uses of it that would be handy in specific contexts. For example, he might realise that he could use it as a spear to kill prey from a distance while hunting or fishing. Similarly, email systems frequently present the on-screen send button in the symbolic form of a flying envelop to help users uncover its embedded functionality (i.e., message transmission). In other words, the usefulness of SE stems from their potential to elicit cognitive and afective responses from IT users (Scott, 1994) in order to help them uncover the action possibilities of an IT and its features.

## 2.3. Afordances

The concept of Afordances, which was first introduced by Gibson (1986), refers to what is ofered or provided by an object (Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008). According to Norman (1988), who pioneered some of the most influential work on afordances in the IS literature (Volkof & Strong, 2013), the initial conceptualisation of afordances referred to the ways of using an object as perceived by users (Majchrzak, Faraj, Kane, & Azad, 2013). This was later amended to the notion of perceived afordances, a notion that stresses the ease of assimilation of objects and their uses (Norman, 1999). However, since this concept derives from ecological psychology, many researchers view afordances as properties of the relationship between agents and objects and define them as opportunities for action (Hutchby, 2001; Stofregen, 2003). This definition of afordances is the one adopted in the present study.

Afordances are rooted in the action potential ofered by objects (or IT) (Hutchby, 2001; Leonardi, 2011; Majchrzak & Markus, 2012). This means that afordances may or may not be actualised or even perceived, but that they exist regardless (Volkof & Strong, 2013). As a function of the relation between individuals and objects, afordances are shaped by the interdependency of action potential and IT capability (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Norman, 2007; Zammuto, Grifith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). Thus afordances are not latent capabilities inherent in an IT, but instead potentialities that may or may not materialise (Majchrzak et al., 2013). The theoretical lens ofered by the concept of Afordances has recently gained ground among IS researchers, but there is still a lot to be gained. For instance, researchers need to look into the issue of how to identify afordances. Like features (although perhaps not to the same degree), afordances are numerous, hierarchical, and continuously decomposable (Savoli, 2014), and as a result, identifying them may not be a straightforward task.

## 2.4. Spirit

DeSanctis and Poole (1994) coined the term “Spirit” to refer to one of the two components that materialise IT artifacts, the other being Structural Features. The latter are rules, resources, or capabilities related to an IS, whereas Spirit is “a property of the technology as it is presented to users”, a property that represents “the general intent with regard to values and goals underlying a given set of structural features” (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994, p. 126). For example, one Spirit may reflect a culture of sharing and collaboration between employees, while another may reflect an atmosphere of tight organisational control and close employee monitoring. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) compared the spirit of an IS to the spirit of a law, which is distinguishable from how the law is written or enacted. For example, a law stating that any citizen can run for public ofice, regardless of his or her gender, race, and ethnicity, can reflect a spirit of equality between citizens. As the underlying message behind a technology, Spirit can shape the way individuals interpret and interact with IT. As a property of the technology, it expresses the human values that are imbedded in an IT as intended or imagined by IT designers even when these values are not completely realised (Markus & Silver, 2008).

DeSanctis and Poole (1994, p. 126) cautioned researchers interested in the concept of Spirit that they should not focus uniquely on the intentions of IT designers. In their view, these researchers also have to account for the messages and texts – for example, design metaphors and training materials – if they want to uncover the Spirit of an IT artifact. However, their conceptualisation still views Spirit as a property of technologies that exists independently of their structural features and their users (Markus & Silver, 2008). This conceptualisation is problematic above all because it suggests that the Spirit of an IT artifact is a property of technology that reflects overall intent and values, for this implies that the intentions and values of IT developers and implementers are embedded in technologies and exist independently of those who are using them (Markus & Silver, 2008). This is a questionable position since it is conceivable – and in fact often the case – that the intentions of IT developers are not faithfully executed in the IT development process (Grifith, 1999), or that end-users appropriate IT features in ways that conflict with the initial IT design (Leonardi, 2007). Therefore, it should come as no surprise that this ascription of properties or attributes like intentions and values to non-human material objects detached from human agents has been controversial (Jones & Karsten, 2008, 2009; Markus & Silver, 2008).

## 3. What the IT artifact “Is”

Conceptualising the IT artifact means attempting to provide an explanation of what it fundamentally is. The ontological question of what any particular “thing” is (or of what something like technology is) has been a central theme in the thought of many prominent thinkers, but it is especially important in the philosophies of Aristotle and Heidegger. This section revisits some fundamental concepts in Aristotle’s ontology and contrasts them with those of Heidegger in his reflections on technology, which will later guide the IT artifact conceptualisation advanced in this paper.

In his Physics (1957, 194a-b, p. 123), Aristotle frames his ontological enquiry around the words to ti ên einai or to ti esti, which are usually translated by one single word: “essence”, but which literally mean “that which it is to be this”. To explain the “essence” of things, Aristotle theorises four causes, but before discussing these four specific causes, it is important to discuss what the word “cause” means in general. Aristotle uses the Greek word aitia, which is usually translated by the English word “cause”. However, many influential interpreters of Aristotle stress that the meaning of the Greek word implies more than what has come to be understood by causality in modern times, this modern understanding being based on a derivative sense of what being a cause is for the Greeks (Bacon, 1960; Brogan, 2012; Heidegger, 1977; Mayr, 1992). To translate the Greek word aitia, Heidegger uses the German word Ursache, which means “cause” as well as “source” or “origin” (Ursprung), and also the German word Verschulden, which means “responsibility” as well as “debt”. In this interpretation, a cause in the Aristotelian sense is “that to which [a] being is indebted in its very being” (Heidegger, 1977, p. 3).

This interpretation is essential to understanding Aristotle’s theory of the four causes that allow a thing to be what it is: its Material Cause, its Formal Cause, its Eficient Cause, and its Final Cause. The Material Cause of a thing is the matter or material out of which it is made (e.g., bronze or clay). Its Formal Cause is the shape (form) and/or the attributes that are given to its matter. Its Eficient Cause is that which does the shaping or forming of this matter, in the sense that a mason or carpenter uses building materials to construct a house. However, the Eficient Cause is not merely the agent that forms some matter or material into a specific shape, since doing this also requires the knowledge and expertise that initiate the change that sets this matter or material on the path to becoming what it is (Brogan, 2012). In the Metaphysics (1996, 1013a, p. 209), Aristotle describes the Eficient Cause as “That from which the change or the resting from change first begins”. Thus, the Eficient Cause initiates the processes that will eventually bring a thing into being. As for the Final Cause (i.e., the telos), Aristotle says that it is the “end” or “purpose” for which a thing or activity exists. For example, “the “end” of walking is health. For why does a man walk? “To be healthy”, we say, and by saying this we consider that we have supplied the cause” (Metaphysics 1996, 1013b, p. 211). In a word, Aristotle’s theory of the four causes ofers an explanation of the fourfold origin of what things are – an explanation of how things are “indebted” to these four causes for their “thingness”.

If we apply Aristotle’s theory of the four causes to IT contexts, the Material Cause will be the IT architecture/infrastructure, and the Formal Cause will be the features of the IT and their design as provided to users. The Eficient Cause will be the IT Designers/Developers and the knowledge and objectives that they use to bring the IT to life, since they provide the resources and the constraints that prospective users will have to work with. As for the Final Cause, it will be the end for which the IT artifact is used in the context of use and the organisational intents behind this use. To a large extent, DeSanctis and Poole’s (1994) conceptualisation of Spirit aligns with this interpretation of the Eficient and the Final Causes because these two causes determine the purpose of an IT artifact as it is understood by those who have helped bring this artifact into being. Interestingly, although the Final Cause is apparently a goal to be attained in the future, Aristotle stresses that it is not an end or a final destination, but instead a beginning. For Aristotle, the Final Cause should be interpreted as a fulfilment (or fulfilling) and not as the “end” of a thing’s existence (of a thing’s being what it is), since the latter is only reached when the thing has ceased to be what it is – in other words, when it has ceased to exist (Brogan, 2012, p. 33). In the Metaphysics (1996, 1013a, p. 211), Aristotle says the following:

Nature is a beginning, and so is “element” and “understanding” and “choice” and “essence” and “final cause” – for in many cases the Good and the Beautiful are the beginning both of knowledge and of motion.

The Final Cause is arguably viewed from the perspective of builders/craftsmen who often have an ultimate predetermined goal or destination in mind for what they are making. For them, the Final Cause is the best possible outcome. On this issue Aristotle states: “the “goal” does not mean any kind of termination, but only the best” (Physics 1957, 194a, p. 123). This aligns with DeSanctis and Poole’s (1994) conceptualisation, which describes any IT feature appropriation that does not follow organisational intents as “unfaithful” (p. 130), since it does not conform to the modes of use that are considered the “best”.

## 3.1. Heidegger on the essence of technology

Heidegger considers Aristotle’s theory of the four causes deeply relevant and uses it to help reveal or “unconceal” the essence of technology (Heidegger, 1977). He illustrates Aristotle’s four causes using a silver chalice as an example. In this example, the Material Cause is the matter or material used in the crafting of the chalice (i.e., the silver). The Formal Cause is the shape or form given to the chalice that makes it distinctively diferent from other types of glasses or cups. The Eficient Cause is the silversmith who produced or brought into existence the chalice. The Final Cause is the outcome/result connected to its use in a social context (Heidegger refers to its use in the rite of communion in a Christian church) that accounts for its existing as a chalice. However, Heidegger also stresses the confining role of this Final Cause: “That which gives bounds, that which completes, in this sense is called in Greek telos, which is all too often translated as “aim” or “purpose”, and so misinterpreted” (Heidegger, 1977, p. 3). Thus, Heidegger appears to give additional emphasis to the bounding responsibility of the Final Cause in his interpretation. In Aristotle’s example, the goal of health is responsible for the action of walking (it causes this action), but this Final Cause may only be relevant for a particular person (or group) and in a specific context.

At first, this may appear to be a minor diference in interpretation, but Heidegger in fact questions whether Aristotle’s theory of the four causes is adequate for revealing the “truth” of technology. There are significant similarities between techne (art and craft) – which was the focal interest of Greek philosophers – and modern technology. In particular, they are both “means to an end” involving instrumental artifacts and they are both forms of “human activity”. As such, they are both revealed by human beings. Nevertheless, Heidegger argues that Aristotle’s theory of the four causes may conceal the true essence of technology and that its influence on the traditional understanding of causality may even expose us to what he considers “extreme danger”. Heidegger explains that techne manifests itself in craftsmanship, that is, in the “activities and skills of the craftsman”, as well as in the knowledge needed “to be entirely at home in something, to understand and be expert in it” (1997, p. 5). He says that technology as craftsmanship “belongs to bringing-forth”, a mode of revealing that “gathers together in advance the aspect and the matter of ship or house, with a view to the finished thing envisioned as completed, and from this gathering determines the manner of its construction” (Heidegger 1997, p. 6). But the idea that, based on expert knowledge and planning, human beings can know in advance what will eventually come into being is unsettling for Heidegger, particularly in the context of modern technology. He uses the term “Enframing” (Ge-stell) to describe this “technolog ical” interpretation of the world as a “standing reserve” waiting to be ordered for human purposes (Brigham & Introna, 2006). By “Enframing” (Ge-stell), he means “the gathering together of that setting-upon that sets upon man, that is, challenges him forth to reveal the real, in the mode of ordering, as standing-reserve”. This “enframing” or restrictive technological approach to the real (Blitz, 2014) may make us lose sight of that which makes us human. It may make us forget our own essence – forget what we are called upon to reveal beyond what is technological (Heidegger, 1977, p. 17).

Heidegger maintains that to understand modern technology we must enter the realm of the technological and seek to explore its non-technological dimensions. Only then will we be led to see its truth. This is what lies behind his claim that “the essence of technology is nothing technological” (Heidegger, 1977, p. 19). To reveal what technology is, we must break free of the “objectivity” of the natural sciences (Blitz, 2014). To do so, Heidegger ofers an interpretation of “being” as presence, a conception of “being” that he traces back to Plato:

We, late born, are no longer in a position to appreciate the significance of Plato’s daring to use the word eidos for that which in everything and in each particular thing endures as present. For eidos, in the common speech, meant the outward aspect [Ansicht] that a visible thing ofers to the physical eye. Plato exacts of this word, however, something utterly extraordinary: that it name[s] what precisely is not and never will be perceivable with physical eyes. (Heidegger, 1977, p. 6)

In this interpretation of Plato, Heidegger is referring to those aspects of technology that may not be detectable by our five senses. It is plausible to assume that these aspects are subjectively (as opposed to objectively) experienced, but even so they may be able to provide us with a holistic understanding of what technology is. Heidegger (1962) argues that we have a tendency to think of technology from an instrumental perspective, viewing technological objects as material things that are separate from users who can choose whether or not they want to use them. However, users function in contexts in which they have goals and concerns that shape who and what they themselves are as users, and this fundamentally impacts how they recognise these objects at diferent points in time (Brigham & Introna, 2006). This means that thinking about technology requires us to focus on the temporal unfolding of events involving human users (Blitz, 2014).

Heidegger’s key point is that the instrumental view of technology can be seen as “correct” from a “technological” perspective, but that it “does not show us technology’s essence” (Blitz, 2014). Aristotle’s theory of the four causes remains relevant for Heidegger because it highlights relevant aspects of technology, but he maintains that focusing solely on Aristotle’s four causes can be misleading because it can completely conceal or close of our access to the truth of what technology reveals. According to Heidegger, the essence of technology “is a mode of being, or of revealing”, since technological things have “their own novel kind of presence, endurance, and connections among parts and wholes” (Blitz, 2014). Indeed, modern technologies are interconnected to the point of being completely intertwined, not only with each other, but also with user practices (Riemer & Johnston, 2014). Because of this, technical ends (based on premeditated intentions) become possible with modern technology, but they are never true ends in themselves. Heidegger stresses that it is only through the use of technological objects that their meanings and properties become intelligible to us (Riemer & Johnston, 2014). As a result, if we only think about IT artifacts in terms of their instrumentality, then we will only be concerned with ways to master them and the technical goals they are used for, and we will fail to grasp their fundamental impact on what comes into being while we are using them to reveal the world. Or as Heidegger puts it, we will “remain held fast in the will to master [and] press on past the essence of technology” (1977, p. 11).

The next section presents a new conceptualisation of IT Spirit by building on Heidegger’s reflections on the essence of technology. Central to this conceptualisation is the premise that the ways in which individuals and groups use, appropriate, and interpret an IT artifact shape its Spirit and that this Spirit evolves over time in and through IT use.

## 3.2. A new take on IT spirit

The initial conceptualisation of Spirit can be challenged on ontological (e.g., Jones, 1998; Latour, 2005) as well as on other grounds (see Markus & Silver, 2008), but it may be useful to try to salvage it to the extent that it ofers a convenient lever for theorising IT artifacts. More specifically, since the concept of Spirit highlights the relevance of values, it may be worth examining how values take shape and how they can relate to a holistic view of a given IT artifact. To this end, it may be useful to borrow from Heidegger (1977) and interpret the IT artifact as both a means to an end and a meaningful component of a human activity. In this interpretation, the IT artifact is not merely “technological”, for it is also comprised of immaterial values that are subjectively revealed within a user group. As for IT Spirit, it is seen as consisting in combinations of values that emerge in the social context in which the IT artifact is embedded. This aligns with existing perspectives in theology and metaphysics where Spirit is often viewed as an incorporeal, ubiquitous, non-quantifiable substance (or energy) associated with all living things, and where it is often contrasted with the material body (here IT features) in which it exists as an integral part of what that body is (Burkhardt, 1989; Grenz, 2000).

![](/api/attachments/3KV5GD8J/fulltext/images/ad9a78f15d5798d67af0c295c6bec0cf5959767c8cee2744588f48cd32f348f4.jpg)

DeSanctis and Poole (1994) conceptualised Spirit in terms of intended values that originate in the minds of IT designers and that are then transmitted to the IT artifact (i.e., as properties of the technology), but in the present study IT Spirit is viewed as situated in the relational interaction between the IT artifact and a group of users. The values that compose IT Spirit emerge after several rounds of interactions in the social context in which the IT artifact and its users are imbedded. Thus, it is crucial to focus the analysis of IT Spirit on a sequence of events. Doing so provides a holistic grasp of the IT artifact as it is experienced by its users at specific points in time (Kreps, 2016). Accounting for IT Spirit in terms of the user group’s subjective experience of the IT artifact may help us move from a “correct” technological interpretation of the IT artifact to a “truthful” interpretation that reveals what it is as a manifestation of the essence of technology (Heidegger, 1977, p. 9), and this may deepen our understanding of the impact of IT on societies and organisations.

Although IT features can help promote an IT Spirit, they reside at diferent levels of abstraction and are not necessarily interrelated directly. It is to be expected that this interrelations will be shaped by the action potential (afordances) that the IT artifact allows, the values that this action potential helps reveal, the sense-making of users in their encounter with these diferent possibilities, and the evolution of these factors over time in a specific context. To illustrate what this means, it may be useful to imagine an example with a hammer. The latter has been built using specific materials (e.g., wood and metal) and is presumably designed as a tool for driving nails into wood. However, if a group of persons were stranded in a hostile environment (for example, a deserted island or a jungle), the hammer could become a use-object that afords excellent possibilities as a weapon. Over time, the hammer might evolve into an object that carried values related to this group’s survival, values such as power and/or status. These values would be connected to the hammer’s features, the strong material that it is made of and the afordances that it ofers to its owner as a weapon to attack others, not to mention the afordances that it ofers as a means of self-defence or protecting the group (which are afordances at diferent levels of abstraction). This example illustrates how any object can provide multiple afordances, the most relevant ones being those determined by the contextual boundaries of a group’s social context. In other words, the hammer in this example would still aford nail driving; however, the most relevant afordances in a survival context would be those related to the values that the group would associate with the hammer in this specific context, values such as the power and/or status connected to the possibilities for attacking and protecting that it aforded. Since afordances exist without necessarily being materialised or perceived, their relevance in a given social context is largely determined by the most significant values in that social context.

## 4. Conceptualising the IT artifact

The concepts of IT Features, Afordances, SE, and IT Spirit have usually been studied separately, as if they were completely disconnected from each other, so that little work has been done on integrating them and explicitly elaborating their possible interrelations. An attempt to do so would be useful because it would involve discriminating between these fundamental components of the IT artifact and help facilitate the examination of their efects. The present study aims to provide such a conceptualisation by interpreting the IT artifact as a product of IT Features, Afordances, SE, and IT Spirit as they exist at diferent levels of abstraction (see Figure 1). This conceptualisation will integrate the following: (1) the features of IT artifacts and the symbolic expressions that they project (at the most concrete level); (2) their action potentials (at diferent levels of abstraction); and (3) the values that they promote (at the highest level of abstraction) as a reflection of the overarching IT Spirit. It will be shown that each of these levels is connected to the other ones through user sense-making and appropriation (see Figures 1–4). Note that, since features and afordances are hierarchical and can be decomposed into various sub-entities (sub-features and sub-afordances) existing at diferent levels of abstraction, features and afordances at a higher level of abstraction are represented in Figure 1 as $^ { \mathfrak { e } } \mathrm { F } ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ and “A”, whereas those with a lower level of abstraction are represented by “f ” and “a”.

## 4.1. From material to action potential

IT features are the “building blocks” of IT (Grifith & Northcraft, 1994) and the main resources provided to users. As such, they constitute initial triggers for user sense-making (Grifith, 1999; Rafaeli & Vilnai-Yavetz, 2004), helping users discover the actions that they make possible (i.e., afordances) and the tasks that can be accomplished with them (Goodhue, 1995). Moreover, when users recognise a match between IT features and a task requirement, they adapt their behaviour to leverage this fit (Goodhue, 1995; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Todd & Benbasat, 2000) and enhance their task performance (Jasperson et al., 2005).

However, IT features vary in their concreteness (Grifith, 1999), and their latent capabilities are not always easily deducible, mainly because they are interrelated and their functionalities are interconnected (Gutek, Bikson, & Mankin, 1984). Moreover, since action possibilities are deduced from IT features, the latter are more likely to elicit user sense-making and responses if they have strong visual components. This aligns with Visual Rhetoric Theory, which suggests that communicated visual elements stimulate sense-mak ing and influence behaviour (Cyr, Head, Larios, & Pan, 2009; Scott, 1994). Thus, users make sense of IT features and uncover their action potential on the basis of the experience that they have acquired through a series of interactions (Al-Natour & Benbasat, 2009). Since not all features are equally visible, the ones that have a greater level of visibility are more likely to be richer in SE that help users identify feature-supported actions. As a result, the diferent communicative capabilities of IT features sometimes help explain why they do not have the same efects in diferent user groups (Rogers, 1995; Weick, 1990).

To illustrate these relationships, Figure 2 shows the links between features, SE, and afordances in the case of Social Network Sites (SNSs). As venues for information sharing, SNSs are typically enabled by a variety of concurrent interactivity functions (Hsu, Chen, Huang, & Huang, 2012), and they are usually rich in features with strong SE. Interpreting visual features such as the “Like” (i.e., the thumbs-up symbol) often amplifies the impression that users have of social presence and enjoyment, and it frequently shapes their overall SNS experience, including their intention to continue using the SNS (Cheikh-Ammar & Barki, 2014, 2016). By enabling a popular form of sociotechnical feedback, the “Like” feature afords SNS users the possibility of sharing messages with their network of friends and followers. Beyond message-sharing, many users see a Facebook “Like” as symbolising positive reinforcement from their social network (Cheikh-Ammar & Barki, 2016) that may enhance their SNS reputation (Kietzmann, Hermkens, McCarthy, & Silvestre, 2011). Thus, the symbolism of this sociotechnical feature helps users uncover possible afordances related to the SNS, such as providing support or expressing opinions (Figure 2). Additional features, such as the comment box, also exist on SNSs and ofer similar afordances. This illustrates how many features help uncover afordances and how SE help users in this interpretative process.

## 4.2. From action to values

Users make sense of IT features and their SE in order to uncover the afordances connected to them. Afordances are inherently hierarchical, they exist at diferent levels of abstraction, and they emerge from diferent structural levels (Volkof & Strong, 2013). This is because they are possibilities for goal-oriented actions and because goals themselves are decomposable (Savoli, 2014). For instance, an email system afords users the possibility of collaborating with others (a high-level afordance). This afordance can be decomposed into a series of sub-afordances, such as sending and receiving messages (medium-level afordances), and these sub-afordances can then be further decomposed into lower-level afordances, such as replying and/or forwarding messages (Savoli, 2014). Thus, afordances and goal-directed actions merge together in and through the interrelations between their constituent parts (Elder-Vass, 2007).

This is where the issue of values becomes important. Human actions are often undertaken in the name of one or more values (Schwartz, 1994). The latter are fundamental motives that prompt human beings to initiate voluntary behaviour (Reiss & Havercamp, 1996; Reiss & Wiltz, 2004); they are “core conceptions of the desirable within every individual and society” (Rokeach, 1973). In other words, they are highly appealing drives. Nevertheless, although values are desirable ends that individuals strive to achieve, they are not necessarily a function of a primarily cognitive process, since individuals may not be explicitly aware of them as motives (Reiss, 2000). Here, it is necessary to specify the four main characteristics of values: (1) They are considered relevant by a social entity; (2) They motivate and justify action; (3) Through socialisation, they can develop into dominant group norms based on the learning experiences of individuals; and (4) Since they are shaped by social experiences, they are not universal and can vary significantly among groups and cultures (Schwartz, 1994). This aligns with research in social psychology, where human values are viewed as guiding principles of human action capable of determining individual choices, beliefs, and attitudes (Rokeach, 1973). As a result, afordances at the highest levels are directly linked to the values fostered by a given IS context, and they reside at one level of abstraction below them. Notwithstanding this direct link, afor dances, and values remain conceptually diferent, since the former consist of action possibilities, whereas the latter are sought-after ends (Schwartz, 1994) that can be realised by the enactment of actions.

The idea that values can be associated with IT artifacts is not new (Rafaeli & Vilnai-Yavetz, 2004). Organisational researchers have often emphasised the capability of physical artifacts to communicate intended organisational values (e.g., Jones, 1996; Yanow, 1998), and the view that they have this capability fits with the conceptualisation of Spirit put forward by DeSanctis and Poole (1994). However, the present paper argues for a diferent position on this issue. The view put forward here is that values do not stem directly from the features themselves, but are instead rooted in the enactment of action possibilities made possible by an IT artifact (an afordance or a combination of afordances at the highest level of abstraction). This means that values emerge as a product of IT interactions, develop within a given user group, and reside in the relation between this group and the IT artifact. Thus, values are distinct from organisational intent even if they may be influenced by it, given that the resources and the constraints of the IT are built into it in accordance with organisational intent and that they are what users have to work with. To illustrate these relations, it may be useful to revisit the SNS example and the “Like” feature (see Figure 3).

The “Like” feature afords users the possibility of sharing with their network of friends and followers information that they consider relevant. In addition, the “thumbs-up” symbolism of this feature may also indicate positive reinforcement linked to approval and/ or acknowledgement, which is one of the reasons that when an SNS afords the collection and display of “Likes” received from other users, this often promotes power or status within a social network (Cheikh-Ammar & Barki, 2014, 2016). It is well known that power and status are frequently cited human values capable of motivating behaviour (Rokeach, 1973; Schwartz, 1994). Moreover, along with the diferent feedback afordances that it makes available, an SNS afords users the possibility of accumulating and displaying a list of friends and followers (Boyd & Ellison, 2007), and this promotes Acceptance as an additional value that can be fostered on SNS. Similarly, the number of SNS friends that users accumulate and the frequency of the feedback that they receive on SNS posts are also important factors in promoting power and acceptance. This illustrates how values like power, status, and acceptance can be experienced on SNS and how the combination of these values shapes the Spirit of an SNS and influences user behaviour.

## 4.3. From values to spirit

Human behaviour is often determined by the ways that values are combined together and prioritised (Schwartz, 1994). In the case of IT-related behaviour, the combination and the prioritisation of the values that emerge in relation to an IT artifact and a user group form the IT Spirit that orients users in their present and future IT interactions. At the same time, this “guiding” Spirit is being constantly reshaped itself through the sense-making that occurs in these interactions.

The concept of IT Spirit highlights the possibility of multiple values coexisting within the same IT. For example, a popular SNS such as Twitter can ofer an opportunity for public figures like politicians and opinion leaders to consolidate or increase their power by providing them with a venue to difuse their ideas, enhance their public visibility, display their existing support base, and increase their network of followers. A venue like Twitter can also be experienced as a platform for curiosity-seekers who want to feel closer to their favourite celebrities and idols or stay informed about topics of interest. Thus, the sense-making connected to the human values that users interpret as meaningful on a specific SNS helps to shape the Spirit of that SNS. However, this does not entail that this Spirit is stable over time, for it takes shape and continues to develop through the continuous appropriation of the SNS.

IT Spirit is the incorporeal essence of an IT artifact. As such, it can be contrasted with the material components of an IT through which it develops as an integrated expression of overarching values. IT Spirit consists in these overarching values to the extent that they are promoted by IT artifacts and their features, by the appropriation actions of their users, and by the context of their use. This means that it cannot be reduced to what the system looks like in terms of configurations, to the features that the system contains, or to what these features symbolise. This conceptualisation of IT Spirit underscores the distinction between Spirit as a high level projection of the values that orient the experience of using a technology and the more concrete, material features of a technology that exist more or less independently of the experience of using it. Although they are partially inspired by features, the human values comprising IT Spirit remain independent from them because features and values are at opposite extremes in terms of abstraction levels. Features are at the most tangible level (indeed, sometimes they are visible, in which case their

SE becomes relevant), but Spirit is at the highest possible echelon of abstraction.

IT Spirit was initially conceptualised as a property of an IT (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), but here it is conceptualised as residing in the relation between a user group and an IT in a given context. This conceptualisation highlights the emergence of meanings in and through the use of technical objects (Bijker, 2010), a process that is shaped by the context of IT use as well as by the forms and functionalities of IT features (Orlikowski, 1992). IT Spirit is infused with the intentions of the designers who have brought a specific set of features into existence, yet it is continuously reshaped by the interpretations and actions of IT users (i.e., by SE, Afordances, and Values), and also by the perceptions that various other stakeholders have of its use (Scott, Quinn, Timmerman, & Garrett, 1998). This emergence of meaning is also afected by the intervening actions of IT implementers (Gottschalk, 1999), who are responsible for the successful use of the IT (Markus, 1983; Rivard & Lapointe, 2012), and who sometimes attempt to influence users’ perceptions of the IT artifact or of its potential outcomes (Joshi, 1991; Martinko, Zmud, & Henry, 1996).

## 4.4. From sense-making to appropriation

In Figure 4, the IT artifact is depicted as having layers of interconnected conceptual elements that are linked in a many-to-many relationship, with every afordance shaped by one or multiple combinations of features and with every feature with any level of symbolism afording one or multiple action possibilities. As for the interconnections between afordances and values, every afordance helps make multiple values possible, whereas every value is promoted by one or multiple afordances. Each of these conceptual layers represents opportunities for user sense-making, which is the fundamental way in which individuals and groups undertake appropriation. This process is discussed in more detail below.

![](/api/attachments/3KV5GD8J/fulltext/images/bb1e63586c17512b4ad562f8800f490c3218835b31e6fd06257d03996b1f83e3.jpg)  
Figure 4. Relationship between features, afordances, and values.

Users make sense of IT artifacts and their features and attempt to interpret their capabilities. Their interpretations are often shaped by their personal experiences with the IT (Al-Natour & Benbasat, 2009), as well as by their personality traits (Devaraj, Easley, & Crant, 2008) and their talents and abilities (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). User sense-making is initiated through interactions with IT features in which action potentials are deduced, an interpretative process often facilitated by the rich symbolism that is embedded in features (Grifith, 1999). Since not all features are equally visible, the ones that have a greater level of visibility are more likely to be richer in SE that help users identify feature-supported actions. This explanation of user sense-making aligns with existing views on visual literacy and on the ability of individuals to interpret artifacts and associate meanings with them on the basis of their visual components (Debes, 1969; Fransecky & Debes, 1972). In a word, SE and the visual communication that they facilitate help users interpret IT features and deduce their action possibilities, and this influences users’ subsequent behaviour (Cheikh-Ammar & Barki, 2016).

Although for any given technology only a select and often narrow set of features is used (Davenport, 1998; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; Ross & Weill, 2002), with time and experience feature use frequently changes. The interpretation of features in terms of action potential and meaning evolves within a social context, and this can lead to feature use that deviates from what was initially intended or envisioned (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). Sense-making is often influenced by the changing evaluation schemes employed by users to deal with surrounding threats and control levels (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005, 2010), as well as by the emerging practices of a user group and its appropriations of the technology (Leonardi, 2007). As a result, sense-making is not a one-time event. On the contrary, it is likely to be repeated and to evolve over time as various features are abandoned, routinised, and/ or extended (Hartwick & Barki, 1994) with diferent efects on users (Goodhue, 1995; Grifith, 1999; Tyre & Orlikowski, 1994).

Because a user group continuously enacts and reenacts actions aforded by an IT artifact, a more or less unified set of values emerges within the group’s social context. It is through this process of collective sense-making and of making subsequent appropriations based on shared practices that an IT artifact becomes meaningful (Riemer & Johnston, 2014). Over time, this process results in a combination of prioritised values, a combination that constitutes the Spirit of the IT. However, since changes in the ways users understand IT rules and resources are often brought about by the actions and interactions of the members of the user group (Grifin, Ledbetter, & Sparks, 2014), IT Spirit is constantly shaped and reshaped by the ever-recurring eforts of users to make sense of IT artifacts and their features and to appropriate them. And since the understanding that users have of an IT artifact influences the way that they appropriate IT features (DeSanctis et al., 2008), the values that they associate with an IT help shape their subsequent appropriations. This continuous cycle of sense-making and appropriation moves from the individual level that shapes initial actions up to the group or social level that shapes most subsequent actions (Grifith, 1999).

Appropriation often depends on the restrictiveness of an IS. Restrictiveness refers to the degree of flexibility that an IS has with respect to the ways that it can be used (Wheeler & Valacich, 1996). A restrictive IS limits the action possibilities of its users and often leads to utilisations that are more consistent with, or faithful to, its predetermined intents (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Although it may be worthwhile to examine faithfulness of IT use in relation to organisational intents, it can also be analysed in terms of the values that emerge for a given user group in a specific context. For instance, in SNS contexts there is generally a low degree of restrictiveness because very few information-sharing rules are imposed on users. What information they share, how they share it, and how they use the site in general are subject to little control. However, over time as user groups continue to use an SNS, an implicit consensus about values becomes established (e.g., power, status, and romance) and guides users in their subsequent SNS appropriation. In such a context, unfaithful use must be examined in terms of the appropriateness of an IT with respect to emerging values (i.e., SNS Spirit). For example, the use of a career-focused SNS, such as LinkedIn for non-professional purposes is considered unfaithful given that it diverges from the Spirit of the SNS. Nevertheless, although in some cases this unfaithful use will be automatically rejected by users, in other cases it may catch on and become a new accepted norm that reshapes the Spirit of the SNS and thus the subsequent use of the SNS artifact. Although such alterations of the SNS Spirit generally reflect an emerging consensus among users, some users may still choose to resist and even abandon the SNS because of its new spirit.

## 5. Discussion and implication for future research

Explicitly engaging with IT artifacts and with their material and social aspects is a vital and yet often overlooked issue in IS research (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). This lack of attention to what many consider the core of the IS discipline (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Weber, 1987) remains the subject of an on-going debate (Akhlaghpour et al., 2013). However, it is important to stress that IT artifacts are complex entities coming in many diferent shapes and sizes and that they are always afected by social practices. IT artifacts do not remain static, as they are embedded in spatial, temporal, discursive, and social contexts that determine what they are; therefore, it comes as no surprise that IT artifacts should represent a daunting challenge to researchers who attempt to theorise them. This is why there are no one-sizefits-all conceptualisations with the theoretical power to explain all the discreet intricacies of IT artifacts adequately (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). That being said, the objective of the present paper has been to provide some important conceptual clues to facilitate their analysis and to ease their inclusion in IS research. This has involved examining IT artifacts as products of the multiple and loosely interconnected ingredients that constitute the main sources of sense-making available to users (Rafaeli & Vilnai-Yavetz, 2004).

Although little benefit can be gained from conceptualising IT artifacts as stable, independent, or fixed entities, providing a theoretical articulation of their core components is a worthwhile endeavour (Weber, 1987). As a contribution to this endeavour, the present paper puts forward a conceptualisation that facilitates the incorporation of the IT artifact into IS research. This conceptualisation portrays IT artifacts as complex entities that are fundamentally structured by the intertwining relationships that exist between IT features and their SE, by the afordances related to these features at diferent levels of abstraction, and by the overarching values that are revealed from within a user group, the projection of these values and user adherence to them constituting the IT Spirit that orients all subsequent interactions. Each of these IT artifact components triggers the user sense-making that shapes the appropriation of an IT and its features. Although this conceptualisation is based on existent concepts, it is one of the first to attempt to integrate these concepts in a unified presentation of their interconnections, and we have seen that this presentation involves clarifying and sometimes even redefining them. This efort to refine the conceptual analysis of the components of the IT artifact and to explain their interconnections is an important contribution to the IS literature, since it is only by undertaking this task that the potential value of these constructs can be revealed (Barki, 2008).

IS researchers have often emphasised the need to focus on IT features (Jasperson et al., 2005; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001), but doing so comes with significant challenges. The Repeating Decomposition Problem (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; DeSanctis et al., 1994; Grifith, 1999), as well as the dificulty of identifying theoretically supported and conceptually encompassing sets of features that represent the IT artifact, may explain why researchers have generally avoided this approach. While more manageable concepts, such as Afordances, have attracted significant attention from researchers, they do not ofer a comprehensive representation of the IT artifact and they do not provide a means to tackle the ubiquitous identification problem, since they too are multiple and easily decomposable into numerous sub-categories. Contrary to IT Features and Afordances, the concept of IT Spirit is not hierarchical, which means that it cannot be decomposed into sub-concepts existing at diferent levels of abstraction. Moreover, while it has been suggested that SE and Afordances could replace DeSanctis and Poole’s (1994) Spirit, the present article presents these concepts as both distinct and interrelated at the same time, for SE are seen as instigating the interpretation of IT features that uncovers possible afordances, which – once revealed – can then promote context-specific values. The prioritised sets of values connected to an IS and the combinations of these prioritised sets of values – as promoted by IT features and the afordances that they provide – constitute the IT Spirit in given contexts. This means that the concept of IT Spirit provides a relevant unit of analysis, since the values it projects are generally easier to manoeuvre than the numerous, always decomposable IT features and the multitude of hierarchical action possibilities that they aford. In a word, IT Spirit is a valuable concept for IS research because, by linking pivotal afordances to the emerging values in a given context, it provides a logical approach to identifying these afordances and thus facilitates the study of IT impacts.

In addition, this conceptualisation extends existing perspective on IT artifacts that views them as tools to be examined based on a means-ends relationship and that stresses their preconceived purposes (Brigham & Introna, 2006). This entails also including the social dimension of IT artifacts, so that a better explanation of the real impacts of their use becomes possible (Brigham & Introna, 2006; Ciborra, 1998; Kreps, 2016). Very often the technological aspects of an IT are not solely responsible for IT efects. Indeed, the latter are usually a function of how users adapt and appropriate an IT to meet their needs, especially since it is users who decide whether or not to neglect an IT or work around some of its features. This issue is central to the conceptualisation of IT Spirit presented here, which focuses on the emergence of values in specific sociotechnical contexts as a means of moving from a “correct” technological interpretation to a “truthful” interpretation of the IT artifact that reveals what it essentially is (Heidegger, 1977, p. 9). This ontological interpretation of the IT artifact is essential for studying the impact of IT on individuals, organisations, and societies.

Since IT Spirit is ultimately shaped by IT use and by the enacted appropriations of features, users may only have a partial experience of it or they may not be aware of it at all. Thus, it is IS researchers who have the task of working out an adequate conceptual frame for describing an IT Spirit and its consequences. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) have argued that elaborating such a frame requires focusing on designers’ intentions as well as on

IT features and their implementation characteristics, but the present study has shown that an efort must also be made to provide an account of surrounding IT activities (e.g., Barki, Titah, & Bofo, 2007) and an analysis of emergent values and action potentialities.

For instance, for the past decade Facebook ha defined its mission by focusing on the idea of connecting friends and families together (Chaykowski, 2017). To a certain extent, this mission corresponds to how the Spirit of Facebook would be understood from the perspec tive of DeSanctis and Poole’s (1994) conceptualisation. However, we are living in a world characterised by fake news, cyber warfare, and online extremist propaganda, a world where meddling in foreign political afairs has become feasible because of SNSs like Facebook (Abramson, 2017). Given this new reality, examining the impacts of an SNS solely on the basis of the initial intent of its founders (i.e., Spirit based on DeSanctis and Poole’s (1994) conceptualisation), and the original design characteristics that they set in place, amounts to assessing the impact of the technology blindfolded. Similarly, the oficial mission statement of Twitter is “to give everyone the power to create and share ideas and information instantly, without barriers”, but although this statement reflects values of social equality and idealism, it is clear that to provide an adequate explanation of contemporary Twitter use, it would be necessary to take into consideration other values that have emerged among Twitter users. For example, it would probably be necessary to examine trends in SNS use among politicians, some of whom may see power and status as the strongest values present on this SNS. Twitter has aforded these users with a means to distract public opinion, bypass tradi tional media, and transmit messages that are not filtered through the opinions and criticisms of journalists and reporters. Thus, for politicians, the value of power on Twitter may be connected, not only to the possibility of displaying the size of one’s support base, but also to the possibility of communicating directly with voters without being challenged by the critical assessments of traditional news outlets.

The conceptualisation of IT Spirit presented here could help researchers address these emerging issues following two diferent but equally relevant nomological routes. In the first, available features and the enactment of action possibilities aforded by them within a user group of interest would be examined in order to reveal the values pursued in a given context. In the second, IT Spirit and its values would be the starting point. Here, the objective would be to reveal how IT Spirit helps modify and shape the IT artifact and its features during use as well as how the latter are appropriated, modified or neglected because of IT Spirit. Thus, the conceptualisation of the IT artifact and its Spirit presented in this study ofers rich avenues for future research. Given the ever-evolving nature of IT Spirit, one avenue to explore is related to the efects of IT Spirit and its values on user adoption and continuous use decisions. Especially in voluntary use contexts, IT Spirit plays a significant role in the continuous use decisions made by users on the basis of the possible fit (or misfit) of an IT artifact with the values that they pursue as individuals (Cheikh-Ammar & Barki, 2013). In other words, users are more likely to continue using an IT if they experience its evolving Spirit as being compatible with their own values. Therefore, examining the efects that this fit has on users at specific points in time is an interesting and potentially fruitful avenue for future research.

This conceptualisation may also be helpful for explaining the varied impacts of IT implementations in organisational settings. For instance, Lapointe and Rivard (2005) have examined the implementation of health information systems in hospitals and developed a theory of IS resistance. Among the analysed cases, there were two separate hospitals that attempted to implement the same software package for their electronic medical records (EMRs). In one of the hospitals the implemen tation was considered successful, but in the other it was considered a failure. The organisational (or designers’) intents, which would typically translate into a series of features and afordances, were identical in both imple mentations. However, the IS users (doctors and nurses) in the two hospitals interpreted these IT artifacts and interacted with each other and with the EMR very dif ferently. Indeed, the doctors in one hospital opposed it relentlessly, whereas the doctors in the other hospital accepted it willingly. As an emergent combination of values experienced by a group of users in a given context, and as one of the layers that compose the IT artifact, IT Spirit could help provide a plausible explanation for th discrepancy between these two outcomes. In fact, the values of power and status, more specifically the loss of power and status, were key issues related to the doctors resistance in the one hospital (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). Obviously, IS implementers did not intend or even envision this Spirit, yet it nevertheless emerged in and through user sense-making and appropriation and was possibly also created in part by the various interventions of the implementers (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012). In the failed EMR project, the system was abandoned five years after being introduced. During this period, the various rounds of EMR appropriation revealed that diferent values such as eficiency, power, and status emerged at diferent times. Thus, it is likely that these changes in IT Spirit eventually culminated in very strong resistance and led to the abandonment of the EMR project. This example illustrates how two IT artifacts that appear to be identical, when one only considers their features and their designers’ intentions, can turn out to be very difer ent because of their IT Spirit. Therefore, it is important to analyse the IT artifact in its context of use, following significant events at diferent points in time. To do this, researchers not only have to account for IT features and their appropriation, they also have to consider activities related to the IT artifact and its use (Barki et al., 2007), as well as work practices (Davis & Hufnagel, 2007), implementation strategies, and management interventions (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012).

In other words, using the conceptualisation presented in this study, researchers should examine the evolution of IT artifacts and their impact over time. During the life cycle of an IT, numerous interventions occur (e.g., training sessions, managerial decisions, and communications), various new features are added, and a great many upgrades take place. Often this involves modifications that bring into play new afordances – as well as new features with variant levels of SE – that can (re)-shape some of the values of the IT Spirit. Examining the impact of such chronological alterations on user sense-making would be revealing. In addition, faithfulness could be examined following an upgrade, a managerial intervention, or a design intervention (i.e., an addition of new features), for once an implicit value consensus has come to the fore after numerous rounds of interactions, it becomes possible and sometimes very useful to examine the faithfulness of the update at a specific point in time. Indeed, feature updates/additions can fail to harmonise or fit with the value consensus projected by an IT Spirit. In such a case, the update is considered “unfaithful” and it may lead to significant impacts on users. This is clearly another important issue that future research could look into from the perspective of IT Spirit.

Finally, the conceptualisation of the IT artifact presented in this article could be used in future research on experiential technologies and their entanglement in everyday human activities (Yoo, 2010). With the enhanced reliability and reach of the Internet, and in a world where mobility is the new norm (Middleton, Scheepers, & Tuunainen, 2014), information technologies have become indispensable to human beings in their daily lives (Lyytinen & Yoo, 2002). In everyday environments, people often use multiple technologies simultaneously in entangled bundles that provide support for a wide range of human activities (Yoo, 2010). Although researchers have recently begun to view these technologies as social actors and intelligent decision-making partners, instead of as mere tools for increasing productivity (Al-Natour & Benbasat, 2009), there is still a tendency to focus on discrete IT artifacts in isolation from each other without examining their interdependencies (Yoo, 2010). The conceptualisation of the IT artifact presented here may ofer an extremely interesting approach to this issue. Indeed, to researchers who wish to analyse the entanglement of IT artifacts in experiential computing contexts, it provides a frame that could help account for the interdependencies of diferent technologies by providing a viable theoretical view of them as technological bundles integrated into human activities.

## 6. Conclusion

The field of IS has grown significantly since its inception in the 1970s. IS researchers have traced the progress of their field as a continuously evolving academic discipline, and they have devoted significant efort to reinforcing its legitimacy and defining its domain (DeSanctis, 2003). At times, they have even questioned whether it can make significant contributions to theory and to practice (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Dennis, 2001; Hassan, 2011; Rosemann & Vessey, 2008; Ryan & Mathieson, 2001; Sawy, Galliers, Loebbecke, Watson, & Desouza, 2006). Although the identity discourse that IS researchers have engaged in has undoubtedly helped the field move forward, the opinions of prominent scholars on the evolution of our discipline continue to vary significantly, some seeing it as a field in crisis (e.g., Benbasat & Zmud, 2003; Gill & Bhattacherjee, 2009), others seeing it as one that is progressively coming to maturity (e.g., Grover, Gokhale, Lim, Cofey, & Ayyagari, 2006; Myers & Baskerville, 2009; Power, 2003). Be that as it may, many IS researchers believe that the IT artifact should receive additional theoretical emphasis (Akhlaghpour et al., 2013; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Wu & Saunders, 2003).

It has not been the aim of the present study to engage in a debate on the relevance of the IT artifact for IS research. However, the present study does have the goal of providing adequate conceptual handles for theorising about the IT artifact to those who wish to do so. Behind this goal lies the belief that the efort to articulate the general characteristics of IT artifacts is worthwhile because it can contribute to developing and enriching IS-centred paradigms (Weber, 1987). It is hoped that by encouraging scholars to incorporate the IT artifact and its various components into their theoretical work, the conceptualisation of IT Spirit and the accompanying view of the IT artifact that are presented here will help reduce the anxiety that so often pervades discussions of the value of IS research (Hassan, 2014). It is also hoped that future research will challenge and further develop this conceptualisation by providing further elaborations of the key concepts and their interrelations.
