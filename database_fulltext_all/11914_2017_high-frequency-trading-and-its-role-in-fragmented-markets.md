## Abstract

Securities trading underwent a major transformation within the last decade. This transformation was mainly driven by the regulatory induced fragmentation and by the increase of high-frequency trading (HFT). On the basis of the electronic market hypothesis, which poses that coordination costs decline when markets become automated, and the efficient market hypothesis in its semi-strong form, we study the effect of HFT on market efficiency in the European fragmented market landscape. In doing so, we further incorporate the realm of financialization, which criticizes the increase in transaction speed. By conducting a long-term analysis of CAC 40 securities, we find that HFT increases market efficiency by leveling midpoints between Euronext Paris and Bats Chi-X Europe. On the basis of a crosscountry event study, we analyze the effect of the German HFT Act. We observe that the midpoint dispersion of blue chip securities between the two leading venues Deutsche Boerse and Bats Chi-X Europe increased. We conclude that HFT increases market efficiency in the European market landscape by transmitting information between distant markets. Journal of Information Technology (2017). doi:10.1057/s41265-016-0030-6

Keywords: electronic market hypothesis; high-frequency trading; market efficiency; regulation; securities trading

## Introduction

The two major drivers behind this development are regulatory and technological advancements. On the regulatory side, the largest impact on securities trading had the introduction of MiFID I in Europe and likewise the Reg NMS in the U.S. which liberalized trading by permitting new trading venues (European Commission, 2004; Security Exchanges Commission, 2005). While the goal of these regulatory efforts to break up the monopoly of national markets succeeded, securities markets became fragmented, which resulted in new regulatory challenges. The second major factor shaping today’s securities markets is innovations in IT. These are fostered by the fragmentation of trading like Smart Order Routing (Foucault and Menkveld, 2008) or by the technological advancements like high-frequency trading (HFT). The latter is subject to intense public and academic discussion (Lattermann et al., 2012; Kirilenko et al., 2014) as the market share of HFT in Europe is 24–43% (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2014). Against this background and in the light of market events such as the

May 6, 2010, flash crash or the immense \$480 million Knight Capital trading loss several regulatory measures like HFT regulations or trading halts (also known as circuit breakers; Gomber et al., 2013) were implemented. Despite these efforts, the effects of HFT on securities trading are put into question by academics and regulators and still need to be analyzed carefully (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2016a; Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013; Zhang, 2010). In this paper, we want to shed light on the role of HFT in a highly fragmented market environment. On the basis of a data set covering French blue chip securities, we find that HFT increases market efficiency. To further add robustness to our results, we conduct a cross-country event study by extending our investigation to the German HFT Act. We find in this comparative approach that market efficiency in German securities deteriorated opposed to their unregulated French counterparts.

In our research, we follow a cross-disciplinary approach as we ground our work on following major hypotheses: financialization (social sciences; Aalbers, 2015), electronic markets hypothesis (information systems; Malone et al., 1987) and efficient market hypothesis (finance; Fama,

1970). Financialization is defined as the increasing dominance of financial firms and financial markets over states, economies, firms and households (Aalbers, 2015). IT is one of the key drivers behind this development which helps to increase the transaction velocity in the financial service industry (Lagoarde-Segot, 2016). While this development is criticized in the realm of financialization, we analyze if positive spill-overs can arise for market participants which do not invest in IT. The electronic markets hypothesis predicts that the shift from traditional to digital markets reduces coordination costs. These cost reductions result from a decrease in search costs. In this paper, we argue that not only the digitization of markets plays a role in materializing these cost benefits but also the level of automation. The cost reduction is caused by high-frequency traders (HFTs) who level the prices among venues in the fragmented market system. In doing so, we further draw on the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) (Fama, 1970). Financial markets should be as efficient as possible which is demonstrated by a large literature stream on the EMH (Fama, 1991). In its semi-strong form, the EMH poses that investors should be able to trade on prices which incorporate all public information. If this is not the case, information asymmetries arise which result in an inefficient allocation of capital. As outlined in the first subsection, European equities markets are highly fragmented, thus price dispersions occur among distant venues, an ideal setting to investigate the effect of HFT on semi-strong market efficiency.

## Theoretical background

## Financialization

The term financialization refers to a concept that poses that the increasing dominance of financial firms and financial market participants results in an ongoing transformation of states, economies, their firms and households (Aalbers, 2015). The major drivers behind this development are economic deregulation, the shareholder paradigm (valuing the shareholder above stakeholder value) and IT (Lagoarde-Segot, 2016). In particular, the latter plays a major role as faster IT systems are implemented throughout the financial service industry. While the utilization of modern IT in the financial service sector to foster costs benefits is not entirely new, the rise of a new technology, i.e., HFT-shaped financial markets stronger than any technology before. This technology gradually transforms the financial markets to highly technological sophisticated systems. Putting this technology in the context of the framework of Lagoarde-Segot (2016), it foremost changes the financial sector by increasing transaction velocity.

In this paper, we analyze whether this increase in transaction velocity can be beneficial to all market participants, most importantly to market participants who do not invest in this technology. We argue that HFTs provide a useful function in financial markets as they help prices to become current on all respective markets. In doing so, this increase in transaction velocity can be indeed beneficial to all market participants including retail traders, who typically only have access to their national market.

## Electronic markets hypothesis (ELMH)

The electronic markets hypothesis was postulated by Malone et al. (1987) who theorize that IT will reduce the costs in businesses which operate in markets along their value chain. The theory defines the following two mechanisms: Electronic markets (hierarchies) with low (high) production costs and high (low) coordination costs. We focus on electronic markets, where the main drivers are smaller coordination costs. Strictly speaking, electronic markets can help market participants to find the best offering for a product by automatically comparing distinct properties, for instance, different qualities and prices. Thus, IT can substantially reduce search costs for market participants (Hess and Kemerer, 1994). Malone et al. (1987) outline that this development is primarily driven by three trends in market structure. Firstly, the electronic communication effect, which materializes in increased information speed and reduced communication costs. Secondly, the electronic brokerage effect, where IT collects and matches bids and offerings by taking into account more offerings, increasing the quality of the selection and decreasing selection costs. Thirdly, the electronic integration effect which transports information across different processes saving costs of reentering information (Malone et al., 1987).

Bakos (1991) analyzes the key factors for cost reduction in electronic markets. He concludes that economies of scale and scope, technological uncertainty and switching costs also play an important role. Further, the validity of the ELMH has been investigated for several markets which recently underwent the change from a traditional to an electronic form. Daniel and Klimis (1999) support the ELMH for electronic brokers and web sites which provide financial information and reduce coordination costs. Hess and Kemerer (1994) test the ELMH against loan originating systems for home mortgages by conducting five case studies. In line with Daniel and Klimis (1999), they find supporting evidence for the ELMH, but also note that the effects predicted by the hypothesis require a long period to mature.

This study analyzes markets which underwent a major technological development within the last decade: European securities markets. The outlined trends in market structure could already be observed, i.e., increased information speed by lowering latency through HFT (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013; Riordan and Storkenmaier, 2012) and electronic brokerage such as Smart Order Routers (Ende et al., 2010; Foucault and Menkveld, 2008). Also, European securities markets are electronically integrated by arbitrageurs, leveling prices along distant venues. Against this background and considering the ELMH, we analyze empirically whether we can also observe a reduction of coordination costs in securities markets. By doing so, we further argue that the level of automation in electronic markets is directly related to coordination cost savings. In our investigation, the level of automation is represented by the level of HFT activity.

## Efficient market hypothesis (EMH)

The EMH, formulated by Fama (1970), is the central cornerstone in capital markets theories (Lagoarde-Segot, 2015). It states that a market is efficient if all available information is immediately incorporated within asset prices. The EMH defines three forms of efficiency: the weak form, the semi-strong form and the strong form. The weak form assumes that returns cannot be predicted solely on the basis of past returns, i.e., all past information is incorporated in the current price (Hudson et al., 1996). The semi-strong form poses that all public information of an asset should be immediately reflected within the price. Finally, in its strong form, prices need to incorporate all public and private information to be efficient (Fama, 1970).

Research on the EMH analyzes the efficient financial markets following the three respective forms (Fama, 1991; Hudson et al., 1996). We focus on the semi-strong efficiency which received less academic attention compared to the weak form efficiency. The tests for the semi-strong efficiency focus on the speed of information dissemination. It has been shown that future and commodity market prices (Leuthold and Hartmann, 1979), forward rates as well as future spot rates (Hamburger and Platt, 1975) and macroeconomic data and security prices (Groenewold and Kang, 1993) are not semi-strong efficient. Contrary to this, it has been observed that Indian (Khan and Ikram, 2010) and the Australian (Hameed and Ahsraf, 2009) equity markets are semi-strong efficient.

In this study, we analyze the information coordination between two distant markets. For example, new information arrives at the first market being publicly available immediately. In the context of the EMH, this is a new public information. Hence, the second market needs to receive this information immediately as well, otherwise the market cannot be semi-strong efficient, i.e., the overall efficiency declines in the market system. Against this background, we investigate whether HFT can increase the semi-strong market efficiency within European securities markets. Putting this in the context of the EMH literature stream, we examine which factors lead to semi-strong efficiency rather than only analyzing the efficiency of a market.

## Research context: HFT in European securities markets

## High-frequency trading

Since the Flash Crash on the May 6, 2010, HFT has become prominent in the public, academic and regulatory discussion (Kirilenko et al., 2014; Lattermann et al., 2012). HFT is a part of algorithmic trading (AT) which is defined as ‘‘the use of computer algorithms to automatically make trading decisions, submit orders, and manage those orders after submis sion’’ (Hendershott et al., 2011). While AT is mostly used to buy or sell a large position of securities without a market impact (Gomber and Gsell, 2006), HFT focuses on proprietary trading with a flat position at the end of the day. HFT exhibits a high trading volume and frequent order updates. It typically employs arbitrage, market making, liquidity detection and trend strategies (Menkveld, 2013). To assess the economic value of HFT, market quality is evaluated. The discipline, which among others analyzes trading mechanism, is called market microstructure (Madhavan, 2000). Most studies suggest that HFT decreases volatility, thus being helpful to market quality as it dampens mispricing (Hagstroemer and Norden, 2013; Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013). Contrary to this, Zhang (2010) finds that HFT activity is positively correlated with volatility. Regarding to the spread (difference between best bid and best offer) which represents the indirect transaction costs, Hasbrouck and Saar (2013) show a positive (decreasing) effect of HFT. Likewise, this was shown for AT in general (Hendershott et al., 2011). These results are in line with an event study conducted by Malinova et al. (2013) who observe an increase in the spread due to a message-limit which aimed at reducing HFT activity in Canada. Similar effects were discovered by Friederich and Payne (2015) who show that limiting HFT activity, by introducing an Order-to-Trade (OTR) limit, decreased the liquidity (i.e., quoted depth) on the Italian securities market.

Price efficiency is a measure for the informativeness of the prices. Regarding the semi-strong form of the EMH (Fama, 1970), all information should immediately be incorporated in the price. Brogaard et al. (2014) find that HFT increases price efficiency by trading against the opposite direction of pricing errors. These results are further confirmed by an empirical study by Carrion (2013). However, opposite results could also been shown by Zhang (2010) who warns that HFT might be harmful as he finds that HFT decreases price efficiency. Using a theoretical model, Gerig (2012) shows that HFTs increase the efficiency of financial markets by synchronizing prices among different venues. In particular, the model predicts that HFTs increase the accuracy of prices and lower transaction costs.

## European securities markets Fragmentation

European securities markets have undergone a major development within the last decade. This development is mainly driven by the fragmentation of as a result of MiFID I (European Commission, 2004). Since the introduction of MiFID I securities can be traded at several venues. As of 2016 the following European venues exist: regulated markets (RM, 102 venues), multilateral trading facilities (MTF, 152 venues) and systematic internalizers (SI, 11 venues) (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2016b). Even though not all instruments can be traded on all venues, the sheer amount of 265 possible trading locations led to a fragmented European market landscape (Fidessa, 2016). This resulted in a fierce competition among venues in terms of fee structure (e.g., maker taker pricing), order types (e.g., innovative iceberg order designs) and infrastructure (e.g., latency of exchange systems). The competition for order flow in Europe leads to an overall improvement of liquidity (Chlistalla and Lutat, 2011; Degryse et al., 2015a; Foucault and Menkveld, 2008). Considering price discovery (how information is processed into prices), Storkenmaier et al. (2012) conclude that information is asymmetrically processed at different markets. Further, they show that information affects the level of fragmentation.

## Regulation

Against the rise of HFT in European securities markets and the discussion, regulators started to introduce rules aiming at curbing HFT activity. The first European country to propose such legislation was France which introduced a financial transaction tax (FTT) on August 1, 2012, which also includes a special duty for HFTs. This so-called HFT tax is only applied if the order modifications and cancellations of a single trader exceed 80% of all orders submitted (Lexology, 2012). As HFTs typically submit large quantities of orders while only executing a small portion of them this condition mostly applies to them.

The implementation of the FTT resulted in a decline in liquidity and trading activity (Beccetti et al., 2014; Meyer et al., 2015; Gomber et al., 2016). Further, the information coordi nation between the two leading venues was harmed (Gomber et al., 2016). Similar to France, a FTT with a special ruling for HFTs was introduced in Italy. It implements a special levy of 0.02% which is payable for orders that are deleted or modified in less than 0.5 seconds. This regulation restricts HFTs as they rely on short update intervals using many deletions and modifications. Comparable to the France implementation, this regulation resulted in a decrease in liquidity (Hvozdyk and Rustanov, 2016). Another FTT organized as an enhanced cooperation is to be implemented by ten European countries, e.g., France, Italy and Germany (European Commission, 2016). While the implementation date and tax rate are currently under discussion, this levy will have an impact on HFTs which are active on these markets. Although HFT is regulated with FTTs, which affect all market participants, there are also regulations which concerning HFT only. The first direct regulation of HFT in Europe was introduced on May 15, 2013, the German High Frequency Trading Act which aims at limiting HFT activity by introducing organizational and authorization requirements. In addition to these increased requirements, HFTs are additionally affected by the introduction of excessive usage fees, which are now mandatory for exchanges. These fees were implemented for firms which exhibit an excessive OTR, i.e., sending many orders while executing only a small portion (German High Frequency Trading Act, 2013). As HFTs typically exhibit a high OTR, this levy affects these respective firms. Previous research has shown that liquidity measured by the spread declined while the depth (liquidity behind the spread) remained unaffected (Haferkorn and Zimmermann, 2015). As of 2016, another major HFT regulation affecting all European markets is scheduled, i.e., MiFID II/MiFIR which will become effective on January 3, 2018 (European Securities and Market Authority, 2016c). Identical to the German HFT Act, this regulation will impose requirements on HFTs and trading venues. These are organizational requirements like internal systems and controls, documentation of algorithms and parameters and market making obligations for HFTs. Market operators also need to fulfill organizational and trading system requirements (Busch, 2016).

The large literature body on HFT, regulation and fragmentation shows the growing interest of academia and the practical importance of these three topics. Regarding HFT, no final conclusion has yet been drawn as results contradict (Carrion, 2013; Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013; Zhang, 2010). These deviations might be explained by the use of different data sets (public vs. proprietary) and by the heterogeneity of strategies employed by HFT. Contrary to these results, the regulation of HFT in European countries seems to decrease market quality. In particular, an increase in the spread could be observed, making trading at these markets costlier (Gomber et al., 2016; Haferkorn and Zimmermann, 2015; Hvozdyk and Rustanov, 2016). Taking fragmentation into consideration, it has been shown that the competition among trading venues attracts more liquidity, however, at the same time liquidity is now located at different venues which leads to fragmented liquidity and pricing. The latter one is especially critical as investors need to be able to decide and trade on fully incorporated prices, independent from their trading venue (Fama, 1970). With our research, we want to evaluate whether HFT can increase the semi-strong market efficiency by increasing information coordinating between different venues, thus reducing fragmented pricing.

## Research design

## Research hypotheses

This study analyzes how IT affects securities markets within European markets. It is argued that IT is one key-driver of financialization as it leads to an increase in transaction velocity. This development is seen critical as, for instance, money is transferred fast paced throughout the world solely to evade taxes and regulation (Lagoarde-Segot, 2016). This results in an inefficient distribution of money toward locations where the expected returns are the highest instead of places where it is needed. In our research, we analyze whether this increase in transaction velocity can also exhibit positive side effects. Hereby, we examine if more transaction velocity leads to a more precise and timely (price) information distribution in fragmented securities markets. We are aware that in the realm of financialization it is argued that the price does not reflect the true value (Lagoarde-Segot, 2015) of an asset as it only poses the shareholder but not the stakeholder value. Due to the lack of the true value in our data sources, we rely on the prices resulting from trades at respective market. We believe that these prices are at least a viable indication of the true value of a security.

With this research, we further investigate whether the prediction of reduced coordination costs for electronic markets (Malone et al., 1987) holds true for securities markets. We hereby follow their connotation of electronic markets as our analyzed venues offer identical trading services. In this environment, consisting of numerous venues, market participants need to choose the right trading venue to trade, i.e., assess and coordinate along multiple exchanges. To do so, they have to invest in data links to venues and IT systems which are able to cope with this large amount of data. This increases search costs for investors and also affects trading venues themselves. In particular, this applies to dark pools which use the reference market’s midpoint (MP) to price a trade. The dark pool volume is not insignificant, e.g., our two analyzed markets had a volume of 1.241 billion Euro (3.1%, Germany) and 1.396 billion Euro (4.1%, France) in blue chip securities trading in 2013 (Fidessa, 2016). If the value of an asset (i.e., MP) on the reference market is not current, the trade in a dark pool would be executed on old information, i.e., it would be executed inefficiently. Consequently, coordination of information between the wide varieties of venues is a central requirement within fragmented electronic markets. We argue that the reduction of coordination costs does not only depend on the electronic nature of markets but also on its level of automation. Against the background of the ongoing technological development of securities markets, we evaluate whether the level of automation helps to lessen coordination costs. Thus, we validate this assumption by investigating the relationship between coordination and the level of automation, i.e., the level of HFT activity.

Shifting the theoretical lens toward the EMH, we can observe a similar (positive) effect of a timely information coordination. According to Fama (1970), a market is semistrong efficient if the price of an asset incorporates all public information instantly. This is an important requirement in fragmented markets such as Europe, because investors need to make sure that they are able to trade on fully incorporated prices (Harris, 2003). If prices do not reflect all available information capital might not be allocated or might be allocated inefficiently. Thereby, anybody transmitting information between multiple markets can increase the cumulative welfare of all market participants. Against this background, we wonder whether HFT can reduce the magnitude of mispricing between several venues by fostering information coordination thus increasing market efficiency.

In this study, we widen the scientific scope toward HFT which utilizes many strategies. One of these are arbitrage trading strategies which can create a positive effect as they lessen the price dispersions across several venues by capitalizing on them. Previous research suggests that HFT overall has a positive effect on securities trading. Due to their IT advantage, HFTs are able to adjust their orders very fast and thus trade within very short time spans, resulting in higher price efficiency (Brogaard et al., 2014). Thereby, the nature of HFT being fast might increase market efficiency by quickly coordinating information cross-market. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1 Increasing HFT activity increases market efficiency by fostering timely information coordination between markets.

If we observe that HFT activity and information coordination are positively connected, i.e., validate H1, we still cannot be certain about the causality. Previous research has shown that market quality declined after regulatory events causing HFTs to adjust their behavior or quit the market (Friederich and Payne, 2015; Malinova et al., 2013). We expect to observe similar effects after regulating HFT, thus, we hypothesize:

H2 Limiting HFT activity decreases market efficiency by harming timely information coordination.

## Data set

We use a data set provided by Thomson Reuters Tick History (TRTH). The TRTH essentially provides two types of data: trade data and order book data. The order book contains the limit and the number of orders at the respective limit and is available for both sides of the book, i.e., bid and ask, starting from limit one to limit ten. Once the order book is updated (caused by an update or trade), a new data set is saved by the TRTH millisecond-wise, which captures HFT activity. The trade data contains trades along with information of price and volume. We use the French securities market to analyze the effects as this market is technologically advanced and highly fragmented. Similar to previous studies, we use constituents of the French blue chip index CAC 40 (Cotation Assistee en Continu quarante) as HFTs are mostly active in these highly liquid securities (Menkveld and Zoican, 2014). In this study, we compare the two biggest competitors, defined by turnover: The Euronext Paris (main market) and the London-based BATS Chi-X Europe (satellite market). Both had a market share of 65.4% (Euronext Paris) and of 20.4% (Bats Chi-X Europe) (combined 85.8%) in CAC 40 lit trading in 2013 (Fidessa, 2016). Due to missing data in the

TRTH, we dismissed five constituents. Additionally, three constituents are primarily listed at Euronext Amsterdam, which could potentially bias our results. Consequently, we excluded them as well. The time-span of our first sample is one year, i.e., 2013. The level of volatility (price waviness) was low during 2013, as no major event (like the Lehman bankruptcy in 2008) caused significant market a turmoil.<sup>1</sup> In choosing a year with average volatility, we want to examine how HFTs behave during calm market conditions. Analyzing one year prevents a bias toward certain months’, as financial market activity varies over time (Odgen, 1990; Amihud, 2002). For our second step, the event study, we further extend our sample to German DAX 30 (Deutscher Aktienindex) constituents. Thereby, we analyze the venues Deutsche Boerse and Bats Chi-X Europe, whereas Deutsche Boerse (Bats Chi-X Europe) had a market share of 69.4% (18.6%) in 2013 (Fidessa, 2016). Due to missing data, we had to dismiss two securities. An overview of all securities being analyzed along with their market capitalization is depicted at Table 5 in Appendix. We aggregate the data hour-wise in conformity with other studies (Huang and Masulis, 2003; Longstaff and Wang, 2004).

## Operationalization of key variables

## High-frequency trading

Measuring HFT is quite challenging as public data feeds typically do not contain a flag whether an order book activity or trade is caused by an algorithm or a human. Hence, researchers hence need to rely on a proxy to quantify HFT. One well established measure in academia and industry is the OTR, which utilizes a typical behavior of HFT: Sending many orders while executing only a portion. Previous studies used the OTR (Haferkorn et al., 2013; Malinova et al., 2013) and its validity has been shown using a proprietary data set (Haferkorn et al., 2013). Most importantly, the German legislature acknowledged the OTR as a measure for HFT and passed an act that forces exchanges to implement fees for traders having excessive OTRs (German High Frequency Trading Act, 2013). A similar regulation was passed by the Italian regulatory body as they enforced an OTR fee in 2012 aimed at limiting HFT activity (Friederich and Payne, 2015). Further, the European Commission defines HFT as traders who exhibit a high OTR (Busch, 2016; European Commission, 2014). Our analysis incorporates a main and a satellite market (sat). We quantify the HFT activity at both markets as the mean OTR. This aggregation is necessary as the other variables are also aggregated inter-market wise. The OTR is calculated as follows:

$$
\mathrm{OTR} = \frac {\text { Orders } _ {\text { main }} + \text { Orders } _ {\text { sat }}}{\text { Trades } _ {\text { main }} + \text { Trades } _ {\text { sat }}}\tag{1}
$$

whereas $T r a d e s _ { m a i n } \ ( T r a d e s _ { s a t } )$ is the number of trades and $O r d e r s _ { m a i n } ~ ( O r d e r s _ { s a t } )$ the number of orders at the main (satellite) market. As described previously, the TRTH provides order book data at ten levels for each side sequentially. Thus, order submissions can only be obtained indirectly via TRTH as for example shown by He et al. (2015). We compute the total number of orders by comparing the amount of orders at each limit against the previous order book situation, whereas an increase is a submission. It is important to note that we cannot observe market orders as they are executed immediately and no order book snapshot is being made between submission and execution. It has been shown in previous research that HFT (humans) typically use 4.63% (8.73%) of the time market orders (Jarnecic and Snape, 2014), therefore we assume that we capture most of the orders submitted by HFTs.

## Timely information coordination/market efficiency

According to the semi-strong form, markets are efficient if all public information is reflected in the price (Fama, 1970). This also applies to securities markets where securities can be traded on distant trading venues. If the price of a security between distant venues start to diverge, the efficiency of the whole market system declines. Thus, semi-strong efficiency declines when the price dispersion increases between venues (Chen et al., 2003). In the context of the electronic market hypothesis, diverging prices show a lower level of information coordination among venues. Essentially, this divergence increases search costs for market participants as they need to select the venue with the most recent information, i.e., price.

One drawback of the last price is the so-called bid-ask bounce, where price changes are caused by market orders which hit the top of each order book side (Gosnell et al., 1996; Schlag and Stoll, 2005). To reflect this drawback, we analyze the MP dispersion $( M P _ { d i s p } )$ for our two leading venues. We thereby reflect the fact that a change of the MP can be interpreted as information transmission and coordination between distant markets as it inherits information about the current value of a security. Given the fact that dark pools also use the MP from the reference market, a current MP at the reference market is of high importance. Taking these considerations into account, we calculate the $M P _ { d i s p }$ between the two markets as follows:

$$
M P _ {d i s p} = \frac {\operatorname{abs} \left(M P _ {\text { main }} - M P _ {\text { sat }}\right)}{\frac {1}{2} * \left(M P _ {\text { main }} + M P _ {\text { sat }}\right)}\tag{2}
$$

$$
M P _ {i} = \frac {\text { BestAsk } _ {i} + \text { BestBid } _ {i}}{2}\tag{3}
$$

Equation (3) exhibits the calculation of the MP for each venue, where i is main (sat) for the main (satellite) market. Since we are aiming at quantifying the total dispersion between both MPs, we subtract both from each other (see Eq. 2). As we are only interested in absolute MP dispersion between both venues and not the direction (e.g., which venue has a higher MP), we further compute the absolute value. Taking into consideration that securities exhibit distinctive MP levels, we additionally normalize it.

## Control variables

In addition to our variables in question, we include a set of controls as we expect that the market efficiency is also influenced by other factors. In particular, we include hourly and daily dummies as market activity varies over time (Jain, 1988; McInish and Wood, 1992; Chan et al., 1996). We expect that volatility influences the information coordination as market participants adjust their trading activity accordingly. The volatility is calculated as the mean standard deviation of the price volatility between both markets. Additionally, we control for the relative amount of information processed at each market. The intuition behind this approach is that the more information is processed at one market the more information coordination is necessary. Given that arbitrageurs have only limited capacities (in terms of budget and bandwidth) to exploit these differences it is expected that the information differences between both markets rise if more information needs to be transmitted between them. New information is generated in financial markets by trading. Consequently, we control for the level of new information at our both markets using the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) (Herfindahl, 1950) of the relative notional trading volume.

Our data is aggregated hour-wise and security-wise. Thus, we observe the market on a macrolevel, i.e., the averaged HFT activity overall market participants on both markets. We expect that the OTR of individual trading firms varies strongly due to the different technologies and strategies employed. By taking the magnitude of the OTR into account, we observe that our OTR is similar to previous studies (Haferkorn et al., 2013). The average $M P _ { d i s p }$ is quite low and shows that efficiency is high despite the fact that both venues are located apart. It is important to note that the standard deviation of $\bar { M P } _ { d i s p }$ is high compared to the median, suggesting that even though both markets are balanced at most times the level of information coordination quality varies. This stresses the key problem of fragmented markets where HFT might help by simply coordinating information: Fragmented pricing. We can observe that the HHI, i.e., fragmentation, varies over time from equally distributed (0.5) to concentrated (1). Taking into account that the mean is 0.59, we observe that at most times, trading between our two markets is strongly fragmented.

## Empirical study

## Long-term study of market efficiency

## Regression setup

In this subsection, we investigate our hypothesis H1. Due to the cross-sectional time series character of our data, we make use of a standard methodology used in econometrics: the panel regression. Thereby, we define each security as an entity (i) and every hour as a new time-point (t). Our regression equation is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} M P _ {d i s p _ {i t}} & = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1} O T R _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} O r d e r s _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} T r a d e s _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {4} H H I _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} V o l a t i l i t y _ {i t} + \sum_ {j = 5} ^ {n} \beta_ {j} C _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where i is the entity number, t is the time, $\scriptstyle \sum _ { j = 5 } ^ { n } \beta _ { j } C _ { i t j }$ is a vector of dummy variables controlling for each trading hour and each day, $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the idiosyncratic error term and $\mathsf { \alpha } _ { i }$ is the entity specific intercept. We further stepwise include the number of orders (Orders ) and trades $( T r a d e s _ { i t } )$ as we want to evaluate whether OTR gives us a superior explanation of $M P _ { d i s p } ,$ thus eliminating the individual effect of both originating variables. Even though multicollinearity is not an issue, we estimate our regressions for additional robustness without these variables. First, we run our regression with a fixed effects model. By using the fixed effects (FE) model we allow for constant effects among the entities that are influencing the independent variables which are caused by the unobservable heterogeneity (Wooldridge, 2009). Estimating time series is non-trivial as underlying trends might cause heteroscedasticity. Therefore, we use heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors, i.e., the Huber-White Sandwich Estimator (White, 1980). Further, a regression on time series might exhibit serial correlation in the idiosyncratic errors. Thus, we run a Wooldridge Test for serial correlation which reveals that some autocorrelation within our error term exists (Wooldridge, 2002). Consequently, the regression is estimated with a GLS estimator modeling the disturbance as autoregressive process AR(1) (Baltagi and Wu, 1999). The significance levels stay comparable to the previous approach. Therefore, we dismiss autocorrelation as a major issue in our estimation. As our panel exhibits a small number of entities (small i) and long-time series (large t), we further add robustness by estimating our regression on the basis of the Fama–MacBeth method (Fama and MacBeth, 1973). The significance levels are similar, and due to space limitations these results are omitted. For the following regressions, we only use CAC 40 securities.

Additionally, this could be an indication that one of both markets processes most of the new information. Still, we need to assess the causality through our event study.

## Event study of the German HFT Act

## Event study setup

For our further investigation, we exploit a recent regulatory event in a highly technologically advanced market: The German HFT Act. This event is similar to the introduction of an excessive message fee in Canada and Italy which caused HFTs to reduce their activity (Friederich and Payne, 2015; Malinova et al., 2013). The act came into force on May 15, 2013. Like in previous event studies (Kolari and Pynno¨nen, 2010; Lease et al., 1991), we chose an observation window of ±10 trading days around the event date, i.e., from April 15, 2013, until May 27, 2013.

In line with similar research on regulatory events, we make use of a well-known methodology: The difference-in-differences method. This method is used to analyze the effects of treatment against the untreated group (control group) (Snow, 1855). Thus, our methodology relies on a group that falls under the regulation and one that does not. Consequently, we choose the constituents of the DAX 30 as treatment group which falls under the regulation. As the trading venue, we select the largest venue by turnover in Germany, the Deutsche Boerse, where no changes in the market or fee structure were made during our event horizon. The ideal control group exhibits a high level of similarity. Therefore, we chose the CAC 40 for the following reasons: Firstly, the CAC 40 displays a high price level correlation with the DAX 30 (0.87, 6 months before the event) and further a significant portion of the CAC 40 and DAX 30 constituents are part of the EURO STOXX 50 index. Secondly, both countries are located geographically nearby to each other and use the same currency. Thus, both countries exhibit a macroeconomic similarity. Also, it was ensured that during the event horizon no changes at market or fee structure were made at Euronext Paris.

## Regression setup

Similar to our previous procedure, we examine $M P _ { d i s p }$ between the main market (Deutsche Boerse for DAX 30 and Euronext Paris for CAC 40 securities) and the satellite market (both times Bats Chi-X Europe). Benchmarking against the same satellite market adds further robustness to our approach as the control sample becomes more similar to the treatment sample. We modify our regression in Eq. (4) from the previous subsection by adding an indicator variable for the pre and the post-treatment interval (Time ) and by including an interaction term (German  Time ). As we expect that the event has an effect on the OTR, we dismiss this variable along with orders and trades. The regression equation is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} M P _ {d i s p _ {i t}} & = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1} G e r m a n _ {i} * T i m e _ {t} + \beta_ {2} T i m e _ {t} + \beta_ {3} G e r m a n _ {i} \\ & + \beta_ {4} H H I _ {i t} + \beta_ {5} V o l a t i l i t y _ {i t} + \sum_ {j = 5} ^ {n} \beta_ {j} C _ {i t j} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Thereby, Time changes from zero to one when the German HFT Act becomes active (i.e., on May 15, 2013) and German is zero for observations from the control group (CAC 40) and one for observations within the treatment group (DAX 30). Thus, the interaction term $( G e r m a n _ { i } * { \bar { T } } i m { \bar { e } } _ { t } )$ equals one for the DAX 30 observations after the introduction of the German HFT Act, which shows the treatment effect of the HFT Act. Similar to the procedure in the previous section, we run the regression followed by a test against serial correlation. As we observe autocorrelation in our estimation, we model the disturbance as an autoregressive process AR(1), thereby, we observe similar significance levels to the previous regression. Thus, we dismiss autocorrelation as a major issue in our estimation.

## Regression results

The results of our regressions are depicted in Table 3. Considering our two models (9) and (10), we can observe that the time effect (variable Time) is significantly positive. Thus, the overall $M P _ { d i s p }$ deteriorates between our pre- and postevent period at both market systems, which might caused seasonal varying market activity (Jain, 1988; McInish and Wood, 1992; Chan et al., 1996). Taking into account the positive coefficient of our interaction term (German  Time ), we can observe that the German HFT Act caused a significant systematic increase in the $M P _ { d i s p }$ between DAX 30 securities listed at Deutsche Boerse and Bats Chi-X Europe. These results suggest that the HFTs who balanced the MP between the two markets adjusted their behavior or quit their operation after the introduction of the act. This side effect is critical as it increases the possibility that investors do not trade on full information at both venues, i.e., Deutsche Boerse and Bats Chi-X Europe. Additionally, the probability that dark pools execute trades on an old reference market MP increased significantly. Thereby, the partial absence of HFT affects the semi-strong market efficiency negatively decreasing information coordination. These observations are similar to the observations by Friederich and Payne (2015) and Malinova et al. (2013) who show that regulation with objective to limit HFT activities worsens market quality.

## Discussion

## Theoretical contributions

## Financialization

To the ongoing academic discussion on financialization, we contribute by providing evidence that not all key develop ments exhibit negative effects. With our research, we examine the effect of the digitalization in financial markets. This digitalization was fostered by large-scale IT systems introduced in the financial service industry which lead to an increase in transaction velocity. Against this background, we investigate how this increase in velocity affects securities trading. Our research suggests that the technological advancement helps to distribute information, represented by the value of securities, across different venues. This positive spill-over effect is beneficial to all market participants, also to those not investing in this technology. While we are confident considering the aspect of information transmission, that the increase in velocity is positive, we also want to highlight that there are certainly negative consequences of this development as discussed by Lagoarde-Segot (2016). These effects are not in the scope of this study. Thus, we want to call for more research on this matter to establish a better understanding of the complex interconnection between the digitalization and the financial service industry, in particular with respect to long-term effects.

## Information systems

The electronic markets hypothesis argues that the shift to electronic markets decreases coordination costs as search costs are lowered (Malone et al., 1987). We study this hypothesis within the European securities trading landscape which is electronic on the one hand and highly fragmented on the other hand. In the light of this sheer amount of venues, information regarding the traded securities (i.e., prices and MPs) is fragmented, which increases coordination costs for investors. We want to shed light on this matter by investigating whether a direct connection between the level of automation and coordination costs can be drawn. Hereby, we measure the level of automation by HFT activity. In a longterm and in an event study we find that the level of automation is directly related to the quality of information coordination between our two markets. By validating our research hypotheses H1 and H2, we show that the coordination costs depend on the level of automation. Putting this in the context of the electronic markets hypothesis, we confirm the predictions being made by Malone et al. (1987), i.e., that the digitization leads to cost reductions. Similar to Bakos (1991), we want to highlight that the reduction in coordination costs depends not only on the fact that a market is digital as we further identify the level of automation as a driver of reductions in coordination costs.

## Finance

In the realm of financial research, we focus on the concept of semi-strong strong form of market efficiency (Fama, 1970). Against this background, we investigate whether HFTs make markets more efficient within a fragmented market setting. Our study contributes to two important topics in market microstructure research: HFT and fragmentation. Previous studies suggested that HFT has positive effects on market quality and efficiency (Hasbrouck and Saar, 2013; Malinova et al., 2013; Brogaard et al., 2014). However, contrary effects have also been reported (Kirilenko et al., 2014; Zhang, 2010). We widen the scientific scope by introducing a new aspect to HFT research: Fragmented markets. We can validate our research hypothesis H2 as we empirically show that HFT decreased the MP dispersions among two leading European venues. Thus, we can validate predictions made by Grieg (2012), i.e., that HFT makes prices more accurate. In the regard of our hypothesis H2, we confirm that limiting HFT activity decreases market efficiency. We show that the MP dispersion significantly increased after the act came into force. Thereby, we further contribute to the literature stream on regulation in securities markets in general and HFT regulation in particular (Beccetti et al., 2014; Haferkorn and Zimmermann, 2015; Hvozdyk and Rustanov, 2016). Our research further validates the observations from previous research that the regulation of HFT can lead to a decrease in market efficiency (Gomber et al., 2016).

## Contributions to practice

## Investors

Our results are interesting for investors who need to trade within the fragmented market systems. In these fragmented markets, new information can develop at different markets, hence investors need to evaluate the MP at each venue to find the current value. Additionally, reference price venues such as dark pools use the MP from the main market to price their trades. If the reference market has outdated pricing information, the dark pool trade is priced inefficiently. Thus, coordination costs arise which are either paid by investors directly (if they need to assess each venue) or indirectly (when the MP of the reference system is not current). The direct costs are caused by data links to venues and operation of the IT infrastructure and indirect cost arise by worse executions of dark pools trades. Firstly, our long-term study on the French market shows that more HFT activity lessens the information difference among the two largest venues. Retail investors also benefit from a smaller information difference as they typically trade on their national home market and not considering other venues. Secondly, with our event study, we provide evidence that HFTs in Germany, who balanced the MPs between the main and satellite market, changed or quit their operation. This resulted in less efficient trading of German securities after the implementation as information coordination was harmed. Especially in dark pools, where trades are priced on the MP of the respective reference market, the probability that a trade of a German security is priced on old information has increased.

## Regulators

In light of the upcoming MiFID II/MiFIR, the authorities need more evidence as HFT and securities markets are a central cornerstone of this regulation (European Commission, 2014). Taking into consideration, the most recent HFT regulation, the German HFT Act, our event study suggests that a negative effect spill-over in the form of a higher MP dispersion arose. These results are in line with observations being made in Canada and Italy (Friederich and Payne, 2015; Malinova et al., 2013) where it was shown that limiting HFT activity decreased market quality. With our findings, we want to highlight that the regulatory body needs to carefully assess each intervention as securities trading has become more and more complex due to its high interconnectedness.

## Limitations and future research

Our research approach has some limitations. While we find that HFT increases market efficiency, it is important to note that we analyzed only one aspect of HFT. As HFT employs various strategies, general conclusions cannot be easily drawn from our results. Foremost, it is important to note that our proxy, the OTR, might not capture all HFT activity. For example, HFTs using market orders rather than limit orders or submitting the same amount of orders as executed are not captured precisely. Previous research suggests that HFT uses limit orders in 95.37% of the time and cancels them in 47.01% of the time (Jarnecic and Snape, 2014), therefore, we do not regard this as a major issue in our research approach. Similar to market orders, we cannot capture hidden orders as these are not included in our data set. Degryse et al. (2015a, b) depict that only 4% of the trading volume is executed incorporating hidden orders. Even though this creates a small inaccuracy, we are confident that our estimations are not biased. HFT has been associated with volatility spikes and fat-tail events. While these events certainly need more scientific attention they were not in the scope of this study because we only examined the average HFT activity. Thus, conclusions regarding these events can not be drawn from our research. Furthermore, our investigation uses blue chip securities traded at European markets. Therefore, our observations might depend on this unique combination and thus hinders the generalizability toward other market segments.

There are many interesting research directions which could be pursued in the future. For example, our research could be expanded toward a triple or more market case to gain a deeper understanding of HFT in the interconnected securities landscape. Taking into account, the limitation that we only analyzed the European securities market, a focus on the U.S. market would be helpful in adding generalizability to our results, especially since the U.S. market displays a high level of HFT.

## Conclusion

Securities trading has undergone a major transformation within the last decade. This transformation was mainly driven by the regulation-induced fragmentation and the increase in HFT, which in 2014 accounted for 24–43% of the European traded value (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2014). Given these developments and such disruptive events as May 6, 2010, flash crash HFT is discussed quite controversially by academics and regulators alike. With this paper, we want to add to the discussion by shedding light on the role of HFT in this highly complex and fragmented market system (Kirilenko et al., 2014). As HFT needs to be able to evaluate new information within milliseconds, it relies strongly on IT (Brogaard et al., 2014; Chlistalla, 2011). We wonder if this characteristic leads to higher cross-market efficiency.

Consequently, we analyze the effect of HFT on information coordination (Fama, 1970). On the basis of a long-term study, we find that HFT activity decreases the MP dispersion for French securities traded at Euronext Paris and Bats Chi-X

Europe in 2013. For further robustness, we assess the causality between HFT and MP dispersion by conducting an event study on a recent regulatory event: The German HFT Act. In this cross-country approach, we observe that the act increased the MP dispersion for German securities, i.e., the semi-strong market efficiency declined.

We hereby draw on major hypotheses, i.e., financialization, the electronic markets hypothesis and the EMH (Lagoarde-Segot, 2016; Malone et al., 1987; Fama, 1970). In the realm of financialization, we give evidence that the increase in velocity caused by the introduction of IT in financial markets generates a positive spill-over for market participants not investing in the IT firsthand. In particular, they benefit from current pricing information in fragmented markets. Considering the electronic market hypothesis, we empirically show that the predicted coordination cost savings depend on the level of automation, which we proxy via HFT activity. In light of the EMH (Fama, 1970), we focus on the semi-strong form, i.e., how new public information propagates in the European fragmented market. Given the fact that HFT levels MPs and increases the speed of the price reversion mechanism among distant venues, we conclude that HFT increases market efficiency and therefore further contributes to the realm of market microstructure (Brogaard et al., 2014; Madhavan, 2000).
