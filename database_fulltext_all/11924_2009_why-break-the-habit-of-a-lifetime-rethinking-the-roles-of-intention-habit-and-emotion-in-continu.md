## Abstract

One of the most welcome recent developments in Information Systems scholarship has been the growing interest in individuals' continuing use of information technology well after initial adoption, known in the literature as IT usage, IT continuance, and post-adoptive IT usage. In this essay, we explore the theoretical underpinnings of IS research on continuing IT use. Although the IS literature on continuing IT use emphasizes the role of habitual behavior that does not require conscious behavioral intention, it does so in a way that largely remains faithful to the theoretical tradition of planned behavior and reasoned action. However, a close reading of reference literatures on automatic behavior (behavior that is not consciously controlled) and the influences of emotion on behavior suggests that planned behavior

Keywords: Continuing IT use, IT continuance, post-adoptive IT usage, automatic behavior, environmental triggers, habit, emotion, intention, cognition, reasoned action, planned behavior

and reasoned action may not provide the best theoretical foundation for the study of continuing IT use. As a result, we call for empirical research that directly compares and contrasts the consensus theory of continuing IT use with rival theories that place much greater emphasis on unplanned and unreasoned action.

## Introduction

One of the most welcome developments in recent Information Systems scholarship has been the growing interest in individuals' use of information technology well after initial adoption. What we call continuing IT use has been the subject of important theoretical developments and empirical advances under a variety of labels, such as IT usage (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000; Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004; Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007; Kim and Malhotra 2005b; Straub et al. 1995), IS continuance (Bhattacherjee 2001; Cheung and Limayem 2005; Kim et al. 2007; Limayem et al. 2007), and post-adoptive IT usage (Jasperson et al. 2005).

As a whole, the body of knowledge on continuing IT use maintains theoretical continuity with the literature on IT adoption and acceptance. The basic premise is that continuing IT use is fundamentally intentional behavior—that is, driven by conscious decisions to act. Thus, IT continuance is seen as a series of decisions to continue using IT (Bhattacherjee 2001). These decisions involve two key inputs. The first is rational calculus based on perceptions of ease of use and usefulness, expectations derived from past experience, and various beliefs (Bhattacherjee 2001; Venkatesh et al. 2008). The second is a set of affective or emotional responses to the use of technology (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000; Kim et al. 2007; Zhang and Sun 2006), such as satisfaction or reports of cognitive absorption.

At the same time, the IS literature on continuing IT use makes a major, and very appropriate, conceptual advance, drawing on literature in psychology and social psychology, to posit that much continuing IT use is habitual. When IT use is habitual, so the argument goes, it ceases to be guided by an individual's intentions. Habitual IT use behavior is conceptualized as repeated behavioral sequences automatically triggered by environmental cues; in some studies, habit moderates the impact of intentions on the guidance of IT use (Cheung and Limayen 2005; Kim et al. 2005; Limayem et al. 2003; Limayem et al. 2007); in others, habit influences future intentions to use IT (Gefen 2003); and in some, habit directly affects IT use (Kim and Malhotra 2005a; Limayem and Hirt 2003). $^{2}$

Intrigued by these arguments, we read the most commonly cited literature and gradually went deeper and farther afield into neuropsychology and other areas, covering some of the same territory as Dimoka et al. (2007). As we read, we noticed important theoretical distinctions and arguments in the reference literatures that seem not to be fully reflected in the IS work. One is the difference between the concept of intention (which, as used in the IS literature, means a conscious decision, following theories of reasoned action) and the concept of goal-directed behavior, which in the psychological literature explicitly does not mean consciously decided or planned (Verkanplen and Orbell 2003). We began to develop a set of convictions that differed sharply from the basic assumptions in the IS writing on continuing IT use. Our alternative assumptions differ significantly enough that they cannot be addressed adequately by adding new constructs to a consensus model in a way that preserves the consensus model's core (Silva 2007). Instead, we see our assumptions as forming a true “rival explanation” (Yin 1999). The implication is the two views should not be synthesized into a comprehensive model until rigorous empirical research has compared their differing implications.

We wrote this essay because we believe it is important for the field to engage in debate about the nature of continuing IT use—a topic that is clearly central to the IS research enterprise—at the level of basic assumptions rather than at the level of empirically testable models. Our aim is to engender theoretical discussion and to promote empirical research that tackles core assumptions head on.

The plan of this essay is as follows. We first attempt to document what we believe to be the core assumptions in the published IS literature on continuing IT use. We understand how difficult it is to do this task well. It can be very easy to fall into straw man argumentation—defined as deliberately misstating an opponent's views in such a way that facilitates refuting them. We try not to misstate our colleagues' views; we aim to characterize them fairly, so that a true dialog can begin. Accordingly, we let other authors speak for themselves with minimal paraphrasing. We encourage readers to check the validity of our interpretations.

Next, we sketch the outlines of an alternative perspective derived largely (not entirely) from the reference literature heavily cited in IS scholarship on continuing IT use. Again we use representative quotations, although here we make no claims about the comprehensiveness of our review. Our aim is merely to try to show that there are grounds for interpreting the reference literature very differently than our IS colleagues have done. In light of our goal to promote further discussion, our implications section is quite short.

## What We (Think We) Know about Continuing IT Use

In this section, we present three basic assumptions that we believe fairly characterize the growing IS literature on continuing IT use. To support our contention that these assumptions are fundamental in the literature, we let the authors speak for themselves in the sections below in representative quotations, rather than attempting to paraphrase their arguments. Based on a careful reading of the literature, we conclude that there are three key pillars of IS knowledge about continuing IT use:.

\- At its most fundamental level, continuing IT use is driven by conscious intentions that result from a rational decision making process involving beliefs, expectations, reflections on past experience, etc.

\- Emotion, not just cognition, may be an input to the continuing use decision or intention formation.

• Over time, in stable contexts, continuing IT use becomes habitual, which means that well-learned action sequences may be activated by environmental cues and then repeated without conscious intention.

## Continuing IT Use Is, at its Basis, a Rational Decision

Leading authorities on continuing IT use generally agree that continuing IT use is the result, either in the present or in the past (as input to habit formation), of conscious, cognitive intention formation involving evaluations and expectations.

Beliefs and attitudes correlate more strongly with behavior [behavioral intention] for people who have had direct experience with an object...suggesting a stronger influence of perceived usefulness and attitude on BI [behavioral intention] and subsequent behavior for experienced users....

...Direct experience will result in a stronger, more stable behavioral intention - behavior relationship" (Taylor and Todd 1995, p. 563, emphasis added).

An individual's intention to adopt (or continue to use) the IT is determined by two basic factors: one reflecting personal interests and one reflecting social influence. The personal factor, attitude toward adopting (or continuing to use) the IT, reflects the individual's positive and negative evaluations of performing the behavior (Karahanna et al. 1999, p. 187, emphasis added).

More information about the attitude object may be available through direct experience than indirect experience. Information through direct experience with the attitude object is likely to result in the individual being more able to evaluate the object clearly and confidently (Karahanna et al. 1999, p. 189, emphasis added).

The proposed TAM2 encompasses social influence processes (subjective norm, voluntariness, and image) and cognitive instrumental processes (job relevance, output quality, result demonstrability, and perceived ease of use) as determinants of perceived usefulness and usage intentions. We also hypothesize a decrease in the strength with which social influence processes affect perceived usefulness and intention to use with increasing experience over time (Venkatesh and Davis 2000, pp. 192-193, emphasis added).

Perceived usefulness is an adequate expectation in the IS continuance context because it is the only belief that is demonstrated to consistently influence user intention across temporal stages of IS use (Bhattacherjee 2001, p. 355, emphasis added).

Users may experience cognitive dissonance or psychological tension if their pre-acceptance usefulness perceptions...are disconfirmed during actual use. Rational users may try to remedy this dissonance by distorting or modifying their usefulness perception in order to be more consistent with reality (Bhattacherjee 2001, p. 357, emphasis added).

As a test of nomological completeness and validity, we include the antecedent factors of beliefs (termed perceived consequences by Triandis) and attitude with paths consistent with past models [predicting behavioral intention] (Limayem et al. 2001, p. 277, emphasis added).

It is hypothesized that the experience gained through previous habitual use of an IT, in this case a website, should increase user perceptions of its ease of use and usefulness (Gefen 2003, p. 3, emphasis added).

Users behavioral intention to continue using a specific IT, in this case a given website they have habitually used in the past, will be increased directly due to their habit of going to that specific website (Gefen 2003, p. 4, emphasis added).

We define post-adoptive behavior as the myriad feature adoption decisions, feature use behaviors, and feature extension behaviors made by an individual user after an IT application has been installed, made accessible to the user, and applied by the user in accomplishing his/her work activities (Jasperson et al. 2005, p. 531, emphasis added).

Technology sensemaking occurs as an evaluative cognitive process that transpires when an individual contrasts the outcomes of a post-adoptive behavior episode with those expected from pre-episode cognitions... . Post-adoptive intentions derive from an individual's understanding both of how to use and application's features and how these features complement other work system elements (Jasperson et al. 2005, p. 540, emphasis added).

[Our] two-wave panel model incorporates four different theoretical perspectives—TAM, the theory of belief updating, the self-perception theory, and habit .... A person who intends to take a certain action is likely to carry out that behavior. This motivation to perform the behavior is labeled behavioral intention ...IS adoption and use is guided by rational decision making and action control (Kim and Malhotra 2005a, pp. 742-3, emphasis added).

System usage will be driven by conscious intention when the linkage between stimuli and action is not fully developed (Kim and Malhotra 2005a, p. 746, emphasis added).

User evaluations such as PU and PEOU are updated sequentially with target system experience.... [A]nchors and adjustments work together to guide subsequent user evaluations" (Kim and Malhotra 2005a, p. 751, emphasis added).

The focus of research on IT adoption and post-adoption or IS continuance has been on mainly cognition-oriented behavioral models, such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) ... Cognition (i.e., thinking) means the mental process of knowing, including aspects such as perception, reasoning, and judgment (Kim et al. 2007, p. 515).

## Emotions May Contribute to Intentions to Continue Using IT

Although leading IS authorities on continuing IT use tend to emphasize rational evaluations and expectation formation, they also acknowledge that there are potential nonrational inputs to conscious intention formation. These nonrational inputs include attitudes, feelings, awareness of satisfaction, and other affective or emotion-related concepts.

Attitudes are more likely to influence behavior when the attitude is formed as a result of direct experience with the objects, over a period of time and held without ambiguity (Bajaj and Nidumolu 1998, p.217, emphasis changed).

It is expected that the attitude-behavior link [where behavior is measured as behavioral intention] for users of an IT will be stronger than for potential adopters of the IT...[S]ince more and richer information is available, in general, through direct rather than indirect experience, one would expect users to have a richer more complex set of behavioral beliefs underlying their attitude toward continuing to use an IT than do potential adopters of an IT (Karahanna et al. 1999, p. 189, emphasis added).

Users' IS continuance intention is determined primarily by their satisfaction with prior use. ECT [expectation confirmation theory] posits that user satisfaction is determined by two constructs: expectation of the IS and confirmation of expectation following actual use. Expectation provides the baseline level, against which confirmation is assessed by users to determine their evaluative response or satisfaction (Bhattacherjee 2001, p. 355, emphasis added).

Consistent with TPB [the theory of planned behavior], both affect and social factors are hypothesized to influence behavioral intentions directly. The affect toward a behavior reflects the direct emotional response to the thought of the behavior (Limayem and Hirt 2003, p. 71, emphasis added).

The effects of disconfirmation and satisfaction on consequent belief and attitude may continue to recur over time as users gain additional IT usage experience and thereby revise their prior cognitions in an iterative manner (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004, p. 234, emphasis added).

The basic idea of the IIO [individual's interaction with an object] model is that, an individual's traits influence both affective reactions and cognitive reactions that he or she has toward interacting with the object. These two types of reactions influence each other, either at a different stage of the entire process or at the same time. These two reactions together determine the final behavioral intention or behavior of interacting with the object (Sun and Zhang 2006, p. 303, emphasis added).

Following this argument, our study proposes a framework for thinking and feeling-based human behavior [in a model which shows both thinking and feelings as influencing attitude formation and behavioral intention formation] (Kim et al. 2007, p.513, emphasis added).

Depending on the feelings generated, behavioral intentions emerge to activate plans for avoiding undesirable outcomes or increasing/maintaining positive outcomes (Kim et al. 2007, p. 515, emphasis added).

Feelings can indeed be represented as a direct antecedent of behavioral intention. That is, emotional responses lead either to approach or avoidance behavioral intention (Kim et al. 2007, p. 517, emphasis added).

Satisfaction is the component that both intention and habit share. So, even though intentions are conscious and habits are unconscious, they both have a common root in satisfaction. The reason we can simply bypass them is because they are also both comprised of other factors (Limayem et al. 2007, p.721, emphasis added).

## When Intentional Behavior Becomes Habitual, Learned IT Use Behaviors May Be Repeated Without Conscious Intention

Although leading IS authorities on continuing IT use tend to emphasize conscious intention formation (influenced both by cognition and emotional responses), they are well aware that some or much continuing IT use may be habitual. Habit is portrayed by these authorities as a well-learned action sequence, originally intentional, that may be repeated as it was learned without conscious intention when triggered by environmental cues in a stable context.

The interactive effect of habit is modeled as the link pointing at the relationship between intention and IT usage. Essentially, this means that we expect that different levels of habit should change the relative importance of intention on usage behavior (Limayem et al. 2001, p. 277, emphasis added).

We therefore argue that if individuals are habitually performing a particular behavior, the predictive power of intention is diminished. In other words, we posit habit to exert a moderating effect on the relationship between intentions and IS usage (Limayem et al. 2003, p. 3, emphasis added).

We defined habit as the extent to which people tend to perform behaviors (use IS) automatically because of learning (Limayem et al. 2007, p. 709, emphasis added).

If individuals are habitually performing a particular behavior (for example, using a particular IS), the predictive power of intention is weakened. Thus, the more a behavior is performed out of habit, the less cognitive planning it involves....In summary, modeling IS habit as a moderator does not refute the existence of the relationship between intention and actual IS continuance behavior. It merely asserts that its strength may be weakened depending on the strength of an individual's habit (Limayem et al. 2007, p. 720, emphasis added).

Habit is made possible by a cognitive representation that links a situational cue and an action... [and that] mental linkage is established as a result of repeated performances of the same behavior.... [A]long with conscious intention, past use may explain the variance in future use (Kim and Malhotra 2005a, p.746, emphasis added).

[The authors propose two views on automaticity: IAP (Instant Activation Perspective) and HAP (Habit/Automaticity Perspective).] Because IAP assumes that automatically activated evaluations are equivalent to evaluations formed by careful deliberation, the relationship between evaluations and usage intention is expected to be strong regardless of past use. [Following HAP], we predict that the evaluations-intention path will decrease as past use increases (Kim et al. 2005, p. 421, emphasis added).

With repeated use the overall role of evaluation diminishes in importance as a determinant of behavioral intention (Kim et al. 2005, p. 429, emphasis added).

During initial use of an IT feature, individuals most likely engage in active cognitive processing in determining post-adoptive intention or behavior, however, with any repetitive behavior, reflective cognitive processing dissipates over time, leasing to non-reflective, routinized behavior (Jasperson et al. 2005, p. 528, emphasis added).

At the IS adoption stage, IS initial use is primarily determined by behavioral intention. At the IS continuance stage, as the use of a particular IS becomes habitual, the less cognitive planning it involves" (Cheung and Limayen 2005, p. 474, emphasis added).

In short, we conclude that the IS literature generally assumes that continuing IT use either is in the moment, or formerly was (now crystallized as habit), intentional behavior, and that the continuing use intention is the result of some mixture of rational analysis and emotional response. In the next section, we lay out an alternative interpretation of the reference literatures of psychology and related fields as they pertain to continuing IT use. We believe that some important theoretical nuances in the reference literatures have been missed in the IS literature, perhaps because of the IS field's heavy investment in reasoned action theories. As we subsequently explain, our field has much to gain by exploring rival explanations of continuing IT use behavior.

## What May Have Been Missed about Continuing IT Use

As we read the reference literature in psychology that is frequently cited in the IS research, and as we continued our reading in related fields (e.g., neuropsychology), we came to different conclusions about the mental and behavioral processes that underlie continuing IT use. Our alternative assumptions, explained below, follow:

\- Emotions may drive continuing IT use behavior directly (that is, without contributing to the formation of conscious behavioral intentions).

\- Automatic (i.e., habitual) IT use behavior may be much more extensive than the simple repetition of well-learned behavior sequences.

\- IT itself may be one of the most important triggers of automatic IT use behavior.

Below we elaborate on each of these assumptions.

## Emotions May Drive Continuing IT Use Directly (Rather than Through Behavioral Intentions)

In the IS literature on continuing IT use, scholars seem to agree that emotion is an input (along with rational decision making) to the formation of conscious behavioral intentions to continue (or not to continue) using IT. However, the reference literature on emotion suggests (1) that the connection between emotion and behavior can occur without a person being consciously aware of the connection, and (2) that the effect of emotion may not be to create a particular behavioral intention, but rather to derail a previously formed behavioral intention about continuing IT use. That is, emotion may either block an intention to continue using IT or it may override an intention to stop using IT.

## Consider the following quotations:

Affective processing, in the form of the mere exposure effect of familiarity on liking, can occur prior to and in the absence of conscious recognition of the stimulus (Spielman et al. 1988, p. 300, emphasis added).

[Human behavior is determined by] an encounter of several objects [goals] associated with affects [emotions] of varying strengths, so that one object emerges as dominant .... If some of the objects and strengths happened to be different, so might the behavior (Mohr 1996, p. 79).

Emotion causes an interruption of ongoing processing, and calls for the rearrangement of goal priorities. More specifically, the presence of a strong emotion seems to represent a call for the goal to which that emotion is relevant to be accorded a higher priority than whatever goal is presently being pursued (Scheier and Carver 1982, p. 177, cited in Mohr 1996, p. 82, emphasis in original).

A plausible case can be made that emotional reactions are pervasive behavioral action patterns ... actions that occur at relatively low levels of hierarchy control. A strong emotion draws attention to itself, thereby drawing attention to that lower level of control and away from the higher levels... [I]f the emotion becomes sufficiently intense, overall self-regulation may adjust in such a way that the level at which the emotion is being experienced temporarily becomes superordinate... . As attention is focused at the lower level, the person behaves single-mindedly and stereotypically, in a fashion that is dictated by the emotion (Carver and Scheier 1981, p. 178, emphasis added).

Why do we believe this alternative interpretation of the reference literature on the effects of emotion as potentially derailing behavioral intentions to be significant for our understanding of continuing IT use? First, it suggests that sudden intense emotions, such as the frustration associated with a system crash or the pleasure aroused while playing a computer game, may be more important in its influence on continuing IT use than intentional behavior driven by relatively stable attitudes and expectations formed during, or as a result of, prior experience with IT use. This alternative view of emotion may shed important new light on the emergence of IT phobias and of IT addictions. Second, because emotion is viewed by many psychologists as a lower-level, more basic, driver of human behavior than conscious decision-making, and because emotion-driven behavior may occur largely outside people's awareness, the alternative view suggests that continuing IT use may be much more automatic than the consensus IS view portrays.

## Automatic IT Use May Be More General than Learned Action Sequences

Following Bargh and Ferguson (2000), we use the term automatic behavior to refer to behavior that requires no conscious choice or guidance. We prefer this term to habit because we believe automatic to be broader in its connotations. The IS literature on continuing IT use depicts habit as a process in which an environmental cue triggers the activation of a previously learned sequence of actions in a stable context. It is certainly true that much of the reference literature emphasizes environmental triggers, stable contexts, and well learned behaviors. However, the reference literature also emphasizes several key points that seem to be largely absent or misconstrued in the IS literature on habit.

First, what is triggered in automatic behavior is not a behavior sequence per se, but rather a set of goals and the means that have previously been developed to achieve those goals. It is important to note here that the term goal in the reference literature has a very different meaning than intention as used in the IS literature. Whereas intention is defined in terms of conscious deciding (see quotes in previous section), goal-directed behavior has no such implications.

Most habits that are studied by social psychologists are intentional. Intentionality in this case does not refer to planned or conscious action, but rather to being functional and goal directed. Habits are developed as behaviors that appear to be satisfactory in fulfilling some goal (e.g., taking the car to reach a destination efficiently, using seatbelts for safety, brushing our teeth to keep ourselves healthy, eating fatty food for pleasure). Although most habits are, in principle controllable...it often appears difficult to overrule strong habits (Verkanplen and Orbell 2003, p. 317, emphasis added).

That goals and means, rather than a specific sets of behaviors, are triggered in automatic behavior means that the behavioral sequences put into motion by an environmental cue may differ very significantly from the ones originally learned. This statement holds true not only for motor skills like playing the piano, but for language skills such as the ability to string together novel sentences never before uttered, and for complex social behaviors such as politeness or rudeness.

Automatic processes have been found to play in a wide variety of higher mental and behavioral processes... .Moreover, these effects occur for simple, motoric responses (e.g., posture, mannerisms) and complex, social behavior (e.g., rudeness, intelligence) alike (Bargh and Feguson 2000, p. 928).

Aarts and Dijksterhuis (2000) concluded that habitual behaviors are automatically linked not to relevant environmental events per se but rather to the mental representations of the goal pursuits they serve....Eventually, the goal structure itself becomes active on the perception of the features of the situation (Bargh and Feguson 2000, p. 934, emphasis added).

Although situational constancy is a necessary condition for habits to develop, habit formation may occur in contexts that differ in specificity. Habits may develop in a specific situation, and thus be confined only to that situation....Perhaps more interesting are habits that are more general in nature, occurring in a variety of settings (Verplanken and Aarts 1999, p. 106).

Second, the goals activated in habitual behavior do not need to have been consciously intended by the individual. In experimental studies, it has proved possible to “prime” goals, and even more tellingly, to prime motivations (e.g., achievement motivation), which can drive human behavior for a much longer time than primed goals, without the subjects ever becoming aware of the priming and without the subjects even being able to control the influence of the priming.

Dozens of contextual priming studies [indicated that] primed trait constructs then alter the interpretation of the behavior and hence the impression formed of the target person, without the participants becoming aware of the influence (Bargh and Feguson 2000, p. 929).

In short, social behaviors in the external environment often if not usually access their corresponding mental representations in an immediate and direct manner, without conscious and effortful process of categorization and interpretation being necessary (Bargh and Feguson 2000, p. 929, emphasis added).

People engaged in habitual actions do not consciously access habit intentions, either because they do not need to do so in order to repeat well-learned intentional responses or because the behavior was not intended to begin with and perhaps became well-learned as a byproduct of some other action sequence (see, e.g., Lippa & Goldstone's, 2001, demonstration of the acquisition of unintended responses through association) (Wood et al. 2002, p. 1283, emphasis added).

After all, almost all external, environmental influences on our behavior involve stimuli and messages that are in plain view, yet either we do not realize that the influence is taking place (Wilson and

Brekke 1994) or we are overconfident as to our ability to control any such influences (Bargh 1999) (Bargh 2002, p. 283, emphasis added).

Seemingly trivial things in our environment may be influencing our behavior. Dormant goals are triggered without our even realizing. It's not that we're necessarily unaware of the stimulus itself. However, we are oblivious to the effect that it is having on us. Without our knowledge, we suddenly begin to pursue a goal that has been set off by some seemingly innocuous event (Fine 2006, p. 136, emphasis added).

Third, once goals have been activated by the environment, automatic behaviors do not need to follow exactly the previously learned patterns, but can “operate autonomously in complex interaction with environmental events—once they are in operation, conscious choices and guidance to completion are no longer necessary” (Bargh and Feguson 2000, p.933, emphasis added).

Why do we believe this alternative interpretation of the reference literature on habitual behavior to be significant for our understanding of continuing IT use? First, we think it means that it may be possible for people, while operating automatically and not under the control of conscious intentions, to learn how to apply known IT features in entirely new sequences and contexts or even to “learn” how to use entirely new IT features, through unconscious generalization as a result of environmental priming. This supposition is in direct contrast to the view in the IS literature that people must “decide” to learn new features (see Jasperson et al. 2005).

If it sounds implausible that people may learn how to use new IT features without consciously thinking about it, consider this description of the cognitive capabilities of pigeons.

The pigeons first viewed a page of 16 small pictures that were either all the same or all different. If the first page showed 16 small identical mugs, for example, the birds were able to successfully choose a page showing 16 identical hourglasses over a page showing a variety of pictures. They could also identify pages of all-different pictures.

The birds thus showed an ability to compare two relationships—such as sameness or difference … previously known only in humans and primates….

...[Although this experiment does not necessarily demonstrate that birds use analogic thinking as humans do, it] “is a great demonstration that the birds are making a very abstract discrimination” (Moreira 2006, p. C2).

Second, we believe our alternative interpretation of the reference literature on automatic behavior suggests an important assumption that seems to be missing in the IS literature on continuing IT use, namely, that IT itself may be one of the most important triggers of automatic IT use behaviors, as we discuss below.

## IT Use May Be a Powerful Environmental Trigger of Automatic (Non-Intentional) IT Use Behavior

We propose that social behavior is often triggered automatically on the mere presence of relevant situational features; this behavior is unmediated by conscious perceptual or judgmental processes (Bargh et al. 1996, p. 231).

The IS literature on continuing IT use mentions that habitual IT use may be triggered by environmental cues, but it gives surprisingly little attention to what those cues might be. A possible explanation for this omission is the origin of continuing IT use theory in reasoned action and planned behavior models that concentrate almost exclusively on what goes on inside people's heads. Whatever the reasons, the neglect is unfortunate, we believe, because environmental cues may be the most important means of improving people's continuing IT use, short of training, which is likely to be more expensive.

So what are the likely most important environmental cues that can trigger automatic IT use behavior? One is a task that a user intends to perform. Much if not most IT use is instrumental—that is, performed not for its own sake but to accomplish some other end—such as to communicate with a colleague or to write a paper. “People use IT because it is instrumental in their tasks” (Barki et al. 2007, p. 174). In general, the reference literature on habitual behavior suggests that, although individuals may consciously intend to perform some task, they may never need to form a conscious behavioral intention to use IT, because the goal of using IT and the plan for how to use it is automatically activated by the intention to perform the task.

In particular, the instigation of habitual acts may often be intentional, whereas the further execution of acts may be unintentional. For instance, when we go shopping, the instigation of a whole set of habits may thus be intentional, while the further acts (e.g., taking the car, choosing the same shopping area, going through the supermarket in a fixed order) may be unintentional (Verplanken and Aarts 1999, p. 105 emphasis added).

We argue, in essence, that much continuing use of IT is more like taking the car than it is like going shopping. Hence continuing IT use is much less likely to be intentionally driven and much more likely to be triggered by environmental cues, just as taking the car to go shopping may be triggered by the sight of an empty refrigerator or of a shopping list.

Although we believe that the tasks people use IT to perform are likely to be the most frequent and powerful environmental or situational triggers of automatic IT use, we also believe that information technology itself—at the device or the feature level—may also serve as an extraordinarily powerful environmental cue (Markus 2005; Markus and Silver 2008). Thus, the mere sight of the computer on one's desk may actually trigger working on a paper, and a view of one's mobile phone may trigger calling a friend. Few of us can recall the precise layout of menus and sequences of commands for familiar complex systems when we are not actually working with them, but we do not need to do so. With good design, we can often be guided interactively by cues given by the technology without having to take our attention away from our task—unless we make a serious error or the technology has a failure. However, when breakdowns occur, the technology itself becomes the object of conscious attention and the task is put aside until such time as the technology problem has been fixed and the task can resume.

Why do we believe it is so important to focus heavily on the environmental cues that may trigger continuing IT use, instead of focusing almost exclusively on the mental representations (beliefs, expectations, attitudes, etc.) in people's heads? The answer is that by focusing on the environment, we direct our attention to design characteristics of IT that may have important influences on whether, when, how, how much, and to what effects people continue to use IT. Numerous IS scholars have called for greater attention to the IT artifact and for greater focus on design (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hevner et al. 2004; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001): Increased focus on the environmental triggers of automatic IT use behavior is entirely consistent with these scholars' calls.

## Implications of the Alternative Assumptions about Continuing IT Use

To summarize, we are arguing that continuing IT use may be far less intentional and far more automatic than the IS literature would lead one to presume. The goals and means of IT use can be triggered automatically by the intention to perform a task that requires IT use or by the mere sight (or sound) of

IT tools. While using IT in the automatic mode, people may be able not only to repeat previously learned IT-use behavior sequences, but also to create new ones, because of cues provided by the IT itself. Emotions experienced while using IT may also drive people's continuing IT use behavior outside of their awareness; emotions may actually derail previously formed conscious intentions about the task or about IT use.

What does this alternative view of continuing IT use imply? First, it implies that focus should be more on behavior—action—than on intentions or decisions. It implies that reasoned action models such as TAM and its variants are probably not the most useful theoretical foundations for studies of continuing IT use, however valuable they may be for understanding initial IT adoption decisions and technology replacement decisions. Fortunately, there are numerous alternative theoretical perspectives that are well suited for the study of automatic IT use behavior. They include practice theory (Orlikowski 2000), situated action theory (Nardi 1996b; Suchman 1995), activity theory (Baerentsen and Trettvik 2002; Kaptelinin 1996; Nardi 1996a), distributed cognition (Hutchins 1995; Nardi 1996b), environmental psychologies (Gibson 1979; Michaels and Carello 1981), and various combinations of the above (Baerentsen and Trettvik 2002).

Second, our alternative view of continuing IT use has methodological implications. Self-report techniques such as interviews and questionnaires are not well suited to elicit valid information about individual-level processes that take place outside of people's awareness. Observational studies and experiments of various sorts hold greater promise to shed light on continuing IT use, we believe.

Third, the alternative view of continuing IT use is entirely consistent with the current upsurge in interest in IS design science (Hevner et al. 2004). Unlike reasoned action models that focus on what is in people's heads, the alternative view directs attention squarely to characteristics or features of IT that may restrict or guide users' actions in particular ways (Silver 1991). Automatic behavior does, of course, have down sides, such as limited controllability, but it has enormous advantages—it is very efficient, freeing up people's mental and physical resources for other activities (Verkanplen and Orbell 2003). Thus, information technologies that promote efficient and effective automatic behavior can be beneficial to people and organizations. This makes the study of how IT triggers (or fails to trigger) effective automatic IT use behavior an appropriate subject for the IS field (see Markus and Silver 2008).

## Conclusion

The growing interest in continuing IT use represents a major step forward for the IS community. Much of value has been learned, and many promising lines of future research have already been identified. At the same time, much of the writing on continuing IT use is based squarely on the tradition of IS theorizing about planned behavior and reasoned action. In this essay, we have proposed that the IS field should break away from this tradition, at least provisionally, until we have evaluated seriously and systematically rival theoretic perspectives on continuing IT use.

Naturally, alternative theoretical perspectives such as activity theory or practice theory or the rival assumptions we articulated here need to be tested. And they need to be tested against traditional theories. How the testing is done matters, too. Because such theories differ from the traditional view in their fundamental assumptions, it would be premature to try to combine alternative theories with traditional theories into comprehensive models for empirical testing. Instead, the contrasting implications of the different theories should be derived and pitted directly against each other in a classic comparison of rival propositions (Yin 1999). Doing so, we believe, will add considerable depth and breadth to our field's knowledge of continuing IT use behavior, whatever the findings of the empirical research may be.
