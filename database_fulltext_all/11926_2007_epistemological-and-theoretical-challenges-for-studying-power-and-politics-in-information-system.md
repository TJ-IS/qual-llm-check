# Epistemological and theoretical challenges for studying power and politics in information systems

Leiser Silva

Decision and Information Sciences, C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, 334 Melcher Hall, Houston, TX 77204-6021, USA, email: Lsilva@uh.edu

Abstract. The study of the role of power in managing information systems (IS) still offers a major epistemological challenge to researchers in the field. Although significant work has been done, there is yet to emerge a research approach that permits a penetrating study of the phenomenon of power by virtue of adopting a Machiavellian stance. This paper proposes such an approach in the form of an interpretivist position combined with a theoretical framework whose origin lies in political science and the sociology of technology. In developing its philosophical argument, the paper compares three meta-theories that have been applied to study IS: Phenomenology, Critical Theory and Structuration Theory. All three are compared in terms of their epistemological position regarding the relationship between power and IS. We argue that, although enlightening, those metatheories fail to unravel the hidden and strategic nature of power. The paper concludes by proposing a particular theoretical formulation that, rather than censoring power and politics, will provide the epistemological means for unravelling them.

Keywords: power and politics in information systems, information technology, epistemology, interpretive research, actor-network theory, circuits of power

## INTRODUCTION

The study of power poses an epistemological challenge. Its origin is twofold. On the one hand, power has traditionally been associated with formal power that arises from positions of authority (Barnes, 1986; Weber, 1999) while on the other, its informal dimension, i.e. politics, has been regarded as illegitimate (Clegg, 1989). Indeed, politics is often associated with what Mintzberg (1983) calls illegitimate power and in the field of information systems (IS) has been deemed as the ‘dark side of power’ (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994). However, despite its negative connotations, the study of politics is fundamental for understanding IS. Keen (1981, pp. 31–32) in his classic paper stated it clearly:

Unfortunately, ‘politics’ have been equated with evil, corruption and, worst of all, blasphemy in the presence of the Rational Ideal, but politics are the process of getting commitment, or building support, or creating momentum for change; they are inevitable . . . It is absurd to ignore it . . . A political perspective on information systems is needed in research. It will of necessity be based on comparative field studies that illustrate theoretical concepts . . . It can immensely add to our understanding both of the implications of information technology and the dynamics of effective implementation.

Thus, the epistemological challenge for studying power comes from the belief that politics are ‘dark’ or illegitimate. How can a researcher study something that supposedly should not exist? Such a negative connotation may explain why there is little research concerning IS politics (exceptions are the work by Markus (1983) and Introna (1997); for a complete literature review on the study of power and IS, see Jasperson et al. (2002)). Given the hidden nature of power and politics, we will argue that an epistemological approach that emphasizes the interpretation of meanings, intentions and actions would be the most suitable for making sense of such a complex phenomenon. Hence in this paper we will argue in favour of an interpretivist approach for studying power and politics in IS. Moreover, we will argue that interpretivist studies on power and politics will benefit from drawing on a specific theoretical formulation which, by virtue of its epistemological assumptions, will unravel rather than censor political maneuvers made by actors in and around IS.

This paper is organized in five sections. After the introduction we will discuss the epistemological virtues of an interpretivist approach for the study of power and politics in IS. This section is followed by a reflection on three meta-theories that have been associated with interpretivist research and power, i.e. Phenomenology, Critical Theory and Structuration Theory. In Section 4, we propose an established theoretical formulation that includes epistemological assumptions that would unravel the hidden dimension of power. We conclude with a summary of our contributions and a reflection on the relevance of our argument.

## INTERPRETIVISM AND THE STUDY OF POWER AND POLITICS

## Epistemological principles of interpretivism

To discuss epistemological principles of interpretivism, we will draw on the work of the Canadian philosopher Charles Taylor (1971, p. 4) who defines interpretation as:

. . . an attempt to make clear, to make sense of an object of study. This object must therefore be a text, or a text-analogue, which in some way is confused, incomplete, cloudy, seemingly contradictory – in one way or another, unclear. The interpretation aims to bring to light an underlying coherence or sense.

He adds then that an interpretation should comply with three conditions:

1 An object or text that can be evaluated in terms of its coherence or lack of it; that is, to establish whether the text makes sense or not.

2 The interpretation, i.e. the expression of the meaning, should be distinguished from the object itself. Taylor (1971, pp. 4–5) expresses this in the following way:

We have to be able to make for our interpretations claims of the order: the meaning confusedly present in this text or text-analogue is clearly expressed here . . . Hence the object of a science of interpretation must be describable in terms of sense and nonsense, coherence and its absence; and must admit of a distinction between meaning and its expression.

3 This condition concerns the subject for whom the meanings of the interpretation have to make sense. Taylor (1971, p. 5) insists that ‘Without such a subject, the choice of criteria of sameness and difference, the choice among the different forms of coherence which can be identified in a given pattern, among the different conceptual fields in which it can be seen, is arbitrary’. The question now is: How do we convince a subject that the offered expression is the right interpretation of the text? Taylor suggests the hermeneutic circle:

What if someone does not ‘see’ the adequacy of our interpretation, does not accept our reading? We try to show him how it makes sense of the original non- or partial sense. But for him to follow us he must read the original language as we do, he must recognize these expressions as puzzling in a certain way, and hence be looking for a solution to our problem. If he does not, what can we do? The answer, it would seem, can only be more of the same. We have to show him through the reading of other expressions why this expression must be read in the way we propose. But success here requires that he follow us in these other readings, and so on, it would seem, potentially forever. We cannot escape an ultimate appeal to a common understanding of the expressions, of the ‘language’ involved. This is one way of trying to express what has been called the ‘hermeneutical circle’ (p. 6).

To avoid the hermeneutical circle becoming an eternal vicious circle which would make interpretation impossible, Taylor suggests two ways of getting out: one is the rational one, which is to demonstrate logically that the interpretation is correct, while the other is the empiricist one and consists of demonstrating the rightness of the interpretation through data that is independent from the subjectivity of the researcher. Whatever the case, as will be discussed in the next subsection, the task of an interpretivist researcher interested in power is to make sense of hidden intentions and seemingly perplexing actions.

## Interpretivism and the challenges of studying power and politics

In the social sciences, interpretivist research is a reaction to a naturalist position (Kincaid, 2002). While naturalists advocate that the methods of natural sciences such as physics can be applied to social sciences, interpretivists argue that an account of the social world requires a different epistemological approach, one that bases its accounts on meanings and language (Taylor, 1971). This stems from fundamental ontological differences between naturalists and interpretivists. On the one hand, naturalists conceive the social world as constituted by immutable laws that enforce causality in social life. Interpretivists, on the other, would deem the social world as the result of a hermeneutical interplay between emotions, desires, norms and actions (Burrell & Morgan, 1979). The question here is: Can we justify the use of hermeneutic interpretations as a scientific way for approaching social sciences?

Pavitt (2000) provides a suggestive example supporting the argument in favour of interpretivism. Pavitt asks us to imagine an individual kicking another person. Given the right instruments, it would be possible to calculate how far the person being kicked is going to be displaced. Pavitt calls that movement behaviour; which is totally predictable by applying Newton’s laws of dynamics. However, what we do not know is what the person will decide to do after the kick. She may decide to run, kick back, file a law suit, etc. Whatever she decides to do as the result of the kick would be, according to Pavitt, an action. This will depend on how the individual interprets the act of being kicked and the institutional setting that constitutes her social world on which she draws on to make sense of the kick. Unlike her behaviour after the kick, Pavitt observes that the course of action she is going to take will be much more complex, therefore very difficult to predict. Thus, interpretivist researchers would concentrate on the meaning agents provide to actions.

In studying power, the positivist approach is epitomized in Dahl’s (1957) definition of power: A exercises power over B when A makes B to do something B would not otherwise do (A and B constitute different agencies). As it can be observed in this definition, causality and behaviour – the essence of the naturalist approach – are present since A causes B to have a particular behaviour. This view of power is limited as it disregards structural aspects of the relationship between A and B. It needs to be complemented by an interpretive stance. Clegg (1989) illustrates the limitation of the behaviorist approach to power by drawing on the concept of mobilization of bias developed by Bachrach & Baratz (1962). Bachrach and Baratz proposed their concept of mobilization of bias as a critique of Dahl’s definition of power for not considering situations of non-behaviour or non-decision-making.

Bachrach and Baratz illustrate their idea of mobilization of bias through the example of a professor who attends a meeting and chooses not to express her opposition to a contentious issue. Instead, she decides to postpone it until a next meeting; eventually she abandons her original idea. Bachrach and Baratz formulate three different interpretations to make sense of this situation: first, the professor might have felt afraid of being regarded as disloyal; second, she might have anticipated arousing opposition from the audience; and finally, the professor might have believed that the motion would not be implemented. Whatever the final explanation, her non-action was the result of power. According to Bachrach and Baratz, Dahl’s conception cannot explain situations, such as in the previous example, where there is no externalized behaviour, yet power is pervasive. Because Dahl’s definition of power works only when the researcher can identify behaviour, the mobilization of bias concept suggests that behind any episode of power there is a structure that preconfigures it.

In organizations, legitimate power is derived from formal rules of authority (Mintzberg, 1983; Weber, 1999). However, authority will always be contested as formal rules are open to interpretation, that is, the source of politics.<sup>1</sup> In order to operate, organizations must delegate authority and discretion. Delegation of discretion occurs when powerful agents grant subordinates the right to interpret rules and procedures. However, delegated discretion should be disciplined. This means that those in positions of authority will ensure that their subordinates interpret the rules exactly as the authorities intended. This is the only way by which authorities can be sure that their agents are going to remain reliable deputies. Therefore, organized action will depend on the ability of powerful agents to discipline the discretion of delegated agencies. Organization in armies, for example, is achieved because soldiers obey orders without contest.<sup>2</sup> Thus, any actions outside the scope of formality that seek the fulfilling of personal agendas would be considered to be politics. Consequently, formal authorities in organizations will deploy disciplinary techniques, such as surveillance, direct reporting or sanctions policies to ensure that delegated agents remain loyal and that the discretion can be withdrawn at any time. ‘In the absence of these, by their evasion of malfunction, organizations are ill-advised to put their trust in agencies, as Machiavelli knew only too well’ (Clegg, 1989, p. 201).

Hence, it is the interpretation of rules that must be regulated and disciplined by powerful agents if they want to stop delegated agents from becoming rivals. However, as the interpretation of rules cannot be disciplined fully, some degree of politics will be unavoidable. The seriousness of politics, then, will depend on how effective are organizations in developing techniques of control and discipline as well as in developing organizational members’ identities in which loyalty to the organization is paramount. Thus, given that IS are key instruments of control and that they can radically change work tasks which will impact workers’ identity; it follows that understanding power and politics is fundamental for making sense of managing IS. This was clearly understood and brilliantly articulated by Keen (1981).

Following Clegg’s (1989) insights on understanding politics as the result of unavoidable open interpretations of organizational rules, we argue that interpretivism, which emphasizes language and the meaning of actions, is the most appropriate research approach for making sense of politics in IS. Interpretivist research has been applied in the field of IS through several meta-theories. In the following subsections, we discuss three of those meta-theories: Phenomenology, Critical theory and Structuration Theory. Those three were selected because they have power as one of their components. We have concentrated on their epistemological assumptions and their contributions. By discussing these three theoretical stances, we will observe that none of them delivers a full picture of power and politics. This discussion will lead to our proposal of a particular theoretical approach which by virtue of its concepts and epistemological principles would shed light on both sides of the power phenomenon, the legitimate and the ‘dark one’.

## META-THEORIES ASSOCIATED WITH INTERPRETIVIST RESEARCH AND POWER

## Phenomenology and hermeneutics

Boland (1985; 1991) has argued in favour of a phenomenological approach to research on IS through Hermeneutics.<sup>3</sup> Boland (1991) relates phenomenology, hermeneutics and research on IS by proposing that IS should be considered as texts that need to be interpreted. This perspective stems from Boland’s assumption that organizations and IS are constituted by communication acts and therefore research must become an interpretive task. He argues that rather than representing an objective truth, the output of an IS embodies an objective reality and therefore remains to be interpreted by users. This phenomenological view has had radical implications for the design of IS (see Avison & Wood-Harper, 1990; Rathswohl 1991; Boland et al., 1994; Coyne, 1995; Butler, 2000) as well as the interpretation of practices and artefacts related to IT (Lee, 1994; Klecun-Dabrowska & Cornford, 2000; Introna & Ilharco, 2004).

There are not many pieces of research on IS that explicitly acknowledge a phenomenological or hermeneutical approach that deals directly with power. An exception is the work of Boland (1987) who, based on hermeneutical principles, argued against a belief prevalent in the 1980s that equated information with power. Examples of research concerning with such a belief are Lucas (1984) and Saunders & Scamell (1986). They applied the strategic contingency (Hickson et al., 1971) and resource dependency theories (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978) to analyse the power held by IS personnel. IS personnel, those authors assumed, were supposed to be powerful because of the technical nature of their jobs and the dependence of the rest of the organization on automated processes and their outcomes.<sup>4</sup> Boland called such a belief a fantasy:

The fantasy that information is power puts the management scientist as system designer in the special position of designing a system of power, not just a system of data. It inflates the role of the system designer to include the creation and reallocation of power. (p. 373)

The fantasy of ‘information is power’, Boland adds, stems from ignoring the role of language and meanings in power relations. He emphasizes that, by virtue of agents interpreting the meaning of information, power becomes relational and is produced and reproduced through sense making (Weick, 1988). For example, clerks working in an IS centre may not appreciate the meaning that stock symbols printed on a report may suggest. By contrast, well-trained brokers could be empowered by the same information when interpreting the meaning of the symbols. Thus, one of the contributions of hermeneutics and phenomenology to the study of power in IS is to establish the relationship between power and meanings. Yet, the application of hermeneutics itself does not lead to a concrete theory of power that can help researchers in gathering concrete data capable of depicting a rich picture of the power phenomenon.

## Critical theory

Within the interpretive approach to IS, some authors have proposed Critical Theory as an alternative for guiding researchers (for a thorough review on the state of Critical Theory applied to the field of IS, see Brooke (2002)). The purpose of Critical Theory is to reveal the underlying ideology and interests embedded in scientific and technological knowledge. Critical theory has its origins in the Frankfurt School, a philosophical movement founded in the early twentieth century (Johnson, 1995). Among the most relevant expounders are Adorno, Fromm, Habermas, Horkheimer, Marcuse and Weil. Its epistemology is a mixture of psychoanalysis with Marxism, aiming to emancipate human beings from values of control and efficiency, especially from those discourses based on science and technology (Johnson, 1995). One of the leaders of the Critical Theory movement is the German philosopher Jurgen Habermas. He rejects the notion of positivist science that knowledge is totally objective. In this sense, Habermas (1972) argues that science has inscribed interests of technological domination yet disguises this fact by appearing to be free from value judgment.

Hence, a fundamental idea in the critical study of technology is to reject the positivistic notion that technological artefacts are neutral, i.e. the belief that technology is only a tool with which usage and application can be predicted. Critical theorists hold that technological artefacts are the result of political struggles, so they are carriers of specific interests (Callon, 1986; Ihde, 1990; Latour, 1993). In this sense, more recently, the philosopher of technology Andrew Feenberg, a critical theorist, has argued that technological artefacts are not exclusively the result of pure engineering and design processes but also products of often conflicting views of the world (Feenberg, 1999; 2002). Accordingly, he proposes that the application of a particular technology can only be explained through understanding its specific history and by considering the local interpretations that its users assign to it. Thus, Feenberg holds that the study of technology requires its contextualization, that is, to study its meaning from the point of view not only of the designers but also from the users. Moreover, Feenberg emphasizes that this contextualization requires aesthetical and ethical dimensions which are, in turn, the seeds of emancipation.<sup>5</sup>

In the field of IS, Lyytinen & Klein (1985) argue in favour of Critical Theory as a plausible approach for conducting research (see also Ngwenyama, 1991). Lyytinen and Klein propose Critical Theory as an alternative to the hard science approach, particularly for research on the usage of IS. They argue that IS should be designed not only to increase efficiency in organizations but ‘must also increase human understanding and emancipate people from undesirable social and physical constraints, distorted communication and misapplied power’ (Lyytinen & Klein, 1985, p. 219). The thrust of their argument is to illustrate the socio-political aspects of IS and therefore the shortcomings of undertaking research by drawing exclusively on engineering approaches.

<sup>5</sup>In the area of organization studies, this approach has been applied by Koch (2000) in studying coalitions around technology that are vehicles of organizational change.

According to Lyytinen & Klein (1985), the contribution of Critical Theory to IS is twofold: one consists of classifying existing research and the other consists of providing a broader approach to IS that takes into account social aspects such as user resistance, alienation and lack of use. Those contributions highlight the differences between mere phenomenological approaches and critical studies. While phenomenological and hermeneutical studies on power have concentrated on aspects related to meanings, critical studies have focused on how communication is distorted by power and how this hinders emancipation (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994; Oliver & Romm, 2002; O’Donnell & Henriksen, 2002; Varey et al., 2002; Heng & de Moor, 2003; Puri & Sahay, 2003). The critical researchers’ argument is that open communication propitiates an ideal speech situation which is the first step towards emancipation (Habermas, 1972).

Even though critical research in IS has had as central the concept of power, it does not provide a theoretical background to study such a phenomenon. In this sense, Brooke (2002, p. 50) observes two major limitations: (1) the lack of a social theory on emancipation; and (2) an inadequate conceptualization of power. This coincides with Lyytinen (1992) who pointed out that critical research in general falls short in theorizing about the preconditions, constraints and interests related to power. To address these deficiencies, IS researchers have proposed complementing Critical Theory studies with other theoretical approaches to power such as the works by Foucault (Brooke, 2002) and Actor-Network Theory (Doolin & Lowe, 2002). In addition to the insufficient theorization of power, Alvesson & Deetz (2000) (cf. Doolin & Lowe, 2002) have observed that despite the plausibility of the objectives of critical pieces, they still do not have a great influence in the field. To address these, Alvesson and Deetz suggest conducting more empiric pieces. Thus, given that in critical research, there is, on the one hand, a need for a comprehensive theoretical conception of power, and on the other hand, there is a lack of empiric pieces (perhaps because of the absence of a theoretical background), the objectives of this paper – discussing the epistemological and theoretical challenges of studying power – become relevant.

## Structuration theory

Giddens formulated Structuration Theory as a way of resolving the social science dilemma between the deterministic and voluntaristic approaches to explaining human action. He formulates it as an ontology of social life and argues that it is misleading to separate social structures from agency because they exist in relation to each other (Giddens, 1984). Giddens (1979) proposes Structuration Theory to incorporate two main notions of the interpretive approach into research in social sciences: (1) the need to classify actions vis-a-vis intentions, reasons and motives; and (2) the joint analysis of individual actions and institutions.

Giddens (1984) conceives power as associated with agency, i.e. the capacity of an individual to achieve outcomes. Whether these are germane to the agent is irrelevant in Giddens’ conception. Because of its dual nature, that is, being constraining and enabling at the same time, power can be the means either for emancipation or for domination. The duality of power is manifested in structures of domination which are constituted by resources, i.e. properties of social systems in terms of legitimation and signification. Giddens (1984, p. 25) distinguishes two types of resources: allocative and authorative. Allocative resources are divided in three categories: (1) raw materials (for production); (2) production means and technologies; and (3) artefacts that are the result of the interaction between the previous two. Authorative resources are also divided into three categories: (1) the organization of time and space; (2) the production and reproduction of the body (bringing populations under administrative order); and (3) the organizations of life chances (resources for human expression such as cultural goods). These resources, Giddens argues, are fundamental for coordination in social systems. In sum, Giddens considers power as a dual structure (enabling and constraining) that operates through resources and that has its ultimate manifestation in social organization.

Structuration Theory has been applied as a framework for undertaking research in IS. Flagship work in this area includes that of Orlikowski (1992) and Walsham (1993). Orlikowski is concerned with the interaction of technology and organizations, and to investigate this relationship proposes a model derived from Giddens’ Structuration Theory. She dubs her model the ‘structuration model of technology’, claiming that information technology both constrains and enables its users at the same time. Orlikowski’s contribution to the study of the relationship between power and information lies in illustrating how the introduction of information technology can extend managerial control over users and curtail their discretion. Her research provides an account of the factors that favour the institutionalization of technology.

Walsham (1993) proposes Structuration Theory to ask a different question that of how context influences process.<sup>6</sup> Walsham argues that the answering of this question is crucial for understanding the impact of IS on organizations. Drawing on Giddens’ concept of the duality of structure, Walsham claims that although IS and organizations are both constrained by the context, they can change it at the same time. In Walsham’s proposed model, context assumes the role of structures and IS the role of actions. Structuration Theory, specifically its modalities, interpretive schemes, facilities and norms, is adopted by Walsham to conceptualize the linkage between context and process in IS.

Structuration Theory has drawn criticism from several social scientists. For example, Clegg (1979) criticizes Structuration Theory for underestimating the prior existence of domination because it does not take into account that structures are determined by enduring power relations. Another criticism of Structuration Theory comes from Gregson (1989), who claims that Structuration Theory is not a ‘theory’ in itself but a meta-theory because it does not make explicit what the most important questions for doing research are. In other words, it does not link concepts with data. Another problem is recognized by Sica (1986), who suggests that Structuration Theory is loaded with jargon and therefore is too complex for empirical research. In this sense, Walsham (1993, p. 70) recognizes the limitations of Structuration Theory in research on IS: ‘. . . the duality of structure and the associated modalities could be considered as too detailed and complex for empirical analysis in some instances’. Indeed, any researcher of IS interested in applying Structuration Theory would have to deal with the vast Giddens’ literature and the lack of link between theory and data.

Another challenge in applying Structuration Theory to the study of IS lies in Giddens conception of agency which he considers to be exclusively human (Clegg, 1989). This is problematic in studying IS given that an epistemology focused exclusively on human agency will leave aside the role that technological artefacts play in structuring power relations (Callon, 1986; Ihde, 1990; Latour, 1991). In the field of IS, this has been observed by Monteiro & Hanseth (1995) who criticize the application of Structuration Theory, particularly the works of Orlikowski (1992) and Walsham (1993). Specifically, Monteiro and Hanseth point out the aforementioned epistemological limitation. Orlikowski and Walsham do not describe in detail the technological features of the IS they are researching. Unless the technological attributes are studied in detail, it is not possible to establish which particular aspects affect power relations in organizations.

The application of Structuration Theory as a theoretical framework in interpretive research has enriched our understanding of the relationship between power and IS. Its main contributions consist of explaining the duality of information technology as enabling and constraining individuals and serving as a framework for interpreting the relation between context and processes associated with IS. However, given its complexity – manifested in not linking concepts with data – plus its exclusive focus on human agency, Structuration Theory leaves the black box of technology unopened. This is problematic in understanding power in IS, as it is by virtue of power that technology is black boxed (Latour, 1987; Ihde, 1990). For example, some of the studies on Enterprise Resource Systems deal with these technologies as if they were black boxes by labelling them ‘ERPs’. These systems are so complex, i.e. they are composed of many databases, tables, routines and interfaces, that – by treating them exclusively like black boxes – it is not possible to discern how the technology interacts with organizational members. In fact, the labelling of such a complex technology is an act of power itself. Therefore, following the aforementioned suggestion of Taylor on how to close the hermeneutic circle (either through logic or data), we conclude that Structuration Theory is of little help in completing interpretations in which power is central.

In sum, the meta-theories we have discussed bring about valuable contributions to our understanding of power and IS (Table 1 contains a summary of our discussion). Phenomenology and Hermeneutics make visible the relationship between power and meanings, while Critical Theory rejects the notion that information technology is neutral. As we just discussed, Structuration Theory makes evident the duality of technology and the relationship between context and process. However, these approaches solely would not provide a full picture of the power phenomenon in an interpretive study. They do not contain a concrete theory that analytically conceptualizes power in terms of its components and that directs researchers to the concrete pieces of the data they need to claim validity in their interpretations (Taylor, 1971; Klein & Myers, 1999).

## THEORETICAL AND EPISTEMOLOGICAL APPROACHES FOR THE STUDY OF POWER

However rich, the theoretical approaches discussed above do not provide a full account of power and politics. To do so those interpretive meta-theories would need to be complemented with a theoretical framework that focuses concretely on power. Such a theoretical framework should point out the concepts and pieces of data necessary for a rich interpretation of power and politics in IS. Consequently, to develop our argument, this paper will discuss a theoretical framework for the study of power that incorporates epistemological principles that would not only address the ‘dark side’ of power, but would also consider agency beyond human actors. This theoretical framework is Clegg’s (1989) Circuits of Power, which has been applied in our field (Introna, 1997; Silva & Backhouse, 2003; Backhouse et al., 2006).

## The circuits of power

The Circuits of Power constitutes a rich analytical tool for the study of power and IS. The concepts brought about by Clegg (1989) derive from relevant advances in the sociology of sciences (Callon, 1986; 1987; Law, 1986; Latour, 1987) and the sociology of organizations (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991; Meyer & Rowan, 1991). The framework also includes conceptions centred not only on individual and collective agency but also on Foucault’s (1977) concept of disciplinary power. One of the virtues of the Circuits framework is that it consolidates power concepts from political sciences and sociology and relates them to organizations. Another of the appealing characteristics of the framework stems from conceiving power as fundamentally strategic. Clegg suggests that in studying power, a descriptive approach would be more insightful than a normative one, i.e. research on power should concentrate on describing actors moves and avoid the temptation to provide moral judgments about them. Clegg adopts this strategic approach from Machiavelli and it coincides with the principle of agnosticism promulgated by actor-network theoreticians (Callon, 1986).

The principle of agnosticism is derived from a Machiavellian view of power and is key for making sense of legitimate and illegitimate power. Traditionally, the study of power in organizations has been centred on ‘legitimate power’ (Clegg, 1989). This type of power is associated with formal authorities (Weber, 1999) and formal hierarchies (Mintzberg, 1983). Actions outside the scope of these formal ways of governance are considered to be politics, or as Mintzberg calls them, the ‘dark side of power’. Actions related to politics are localized battles for autonomy and control of resources (Clegg, 1989). As discussed in the introduction, understanding these types of actions is crucial for managing IS, yet their study presents, we argue, two epistemological challenges. One stems from being deemed as ‘illegitimate’ or as ‘the dark side of power’. This immoral connotation hinders researchers from reporting to their readers a complete picture of the phenomenon they are studying. This is why actor-network theoreticians propose the principle of agnosticism. Putting in practice the principle of agnosticism requires an ethnographic approach to the study of power that would allow researchers to report and interpret power-related actions so that they can grasp the local and contingent rules of politica games. That was Machiavelli’s approach to study power. Clegg (1989, pp. 32–33) describes this interpretive approach:

In the Prince Machiavelli offers a rich descriptive ethnography of power conceived in terms of its strategies. Towards these strategies he takes not moral stance: they are neither good nor bad, their only purpose is their effectiveness . . . Power is simply the effectiveness of strategies for achieving for oneself a greater scope for action than for others implicated by one’s strategies. Power is not any thing nor is it necessarily inherent in any one; it is a tenuously produced and reproduced effect which is contingent upon the strategic competencies and skills of actors who would be powerful. From the shadows of the palace Machiavelli observes and describes power at play. It is this stress on description, on interpretation, on the translation of power that signals Machiavelli’s distinctiveness’.

In sum, we found two key ideas in the Circuits framework: (1) power is relational; and (2) that a comprehensive understanding of power requires a threefold perspective (i.e. the three circuits). Clegg uses the metaphor of a circuit to emphasize the relational nature of power in contrast to the idea that power is something that can be owned, as if it were a thing. For Clegg, then, power is a force that, like electricity, circulates through a medium. In the case of power, such media are social relations and discourses. The circuits metaphor also illustrates the elusive essence of power; as with electricity, it cannot be seen, but we are aware of it through its effects (Barnes, 1988). The second idea relates to the Circuits framework itself. There are three circuits of power: episodic, systemic and social. (The main concepts of the Circuits framework and their link to data are summarized in Table 2.) The episodic circuit of power, the most evident of the three, focuses on individuals’ actions, in which these individuals are in a power relation; in the sense that one makes the other do things this other would not otherwise do. Following Dahl’s (1957) terminology, the one exercising power is A while the other is B. Yet, by focusing exclusively on episodic power, Clegg claims, we miss the totality of the power phenomenon. Thus, the concept of episodic power needs to be complemented with the other two circuits.

## Circuit of episodic power

The circuit of episodic power is the most tangible of the circuits and has been the focus of attention of many social scientists. Episodic power is evident because it can be recognized in its outcomes, namely actions (Clegg, 1989). The character of this circuit can be recognized in Dahl’s previously cited definition of power: an ‘A’ exercises power over a ‘B’ when ‘A’ makes ‘B’ to do something ‘B’ would not otherwise do (A and B constitute different agencies). By concentrating on the circuit of episodic power, we focus on the resistance that actors may present to the adoption of IS, as this circuit makes itself evident when B resists A’s exercise of power. John Law (1991) calls this type of power ‘power over’ or causal power. The essence of ‘power over’ he maintains is in its relational nature which can be seen in the relationship between A and B. In other words, A creates for B an obligatory passage point.

## Obligatory Passage Points

In order to complement the circuit of episodic power and how this is related to the other circuits, Clegg draws on the works of actor-network theoreticians, particularly the concept of Obligatory Passage Points (OPPs) and the principle of symmetry (Callon, 1986; Latour, 1987). In an established power relation, A will establish an OPP for B. OPPs are the result of ‘translations’ after which B has no other choice but to accept the OPP as created by A. A complete depiction of episodic power should describe how A created an OPP for B through ‘translations’. There are four ‘moments’ of translation: problematization, interessment, enrolment and mobilization. Callon (1986) explains them in detail.

The first step is problematization or how to become indispensable. Problematization is when given a problem, one actor, through rhetorical means, presents the solution of that problem in terms of his or her resources. In this way, one group of actors defines an OPP. Also, throughout this step, the identities of the actors are defined. Here actor-network theoreticians do not differentiate between human and non-human actors. Researchers should abandon any a priori differentiation. This is called the principle of free association. Furthermore, in describing the identities of the actors, the researcher should use the same language for human and nonhuman actors. This is what actor-network theoreticians call the principle of symmetry. Both principles, symmetry and free association, are fundamental contributions of this theoretical framework because they allow us to see how technological artefacts are participants in the creation of power relations (a summary of these principles is presented in Table 3).

Following a successful problematization, the group of actors that experiences the problem must be convinced that the only way to resolve their problem is by traversing the OPP. The second translation step is called interessment. After the identities of the actors and OPPs have been defined, the group of actors experiencing the problem must be isolated. This isolation consists in impeding other possible alliances or interference that might challenge the legitimacy of the OPP. If the interessment is successful, it will confirm the validity of the problematization and the alliances. The third translation step is enrolment. During this step, the alliances are consolidated through bargaining and making concessions. The fourth and final step is the mobilization of the allies. This mobilization implies that actors will become spokespersons of the groups they claim to represent. This step consists of determining the legitimacy of the spokesperson. The movement between each step is called displacement, which involves discursive practices. When displacement occurs, power is exercised. IS can be viewed as OPPs. For example, some commercial airlines practically force travel agents to use their reservation systems. Those airlines have converted their IS in successful OPPs that travel agents must traverse if they want to sell airplane tickets.

## Circuit of social integration

The circuit of social integration is the circuit of dispositional power and its main concepts are the rules that govern meaning and membership in organizations. While episodic power deals with causal power, the circuit of social integration deals with dispositional power. Wrong (1995)

conceptualized dispositional power as a set of capacities and made a distinction between the notions of having and exercising power. In this sense, a capacity is a characteristic or position that entitles or enables someone to exercise power, but it does not necessarily imply its exercise. Power conceived in this manner is a facility that can cause something to happen. Clegg illustrates the idea of dispositional power by giving the example of a traffic police officer on a busy street who has the power to stop the traffic, regardless of whether he actually does it or not. The dispositional power of the police officer is embedded in the shared norms that bind the institutions of traffic regulations and the police in an urban society. The circuit of social integration, then, entails dispositional power.

## Circuit of systemic integration

This circuit considers power as facilitative (Parsons, 1967). This type of power is understood in terms of its ability to produce and achieve collective goals, i.e. ‘power to’ (Law, 1991). The facilitative notion of power is characterized by a nonzero sum game and a productive conception of power. These characteristics give facilitative power a positive character. We can put the facilitative notion of power in context with the others discussed so far by again using Dahl’s definition of episodic power (A is exercising power over B when A gets B to do something B otherwise would not do). Dispositional power is concerned with the capacities that preconfigure the standing conditions necessary for episodic power to occur. According to Parsons, A’s power is facilitative because it is A who decides the collective goals that are to be facilitated by B’s actions. The virtue of the facilitative aspect of power is that it broadens the understanding of power beyond mere conflict.

## Example

To illustrate the analytical strengths of the Circuits framework, let us discuss an example: a manager who makes her subordinates use a new IS even though its use is perceived by those as against their interests. The outcome of power is the organizational members using the system. In this situation, the episodic circuit of power is the most evident and it can be seen in the actions of the managers and employees: the latter following the instructions of the former. This perspective, although informative, provides a narrow view of the power issues. Hence, the need for the other two circuits to complete the analytical picture. The circuit of social integration draws our attention to the relation managers–subordinate (A–B) which is embedded in the organizational structure and provides the manager with access to resources and endows her instructions with legitimacy. The rules of meaning and membership are provided by the institution of management and the organizational structure that dictates what is meant to be a manager and the requirements to become one.

The analysis of the example is completed with the circuit of systemic integration. This circuit concentrates on techniques of production and discipline; in the example they come into being in two modalities. One is the set of disciplinary measures that would be deployed in case employees were deviating from their expected roles or were disobeying instructions.

Organizations have a full set of these disciplinary techniques (Mayo, 1975; Townley, 1993). The other modality by which facilitative power is manifested is through the techniques of production, for the system has inscribed specific ways of executing work tasks that favour particular interests. Personnel using the system would be doing things in a manner that would probably be different if the system were not present.

## CONCLUSION

This paper has addressed the epistemological and theoretical challenges presented by the study of power and politics in IS. While recognizing the contributions made by three metatheories, namely Phenomenology, Structuration Theory and Critical theory, our attention has been drawn to the limitations of these approaches, particularly on their epistemological assumptions when considering power. Furthermore, we have argued that an interpretive study of power applying any of those meta-theories should be complemented with a specific theoretical framework. Hence, we have proposed the Circuits of Power as a framework for conducting research on power in IS. The Circuits of Power will help researchers by providing them with analytical concepts on power that are linked to data. Furthermore, the Circuits framework offers researchers of IS a set of strong epistemological principles to make sense of a complex and mostly hidden phenomenon, i.e. power and politics.

Disregarding politics from our studies because of its negative connotation will hinder us from seeing how organizations, managers and IS professionals draw on technology and its associated discourses to achieve particular goals. To do so, we need to rely on research approaches that pay attention to meanings and intentions, and that give the researcher the right to make interpretations of context and actions. Power and particularly politics may seem hideous to most of us; however, we ought to resist the temptation to look away whenever we face them. Without making sense of them, our understanding of IS management will be incomplete.
