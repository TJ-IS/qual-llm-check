# Post-adoption behavior of users of Internet Service Providers

Joan Spiller <sup>a</sup>, Anthony Vlasic <sup>b</sup>, Philip Yetton <sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> CORDS Pty. Ltd., Melbourne, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Australian Graduate School of Management, University of NSW, Sydney, Australia

Received 27 September 2004; received in revised form 1 August 2006; accepted 20 January 2007 Available online 16 July 2007

## Abstract

We examined post-adoption behavior in the Internet Service Provider (ISP) industry and found that the needs of service continuers and service discontinuers are different and customers who discontinue generally do so in the first six months. Also, business customers are less likely to discontinue than are personal users and, while prior behavior is a powerful predictor of future behavior, socio-demographic characteristics are weak predictors. We generated a combined model, which accurately predicted 82.6% of customer choices to continue or discontinue. The findings have important implications for both theory development and practice.

## 1. Introduction

Maintaining a customer base is critical for any business. In IT-based industries, the ability to do this has been limited: some ISPs have reported that up to 100% of their customers discontinued each year [12]. Discontinuance rates of 35% were also reported for telecommunications [6], and 30% for wireless applications [8].

Research has demonstrated that it costs about six times as much to recruit a new subscriber as to maintain an old one, in paid membership contexts [32]. For ISPs, an extra 1% retention can add as much as 5% to the bottom line of the business [2]. Existing subscribers provide base revenue, eliminate the need to search for and acquire new customers, create opportunities for cross-promotion and sales, and generate referrals.

The analytical frameworks developed to explain adoption behavior have often been extended to explain post-adoption behavior [11,37,38]. For example, diffusion theory [31], which was developed to explain adoption behavior, accounts for aspects of postadoption behavior within stage five of its innovation–diffusion process, confirmation, where adopters re-evaluate their initial decision to adopt.

Using the same factors to explain both behaviors is, however, constrained by the implicit assumption that continuance can be modeled as an extension of adoption [3]. Instead, an alternative framework allows users to make an initial decision to adopt, then, as their knowledge of the service matures, to continue or discontinue the service due to factors not considered in their initial decision-making. Then the antecedents of post-adoption behavior vary from those made in their initial adoption decision. Here, we adopted this approach to model the switching behavior of customers who had a continuing need for a service. We tested the model with data from the ISP industry.

## 2. Literature review

Our study explored the continuance versus discontinuance behavior of customers. There are two relevant research streams covering these activities. The first, founded on expectation–confirmation theory, developed a behavioral model to explain post-adoption behavior, with such parameters as perceived usefulness, satisfaction, confirmation, and continuance intentions. The initial model was extended to include loyalty-based incentives [4] and an integrative model of adoption and post-adoption behavior with IT as a key component has been developed [21]. In related research, Bhattacherjee and Premkumar developed a model to explain changes in beliefs and attitudes towards IS usage [5]. They concluded that disconfirmation and satisfaction were critical to understanding changes in IS users’ beliefs and attitudes.

The second research stream combined the theory of reasoned action [14] with diffusion theory. It contrasted adoption with post-adoption behavior, showing that normative pressures influenced adopters, whereas postadopters were influenced by attitudes, with adopters basing their decisions on a richer set of innovation characteristics than did post-adopters [15].

Jasperson et al. presented an exhaustive review of both research streams to explain post-adoption behavior within an organization [13]. They developed a two level (organization and individual), three factor (prior use, habit and feature-centric) model. We extended this model by treating the vendor (ISP) as the organization level of analysis and the customer as the individual. The market then acts as the mechanism through which vendors influence post-adoption behavior. In the competitive ISP market, vendors act to minimize the effect of habit and prior use, developing features to attract competitors’ customers. Therefore, the model developed here of market-based, rather than intraorganization-based, post-adoption behavior includes the influence of competition on post-adoption behavior.

The factors that influence post-adoption customers to continue with or replace their current ISP can be grouped under five headings:

\- Features: What are the customer’s service requirements or needs?

\- Purpose: What influence does the purpose of the service, e.g., business versus personal, have on customer behavior?

\- Prior behavior: How does the customer’s prior behavior influence future behavior?

\- Demographics: What demographic factors influence customer continuance or discontinuance behavior?

\- Competition: How is post-adoption customer behavior influenced by the competitive behavior of vendors?

## 2.1. Features

SERVQUAL [39] identified five dimensions that customers consider in evaluating a marketplace: tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance and empathy [28,29,36]. Research has shown that churn (rate of turnover in the customer base) is generally due to poor service on these dimensions [16], with customer behavior typically explained by perception-based rather than technology-based characteristics [17].

ISPs also exploit cost-based strategies [22,30,34], with customers evaluating the balance between cost and service when making decisions about post-adoption continuance or discontinuance. With switching costs low, ISPs may be able to retain their existing customer bases by providing appropriate features, but overpricing would be a major barrier to recruiting new subscribers [18]. Indeed, an ISP may have to offer an introductory price that is 20–30% below average market rates to attract new customers. Consistent with that, one common approach used by ISPs to attract new customers is to adopt a lossleader strategy. Thus we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1. The probability of a customer continuing or discontinuing is a function of service features (requirements)

Hypothesis 2. Cost is more important than service quality for discontinuers (service/cost).

## 2.2. Purpose

The purpose for which a service is used also influences the decision to continue or discontinue. Prior research suggested that discontinuance is higher when a system is chosen for business rather than for personal use. We therefore hypothesized:

Hypothesis 3. Business customers are more likely than are personal use customers to be discontinuers (purpose).

## 2.3. Prior behavior

Typically, research on post-adoption behavior has used a cross-sectional methodology. However, as

Jasperson et al. argued: ‘‘By its nature, the study of postadoptive behavior situates an individual’s use of an IT application within a stream of use experiences. . .’’ Little is known about the factors that influence customers over time.

The diffusion and loyalty literature has reported that dissatisfaction is more common among late adopters [26]. Typically, they had higher expectations than the technology delivered, whereas early adopters were more realistic. The research also reported that satisfaction affects loyalty [20,23,25]. Satisfied customers did not actively search for alternative suppliers and the longer customers remained with a service, the less likely they were to discontinue. Thus:

Hypothesis 4. Customers with a history of discontinuance have an above average probability of discontinuing (history)

Hypothesis 5. Discontinuance is a negative function of tenure (tenure).

## 2.4. Demographics

There has been limited work explaining the influence of demographics on on-line customer post-adoption behavior. In marketing, demographic differences have been treated typically as biasing variables and held constant in the primary analysis [10,35]. In a study of US ISP users, it was found that young users were more likely to discontinue, and that there was no difference between the continuance behavior of males and females [19]. A study of Australian ISP users reported similar findings for age, but gender similarities were not supported. Instead, males were more likely to be discontinuers than were females.

Finally, little is known of the impact of the number of users covered by a contract. One study showed that, as the number of users increased, the rate of discontinuance increased. For a group of users, at any point in time, one or more of the group might be dissatisfied and motivated to search for a better service. We therefore hypothesized:

Hypothesis 6. Males have a higher probability than females of being discontinuers (gender).

Hypothesis 7. The rate of discontinuance is a negative function of age (age).

Hypothesis 8. The probability that an account holder is a discontinuer is a positive function of the number of users of the account (number of users).

## 2.5. Competition

Typically, vendors quickly emulate any successful competitive feature introduced by another ISP vendor. It follows that features that previously were the bases of competitive advantage become a competitive necessity as others provide those features, and they no longer influence choices to continue or discontinue. Thus:

Hypothesis 9. There are valued features, which are the bases of competitive necessity, and which therefore do not influence the probability of a customer continuing or discontinuing (requirements/competition).

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Data collection

The research site was a medium-sized Australian ISP franchise, with a central headquarters and geographically distributed franchisees. The system allowed the franchisees to tailor their services to local requirements.

The stratified random sample of 200 subjects was selected to have equal numbers of current (continuing) and former (discontinuing) customers. A phone-based questionnaire was developed to survey each sub-sample. The two questionnaires were similar, with the addition of supplementary questions for the customers who had discontinued. Those supplementary questions asked for the primary reason the customer had discontinued; whether that customer would reconsider the decision and, if so, why; and whether the customer had signed with another ISP. The survey took about 10 min to complete.

The ISP reviewed early drafts of the questionnaires to comment on item clarity. The final draft questionnaire was sent to ten franchisees for their comments on interpretability, relevance and coverage. In addition, three senior executives from the ISP, including the General Manager-Sales and Marketing, read and commented on the drafts. Based on their feedback, changes were made to improve the interpretability and coverage. This process also provided face and content validity checks.

To ensure consistency in the interview technique, an independent professional interviewer was hired to conduct the interviews. She was unaware of the hypotheses being tested.

The subjects were selected from the ISP’s customer database, in which discontinuers’ names and contact information had been retained, along with continuing customers’ data. The interviewer was given 500 pages printed from the database. The first page was selected randomly and then each subsequent page was selected by copying the 100th next page in the database. Each page typically contained 19 names, with their telephone numbers and other customer data.

The recruitment process proceeded as follows. The interviewer began by calling the first contact number (business, home or mobile service) on the first page. If the customer answered, he or she was invited to be a subject for the research and asked to reply to the survey. If the number was engaged, or there was a recorded message that matched the details on the database, or the customer was not available but a call-back time was suggested, additional calls were made to that number until the customer was contacted and invited to be a subject for the research.

If contact could not be made, the next customer on the page was called. This continued with the interviewer working down the page to recruit a subject. When a customer had been recruited and the survey completed, or while waiting to contact a customer who was not available when called, the interviewer moved to the top of the next page and followed the same protocol to recruit the next subject.

On average, it took five calls to contact a continuer and six calls to contact a discontinuer. Of the customers contacted, only one continuer and one discontinuer declined to be interviewed. In identifying potential subjects, the response rate was 20.0% for continuers and 16.7% for discontinuers. The success rate for those agreeing to be interviewed on the telephone was 99.0% for those contacted. The initial response rate is similar to other field studies. Using a telephone interview was a success, as the conversion rate from contact to subject was very high.

## 3.2. Measures

Typically, data on post-adoption behavior has been either self-reported or intention-based [1]. The concern about using either self-reported or intention data is that the strength of a relationship is often over-estimated due to respondent dispositional bias [24]. In our study, measuring actual behavior eliminated that bias.

## 3.2.1. Dependent variable

Each subject’s status as a continuer or discontinuer was confirmed during the interview. To distinguish between replacement-discontinuers and disenchanteddiscontinuers, discontinuers were also asked if they had subsequently signed up with a new ISP.

In the sample of 100 discontinuers, there were 96 replacement-discontinuers—that is, discontinuers who had switched vendors, signing up with another. There were four disenchanted-discontinuers—those who had not signed up with another ISP. As our study was concerned with the former (replacement-discontinuers), we excluded those four from our analysis.

## 3.2.2. Independent variables

Closed-ended questions were used to collect data to test Hypotheses 3–8, with each subject’s responses cross-validated against the data in the database during the interview. Where there were discrepancies in the data, the interviewer specifically checked and validated the responses.

Open-ended questions were used to collect data to test Hypotheses 1 and 2. During the telephone interviews, subjects were asked to identify the important service requirements (features) that they wanted delivered by an ISP (for Hypothesis 1). In addition, discontinuers were asked their primary reason for discontinuing the service with the ISP (for Hypothesis 2).

If the subjects expressed difficulty in describing the features they considered important, the interviewer provided a list of responses. Only three continuers and four discontinuers asked to be prompted. Excluding those subjects from the data did not materially affect the findings.

The interviewer coded the responses against the following list of potential responses from the literature:

(1) Reliability of service

(2) Quick response to queries

(3) Communication—staff/agent

(4) Speed of Internet connection

(5) Reliability of connection

(6) Price

(7) Company reputation

(8) National access

(9) Technical support

(10) Easy access to support

(11) Competence of staff/agent

(12) Courtesy of staff/agent

(13) Flexibility of service offered

(14) Payment options

(15) Web site

## (16) Account management (billing)

When she had any doubt about how to code a response, she noted the specific words used by the subject either against the category (feature) she selected or under the default heading ‘Other’. When subjects identified more than three features as important or very important, they were asked to identify the three most important. Only ten of the features listed above were ranked in the top three by the subjects (see Table 1).

Thirty-two of the 196 respondents identified fewer than three important features (Hypothesis 1). Six continuers and 15 discontinuers identified only two. Seven continuers and two discontinuers identified one. One continuer and one discontinuer were unable to identify a single important feature. The discontinuer said that he simply wanted ‘‘the service to work right, first time, every time.’’ All 96 replacement-discontinuers identified their primary reason for discontinuing without prompting.

When the interviews had been completed, one of the authors reviewed the coding of those cases where the interviewer had any difficulty in classifying the response. No changes in coding were recommended. However, he identified two issues to be examined when considering the implications of our findings.

\- Twenty responses were coded by the interviewer under ‘Other’, ten from continuers and ten from discontinuers. Reviewing those responses, the author concluded that they did not cluster around one or more constructs. Thus, there was no evidence indicating the presence of an important feature omitted from the 16 listed above.

\- Nine features were identified as the primary reason for discontinuing by at least two subjects (received better offer, too expensive, wanted to check out the competition, disconnection problems, billing problems, connection too slow, too many engaged signals, not happy with support, and not enough features). The words chosen to label these features were taken from those most frequently used by the subjects.

Inspecting the subjects’ actual words, two additional observations could be made: The three features that were most frequently given for discontinuing were all directly related to cost, and four of the ten features identified as important service requirements (for Hypothesis 1) were not included among the reasons given for discontinuing (reliability of service, quick response to queries, flexibility of service offered, and payment options).

This review of the written responses also confirmed that nine of the ten important service features had the same meaning for continuers and discontinuers, with the exception being payment options. All seven continuers who identified this feature as important referred positively to the option of going to their agents offices to pay. None of the discontinuers who identified this feature as important referred to that option, but nine of them objected to providing a credit card number to which the ISP could charge excess fees. It would appear that this feature had two dimensions, as discussed later.

## 3.3. Analysis

We used Pearson’s chi-squared $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ methodology to test Hypotheses 1–9, because it fitted the data structure of this study—nominal categorical data [9,33,27]. Few other statistical methods are designed to do this.

To estimate the combined effects of the results, a logistic regression model was developed in which discontinuance was a function of features, purpose, prior behavior and demographics. Logistic regression was chosen to fit the data structure: the dependent variable was dichotomous and the independent variables were categorical [7]. The analysis was conducted using SPSS.

The total sample size was 196, with 100 continuers and 96 discontinuers. Incomplete data reduced the effective sample size for some of the statistical tests, with the sample size for each test reported in the relevant table.

## 5. Discussion and implications

## 5.1. Findings

We found that three features (reliability of service, quick response to queries and payment options) affected customers’ decisions to continue or discontinue. We also discovered that discontinuers gave higher weight to cost than to service quality. Sixty-one of the 96 discontinuers cited one of three cost-based features as their primary reason for discontinuing a service. The four important features for delivery by an ISP in Table 1 that were not also cited as reasons for discontinuing were all service-based features. In addition, only six of the 96 discontinuers said that they would reconsider their decision to discontinue if the service were improved, whereas 46 said that they would reconsider if the cost were reduced.

The four features (reliability of connection, price, speed of connection, and easy access to support) most frequently mentioned as important were cited equally by continuers and discontinuers (see Table 1, no significant differences).

Our results for purpose were unexpected. They showed that customers who used the service for personal reasons were more likely to discontinue than business users. As expected, prior behavior (history, tenure) was found to be a powerful predictor of postadoption behavior. Customers who had a history of switching and/or tenure of less than six months at the ISP were more likely to switch.

Somewhat surprisingly, we found that demographic characteristics (gender, age) did not influence continuance/discontinuance choices. Table 6 also shows that the rate of continuance/discontinuance behavior was independent of occupation $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 . 6 , \ \mathrm { n . s . } )$ and education $\stackrel { \cdot } { ( } \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 . 3 , \mathtt { n . s . } )$ . However, there was a significant relationship between continuance/discontinuance and the number of customers using a single account: the greater the number of users of a single account, the lower the probability that the account holder discontinued.

## 5.2. Potential validity threat

The findings are subject to one potential construct validity threat. Continuers and discontinuers identified different characteristics of the construct, payment options, as an important feature. Payment options apparently had two dimensions: (1) the option to go into an agent’s office to pay an invoice, favored by the seven continuers who identified this feature as important and (2) the need to provide a credit card number to the ISP to cover excess charges, objected to by nine of the 27 discontinuers who also thought this feature was important. Of the other 18 discontinuers in this category, eight did not explain their behavior further and ten gave unrelated reasons. This potential validity threat does not question the importance of payment options as a feature but does require that the nature of the feature be clearly defined.

## 5.3. Implications for research

Our results make several contributions to the understanding of post-adoption behavior of ISP online customers. The three features that discriminate between continuers’ and discontinuers’ behavior were not the features most frequently identified by customers as important in the service delivered by their ISP. Four other features were cited more frequently—but, while seen as important, those features did not differentially affect customer post-adoption behavior. The literature typically disregards the latter data and, instead, focuses on those features that differentiate between continuance/discontinuance behaviors. Models of marketbased post-adoption behavior need to be extended to explain both data sets.

Age, gender, education and occupation had no influence on the probability of continuing or discontinuing. Combining these findings with earlier research suggests that the features influencing post-adoption behavior have changed over time, including over the life cycle of a service. For example, gender influenced postadoption behavior in the early adoption phase but not in the present mature market. Models of market-based post-adoption behavior should be dynamic.

The methodology adopted here collected actual behavior and was based on a stratified random sample.

By analyzing actual data, we were able to reduce biases in results based on customers’ intentions rather than on their actions. The stratified random sample methodology reduced the potential validity threat from nonresponse bias in surveys.

The predictive model of post-adoption behavior estimated above includes only four independent variables: two features, and purpose and tenure. In contrast, the implicit post-adoption mental model analyzed above includes at least three and up to ten features, as well as history, tenure, purpose and number of users. In that case, the complexity of the postadoption model could be significantly greater than the number of variables required to predict continuance and discontinuance behavior. The literature under-estimates the complexity of post-adoption behavior when it accepts the number of significant variables in a parsimonious predictive model as a measure of the complexity of post-adoption mental models.

## 5.4. Implications for practice

The features most frequently cited as important for an ISP to deliver (reliability of connection, price, speed of connection and easy access to support) were competitive necessities, while three less frequently cited features (quick response to queries, reliability of service, and payment options) were potential bases for competitive advantage. It would be a serious risk for a vendor to reduce service levels on the four necessities. Vendors should also assess the sustainability of any competitive advantage based on the other three features.

Our results suggest that ISPs should focus resources on retaining their customers during the first six months of service, thereby decreasing the likelihood of future switching behavior. In addition, while age and gender had no influence on switching behavior, the number of users of an account predicted switching behavior. These results, in combination with earlier findings, suggest that the market served by ISP vendors has changed over time with implications for segmentation strategies.

Finally, a combined model of the significant findings accurately predicted 157 of 190 choices by customers to continue or discontinue. There are, apparently, two segments: a stable continuer segment and a segment of discontinuers with a very high churn rate. Essentially, there is a differentiated market segment based on service quality, and a commodity segment based on cost. For the ISP we studied, modeling its market as two segments with different dynamics, rather than as a single market, resulted in very different projected sales revenues over the following three years, giving the ISP different strategic choices. This suggests that ISPs should adopt different marketing strategies to focus on the continuer and discontinuer market segments.
