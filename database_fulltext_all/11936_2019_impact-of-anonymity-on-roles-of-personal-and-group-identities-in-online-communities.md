## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online anonymity Group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Personal identity Social identity model of deindividuation e<sup>f</sup>ects Online discussion community

## A B S T R A C T

Extant research on the control of anonymous human behavior is inconclusive because of its focus on partial aspects of the self-concept. Multiple self-concepts are usually e<sup>f</sup>ective in any given situation. Hence, a holistic approach is preferred over a fragmented picture of the self. We investigate anonymous online communities to determine whether multiple self-concepts concurrently control human behavior.

Data were collected from 1453 users through a web-based survey. The results showed that multiple self concepts concurrently in<sup>fl</sup>uenced argument quality through group norm conformity, whereas private-self directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced argument quality. Furthermore, online anonymity decreased the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of group identity, contrary to existing assertions.

## 1. Introduction

As information technology (IT) becomes increasingly fused with the real world, IT artifacts such as online communities and social networking services enable human behaviors that have not been feasible previously. In particular, in online communities, collective knowledge is generated, updated, and distributed through the online contributions of many people across time and space [87]. In this process, anonymity the inability of others to identify an individual or for others to identify one’s self” ([15], pp. 3039–3040)—is a key aspect of user engagement because it encourages active participation, particularly from members under various constraints. Even though anonymity is necessary to ensure information privacy, it also creates an environment that allows negative behaviors such as hate speech and juvenile levels of responsibility [52,57]. As the consequences of such behaviors enabled by online anonymity gain signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, questions related to these behaviors, e.g., which “self” controls human behavior in highly anonymous online communities, have recently attracted much attention from various disciplines including information systems (IS), commu nication, healthcare, and psychology (e.g., [22,35,56,57,105,106]).

The self-concept refers to the totality of a particular person’s thoughts and feelings toward oneself as an object of re<sup>fl</sup>ection [85]. Thus, the self-concept is a broader construct that is inclusive of identity, which “re<sup>fl</sup>ects the roles that a person occupies in the social structure” ([94], p. 226). The self-concept is important because it helps people make sense of themselves, others, and the world. In addition, people may have multiple self-concepts (e.g., the private self vs. the public self, or personal identity vs. social identity) that are available in memory for use in any given situation [64]. These self-concepts are sometimes related and can reinforce each other; at other times, they may work independently [55]. Hence, a holistic approach to self-concepts is preferred over a fragmented, disjointed picture of the self [79].

However, existing research on the control of human behavior in anonymous online environments is inconclusive, mainly because of its emphasis on partial aspects of an individual’s self-concepts. For in stance, an early attempt to explain dysfunctional anonymous behaviors, the classical deindividuation theory, asserted that the lack of account ability cues causes people to be less aware of their public selves and induces a state of depersonalization [108]. However, the deindividuation theory has been criticized for overemphasizing environmental conditions and public selves as the main causes of this depersonalized state. The social identity model of deindividuation e<sup>f</sup>ects (SIDE) was developed to critique the deindividuation theory, thus attempting to explain why anonymity within a social group does not always induce uncontrolled behaviors. SIDE asserts that “in becoming part of a group, individuals do not lose all sense of self, rather they shift from the personal to the social level of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation” ([83], p. 177). It further explains that behavioral control under anonymity depends on the relative salience of personal identity versus group (social) identity in an online community [15]; group identity refers to a shared understanding among group members regarding what belonging to the group means [101], whereas personal identity refers to “a unique individual with self-descriptions drawn from one’s own biography and singular constellation of experiences” ([67], p. 479). Meanwhile, SIDE does not delineate what would happen when personal identity is relatively salient or when it con<sup>fl</sup>icts with group identity. For example, Postmes et al. [78] researched social in<sup>fl</sup>uence in computer-mediated communication (CMC) environments and predicted that anonymity can increase social in<sup>fl</sup>uence if group identity is salient. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting their re search objective of seeking support for SIDE, experimental participants were divided into two groups: anonymous and identi<sup>fi</sup>able. Both groups were manipulated to develop a salient group identity, ignoring cases of salient personal identity.

By contrast, the identity theory asserts that personal identity is the lowest level of self-categorization [36] and thus a fundamental driving force for human behavior [92]. Indeed, personal identity might even play a primary role in enforcing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of group identity. In an attempt to link personal identity to group identity, Deaux [17] suggested that some features of an individual’s group identity are based on personal feelings and values. In addition, Stets [91] argued that these two identities might be related through a common system of meaning. This approach undermines the treatment of personal and group identities as alternative drivers of human behavior under conditions of online anonymity. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rst objective of this paper is to apply a holistic approach to self-concepts and investigate whether they can concurrently control human behavior in anonymous online communities. Our results showed that multiple self concepts such as the public self and group identities concurrently in<sup>fl</sup>uence argument quality through group norm conformity, whereas the private self directly in<sup>fl</sup>uences argument quality.

Another important aspect of group identity is that identity’s acti vation, i.e., group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Whereas group identity manifests among what is consensually held by group members to be a group [76], group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an individual-level activity that embodies the group identity within a particular individual member. In other words, group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is an important individual activity in which meanings and expectations associated with group membership are in corporated into the self [95]. Stets and Burke [92] noted, “despite the focus on the activation of a group identity, the source of such activation is left to individual and situational variability and apparently does not depend on social structural characteristics” (p. 230, italics original). In particular, Oakes [63] asserted that the activation of group identity is determined by the congruence between an individual’s stored category speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and their perceptions of the situation.

However, existing research has raised con<sup>fl</sup>icting arguments regarding the e<sup>f</sup>ect of online anonymity on group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. SIDE asserts that when group identity is strong, online anonymity can promote group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation by obscuring within-group interpersonal di<sup>f</sup>erences [49]. Conversely, the theory of self-defense mechanisms proposes an alternative behavior pattern. Facing a strong group identity, one can choose to dissociate from—rather than associate with—the group if one disagrees with the group’s identity [5]. Therefore, the second objective of this paper is to investigate whether online anonymity leads to group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with or dissociation from online communities with strong group identities. Our results showed that online anonymity actually decreased the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of group identity rather than increasing it, thus contradicting the prediction of SIDE.

This study focuses particularly on online discussion communities, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “groups of people with shared interests who communicate over the Internet through a common platform” ([8], p. 700), because it provides a suitable research context for examining self control in a highly anonymous online environment. The next section compares the self-concept with identity and reviews the existing literature on these constructs. Section 3 develops our research framework and hypotheses. Subsequently, we describe the research methodology used in this study and present the results. We conclude the paper by discussing the contributions of this research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Self-concept and identity

Even though identity and self-concept are sometimes used interchangeably, most psychology literature converges on a hierarchy wherein identity is nested within the more inclusive construct of selfconcept [67]. Oyserman et al. [69] de<sup>fi</sup>ned self-concept as “cognitive structures that can include content, attitudes, or evaluative judgments and are used to make sense of the world, focus attention on one’s goals, and protect one’s sense of a basic worth” (p. 72). In particular, Rosenberg [85] considers self-concept as consisting of three broad classes of individual attributes, i.e., self-referring dispositions, physical characteristics, and identities. Self-referring dispositions refer to the abstract categories that one develops throughout one’s life courses and uses to shape one’s response tendencies (e.g., attitudes such as liberalism and values such as patriotism) [67]. As can be seen from the above explanation, self-concept includes components that might not be part of one’s identity.

Further, identities are the characteristics, social relations, and roles that de<sup>fi</sup>ne who one is [66]. Accordingly, online identities refer to a set of idiosyncratic personality characteristics that a person has in an online space [36]. Identity theorists suggest a strong social component in identities in that they are located at the intersection of position and role-playing in social contexts. Identities are situated, pragmatic, and attuned to the a<sup>f</sup>ordances and constraints of the current context. In other words, an identity is formulated “through a complex interplay of cognitive, a<sup>f</sup>ective, and social interaction processes, occurring within particular cultural and local contexts” ([102], p. 309). Thus, identity theories link an individual to the social world by de<sup>fi</sup>ning the self in terms of various identities (e.g., role based and group based) [34]. By contrast, self-concepts exist outside particular social contexts and social structures, and they are quite stable.

Identities provide a meaning-producing lens and direct attention to speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of the immediate context [65]. Subsequently, the range of contextual diversity that an individual might experience could trigger the formation of multiple identities on the basis of these different contexts. There are multiple primary sources of identities, including personal identity, role-based identity, and group identity [85], each of which can signify the workings of identities. Personal identity refers to self-ideas abstracted from one’s biographical details and framed in terms of broader social categories (e.g., I am Gold Smith, Lynda Smith’s spouse; I served in Patton’s Third Army during World War II) [96].

By contrast, group identity is derived from being members of social groups and categories. When people de<sup>fi</sup>ne and evaluate themselves in terms of a self-inclusive social category, self-rede<sup>fi</sup>nition occurs through a series of steps such as (1) social categorization of the self, (2) in-group prototyping, (3) contextual salience, and (4) depersonalization [39]. First, in intergroup contexts, the self is categorized as part of the ingroup on the basis of the similarity between a member’s perceptions and actions to those of other group members. Second, meanings and expectations that are associated with group membership cognitively assimilate the self into the in-group prototype [98]. Third, contextual salience indicates the activation of group identity that forms a set of standards guiding members’ behavior in the community [6]. Finally, when the self is immersed in the in-group prototype, that person no longer represents a unique individual (depersonalization) but is rather an embodiment of the group identity [37]. This process is called group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

## 2.2. Self-concept and identity in the existing online anonymity literature

Extant literature on online anonymity is inconclusive mainly because of its biased treatment of the self-concept and related identities. First, personal identity has not received due attention in prior studies, as both classical deindividuation theory and SIDE focus on decreased in-<sup>fl</sup>uence of personal identity in anonymous environments. In particular, the classical deindividuation theory emphasizes that cues of the lack of accountability are a main cause of personal identity loss, whereas SIDE asserts that group identity controls human behavior at the expense of personal identity consequent to reduced self-awareness. However, personal identity is the most basic level of self-categorization and the basis for a person’s other identities [36]. McCall and Simmons [58] emphasized the central role of personal identity, namely, that “if an individual could not be recognized from one occasion to another as the same person, no stable social relationships could be constructed” (p. 65).

Furthermore, consequent to belonging to multiple social categories, one may have multiple social identities, which in turn might impose inconsistent demands that sometimes con<sup>fl</sup>ict with one’s personal identity [48]. Personal identity must then resolve identity con<sup>fl</sup>icts cognitively by ordering, separating, or bu<sup>f</sup>ering identities [2]. Thus, future research needs to empirically examine whether these identities can concurrently operate at any given time.

Second, regarding the application of the self-concept, the classical deindividuation theory only looks at a partial aspect of the self-concept, i.e., the public self, in explaining a state of depersonalization [108]. However, when presenting themselves to others, people can take themselves to be the object of thought and be aware of traits, feelings, and behaviors. Prentice-Dunn and Rogers [81] di<sup>f</sup>erentiated selfawareness into two domains: the public self and the private self. Tra <sup>fi</sup>mow et al. [97] explained that “the private self includes cognitions that involve traits, states, or behaviors (e.g., I am honest) […] the public self includes cognitions about how some generalized other views of the person (e.g., people think I am honest)” (p. 649). Public self awareness refers to the attentiveness to one’s self as a social object, whereas private self-awareness refers to the attentiveness to internal aspects of one’s self [27]. At any given moment, people can pay more attention to the private self or the public self [19].

Based on the above arguments, we assert that human behavior can be controlled by not only public self-awareness but also private self awareness [90]. In particular, a person evaluates current behavior by referring to internal standards and values (the private self). If discrepancies occur between internal standards and behavior, an individual can change their behavior to create a new balance. Existing research on online anonymity has supported the self-awareness theory, thus <sup>fi</sup>nding that public self-awareness can be absent in an anonymous environment but that private self-awareness can still control human behavior [41,61]. In sum, private and public self-awareness may operate independently or concurrently to control human behavior in anonymous online environments.

## 2.3. Operationalization of anonymity in existing studies

Extant research on online anonymity has limitations in the oper ationalization of the anonymity and research contexts. Some studies have operationalized it as bidirectional visual anonymity (i.e., without visual cues such as facial expressions and body language) that obscures interpersonal di<sup>f</sup>erences [31,35,47,68,71,78,105]. However, Christensen [15] holds that “anonymity does not necessarily mean that one person is not visible to others[;] it is only necessary that others are unable to identify that person as an individual” (p. 3044). Oper ationalizing anonymity as visual anonymity, many studies have re cruited experimental participants from the same social category, such as undergraduate students registered in the same course at the same university (e.g., [10,106]), members attending the same church (e.g., [11]), or employees working for the same <sup>fi</sup>rm [62,100]. Then, they used CMC environments to secure visual anonymity in their research contexts. However, when participants know that they are from the same social group, their preformed group identity (e.g., the same <sup>fi</sup>rm employees, the same church membership, or the same university attended)

could confound their behavior during the experiment. In acknowledging that other members are from the same social group, an individual could become aware of their public self, not to mention their private self, and not engage in behaviors disapproved by others, regardless of personal visibility. In other words, even though individuating information is not exchanged and others are not visible, the research context is not highly anonymous and is thus inappropriate for testing anonymity’s e<sup>f</sup>ect on human behavior.

In another stream of research, early IS studies on online anonymity were conducted in the context of group decision support systems (GDSS) (e.g., [18,73]). A decision support system (DSS) is an organizational computer-based application that collects, organizes, and analyzes business data to facilitate quality decision-making for management, operations, and planning. A primary objective of DSS is to provide decision support for problems within an organization where an organizational hierarchy outlines clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned job roles and responsibilities. As an extended application of DSS, GDSS can advance the promotion of participation, help streamline group communications, and foster learning. However, GDSS applications do not provide appropriate environments for testing the impact of anonymity because employee behavior would be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their preformed organizational identity (e.g., the same <sup>fi</sup>rm identity). GDSS participants may not engage in behaviors disapproved by others because the level of anonymity is very low, if not completely absent, although they are not physically visible. Hence, early IS studies can also be criticized regarding the inappropriate operationalization of online anonymity.

## 3. Research model and hypothesis development

In this research, the model includes argument quality as the dependent variable because it represents an important consequence of behavior control in anonymous online discussion communities. Some researchers (e.g., [53]) argue that the nature of content <sup>fl</sup>ows determines the value of an online community. In an online discussion community context, network content is the digital content contributed by users, which may provide information, in<sup>fl</sup>uence, or social support [7]. Hollingshead [40] further asserted that in knowledge-oriented discussion communities, argument quality is more important for community health and survival than is merely the quantity of participation. Thus, considering that knowledge collaboration is the primary activity in online discussion communities [21], high-quality arguments constitute valuable inputs to the community in its entirety [60].

Our research model accommodates embodied group identity as well as public and private self-awareness as the “self” variables in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by online anonymity. Embodied group identity refers to the extent to which a group identity is embodied in the self because of group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This study adopts self-awareness as the self-concept (inclusive of personal identity). It refers to the extent to which one is conscious of one’s personal identity and the extent to which one’s selfconcepts are internally uni<sup>fi</sup>ed and congruent with the way in which others perceive that person [29]. Self-awareness contains more of evaluative components (e.g., quality and accuracy) of self-concepts, whereas identity is more of a description of what the sense of self is. Then, self-awareness and identity are closely related in that one’s identity is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by how one is aware of the roles and situation in relation to other people [92]. In addition, following the existing literature [47,49,89], conformance to group norms is modeled as a mediator between embodied group identity/public self-awareness and argument quality in online discussion communities.

## 3.1. Embodied group identity, public/private self-awareness, and online anonymity

An ongoing debate in the literature concerns the question as to whether online anonymity promotes member identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the group [77]. This debate arises from the notion that when a person encounters a strong group identity, she can choose between two alternatives: (1) embody the group identity and in return increase perceptions of belonging or (2) operate self-defense mechanisms and dis sociate from the group identity. Members who agree with the group’s strong identity tend to embody it, whereas those who have reservations regarding the group’s strong identity might dissociate from it.

First, as SIDE proposed, online anonymity could strengthen the effect of group identity by concealing personal features and therefore diminishing the relative importance of interpersonal concerns in favor of the group’s distinct characteristics in its entirety [78]. Consequently, “a group of individuals [is] transformed into a united entity that seems to develop a collective mind” ([73], p. 95). This process is underpinned by self-categorization into a social group generating a sense of in-group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation that drives a member’s perceptions, feelings, and behaviors to conform to in-group prototypes [38]. Thus, online anonymity accentuates e<sup>f</sup>ects of salient group identity together with the dominant normative response with which it is associated [83].

By contrast, when a person does not agree with a strong group identity, they could feel negative emotions such as fear and anxiety. These would trigger the operation of psychological defense mechanisms to restore comfort by attenuating negative emotions [5]. Psychology literature proposes that a key defense mechanism is dissociation, i.e., the ability to detach from adverse emotional states [99,102]. Dis sociation could therefore prevent the activation of group identity in the person’s mind. We do not have an ex ante expectation regarding whether group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation or dissociation has to occur when a person faces a strong group identity because it depends on an individual’s level of agreement with the identity of the group. Thus, we propose the following nondirectional hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 1. Online anonymity a<sup>f</sup>ects embodied group identity.

As discussed above, two types of self-awareness, public and private, are involved in controlling human behavior. Public self-awareness involves paying attention to oneself as a social object and concerns external appearances [81]. Public self-awareness is determined by ac countability cues, or the extent to which people think that they will be held accountable for their actions. In an anonymous environment, others cannot identify an individual, thus limiting accountability. Thus, online anonymity decreases public self-awareness.

Private self-awareness might still in<sup>fl</sup>uence human behavior in anonymous discussion communities. Private self-awareness refers to a focus on internal feelings and standards. However, attentional cues such as group cohesiveness induced by anonymity can decrease private self-awareness when attention is distributed outward, away from the self [78]. In particular, one’s ability to self-regulate through private selfawareness is reduced by heightened responsiveness to environmental cues. Experimental designs in previous studies have employed various methods of reducing private self-awareness, including loud rock music and video games on monitors as distractions [80]. These stimuli attenuate internal self-focus [41]. Thus, both public and private self awareness are decreased because of online anonymity.

Hypothesis 2. Online anonymity decreases public self-awareness.

Hypothesis 3. Online anonymity decreases private self-awareness.

## 3.2. Embodied group identity, public self-awareness, and group norm conformity

Faraj et al. [21] characterized online communities as <sup>fl</sup>uid objects that lack formal role structures, hierarchies, and governance mechan isms. Thus, member behavior tends to be loosely coordinated, volun tary, and self-organizing. In the absence of formal control mechanisms, informal controls—such as group norms—are the main mechanism for controlling member behavior. Cialdini and Trost [16] explained group norms as “rules and standards that are understood by members of a group, and that guide and/or constrain social behavior without the force of laws” (p. 152).

The SIDE model asserts that anonymity increases conformance to group norms through group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation [49]. In particular, if a particular group identity becomes a salient basis for self-conception through group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, the self is assimilated into the perceived in-group norms such that one’s feelings and behaviors are de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of the group norms [94]. In other words, an individual is more likely to engage in a particular behavior if it coincides with the norms of a group membership. This way, the group identity in<sup>fl</sup>uences member behavior through the mediating role of group norms. As empirical evidence for this argument, Spears and Lea [89] found that the anonymity provided by CMC serves to strengthen the e<sup>f</sup>ect of group norms when group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is strong. Postmes et al. [78] also found that embodied group identity mediates the e<sup>f</sup>ect of anonymity on group in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Consequently, group norms are more likely to be followed when an individual has a strong sense of group identity.

Hypothesis 4. Embodied group identity increases group norm conformity.

Individuals conform to group norms for various reasons including self-related reasons [75]. The social in<sup>fl</sup>uence theory asserts that members adhere to group norms to increase their positive feelings and perceptions of belonging [78]. Social in<sup>fl</sup>uence is an internal process stemming from public self-awareness, which motivates group members to care about appearance and social impressions [75]. In other words, when an individual focuses on their public self, their behavior becomes consistent with the rules and standards accepted by other members of the community [24]. Group norm conformity engenders a positive impression of the self, as well as social validation of the self as a group member [98]. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5. Public self-awareness increases group norm conformity.

## 3.3. Group norm conformity, private self-awareness, and argument quality

By representing a set of shared goals and values accepted by group members, group norms provide order and meaning to what might otherwise be an ambiguous or uncertain situation [82]. Studies show that group norms in online communities have a powerful, consistent in<sup>fl</sup>uence on group members' attitudes and behaviors [39]. Group norms implicitly generate consensus among members regarding acceptable community behavior; for example, a norm might require members to make high-quality arguments for the community’s bene<sup>fi</sup>t [104]. Online communities rely heavily on members’ continued involvement in making high-quality arguments for the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the community in its entirety [8]. Thus, developing group norms that encourage members to make quality arguments is important. Furthermore, group norms are formed to protect participants’ interests by ensuring that they receive appropriate attention for contributing and maximizing the value of their knowledge [93].

## Hypothesis 6. Group norm conformity improves the quality of arguments.

Under conditions of self-awareness, people use increased adherence to salient standards to guide their behavior. If individuals attention is focused on the private self, their internal standards as opposed to their social standards are likely to be used to guide behavior [24]. The pri vate self exists outside social structures, and it is quite stable [66]. In other words, a private self-aware person is attuned to what constitutes an appropriate behavior in any current situation regardless of group norms. If a person perceives that their current behavior is inconsistent with their internal standards, their private self-awareness should enhance their motivation to correct this inconsistency [26], thereby leading a self-aware person to conform to correct behavioral standards. In sum, an individual’s self-awareness of internal standards is likely to enhance correct behaviors in terms of personal perceptions, such as making high-quality arguments in online discussion communities in which the individual participates.

Hypothesis 7. Private self-awareness improves the quality of arguments.

To control the confounding e<sup>f</sup>ects of extraneous variables on our dependent variable, the research model includes other variables such as motivation, formal community control, community tenure, and demographic variables (age and gender). Extant research supports the assertion that those variables signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence community outcomes. For example, intrinsic motivation has been a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant driver for message posting and information sharing in the context of opensource software communities [46] and online discussion communities [103]. In addition, social control in online communities, such as a control that discourages inappropriate behavior and manages disputes, has been found to a<sup>f</sup>ect members’ contributions to discussions (e.g., [9]). Other studies on information sharing in online communities (e.g., [14,44]) have considered members’ community tenure as a control variable in addition to demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, and frequency of postings). Thus, we include these extraneous variables as covariates.

## 4. Research methods

## 4.1. Data collection and sample

The data used in this study were collected through a web-based survey conducted in a well-known, fast-growing online discussion community in South Korea: ILBE\_Storage (www.ilbe.com), which averages more than 300,000 unique daily visitors and has hundreds of thousands of members [45]. Many online discussion communities allow high levels of anonymity to encourage active participation, particularly from members with less power or other social-norm constraints. In particular, this community allows members to use multiple online identities that di<sup>f</sup>er from their o<sup>fl</sup>ine identities using pseudonymity, i.e., “a pro<sup>fi</sup>le with a consistent identity within the network, despite no clear connections to any o<sup>fl</sup>ine identity” ([43], p. 289). Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting such a highly anonymous environment, the mean value of anonymity in our sample was 6.06 on a seven-point scale. Approximately 92% of the respondents in our sample reported that perceived anonymity in this community is quite high (greater than 5 on a seven-point scale). Hence, this community is appropriate for studying identity-related issues in an anonymous online environment.

Diverse subjects are discussed in this community, including poli tical, economic, and social issues. Members can express their opinions freely and conduct blunt conversations on popular and sensitive issues in an environment where their identities are unknown. In addition, this community is known for its strong group identity. Shim [88] described ILBE’s characteristics as follows: "ILBE’s two fundamentals are ultraconservatism (i.e., what many regard as extreme ‘right-wingism’) and commitment to absolute freedom of speech. This mix manifests itself a vigorous advocacy of its right-wing views" (p. 166).

On October 20, 2013, with permission from the administration, we posted the description of our survey on the top (billboard section) of the community’s front page, thus providing a survey link for 12 h. To attract a su<sup>fi</sup>cient number of respondents, we announced that random participants would be awarded \$10 in electronic coupons. In all, 1898 online survey responses were received. Of those results, lurkers and incomplete responses were eliminated, thus leaving a <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of 1453 responses. Table 1 shows the pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the <sup>fi</sup>nal sample.

## 4.2. Measurement

To measure our research variables, we adapted existing validated instruments, all of which used multi-item, seven-point Likert scales to the context of this study. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned online anonymity as the perception that one’s self is unidenti<sup>fi</sup>able to others due to the lack of individuating cues [1]. Pinsonneault and Heppel [73] developed a com: prehensive measure of online anonymity containing <sup>fi</sup>ve dimensions: identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, proximity, di<sup>f</sup>used responsibility, con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the system, and knowledge of other group members. Among these dimen sions, we used the measure for the “knowledge of other group members” dimension—the degree to which people know each other—because it <sup>fi</sup>ts our operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition well. Other dimensions were excluded owing to their inconsistencies with our de<sup>fi</sup>nition (e.g., proximity) or irrelevance to our research context (e.g., identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, which was treated as an experimental condition in their study; di<sup>f</sup>used responsibility; and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the system).<sup>1</sup> The “knowledge of other group members” dimension is also in line with other literature. For example, P<sup>fi</sup>tzmann and Hansen [72] departmentalized online anon ymity into two dimensions relevant to this research, unobservability and pseudonymity, on the basis of relationships between senders and messages. Unobservability represents the degree to which there are no connecting cues between senders and messages (information on who creates a certain message). Pseudonymity is the use of pseudonyms as explained above. If a sender uses the same pseudonym consistently, recipients can distinguish the messages through the sender’s pseu donym. However, the level of anonymity that one can maintain in an online community depends on the community’s design choice and/or a member’s personal choice. For example, on 4chan.org, an imagesharing site, no user pro<sup>fi</sup>les exist and anonymous contributions are the norm [51]. In the ILBE\_Storage community, a user can use a consistent pseudonym or change it from time to time by personal choice. On the basis of these various views, we operationalized anonymity as the extent to which people can identify the author of a message in the online discussion community [73].

Embodied group identity was measured as the degree of an individual’s sense of belonging and positive feelings toward the online discussion community [84]. Group norm conformity was operationalized as the extent to which community members conform to group norms, which provide standards for evaluating appropriateness of group members’ behaviors [107]. Public self-awareness refers to the extent to which a person is aware of their public self, whereas private self-awareness refers to the extent to which a person is aware of their private self. Items of public self-awareness were adapted from Pinsonneault and Heppel [73], and items of private self-awareness were adapted from Ben-Artzi [4].

Argument quality was assessed by the self-reported quality of shared information through the following four dimensions: relevance, accuracy, reliability, and timeliness [14]. With regard to control variables, motivation was operationalized as personal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts likely to accrue to individuals who bene<sup>fi</sup>t the community [103]. Formal com munity control refers to ways in which participants’ behaviors are regulated in an online community. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c items were adapted from Butler et al. [9] and McWilliam [59].

## 5. Data analysis and results

The proposed model was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM) supported by partial least squares (PLS), SmartPLS version 3.2.3., which has been widely used in prior research and supports a simultaneous test of the measurement and structural models [13]. Many researchers suggest a two-stage model-building process using SEM [54]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step is to test the measurement model; the second step is to test structural relationships among constructs.

## 5.1. Assessment of the measurement model

We compiled questions from multiple sources and made minor wording changes to original questions to customize the instrument to this research setting; therefore, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was <sup>fi</sup>rst performed on the data to ascertain the integrity of each measure using VARIMAX rotation in SPSS 21.0. Using EFA, we determined whether items were loaded on their intended factors and whether factor loadings were greater than 0.50 [28]. Cross-loaded items and loading values that were lower than the cut-o<sup>f</sup> value of 0.5 were discarded from subsequent analyses (except one item, GNCF4, which had a loading value of 0.495). EFA results are presented in Appendix B (Table B1).

Next, we conducted con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) using PLS. All constructs were modeled to be re<sup>fl</sup>ective because they are indicators in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by latent variables [25]. In the CFA process, we revised the measurement model by eliminating items with standardized loadings lower than the threshold of 0.6 [32] and items with t-values lower than 1.96 [25]. Consequently, four items (ANMT2, CCTL1, PRSA3, and MOTV4) were removed from subsequent analyses.

The convergent validity of the measurement model was evaluated according to item-construct-loading, composite reliability (CR), Cronbach’s alpha, and the average variance extracted (AVE) [25]. The results of the convergent validity testing are presented in Appendix B (Table B2). All standardized factor loadings were greater than the threshold of 0.6 [32], CR and Cronbach’s alpha for all constructs exceeded 0.7, and the AVE for each construct was greater than 0.5, which are the respective recommended threshold values [23,25]. The convergent validity of our measurement was established because all mentioned conditions were met. The discriminant validity of the measurement model was examined by comparing the square root of AVE for each construct to the inter-construct correlations [23]. As illustrated in Table 2, the square root of AVE for each construct was greater than all related inter-construct correlations, thus establishing the discriminant validity of all scales.

We examined variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) scores to assess potential multicollinearity problems among constructs. Resultant VIF scores ranged from 1.012 to 1.345, well below the recommended threshold of 10 [12], thus indicating that multicollinearity was not an issue in this study. Because all questions were answered by a single individual, we also tested the extent of common method bias with Harman’s one-factor test [74]. In this test, the threat of common method bias is high if a single factor accounts for more than 50% of the variance [30]. The results indicated that none of the factors signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly dominated the explanation of variance (with the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential factor accounting for 18.798% of the variance). Other evidence of common method bias includes exceptionally high correlations $( \mathbf { r } > 0 . 9 0 )$ among research variables [70]. The inter-construct correlation matrix (Table 2) does not show any unusually high correlations in our sample. Therefore, we concluded that common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern for this study.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

The structural model was examined using PLS. To test the hypotheses, we examined path coe<sup>fi</sup>cients and statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance (tvalues). The results of the structural model analysis are presented in Fig. 2. As hypothesized, anonymity showed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative asso ciations with public self-awareness (H2, t = –12.441, p = 0.000) and private self-awareness (H3, $t = - 4 . 8 2 8 , p = \ 0 . 0 0 0 )$ . Anonymity also showed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative relationship with embodied group identity $( \mathrm { H 1 } , t = - 2 . 9 2 8 , p = \ 0 . 0 0 3 )$ . Both embodied group identity (H4, t = 13.368, p = 0.000) and public self-awareness $( \mathrm { H } 5 , t = 4 . 3 3 7 , p =$ 0.000) showed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive relationships with group norm conformity, thus explaining 15.3% of its variance. With regard to the e<sup>f</sup>ects on argument quality, group norm conformity $( \mathrm { H } 6 , t = 5 . 1 3 4 , p$ = 0.000) and private self-awareness $( \mathrm { H } 7 , t = 2 . 5 9 7 , p = \ 0 . 0 0 9 )$ had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ects, explaining 21.2% of its variance. Therefore, all hypotheses were supported.

Among the control variables, motivation and formal community control had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive e<sup>f</sup>ects on argument quality. However, members’ community tenure, gender, and age did not have any sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on argument quality.

To further con<sup>fi</sup>rm the validity of the model, we performed formal tests of mediation for group norm conformity, which was suggested by Baron and Kenny [3]. The following three regressions were conducted: Model 1, regressing the mediator (group norm conformity) on each independent variable (embodied group identity and public self-awareness) separately; Model 2, regressing the dependent variable (argument quality) on the independent variable; and Model 3, regressing the dependent variable on both the independent variable and the mediator. The results in Table 3 show that the effect of public self-awareness or argument quality was fully mediated by group norm conformity, whereas that of embodied group identity on argument quality was partially mediated. Although signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of embodied group identity on argument quality was much less in Model 3-2 (path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient = 0.246) than that in Model 2-2 (path coe<sup>fi</sup>cient = 0.340). This implies that “a given mediator [group norm conformity] is indeed potent, albeit not both a necessary and a su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for an e<sup>f</sup>ect to occur” ([3], p.1176, emphasis added). Overall, these mediational analyses provide additional con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the veracity of our research model.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

This study pursued two research objectives regarding online anonymity: (1) investigating whether various aspects of the self-concept concurrently control human behavior in anonymous online commu nities and (2) investigating whether online anonymity leads to group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with or dissociation from online communities with a strong group identity.

For the <sup>fi</sup>rst research objective, this study incorporated three “self” variables on the basis of self-theories. The results showed that embodied group identity and private/public self-awareness concurrently in-<sup>fl</sup>uenced human behavior. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings o<sup>f</sup>er important theoretical contributions to the existing literature on online anonymity. The cur rent literature is not clear on how various online identities are for mulated or how they are interrelated to in<sup>fl</sup>uence online user behavior. In an online discussion community, one can build a unique user pro<sup>fi</sup>le from various sources such as “user-supplied content, content provided by other users, and/or system provided data” ([20], p. 154). This digital pro<sup>fi</sup>le “might represent an individual, an organization, a group … or a role (e.g., community moderator, subject matter expert)” ([43], p. 287). In other words, online identities may consist of multiple facets as in o<sup>fl</sup>ine identities, including personal online identity, role-based online identity, and social online identity. These online identities can be related through a common system of meanings, and these interrelated identities can be salient at the same time as discussed above. A holistic approach to various self-concepts adopted in this research showed that they in<sup>fl</sup>uence online user behavior concurrently under conditions of anonymity.

Conversely, SIDE contrasts the relative salience of personal versus group identity in anonymous online communities and argues that, under conditions of strong group identity, personal identity yields the control of behavior to group identity. However, the salience of group identity does not occur at the expense of personal identity: “social identi<sup>fi</sup>cation is not an all-or-none phenomenon […] the extent to which the individual identi<sup>fi</sup>es with each category is clearly a matter of degree […] such identities tend to be viewed positively inasmuch as the individual vests more of his or her self-conceptions in valued personas” ([2], p. 21, italics original). This line of reasoning di<sup>f</sup>ers from that of SIDE in that personal identity plays a central role in choosing which group identity to activate in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c situation. Our results showed that both private/public self-awareness and group identities are salient si multaneously in online communities.

For the second research objective, our results showed that anonymity in online discussion communities actually decreased embodied group identity rather than increasing it. This result contradicts the prediction of SIDE that online anonymity intensi<sup>fi</sup>es group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation when group identity is relatively salient and calls for further scrutiny because the studied community, ILBE\_Storage, is known for its strong group identity [88], i.e., far-right politics and ultraconservatism.

A plausible explanation can be made with reference to the theory of self-defense mechanisms. We speculate that, for example, when a person espousing moderate rightwing politics experiences the strong group identity of far-right politics, they could experience more or less negative emotions (e.g., fear and anxiety). Then, psychological defense mechanisms (e.g., dissociation) could occur to cope with such negative emotions and restore comfort [5]. In other words, the person who does not fully agree with the strong group identity can easily disassociate themselves from the group to maintain emotional homeostasis. This result implies that SIDE needs to be revised to include the possibility of dissociation instead of group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

This study has certain limitations. First, the data used were collected from one country. Because online discussion communities are a social space, they are most likely subject to cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uences; therefore, it would be interesting to analyze how cultural factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence arguments in this study. Second, this research adopted <sup>fi</sup>eld study as the research method (primary source) for two reasons: (1) the research model is too complex to be tested through laboratory experiments and (2) laboratory experiments can provide an arbitrary setting that represents only limited facets of real-world environments. Future research might incorporate additional <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study. Third, our sample has a skewed distribution for online anonymity, as it was drawn from a highly anonymous group. This distribution limits our ability to test the e<sup>f</sup>ect of low anonymity on identity variables. However, considering our objectives of attempting to explain the e<sup>f</sup>ects of anonymity on “self” variables, this skewed distribution does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impair our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Fourth, this study adopted self-reported measures for all research variables. Upon asking respondents to assess “self” variables, group norm conformity and perceived online anonymity might not cause a serious problem because they represent a person’s internal states. Conversely, argument quality can be measured objectively, for example, using others’ evaluations of a person’s posts (e.g., the number of likes divided by the total number of likes and dislikes). However, it was di<sup>fi</sup>cult to trace a speci<sup>fi</sup>c individual’s comments because most members in this community change their pseudonyms frequently.

As for future research, this study investigated only one type of online community (a discussion forum); however, core aspects of identity activation (e.g., group cohesion, goal clarity, motivation, etc.) might vary in di<sup>f</sup>erent types of online communities. For example, people participate in various online communities with diverse objectives such as making friends, seeking entertainment, professional networking, gaining fame, supporting causes, social shopping, and entering competitions. To evaluate which “self” controls behavior in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c online community, future research could develop a framework to classify these online communities in terms of the core dimensions of identity activation and examine the external validity of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study.

Furthermore, many online communities operate in virtual-world environments. Virtual worlds are computer-based, simulated, persistent environments that support synchronous interaction between users personi<sup>fi</sup>ed as avatars” ([69], p. 2). In such environments, users whose interactions were previously mediated by electronic media (such as email and online messages) now assume a virtual body (i.e., an avatar) to communicate [86]. In other words, virtual worlds require users to present online identities by de<sup>fi</sup>ning who they are online [42]. In this process, they can take on multiple online identities that di<sup>f</sup>er from their o<sup>fl</sup>ine identities. Online identities can represent many facets of the self, including the ideal self or ought self [33], which implies that multiple personal identities must be compared with social identity to determine the relative salience of social identity versus personal iden tity. It would be interesting to investigate these issues of online iden tities in virtual-world environments.

This research also produced some practical implications. Online anonymity decreases group identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and public/private self awareness in online discussion communities. Online community ad ministrators should keep this in mind when they set guidelines fo online anonymity. Online anonymity can be considered to be a continuum from nearly complete anonymity (e.g., not very meaningful pseudonyms) to close-to-real identity (e.g., medical doctor Smith) [50].

If an online community is equipped with technical facilities for <sup>fl</sup>exible identity construction, administrators may establish anonymity policies detailing acceptable ranges of online anonymity while considering both community objectives and the motivations of individual members. For example, some social media platforms (e.g., Snapchat) do not allow others to associate content with a user’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le by automatically deleting that content after a short while, whereas other platforms (e.g., Instagram) do allow others to link content to the pro<sup>fi</sup>le [43]. Then, community members may experiment with di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of online anonymity on the basis of their motivations.

Argument quality is another important factor that determines the value of an online community [53]. Our results showed that conformity to group norms is a mediating factor in the relationship between argument quality and embodied group identity/public self-awareness. To encourage participants to make high-quality arguments, online communities may need to develop group norms that reward quality arguments. For example, they could adopt a scoring system in which people vote on activities that bene<sup>fi</sup>t the community so that the community could then recognize good citizens of the month and provide them with certain privileges.
