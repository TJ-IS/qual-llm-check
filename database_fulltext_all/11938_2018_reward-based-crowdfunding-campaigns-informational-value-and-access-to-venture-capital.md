# Reward-Based Crowdfunding Campaigns: Informational Value and Access to Venture Capital

Abstract. We consider an entrepreneur who designs a reward-based crowdfunding campaign when the campaign provides a signal about the future demand for the product and subsequent venture capital is needed. We find that both the informativeness of the campaign and considerations related to gaining access to venture capital funding afect the entrepreneur’s choice of campaign instruments, as well as her decision of whether to run a campaign. In particular, entrepreneurs should launch the campaign either when it is highly informative or when it is not informative at all. For relatively low levels of informativeness, but not so low that the venture capitalist (VC) completely ignores the campaign outcome in his funding decision, our study suggests that the entrepreneur might forgo the opportunity of acquiring information via crowdfunding because the benefits of crowdfunding are insuficient to ofset the risk of campaign failure. We also find that the preference of entrepreneurs in favor of crowdfunding is stronger than that of VCs. For backers, we point out that in addition to the risk inherent in new product development, they should be cognizant of the risk that successful campaigns do not guarantee subsequent VC funding. As the use of crowdfunding platforms becomes more prevalent for entrepreneurial projects, our study ofers a deeper understanding of the dual role of reward-based crowdfunding in terms of acquiring demand information and raising capital for early-stage start-ups.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdfunding is a novel method for raising capital to finance new projects, allowing founders of entrepreneurial, cultural, or social projects to solicit funding from many individuals, that is, the crowd, in return for future rewards or equity (Mollick 2014). In reward-based crowdfunding, in exchange for funding, the entrepreneur promises the funder a reward, which often takes the form of the completed product if it is successfully produced (Grant 2013, Steinberg 2012, Snow 2014). By contrast, in equity-based crowdfunding, funding is provided in exchange for an equity stake in the start-up (Belleflamme et al. 2014). Crowdfunding has rapidly gained in popularity, with \$34.4 billion raised across the globe in 2015 and the amount expected to top \$60 billion in 2016 (Hogue 2015). Kickstarter, a leading platform for reward-based crowdfunding worldwide, has launched more than 364,000 crowdfunding campaigns, with 36% successfully funded by more than 13 million individuals.<sup>1</sup>

In recent years, there has been a trend of using reward-based crowdfunding for developing consumer technology products. On Kickstarter, games, technology, and product design are the top three categories in terms of total dollars raised. These projects typically require large amounts of capital to support development and large-scale manufacturing and/or commercialization (Hogg 2014). Given that the amount of capital raised in a typical reward-based crowdfunding campaign is below \$1 million (Caldbeck 2013, Shane 2013), marketing of new consumer products in these categories necessitates subsequent rounds of funding from professional investors, for example, venture capitalists (VCs; Segarra 2013). However, a successful campaign does not guarantee the support of VCs. According to CB Insights, only 9.5% of crowdfunded hardware campaigns receiving at least \$100,000 in campaign funds secured subsequent funding from VCs.<sup>2</sup> Thus, apart from the inherent uncertainty in new product development, the prospect of lack of VC funding in spite of a successful campaign makes technologyrelated projects highly risky. In fact, campaigns of technology projects have the lowest success rate, 19.83%, on

Kickstarter, compared to dancing or theater projects, which enjoy success rates of more than 60%.<sup>3</sup>

Consumers who pledge in reward-based crowdfunding campaigns, whom we refer to as backers, are typically interested in experimenting with early prototypes and in gaining early access to new products. Because backers put down money for a product that has yet to be produced, and because they tend to be drawn from the population of potential consumers, the number of backers and the overall capital raised in the campaign may serve as an early indication of the enthusiasm for the product (Agrawal et al. 2014). This view has been expressed, indeed, by serial entrepreneur Phil Windley, who stated, “The primary reason I like the idea of Kickstarter is that it validates an idea . . . . The money we’ll make is likely small potatoes compared to what we’d raise in a typical funding scenario . . . . But the big payof is the information about the potential market” (Conner 2013). The community of professional investors shares similar views (Cao 2014). In fact, because of the high risk of backing start-ups, VCs often do not invest until a company has validated the market, gained traction, and demonstrated it can execute the project (Grant 2013). In this regard, equity-based crowdfunding might be of limited value because the complex legal issues involved tend to attract professional and accredited investors such as angel investors or VCs (Barnett 2014, Payne 2015), whose behavior is unlikely to be representative of general consumers.

While reward-based crowdfunding campaigns ay provide information on the market potential of the product, running them carries some risk to entrepreneurs. As suggested by industry practice, VCs typically interpret a failed campaign as a grim signal of the potential success of the product and the managerial capabilities of the entrepreneur. As a result, it becomes harder for the entrepreneur to access VC funding after a failed campaign (Strohmeyer 2013, Houssou and Belvisi 2014). This provides the entrepreneur an incentive to set the campaign goal at a low level, because a campaign is deemed successful only when the amount raised in the campaign exceeds this goal. However, because VCs typically run their own market research before making funding decisions, choosing a low goal increases the likelihood that the campaign is successful but the project is not subsequently funded by the VC. In this case, the entrepreneur may have already spent part or all of the campaign funds in early activities of new product development, such as applying for patents. Because of insuficient funding, the entrepreneur will have no choice but to terminate the project, in which case each backer may receive a partial refund, an underdeveloped product, or nothing at all.<sup>4</sup> Thus, a lower goal increases the risk backers face of losing their pledges and receiving little in return, which may discourage them from pledging. This dilemma facing the entrepreneur showcases the interesting research questions that can arise in an environment where crowdfunding campaigns serve as a source of information about future demand and where VC funding is essential for developing and commercializing the full product. Specifically, how should the entrepreneur choose the campaign goal and the pledge level that entitles backers to receive the product if it becomes available? Does the entrepreneur always prefer to run a crowdfunding campaign prior to approaching VC for funding? What is the VC’s preference regarding the entrepreneur’s choice of running a campaign?

To address these questions, we develop a three-stage game. In the first stage, the entrepreneur sets the goal and the pledge level. These two instruments determine the target number, that is, the minimum number of backers required for the campaign to be successful. Once the entrepreneur runs the campaign, the number of backers and the total amount of pledges realize. Following the rule of Kickstarter, if total pledges fall short of the declared goal, the campaign is considered a failure and the entrepreneur does not receive any of the backers’ funds. The entrepreneur may still approach the VC upon campaign failure, but, consistent with industry practice, the likelihood that the VC considers the possibility of funding in this case is quite small. If total pledges exceed the declared goal, the campaign is successful and the entrepreneur receives the funds raised in the campaign. The entrepreneur then uses the funds to further develop the product, while approaching the VC for additional funding. If the VC agrees to consider the possibility of funding, the game proceeds to the second stage, where he conducts independent market research to evaluate the prospects of the project.<sup>5</sup> The VC then uses the outcome of this market research combined with the observation of the campaign to decide on whether to fund the project. If the VC declines to fund, the entrepreneur terminates the project and, in case of campaign success, sends each backer an underdeveloped product or partial refund. If the VC decides in favor of funding, the game proceeds to the third stage, where both parties negotiate on how to split the future profit if the product is successfully commercialized to the mass market. As we consider only reward-based crowdfunding, the term “rewardbased” is omitted in the rest of this paper.

Our study illustrates that both the extent of informativeness of the campaign and considerations related to gaining access to VC funding play important roles in setting the campaign instruments. When the campaign is not informative of future demand so that the VC ignores it in his funding decision, the target number and the goal should be set at a low level to ensure campaign success. When the campaign becomes more informative, the entrepreneur chooses to raise the target number and the goal because a more demanding goal, once reached, demonstrates better prospects for the product, thus supporting higher pledge levels. However, when the level of campaign informativeness is very high, the VC’s decision relies mostly on the campaign outcome, so backers are less concerned about lack of VC funding following a successful campaign, and the entrepreneur might choose to lower the target number and the goal to improve the odds of campaign success.

We identify three potential benefits that crowdfunding ofers the entrepreneur to ofset against her risk of campaign failure. In addition to serving as a source of information regarding future demand, crowdfunding can be used as a vehicle to practice price discrimination between backers and consumers. In addition, the entrepreneur benefits from receiving the entire contributions from backers without sharing them with the VC when the campaign is successful. In the absence of crowdfunding, she receives only a portion of the expected profits from selling the product to backers in the future market. When the campaign and the VC’s market research produce independent signals of future demand, we show that for relatively small projects, running a campaign before approaching the VC is definitely the right choice for the entrepreneur. By contrast, for projects that require large development costs, the entrepreneur’s preference in favor of running a campaign depends on the relative informativeness of the campaign in comparison to the VC’s market research. Surprisingly, the entrepreneur prefers to run the campaign not only when the extent of campaign informativeness is high but also when it is very low. In the latter case, the strict preference originates exclusively from benefits unrelated to the informativeness of the campaign. For relatively low informativeness, but not so low that the VC ignores the number of backers in his funding decision, the entrepreneur might forgo the opportunity of acquiring information via crowdfunding because the benefits of crowdfunding are insuficient to ofset the risk of campaign failure. These results remain qualitatively the same when the campaign signal is (positively) correlated with the VC’s market research outcome, although correlation diminishes the informational value of crowdfunding. Because the only benefit from crowdfunding that accrues to the VC relates to the informativeness of the campaign, we also find that the VC prefers crowdfunding over a smaller region of parameter values than the entrepreneur does.

Our findings are consistent with observations in the industry. Crowdfunding appears to provide valuable information for consumer hardware products, which are relatively easy to evaluate and tend to attract backers who are representative of the future market. For example, after succeeding on Kickstarter or Indiegogo, consumer hardware start-ups such as Scanadu, Formlabs,

Lifx, Romotive, and Canary received VC funding for product development.<sup>6</sup> By contrast, reward-based crowdfunding does not seem to be particularly informative for chemical products or consumer medical devices, which require special skills in assessing their value or are unlikely to attract individuals in their target market to pledge (Grant 2013, Hogg 2014). This unfortunately has been the case for BeActive Brace, a new pressure brace for back pain that its inventor tried to promote through crowdfunding without success. Later he “ditched” crowdfunding after realizing that “his true target audience was not among the backers who frequent Kickstarter or Indiegogo” (Samson 2015). For products that are unlikely to yield valuable information via crowdfunding campaigns, our results indicate that entrepreneurs might either approach VCs directly without running a campaign or, if running a campaign, set a low goal to ensure campaign success.

This is the first paper that examines the dual role of reward-based crowdfunding as a source of information about future demand as well as a mechanism for raising funds by early-stage start-ups. Our study illustrates how considerations related to gaining access to venture capital and to acquiring demand information afect the entrepreneur’s decision regarding the design of the campaign and the decision on whether to utilize this mechanism at all. We also contribute to the general literature on platforms and information systems by exploring the behavior of three types of platform users: backers who pledge funds, entrepreneurs who design the campaigns, and venture capitalists who use the campaign outcomes to make investment decisions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature. We develop the model of crowdfunding in Section 3 and compare it to no crowdfunding in Section 4. Section 5 presents two extensions: when the campaign outcome is correlated with the VC’s market research and when the entrepreneur has private information about the technical risk of the project. We discuss managerial implications and conclude in Section 6.

## 2. Literature Review

The nascent literature on crowdfunding has investigated the problem mostly from an empirical perspective (Ordanini et al. 2011; Agrawal et al. 2014; Mollick 2013, 2014; Ahlers et al. 2015; Colombo et al. 2015; Mollick and Nanda 2016; Burtch et al. 2013, 2015; Roma et al. 2017). Few papers have studied crowdfunding from a theoretical perspective. Belleflamme et al. (2014) compare the profitability of two common forms of crowdfunding: reward-based and equity-based crowdfunding. Hu et al. (2015) show that under crowdfunding, ofering a product line rather than a single product is more likely to be optimal and the quality gap between products is smaller. Bender et al. (2015) show that allowing consumers to pledge can lead to more successful surplus extraction when heterogeneity in the consumer population is suficiently large and when the development cost of the product or the anticipated surplus generated from it is relatively small. We contribute to this literature by examining the design of a reward-based crowdfunding campaign when the campaign generates demand information and subsequent VC funding is essential for developing the full product and commercializing it to the mass market.

Our study is also related to the literature on crowd involvement in the innovation process, including Internet-enabled financing, crowdsourcing, problem solving, and consumer voting (e.g., Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Boudreau et al. 2011, Marinesi and Girotra 2013, Bayus 2013, Huang et al. 2014, Chen et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015, Araman and Caldentey 2016). Similar to consumer voting systems, reward-based crowdfunding can be used as a participative mechanism that enables firms to gather information about consumers’ preferences. Diferent from the literature above, the issue of raising capital is central to crowdfunding, and the entrepreneur already has a well-formulated idea for a new product. As a result, consumers commit with their money rather than simply voting for an innovation. These diferences have important implications in terms of findings. Indeed, in crowd-voting platforms, entrepreneurs who need to make product development decisions always benefit from information obtained via consumer votes. By contrast, we show that considering the dual role of crowdfunding may lead the entrepreneur to consciously forgo the option of obtaining demand signal via the campaign to improve the odds of obtaining funding from the VC.

With a focus on the role of crowdfunding as a mechanism to gather market information, our paper is related to the extensive literature on the economics of information. Starting with the early work of Stigler (1961), Hirshleifer (1971), and Arrow (1972), the role of information in uncertain environments has been studied in a variety of applications, including adverse selection, moral hazard, auctions, and bargaining (Arrow 1984). By showing that it is sometimes better to sidestep the opportunity to obtain demand information via crowdfunding, our work is related to the literature on the advantages and disadvantages of observing more precise information (Rotemberg and Saloner 1990, Vives 1984, Gal-Or 1987, Raju and Roy 2000). In particular, our study is related to the work on the informative value of experimentation, where by manipulating their pricing strategy, firms can learn about the state of the demand while concurrently generating revenues (Aghion et al. 1991, Mirman et al. 1993). Our work difers by analyzing how the extent of informativeness of the crowdfunding campaign influences the campaign instrument design, as well as the entrepreneur’s decision of whether to launch a crowdfunding campaign.

## 3. The Model: Crowdfunding

Consider an entrepreneur with a design for a new product or service who is seeking capital to cover the cost K of developing, producing, and selling the product to the mass market. He decides to launch a crowdfunding campaign, but the funds raised from the crowd are insuficient to cover the entire cost K. Thus, even if the campaign is successful, the entrepreneur still needs to raise the remaining funds from professional investors. There are two groups of potential consumers in the market. The first group consists of hardcore fans with high valuation $v _ { H }$ . They are enthusiastic about the new design, and thus have an incentive to pledge in the campaign to support the development and production of the product.<sup>7</sup> The second group consists of consumers of the potential future market, who have a lower valuation $v _ { L } < v _ { H } ,$ as they tend to value the product less than the fans and will become active only if the product is successfully commercialized. Because the mass market consists mostly of consumers with the lower valuation $v _ { L } ,$ if the product is produced, the entrepreneur expects to sell it at price $v _ { L }$

The size of each group is unknown before the crowdfunding campaign. Let N denote the random number of fans with realization n. For simplicity, we assume a continuous instead of a discrete density for N. In particular, we assume that the marginal distribution of N is uniform on <sup>[</sup>0, N<sup>¯</sup> <sup>]</sup>. Once the entrepreneur runs the campaign, this random variable N realizes. Because crowdfunding requires backers to put down money for a product that has yet to be produced, when a big number is willing to do so, an early indication of enthusiasm and overall higher demand for the product can be inferred. Specifically, we assume that once the campaign reveals the size of the first group (i.e., the number of backers), this number can be used to infer the size of the second group (i.e., how large the mass market is).

In this paper, we assume that the entrepreneur, VC, and backers are risk neutral. Individuals who are drawn to campaigns to provide funding for nonexisting products and venture capitalists who consider funding for start-ups on a regular basis are unlikely to exhibit significant risk aversion. In the concluding remarks, we point out the likely efect of risk aversion on our findings. In addition, we assume that all players possess the same information about the uncertainty. This allows us to focus on the main objective of this paper, which is to discern the exact role of crowdfunding as a source of information about future demand. We explore the issue of private information and signaling in Section 5. The model consists of three stages:

Stage 1. The entrepreneur sets the goal G that specifies the minimum amount of funds necessary for the campaign to be considered successful and the pledge level r that entitles backers to receive the product for free if it becomes available.<sup>8</sup> In practice, some campaigns have multiple pledge levels, with a higher level entitling the individual to a more generous reward. Setting multiple levels allows the entrepreneur to more successfully extract surplus from fans if there is some heterogeneity in the population of fans. However, given our objective to focus on the informative value of crowdfunding campaigns when VC funding is needed, we restrict our attention to a homogeneous population of fans, and therefore to a single pledge level. We discuss the implications of heterogeneous fans in the concluding remarks. Empirical evidence also suggests that the majority of fans pledge at the level corresponding to the basic product. For instance, for the game console Ouya, more than 73% of fans pledged at the level that enabled them to receive the basic product for free.

Fans are forward looking when deciding on whether to pledge r in the campaign now or to purchase the product in the future if it is commercialized. Because of our assumption that all fans are identical, they choose the same action either to back the project or not. Let $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } } = G / r$ be the target number of the campaign, that is, the minimum number of backers required to reach the campaign goal. I ${ \mathrm { ~ f ~ } } n < N _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } ,$ then $n r < G$ , that is, the total amount raised in the campaign does not meet the goal and the campaign fails. As is the practice on several crowdfunding websites including Kickstarter, we assume that in this case no funds will be collected from backers and the entrepreneur receives no money from the campaign. Otherwise, if $n \geq N _ { \operatorname* { m i n } }$ the campaign goal is met and each backer contributes his pledge r. Before transferring the campaign proceeds to the entrepreneur, the crowdfunding website $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Kickstarter) subtracts a fee. Without loss of generality, we normalize this fee to zero.

Note that the entrepreneur cannot influence this realization n. She can only influence whether fans pledge in the campaign or not. The manner in which she sets her goal and pledge level is supposed to incentivize each of these fans to pledge, thus revealing the number of backers n. Because fans are identical, they make the same decision when confronted with the campaign instruments. This implies that although the decision of whether or not to pledge is endogenously determined, the realization n does not depend on the entrepreneur’s decision. Indeed, one of the objectives of running the campaign is to observe n, which helps in predicting future demand.

Stage 2. Following the campaign, the entrepreneur approaches a VC to finance the remaining cost for commercializing the product to the mass market. After a successful campaign, the VC is willing to evaluate the possibility of funding the project. Yet if the campaign fails, the VC is less likely to entertain such a possibility because campaign failure not only sends a negative market signal but also suggests poor managerial abilities of the entrepreneur (Houssou and Belvisi 2014,

Strohmeyer 2013). After the campaign fails, with probability ξ the entrepreneur finds a VC who is still willing to evaluate the project. With probability $1 - \xi ,$ , she terminates the project. Our results remain qualitatively similar if the VC may refuse to consider the project even after a successful campaign. For ease of exposition, we normalize this probability to zero.

If the VC considers the possibility of funding, he conducts independent market research to evaluate the prospects of the project. We assume that the outcome of the $\mathrm { V C } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ market research is a discrete random variable X that can take one of two values: a bad outcome $x _ { L } ,$ indicating poor prospects, and a good outcome $x _ { H } ,$ indicating good prospects, where $\bar { 0 } \leq x _ { L } \leq x _ { H } . ^ 9$ The marginal distribution of this random variable is binomial with probabilities $p$ and $1 - p$ that $x _ { L }$ and $x _ { H }$ realize, respectively. Both the VC and entrepreneur can observe the realization of X. Without loss of generality, we normalize the market research cost to zero, reflecting the reality that many venture capitalists, by virtue of funding diferent projects, have the infrastructure and resources in place to conduct market research at a relatively low additional cost.

Both the number of campaign backers n and the signal $x _ { i } ~ ( i = H , L )$ may contain valuable information in predicting the future demand y. We introduce the parameter $h$ to designate the informativeness of the campaign relative to the informativeness of the $\mathrm { V C } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ signal and assume that the future demand will be αn with probability h and $\alpha x _ { i } \ ( i = H , L )$ with probability $1 - h .$ . The parameter α is a scale parameter that predicts how observations used to generate the two signals translate to a prediction about the market size.<sup>10</sup> We have normalized the realization of X so that the same scale parameter applies to both signals. In this paper, we consider two extreme cases: complete independence and strong correlation between the two signals N and X. The qualitative results with correlation are similar to the results with the independence assumption. We present the independent case in Sections 3 and 4 and provide details of the correlated case in Section 5.

When the two signals are independent, the above specification implies that the marginal distribution of the future demand $y \ \mathrm { i s ^ { 1 1 } }$

$$
f (y) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} h / \bar {N} & n \in [ 0, \bar {N} ], y = \alpha n, \\ (1 - h) (1 - p) & y = \alpha x _ {H}, \\ (1 - h) p & y = \alpha x _ {L}. \end{array} \right.
$$

After obtaining signals n and $x _ { i } \ ( i = H , L )$ , the VC updates his belief about the future demand so that the posterior distribution of y is

$$
f (y \mid n, x _ {i}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} h & y = \alpha n, \\ 1 - h & y = \alpha x _ {i}, \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

thus leading to the expected size of the future market

$$
E [ y \mid n, x _ {i} ] = \alpha [ h n + (1 - h) x _ {i} ], i = H, L.\tag{2}
$$

This linear model is consistent with Kwark et al. (2014). Following Bates and Granger (1969), who use minimum variance estimation, Kwark et al. (2014) assume that a shopper’s expected quality diference is a linear function of two information sources, where the weight assigned to each source reflects its precision as an information source.

The parameter h reflects the predictive power of the crowdfunding campaign relative to the market research conducted by the VC in forecasting future demand. Therefore, its value depends on whether the preferences of backers represent the preferences of consumers in the mass market. When h is close to 1, the crowdfunding campaign is highly informative. This may be the case, for instance, for some consumer hardware products. However, if the product is too complex to evaluate (e.g., chemical products) or when fans support the venture for reasons unrelated to actual consumption (such as an environmentalist supporting “green” causes), h is likely to be close to 0, implying that the realization n provides little information about future demand.

Many pitfalls may occur in the development process of new products. Hence, even after the VC funds the project, there is still some risk in bringing the product to the market. We model this possibility by assuming that there is a probability 1 <sup>−</sup> z that the entrepreneur will fail to develop the full product and commercialize it to the mass market after being fully funded. In this case, we normalize the residual value of the project to be zero. With probability z, the product is successfully commercialized, in which case the entrepreneur rewards the product to her backers for free and sells to the mass market at unit price $v _ { L }$ . Including a variable unit production cost in the analysis does not afect our findings qualitatively, and therefore we normalize it to zero. We designate by $R ( n , x _ { i } )$ the future expected revenue of the product if it is successfully commercialized. Given the posterior expected market size (2), it follows that $\bar { R ( n , x _ { i } ) } \doteq v _ { L } \alpha [ \bar { h n } + ( 1 - h ) x _ { i } ] , i = H , L$ The VC will fund the project if the posterior expected total profit is nonnegative,<sup>12</sup> namely, if $z R ( n , x _ { i } ) – K \ge 0$ $i = { H , L }$ . If this inequality holds, the VC knows that via negotiations he will be able to reach an agreement with the entrepreneur so that each party shares the positive proceeds. Otherwise, the VC decides against funding.

If the crowdfunding campaign is successful, the entrepreneur receives the campaign fund nr and then spends this money to develop patents or to prepare an early prototype of the product while looking for additional VC funding. If the entrepreneur fails to secure VC funding upon depletion of the campaign fund, then she does not have suficient financial resources to fully develop and commercialize the product. As a result, the entrepreneur terminates the project and provides backers a partial refund or an underdeveloped product that is less valuable than the fully developed product. We model this possibility by assuming that in the absence of VC funding following a successful campaign, the entrepreneur terminates the project and provides each backer an underdeveloped product of value $\beta v _ { H } ,$ where $0 \leq \beta < 1$ and $\beta v _ { H } < \bar { r } . ^ { 1 3 }$ <sup>3</sup>Based on the arguments in Endnote $^ { 4 , }$ it is reasonable to assume that the reward backers receive in case of project failure is of lesser value than the initial pledge r. Note that even after obtaining VC funding, the project might still fail with probability 1 <sup>−</sup> z because of technical risk. In the same vein, we assume that the entrepreneur in this case provides each backer with a reward valued at $\beta v _ { H }$ (assuming a diferent level of reward will not change our results qualitatively). This implies that backers face the risk of partially losing their contributions when the campaign is successful.

Stage 3. If the VC approves funding for the project, we assume that the entrepreneur and VC split the nonnegative future expected value of the project, $[ z R ( n , { \overline { { x } } } _ { i } ) - K ]$ , according to their relative bargaining positions δ and $1 - \delta$ , respectively.<sup>14</sup> If the campaign is successful, the entrepreneur receives nr from backers, but in the case where the project fails for technical reasons, she has the obligation to provide each backer an underdeveloped product of value $\beta { v _ { H } } . ^ { 1 5 }$ Therefore, her overall expected payof is given by $\delta [ z R ( n , x _ { i } ) - K ] \ -$ + $n r - ( 1 - z ) \beta v _ { H } n$ . ## 3.1. Case 1: $N _ { { I . } } \leq \bar { N }$ (Observing $x _ { L }$ Is Not Fatal for the Project)

In this case, it is possible for the VC to fund the project in spite of observing a bad outcome in his own market research. This happens when the campaign outcome is suficiently good, that is, $n \geq N _ { L }$ . Fans decide on whether to pledge now or purchase the product if it becomes available in the future. To capture their enthusiasm for the project, we assume that fans derive extra utility from sponsoring the new venture compared to simply consuming the product if it becomes available. Specifically, we assume that each fan, if pledging, derives the utility $v _ { H }$ from consumption. He derives the lower utility $\gamma v _ { H } , 0 \leq \gamma \leq 1 .$ , if he purchases the product later. The extra utility of backers may represent their pride in being the first to identify the great potential of the product and help it become a reality (Gerber and Hui 2013, Ordanini et al. 2011). We assume that even if fans do not pledge, they will still purchase the product if it becomes available; namely, $\gamma v _ { H } - v _ { L } > 0$

Upon observation of the pledge level r and $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ (and thus, the goal G) selected by the entrepreneur, a fan chooses to participate in the campaign if the following inequality holds:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {- (r - \beta v _ {H}) p \frac {N _ {L} - N _ {\mathrm{min}}}{\bar {N}} + (z v _ {H} + (1 - z) \beta v _ {H} - r)} \\ & {\quad . \left[ \frac {(1 - p) (\bar {N} - N _ {\mathrm{min}}) + p (\bar {N} - N _ {L})}{\bar {N}} \right]} \\ & {\quad + z (\gamma v _ {H} - v _ {L}) \bigg [ \xi \frac {(1 - p) (N _ {\mathrm{min}} - \max (N _ {H} , 0))}{\bar {N}} \bigg ]} \end{array}

$$
\begin{array}{r} \geq z (\gamma v _ {H} - v _ {L}) \bigg [ \frac {(1 - p) (\bar {N} - N _ {\mathrm{min}})}{\bar {N}} + \frac {p (\bar {N} - N _ {L})}{\bar {N}} \\ + \xi \frac {(1 - p) (N _ {\mathrm{min}} - \max (N _ {H} , 0))}{\bar {N}} \bigg ]. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

From Lemma 1, it is optimal to set the target number between max $( N _ { H } , 0 )$ and $N _ { L }$ because $\overset { \smile } { N _ { L } } \le \bar { N }$ in Case 1. The right-hand side corresponds to a fan’s expected utility when he simply waits for the product to become available, and in this case, derives the net surplus $\gamma v _ { H } - v _ { L }$ because his consumption benefit is discounted by γ and he pays the market price $v _ { L }$ for it.

The left-hand side of the inequality is the expected utility of a fan who pledges in the campaign.<sup>16</sup> The first term corresponds to the risk of losing his pledge and receiving an underdeveloped product in return. This happens when the campaign is successful $( n \geq N _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } )$ but the project is not funded by the VC because the independent research yields a bad outcome $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $X = x _ { L } )$ and the number of backers in the campaign is insuficient to convince the VC to fund the project $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $n < N _ { L } )$ . The second term corresponds to the fan’s utility when the VC funds the project. This happens when the funds raised in the campaign reach the goal $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $n \geq N _ { \operatorname* { m i n } } )$ and the market research yields either a good or bad outcome, but the number of backers in the campaign is suficiently high $( \mathrm { e . g . } , n \ge N _ { L } \mathrm { i f } X = x _ { L } )$ . Under such circumstances the backer’s net utility is $z v _ { H }$ + $( 1 - z ) \beta v _ { H } - r ,$ his consumption benefit is $v _ { H } ,$ which materializes with probability $z ,$ and $\beta v _ { H } ,$ which materializes with probability $1 - z _ { \mathrm { { . } } }$ , net of his pledge r that is paid in the campaign. The last term corresponds to the case where the campaign fails but the VC decides to fund the project, which happens with probability ξ. Note that this term cancels out in the inequality because it shows up on both sides, suggesting that the probability ξ does not afect the backer’s decision.

From the above inequality, fans will pledge in the campaign only if the threshold $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ satisfies the condition

N _ {\mathrm{min}} \geq \frac {(r - \beta v _ {H}) \bar {N} - z [ (1 - \gamma - \beta) v _ {H} + v _ {L} ] (\bar {N} - p N _ {L})}{r - \beta v _ {H} - z [ (1 - \gamma - \beta) v _ {H} + v _ {L} ] (1 - p)}.\tag{6}

The entrepreneur chooses r and $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ to maximize her expected profit $\pi _ { E } ^ { C } .$ , knowing that the optimal $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ lies in the region specified in Lemma 1 and that fans find it optimal to pledge.

The decline in this risk makes it less costly for the entrepreneur to convince fans to pledge (allows her to raise the pledge level). Conversely, setting $N _ { \mathrm { m i n } }$ closer to $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { m a x } } ( N _ { H } , 0 )$ increases the likelihood of a successful campaign, which implies a higher probability that fans will receive little benefit from their pledges, thus depressing their pledge levels. In the former case, the entrepreneur cannot influence the VC’s decision when $x _ { L }$ realizes, and therefore sets the target number at its lower bound to maximize her prospects for winning the campaign. This results in the highest risk for backers, and, correspondingly, in the lowest pledge level. In the latter case, observing $x _ { L }$ is not necessarily fatal and the VC is very unlikely to observe $x _ { L }$ . Therefore, backers are not particularly concerned, and the entrepreneur can aford to set the target number at the lowest level, $\mathrm { m a x } ( N _ { H } , 0 )$

The expressions derived for the pledge level $r ^ { * }$ are consistent with the reality that the pledge in a crowdfunding campaign typically falls short of the price the product can command in the future market, because backers factor in the dual risk of lack of VC funding and technical failure. Therefore, their pledges r could be (much) lower than the future market price $v _ { L } ,$ in spite of their higher valuation $v _ { H }$ . However, note that when $v _ { H }$ is much bigger than $v _ { L }$ and $\gamma$ is suficiently small, the pledge level may exceed the future selling price in spite of the risk backers face.

The parameter $\gamma$ captures the extent to which the campaign can serve as a price discrimination device. When backers derive a significantly higher benefit from participating in the campaign, namely, when γ is smaller, the entrepreneur can extract via crowdfunding more surplus from backers in comparison to regular consumers. By contrast, when $\gamma = 1$ , backers derive the same consumption benefit regardless of whether or not they pledge in the campaign. In this case, the net revenue the entrepreneur receives from each backer is equal to the expected price she can fetch from him in the future market, and crowdfunding loses its function as a price discrimination device. ## 4. Comparison with No Crowdfunding

In the absence of a campaign, the number of fans is no longer observable, and the best estimate of this number is the prior expected value of the random variable $N ,$ which is equal to ${ \bar { N } } / 2$ . The game has two stages. In Stage 1, the VC decides on whether to fund the project after conducting his market research and the external signal X realizes. If the VC decides against funding, the project terminates. If the VC decides in favor of supporting the project, negotiations between the entrepreneur and the VC take place in Stage 2.

Under no crowdfunding, the total surplus to be split between the parties includes the potential sales to fans who would otherwise have pledged in the campaign. The rules of the negotiations remain as described in the model with crowdfunding. Given the entrepreneur’s and the VC’s bargaining power δ and $1 - \delta ,$ , respectively, δ and $1 - \delta$ are also the shares of the expected total surplus of the new venture that accrue to the parties (assuming that diferent bargaining power under no crowdfunding will not change our results qualitatively). The detailed analysis can be found in the online appendix.

In the absence of a crowdfunding campaign, the VC will never fund the project if the expected size of the fan group is very small $( \mathrm { i . e . } $ , when $\bar { N } < ( 2 \alpha h /$ $( \alpha h + 1 ) ) N _ { H } \overline { { \mathbf { \Omega } } } ^ { }$ . In the intermediate range $( 2 \alpha h / ( \alpha h + 1 ) )$ $N _ { H } \leq \bar { N } \leq ( 2 \alpha h / ( \alpha h + 1 ) ) N _ { L } ,$ with probability $1 - p ,$ the good signal $x _ { H }$ realizes and the VC will fund the project. With probability ${ \boldsymbol { p } } ,$ the bad signal $x _ { L }$ realizes, and the VC will not fund the project. We exclude the range $\bar { N } > ( 2 \alpha h / ( \alpha h + 1 ) ) N _ { L } .$ , because in this range the entrepreneur always receives funding from the VC, which is a very unlikely event.

Running a crowdfunding campaign does not always increase the odds of obtaining funding from the VC. On one hand, running the product design through the fans helps terminate projects that are not promising, thus lowering the probability of VC funding. On the other hand, the availability of campaign funds and a big number of backers may help convince the VC to fund the project. For instance, Pebble Technology’s founder Eric Migicovsky was initially rejected by VC investors, but later received around \$15 million from a VC firm after he was able to “kickstart” around \$10 million from 69,000 people (Kosner 2012, Burns 2013). Indeed, the percentage of hardware start-ups receiving VC funding after successful crowdfunding (i.e., 9.5%) is reported to be higher than the typical funding rate of VCs (i.e., 1%–2%) (Caldbeck 2014).

In comparing the profitability of the two options, it may be worthwhile to understand the advantages and disadvantages of crowdfunding. The main advantage that accrues to both the entrepreneur and the VC is that crowdfunding produces a signal of the future demand for the product and the managerial capabilities of the entrepreneur. This signal helps eliminate projects that are doomed for failure. The main disadvantage for the entrepreneur is that failure to reach the campaign goal might hurt her access to VC funding. However, the entrepreneur benefits from two additional advantages that accrue exclusively to her but not to the VC. The first is that a successful campaign generates funds upfront that the entrepreneur can benefit from even if the VC decides against funding. Specifically, the entrepreneur does not have to split the pledge of fans with the VC, whereas under no crowdfunding she receives only the portion δ of the expected profits from selling the product to them in the future market. The second advantage is that conducting the campaign may serve as a price discrimination device to extract extra surplus from fans, when fans derive more utility from backing the campaign, namely, when γ is relatively small.

The values of δ and γ determine the magnitude of the two additional advantages from crowdfunding that accrue exclusively to the entrepreneur. In particular, when $\gamma = 1 .$ , fans derive the same consumption benefits regardless of whether they contribute in the campaign or simply wait for the product to be commercialized. In this case, the entrepreneur has no opportunity to price discriminate. When $\delta = 1$ , the entrepreneur receives the entire revenue from selling the product in the future market. Therefore, she is indiferent between receiving the pledge from fans upfront or selling to fans in the future market. We start the comparison by setting $\gamma = 1$ and $\delta = 1$ , in which case the two advantages that accrue only to the entrepreneur are eliminated. While this case may not sound realistic, it allows us to focus exclusively on the informative role of crowdfunding and obtain the entrepreneur’s preference when the only potential advantage relates to the signal of future market demand. Also, the results in this special case help shed light on the general case that we will present later.

## 5. Extensions

In this section, we consider two extensions of the model. The first extension is that N and X are strongly correlated, and the second is that the entrepreneur has private information about z. ## 5.2. Private Information of z

We relax the assumption that all of the parameters of the model are common knowledge by assuming that the entrepreneur has private information about the likelihood of technical success of the project, namely, the parameter z. In such an environment, the uninformed parties (backers and VC) use the actions taken by the informed party (the entrepreneur) to draw inferences about the value of z. In particular, the target number chosen by the entrepreneur in the case where she runs a campaign, or her decision to run the campaign, can be used by the backers and the VC to infer the value of z.

In similar models of incomplete information, both pooling and separating equilibria may arise. In the former case, the value of the unknown variable cannot be inferred perfectly because the informed party selects the same actions over a region of diferent possible values of the unknown variable. By contrast, in the latter case, the value of the unknown variable can be perfectly inferred by the uninformed parties, because the informed party chooses a diferent action for every possible realization of the unknown variable. In our environment, we restrict our derivation to $N _ { H } > 0$ so that a separating equilibrium exists if the entrepreneur decides to run a campaign.

We first investigate whether the existence of private information regarding z afects the instruments chosen by the entrepreneur. Our analysis in Online Appendix B.2 illustrates that if Case 2 arises, the entrepreneur has no incentive to distort her target number. By contrast, if Case 1 arises, at the separating equilibrium the entrepreneur may distort her target number. The direction of distortion depends on whether raising pledges in the campaign or gaining access to future profits is more important to the entrepreneur. If raising pledges is more important, the entrepreneur sets a higher target number, and if future profits are more important, she sets a lower target number, than in an environment of common knowledge.

Second, we explore whether the existence of private information changes the preference of the entrepreneur for running a campaign. Because we cannot obtain closed-form solutions for Case 1, we conduct this investigation only for Case 2. In Proposition $^ { 9 , }$ we report that the entrepreneur is more likely to run a campaign when she possesses private information about z than in an environment where z is public information. This is because the decision of the entrepreneur to approach the VC directly is interpreted as a signal that the entrepreneur has a low likelihood of succeeding in the development process.

Proposition 9 (Private Information About z). The entrepreneur is more likely to launch the crowdfunding campaign before approaching the VC when she possesses private information about z than in an environment where the value of z is publicly available information.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Running a reward-based crowdfunding campaign may be extremely valuable, especially for projects that aim at developing new technology-based consumer products, which typically face high market uncertainty and require supplemental capital from professional investors. Indeed, crowdfunding can provide information about the market potential of the product and thus, in case of positive signals from the campaign, convince skeptical VCs to invest in the project. However, campaign failure may adversely afect the entrepreneur’s access to VC capital. Our study provides a number of insights for crowdfunding projects requiring subsequent funding from VCs. For backers, we point out that they should be cognizant of the risk that successful campaigns do not necessarily lead to successfully commercialized products, in which case they lose some of their contribution. For VCs, we suggest that in making their funding decision, they should first assess whether the outcome of the campaign is helpful in predicting the future success of the product. The considerations of the VC and the backers should guide the entrepreneur in choosing her campaign instruments, which depend on the predictive power of the campaign.

Regarding the entrepreneur’s decision of whether to run a crowdfunding campaign before approaching the VC, we identify three main characteristics of the environment, namely, the size of the development cost, the informativeness of the campaign, and the correlation between the demand signal obtained in the campaign and the signal the VC obtains in his own market research. When these two signals are independently distributed, our findings suggest that the entrepreneur should run a campaign when projects are relatively small. However, for technology-based products that require large investments, circumstances may arise under which the entrepreneur should forgo the opportunity to acquire information via crowdfunding by directly approaching the VC for funding. We also find that the entrepreneur has reduced incentives to run a campaign when signals are correlated because the informative value of crowdfunding declines in this case. Finally, when the entrepreneur has private information about the technical risk of the project, we encourage the entrepreneur to use crowdfunding more often than she would if this information were publicly available. This is because when the entrepreneur chooses against running a campaign, the VC may interpret her choice as a signal that she cannot successfully complete the project.

Most of our simplifying assumptions can be relaxed without afecting the results qualitatively. For instance, we assume that all decision makers are risk neutral. If, instead, backers were risk averse, the entrepreneur would have to lower the pledge level to compensate for the risk backers face, and consequently would be less likely to prefer crowdfunding. Similarly, we can change the two-state distribution for the VC’s external signal. Assuming a continuous distribution would generate cumbersome derivations without afecting the tradeofs we identify in the paper.

As we pointed out, relaxing the assumption that the start-up’s bargaining power is not afected by her decision on whether to run a campaign or to approach the VC directly does not change the qualitative results. Indeed, we could assume that under crowdfunding, the bargaining power of the entrepreneur improves in the case of a successful campaign and deteriorates in the case of a failed campaign in comparison with the option of no crowdfunding. In this case, we conjecture that the region of the entrepreneur’s preference in favor of crowdfunding would expand or contract depending on whether the benefit of the enhanced bargaining power following success dominates the disadvantage of the weakened bargaining power following failure. Moreover, our analysis is based on the rule of Kickstarter that allows entrepreneurs to keep the amount pledged only if the campaign goal is met. Platforms such as Indiegogo allow the entrepreneur to keep a portion of the amount pledged even when the campaign goal is not met. This diferent rule reduces the negative consequences of a failed campaign and is likely to induce the entrepreneur to raise the level of the campaign instruments and to prefer crowdfunding.

Consistent with the practice that the majority of backers submit the same pledge that entitles them to receive the basic product, our model considers a homogeneous population of backers. Introducing heterogeneity in the backer population would open the door for the entrepreneur to announce multiple pledge levels. For instance, the campaign might attract some individuals who are not particularly interested in the product itself, but still support the project for altruistic reasons. The level of support of an altruistic backer is typically small (like \$10), but he tends to be nonstrategic and normally expects a very small reward (a thank-you note, for instance). The additional source of revenue from nonstrategic backers would allow the entrepreneur to raise both the pledge level of the strategic backers and the goal. With multiple pledge levels, it is also conceivable that some groups of backers are more representative of the general population in the mass market. The VC can assign diferent weights to the realized number of backers in each group when forecasting future demand.

Our main derivations rely on the assumption that the values of all of the parameters are publicly known. In the extension, we explore the case when the entrepreneur has private information about the likelihood of technical success of the project. Future research could examine more complex information structures and how they afect crowdfunding campaigns. Another important future research direction is to explore consumer learning during crowdfunding or in the mass market, an issue related to the literature on information acquisition via online word of mouth.
