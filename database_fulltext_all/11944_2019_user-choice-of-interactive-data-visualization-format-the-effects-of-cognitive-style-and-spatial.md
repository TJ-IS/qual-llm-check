# User choice of interactive data visualization format: The efects of cognitive style and spatial ability

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Cognitive style Interactive data visualization Spatial ability Visualization format Decision support

## A B S T R A C T

Interactive data visualization has become an important tool for business executives in monitoring key perfor mance indicators, analyzing business process data, and managing individual and organizational performances. One of the key features in interactive data visualization is that users have direct control over how information is presented during the decision-making process. Few studies have examined how users choose visualization formats in such an environment. In this study, we investigate how cognitive style, spatial ability, and task dificulty afect the preference and choice of visualization formats and how the chosen visualization format in turn afects decision accuracy and confidence. Our results show that the choice of visualization format is affected by a user's cognitive style and task dificulty. While decision performances in accuracy and confidence are afected by task dificulty, spatial ability, and visualization format, they are largely not afected by the interactions between visualization format and cognitive style, spatial ability, and task dificulty.

## 1. Introduction

Interactive data visualization has become an important tool for business executives in monitoring key performance indicators, analyzing business process data, and managing individual and organiza tional performances [3,11,24]. It is a core feature in modern self-service business intelligence (BI) systems designed to help executives, managers, and other knowledge workers make better decisions [3,13,40]. While graphics have long been used in early generations of decision support and executive information systems, what makes interactive data visualization diferent is that users can interact with the graph and tables in real time to manipulate the data and its presentations [50]. Users can now interactively filter data, drill up and down to view diferent levels of data granularity, and switch between diferent data representations. Such interactivity is the reason why self-service BI systems are expected to better enhance the cognitive ability of business users and provide better support for fact-based decision making [38,42].

Although Information Systems (IS) research has begun to investigate the efects of interactive data visualization (e.g., [11,38]), most existing studies on data visualization still assume the representations of data in tables or graphs are static. Consequently, research on the efects of information representations (e.g., tables versus graphs) focuses on relationships between information representation formats and task characteristics. For example, the cognitive fit theory suggests that when there is a match between the type of information presented by the system and that required by the task, a user's cognitive process would be augmented by the available information [45,46]. A mismatch would require a user to invest additional cognitive resources to mentally transform information to fit required task representations and thus negatively afect the user performance. Since it is assumed that information representations are static, it would be up to system designers to determine an appropriate representation format based on task characteristics during the design phase. As a result, research has paid little attention to diferences in user characteristics [14]. Even when studies considered user diferences, they were often treated as moderating or control variables [34,36].

With interactive data visualization, users can change the visualization format during the decision-making process. Most BI tools provide a variety of charts and tables and it is relatively easy for users to choose or switch among these visualization formats. For example, the “fast change” feature in QlikView allows users to toggle from a bar chart to a line chart or a table with just one click. Although system designers still play an important role in setting up the initial presentation and visualization options, users have direct control over how they want the information to be represented. Even if the initial representation is not optimal, users would have the opportunity to find an appropriate visualization format that fits with their own individual preferences and task characteristics. Thus, interactive data visualization can potentially enhance the ability of users to choose a more appropriate visualization format for the task at hand. On the other hand, it is also possible that users may switch out of an optimal configuration set up by the designers. Some studies have suggested that users do not always make rational choices when it comes to using decision aids [49]. However, few studies have examined how users would choose visualization for mats in an interactive data visualization environment.

In this study, we examine how users choose a visualization format $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , graphic or table format) and the impact of their choice on decision outcomes. Specifically, we investigate the extent to which cognitive style, spatial ability, and task dificulty afect the preference and choice of visualization formats and how the chosen visualization format afects decision accuracy and confidence.

This study difers from previous research on information presentation formats in two important aspects. First, cognitive style is a multifaceted construct. Many existing studies define cognitive style along the analytic-intuitive dimension, which varies on how much information an individual tends to collect and process when performing tasks $[ 1 4 , 2 3 ]$ An analytical individual tends to rely on gathered information for decision making, while an intuitive individual puts more emphasis on perceptions and gut-feelings [33]. Following the dual coding theory, we conceptualize the cognitive style along the visualizer-verbalizer dimension, which measures an individual's preference for visual or verbal subsystems when performing tasks [29,30]. The visualizer-verbalizer dimension provides a diferent perspective of cognitive style and is perhaps more direct in predicting the preference and choice of visualization formats. Second, the focus of this study is user preference in the choice of visualization format, so participants are not randomly assigned to various fit conditions. Instead, participants are asked to in dicate their preference and choose a format to use for a task, which allows us to compare whether user choices would match the theoretical fits between visualization format and cognitive style, spatial ability, and task dificulty. Our study contributes to the growing literature on data visualization by understanding how users make choices on visualization formats and how their choices afect decision performance.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the literature on data visualization formats and their relationships to cognitive style and spatial ability. We then develop the research model and hypotheses based on the dual coding theory. In section $^ { 4 , }$ the research method will be presented, followed by the results. Finally, we discuss the implications, limitations, and further research directions.

## 2. Literature review

Although the use of data visualization for decision support has been a topic of IS research for decades, the primary focus of this line of research started with the efective design of information representation formats for decision support $[ 1 0 , 1 6 , 4 5 ]$ . One of the most influential theories on this topic is the cognitive fit theory, which suggests that the information representation formats provided by decision aids would be efective only when they match with the mental representations required by the tasks [45]. Hence, task characteristics are the key to understand what kind of mental representations are needed and which information representation formats should be provided to match the requirements. The theory has been applied in a variety of context with diferent task types [46], task complexity [36,37], and task domains [15,34,35,41].

Cognitive fit theory has served well as a general framework for understanding the efects of information presentation formats on task performance. However, there have been repeated calls for more research into the relationships between user characteristics and in formation representation formats [3,11,14,18]. For example, in a review of IS research in representation formats, Kelton, et al. [18] found that most research treated a user's mental representation and its re lationship with the representation format as a black box.

There are several theoretical frameworks in cognitive science literature that help to explain how information representation formats interact with mental models during problem-solving. The distributed representation framework suggests that cognitive tasks can be represented by a combination of external representations, such as tables and graphs, and internal mental models [52,53]. External representations and internal mental models can work together in a complementary fashion to solve problems. However, this is not the only mode of association. The role of external representations may not be just a helper to internal mental models. External representations can directly evoke cognitive and perceptual operations without the internal model. On the other hand, an internal mental model may not be a mirror image of the external representations. In addition, there could be one or more external representations and internal mental models involved in a cognitive task. Consequently, there could be multiple ways to match the external representations and internal mental models in performing cognitive tasks.

Dual coding theory focuses on the internal cognitive processes and suggests that human cognition is governed by two distinct but interconnected subsystems [27]. The symbolic or verbal system deals with language and other linguistic information. It represents verbal information hierarchically and processes it in a serial manner. The sensorimotor or visual system deals with imagery and other nonverbal objects. It represents visual objects in a synchronous manner and processes them simultaneously. The two subsystems are assumed to be functionally and structurally independent in that they store and process visual and verbal information separately and independently. They are also interconnected via associative and referential interconnections so that both systems can work on the same task. Thus, for any given task, one or more verbal and/or visual mental models may be activated based on a function of stimulus variables (e.g., text or graphs as external representations), contextual stimuli (e.g., problem statement or task instructions), and individual characteristic variables (e.g., visual or verbal ability). Dual coding theory has provided an important theoretical framework for understanding how individual characteristics can afect the internal cognitive process in areas such as learning and education [7,43], consumer behavior [17], and multimedia design [20].

Among many of the individual characteristics studied in dual coding theory, two stand out as particularly relevant to interactive data visualization. One is the individual's ability in processing visual information. Spatial ability, which refers to the skills in representing, recalling, and manipulating two- or three- dimensional visual objects, is one of the intellectual traits that has been shown to impact how users interact with information visualization systems $[ 5 , 8 , 2 6 ]$ . Research has shown that there are significant diferences in spatial ability among the general population [22,32]. While there are ongoing debates about the origins and causes of spatial ability diversity, the efects of such di versity on learning and problem solving are well documented. In a longitudinal study of spatial ability in the STEM domain, Wai, et al. [48] found a significant correlation between spatial ability and achievements in the STEM fields among high school students. High spatial ability seemed to be a common psychological attribute among those students who went on to obtain advanced education and degrees in the STEM fields. They argued that spatial ability, in addition to mathematical and verbal ability, should be used to identify intellectually talented students.

Research in information visualization has studied extensively the relationship between spatial ability and the ability to use a variety of visualization artifacts [26]. For example, individuals with high spatial ability generally show better performance in searching for visually arranged information $[ 6 , 4 7 ]$ , understanding new visual representations [5,44], and working with three dimensional and animated visualizations [8,44]. However, not all studies supported a direct positive relationship between spatial ability and task performance. Speier and Morris [37] found that spatial ability had no significant direct efects on subjective mental workload, decision accuracy, or decision time but there was an interaction efect between spatial ability and visual interface on decision accuracy. Similarly, Speier [36] included spatial ability as a control variable in a study of information presentation format and task complexity. Their results showed that the efects of spatial ability on task performance were not significant.

The other relevant individual characteristic suggested by the dual coding theory is cognitive style, which refers to the tendencies and preferences of an individual in acquiring, representing, and processing information [2,9,30]. Cognitive style has been examined along the analytic-intuitive and visual-verbal dimensions. The analytic-intuitive style dimension focuses on how much an individual tends to collect and process information when performing a task [1,33]. On one end of the spectrum, analytical or rational individuals prefer to gather a large amount of information, consider many alternatives, and systematically and logically derive a decision based on the information. At the other end, intuitive individuals prefer to rely on their perceptions and gut feelings in problem-solving.

The analytic cognitive style has been used to explain user behaviors in many existing IS studies [9]. For example, Lu, et al. [23] showed that analytic cognitive style afected how users perceived the usefulness of various DSS models and their potential adoption decisions. Engin and Vetschera [14] suggested that analytic users require detailed information and thus would prefer information to be presented in a tabular format, while intuitive users could benefit from a more holistic view of the information provided by a graphical representation. They examined the efects of matching between cognitive styles (analytic vs. intuitive) and information presentation formats (table vs. bar chart) on perfor mance and found that subjects under the matching conditions, i.e., analytic-table or intuitive-bar chart, produced lower error rates than those under mismatching conditions.

The visual-verbal style dimension classifies individuals along a continuous scale of verbalizer-visualizer based on the preference of visual or verbal subsystems when performing cognitive tasks [29,30]. This dimension of cognitive style has been widely used to explain dif ferent learning styles. Most studies are concerned with how to measure and identify the verbal or visual learners so that learners can be effectively targeted with diferent learning materials [43]. Riding and Watts [31] found that students with verbal cognitive style preferred instruction material with text information whereas those with visual cognitive style preferred instruction material with images. Mayer and Massa [25] showed that the visual-verbal cognitive style was distinct from spatial ability and learning preferences. It was possible to target learners with diferent multimedia lessons and instruction plans based on visual-verbal cognitive style. Koć-Januchta, et al. [21] reported an eye-tracking study in which they investigated how individuals with diferent visual-verbal cognitive styles learned from pictures and texts. They found that visualizers spent more time on pictures, identified relevant information quicker, and performed better on a comprehensive learning test than verbalizers. Although few empirical studies in the IS literature have examined the cognitive style from the visual-verbal dimension perspective, a number of studies call for better understanding of the data visualization process, i.e., the role of cognitive style and other human factors in this process and its impact on the efective design and use of data visualization tools [39,40]. The current study is an attempt to answer such calls.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

This study examines how users choose visualization formats (i.e., graphics ys. table) and consequently how their choices affect decision performance. As presented in Fig. 1, a research model is developed based on dual coding and cognitive fit theories. We postulate that a user's preference and choice of visualization format is determined by her spatial ability and cognitive style, as well as task dificulty. Since a user is free to choose any visualization format, we assume that she would choose the preferred visualization format, and thus the selected and preferred visualization format are the same. Furthermore, decision performance in terms of decision accuracy and confidence is afected not only by spatial ability and task dificulty directly but also by the matches between chosen visualization format and spatial ability, cognitive style, and task dificulty.

Considerable research in information visualization has shown that spatial ability is positively associated with the efective use of graphs and charts [26]. Individuals with high spatial ability are found to be more skilled in extracting information from graphs, recalling detailed information presented in graphs, and making sense of visual patterns. Over time, individuals with high spatial ability may realize that they have the talent and advantage in working with visual objects. Wheeler and Jones [49] found that when users were forced to make a choice between two decision aid features, they tended to pick the one that they perceived as more competent in using it. We expect individuals with high spatial ability would feel more confident in using the graphic visualization format, therefore we hypothesize:

H1. : Users with high spatial ability are more likely to choose a graphic visualization format than those with low spatial ability.

Dual coding theory suggests that human cognition consists of visual and verbal subsystems and each subsystem has its distinct mental representations and activation process [27]. The verbal subsystem takes in language and other linguistic information, represents it hierarchically, and processes it in a serial manner. On the other hand, the visual subsystem takes imagery and other nonverbal objects as input, represents them in a synchronous manner, and processes them simultaneously. Individuals can be classified along the visualizer-verbalizer dimension based on how they use the two subsystems [31]. Visualizers rely more on the visual subsystem when performing cognitive tasks, whereas verbalizers prefer the verbal subsystem more. Since the graphic visualization format represents information in charts and figures which is better aligned with the visual subsystem, a visualizer is more likely to choose the graphic visualization format over the table format. Therefore,

H2. : Users with visualizer cognitive style are more likely to choose a graphic visualization format than those with verbalizer cognitive style.

Users may also choose a visualization format based on task characteristics. One important task characteristic is task dificulty, which can be defined by the amount of information needed, the number of diferent cognitive processes required, and the number of relationships between acquired information and cognitive processes involved in completing the task [4,36]. Task dificulty has been shown to moderate the relationship between visualization format and task performances. For example, Speier [36] found that, for complex tasks, graphic visualization format generated at least as accurate and timely decisions as table visualization format. Because individuals have limited cognitive resources, complex and dificult tasks force individuals to find ways to reduce cognitive load. From a user perspective, a graphic visualization format can present more information in any given space, better demonstrate the relationships among data points, and highlight the magnitude of diferences. Thus, it allows users to access and process more information at any given time. On the other hand, for simpler and less dificult tasks, such as finding a specific value, the table visualization format is better suited because it allows users to quickly look up the information. Therefore,

H3. : Users given a more dificult task are more likely to choose a graphic visualization format than those given a less dificult task.

We focus on two aspects of decision performance: decision accuracy and decision confidence. Decision accuracy measures how close a proposed solution is to the correct answer. For structured problems with known answers, decision accuracy is positively associated with the amount of relevant information considered. A correct answer is more likely to be found when more relevant information is gathered, evaluated, and applied. Decision confidence refers to the degree to which individuals believe that their proposed solution is the correct answer. Decision confidence is also determined by the amount and perceived quality of information used to generate the answers.

Spatial ability is a part of one's analytical skill set and reflects one's overall analytical capabilities. For example, high spatial ability was found to be linked to high achievements in science and technology [48]. Users with high analytical capabilities are more likely to explore more information, evaluate more alternatives, and base their solutions on the information because they not only have the propensity to rely on information and facts but also can process information more eficiently and efectively. Therefore,

H4a. : Users with high spatial ability will produce higher decision accuracy answers than those with low spatial ability.

H4b. : Users with high spatial ability are more confident about their answers than those with low spatial ability.

Task dificulty can negatively afect decision accuracy and confidence. As task dificulty increases, users are required to gather and process more information and relationships. However, given limited cognitive resources, users may be overwhelmed by the information processing requirements and need to skip certain information in order to handle the cognitive load. That would increase the chance that important cues being overlooked and thus lead to less accurate decisions. At the same time, knowing that not all information is being fully examined, users would be less certain about the decision they make. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H5a. : Users performing more dificult tasks will produce less accurate decisions than those performing less dificult tasks.

H5b. : Users performing more dificult tasks will be less confident in the decisions than those performing less dificult tasks.

For each task, when a user chooses a visualization format, it will result in an expected match or mismatch between the visualization format and the task dificulty level and the user's cognitive style and spatial capability. For instance, as we have argued in H1, users with high spatial ability should choose the graphic visualization format to leverage their strength and ability in manipulating visual information presented in the graphics and, conversely, users with low spatial ability should avoid their weakness by relying on the table visualization format. So. there is an expected match when high spatial ability users choose a graphic visualization format or low spatial ability users choose a table visualization format. Otherwise, there would be an expected mismatch. Similarly, there is an expected match when users with visualizer cognitive style choose a graphic format or users with verbalizer cognitive style choose a table format because they are more familiar and comfortable with the corresponding visualization formats. For task dificulty, the graphic visualization format is expected to match with more dificult tasks and the table visualization format is expected to match with less dificult tasks. Any mismatches would negatively afect a user's decision performance because it would require users to spend additional cognitive resources to adiust to the task characteristics, or to work from their weaknesses in spatial ability, or to deal with a con flicting cognitive style. Therefore,

H6a. : Users choosing a visualization format that has an expected match with spatial ability, cognitive style, or task complexity will produce more accurate decisions than those choosing a visualization format that does not have a match.

H6b. : Users choosing a visualization format that has an expected match with spatial ability, cognitive style, or task complexity will be more confident in their decisions than those choosing a visualization format that does not have a match.

## 4. Research methods

The proposed hypotheses were tested in an experimental study, which involved two steps. First, participants were given a task and an overview of two visualization format options. They were asked to indicate their preference and choice of which visualization format they would like use. In the second step, participants were presented detailed information in their chosen format and asked to submit their answers based on the information.

Since the purpose of the study was to examine what afects the initial choice of visualization format and its subsequent decision performance, we focused on information-intensive tasks where the visuali zation format would have more significant efects on decision performance [14]. In an information-intensive task, the information requirements could be high and varied but the processing requirements are relatively low and consistent for the participant pool. In a study examining the efects of visualization and interactivity, Tang, et al. [38] developed and validated a set of financial decision-making questions with two levels of task dificulty. We followed their approach in operationalize and manipulate the task dificulty construct.

Following Tang, et al. [38], the tasks in this study required participants to answer a series of questions based on financial statement information from three fictitious companies. Each task required participants to look up various data points such as cost and revenue for comparisons and simple calculations. Task dificulty was operationalized as the number of information cues need to be acquired and processed for the task. For instance, a less dificult task involved comparing the total revenue across three companies and deciding which company had the highest total revenue in a four-year period. A more dificult task involved determining which company had the greatest changes over several years. In the former task, participants only needed to identify three data points and compare three values. The latter task required participants to estimate the trend over the years for each company and then decide which company had the most significant change, so it would take significantly more cognitive resources to find the answer. There was a total of seven tasks, of which four were deemed as less dificult and three as more dificult.

Like prior studies, the two visualization format options were tables and graphs. In the table format option, financial data, including revenues, expenses, and income before tax, was presented as numbers.

Each table contained data from one company for four years. For the graphic format option, data was presented in bar and line charts. We chose these basic chart types because they are commonly used in business reports and thus do not require special training during the study. In both options, the tables and graphs fit onto one computer screen so that they can be viewed all at once. Screenshots of an example problem are provided in Appendix A.

The user characteristics investigated in our study were cognitive style and spatial ability. The most commonly used measures of verbal-visual cognitive style were the Verbalize-Visualizer Questionnaire (VVQ) [29]. Although the original VVQ consisted of 15 items with true-false responses. Kirby et al. developed an extended version with 20 statements with 10 each in visual and verbal thinking [19]. We adopted the extended version for this study using a 7-point Likert-type scale, ranging from −3 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree). After reversing the responses for consistency, the cognitive style score ranged from −60 to +60 with a positive (negative) score indicating visualizer (verbalizer).

We operationalized spatial ability as “the ability to perceive spatial patterns or to maintain orientation with respect to objects in space” [12]. Following Mayer and Massa [25], a 3-min version of the Card Rotations Test was administered to assess the spatial ability. The spatial ability score was calculated as the sum of correct answers minus the sum of incorrect answers. Given that there were 40 questions, the spatial ability score theoretically ranged from −40 to +40 although it is unlikely that the spatial ability score would be < 0. We defined high (low) spatial ability individuals as those above (below) the average spatial ability score.

For each problem, the preference of visualization format was measured using a 7-point Likert type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly prefer Table) to 7 (strongly prefer Graph). The choice of visualization format is coded as 1 if a graphic option was selected and −1 if a table option was selected. The choice of a visualization format resulted in an expected match or mismatch with personal and task characteristics. There were three matching scores. The cognitive style matching score was coded as 1 if a visualizer chose a graphic format or a verbalizer chose a table format and as 0 otherwise. Similarly, the spatial ability matching score was recorded as 1 if a high spatial ability individual chose a graphic format or a low spatial ability individual chose a table format and as 0 otherwise. Finally, if a graphic format was chosen for a more dificult task or a table format was chosen for a less dificult task, then the task matching score was coded as 1. Otherwise, the task matching score was 0.

The task performances were measured by decision accuracy and decision confidence. Since the tasks were information-intensive tasks with known correct answers, decision accuracy was measured as 1 if the provided answer was correct and 0 otherwise. After providing the answer to each question, participants were asked how confident that their answer was correct on a 0–100 point scale.

The experiment started with participants consenting to the partici pation agreement required by the Institutional Review Board and providing basic demographic information. They were then directed to answer the extended verbalizer-visualizer questionnaire. An example decision task was given to participants to practice so that they could get familiar with the two visualization format options and user interfaces. The example used the same tables and charts but with diferent company data. The actual decision tasks consisted of seven problems (four less dificult and three more dificult). For each problem, participants were first presented with a question and two options of visualization formats. They were asked to indicate their preference and choice of the visualization format. In the next screen, the chosen visualization format was displayed. Participants could provide the answer and their confidence level with respect to the answer. Finally, the 3-min card rotations test was administered.

Two hundred forty-eight business students who were enrolled in several undergraduate and graduate IT and analytics courses at a pri. vate university in the United States participated in the study. Among all the participants, one hundred eleven participants were female (45.2%) and one hundred thirty-six were male (54.8%). The average age was 23.82. All participants have taken basic accounting and finance courses based on their class standing so they had the necessary business analysis skills to perform the tasks. Participants were recruited to voluntarily participate in the study due to their interest in data visualization.

## 5. Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations for all measures are presented in Table 1. All correlations among measures were under 0.50, so multicollinearity was not a significant problem. Since task dificulty was defined based on information processing requirements, we checked the manipulation by comparing the correct response rates between less and more dificult tasks. (Tang, Hess, et al. 2014). The average correct response rates for less dificult tasks (4 tasks) and more dificult tasks (3 tasks) were 0.81 and 0.72, respectively. A t-test showed that the difference in correct response rates between less and more dificult tasks was significant (t = 4.42, p < 0.01) and the manipulation of task difficulty was successful. We also checked the consistency between the preference and the actual choice of visualization format. On a 7-point Likert scale (1 = prefer table and 7 = prefer graph), the average preference scores for selecting table and graph were 1.97 and 6.00, respectively, which indicated that participants consistently chose visualization formats based on their preferences.

Given that participants performed seven tasks during the experiment, we decided to use the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) approach in order to handle the within-subject condition. The GEE approach is an extended generalized linear model that allows for the analysis of repeated measurements [51]. It not only estimates regression coeficients and standard errors but also accounts for any serial correlations among repeated observations where participants can have a diference in pre ferences and choices of visualization format for diferent tasks. We pooled all observations of 248 participants with 7 tasks each into one file (n = 1736). To test our hypotheses, we first analyzed the direct efects of spatial ability, cognitive style, and task dificulty on visualization format preferences with participant age, gender, and class standing included as control variables. The GENMOD procedure in SAS 9.4 was used to per form the analysis and the results are shown in Table 2.

In Model 1, visualization format preference was evaluated with control variables only. None of the control variables were found to have significant efects on the dependent variable. In addition, the actual choice of visualization format was also evaluated as a dependent variable with the same independent variables. The re sults were consistent with the results shown in Table 2.

In the second part of the analysis, we examined the efects of spatial ability, task dificulty, and three types of expected matches on decision outcomes, i.e., decision accuracy and decision confidence. Since deci sion accuracy was measured as either correct or incorrect answers, a binomial distribution with a logit link function was specified in the GENMOD procedure. Table 3 presents the results in three models. Model 1 only included control variables. Class standing, which classified participants as undergraduate or graduate students, was sig nificantly related to decision accuracy. It is not surprising that graduate students, perhaps with more training and experience, tend to outper form undergraduate students in decision accuracy. Cognitive style, spatial ability, and task dificulty were added in Model 2 to test the hypotheses. As shown in Table 3, the high spatial ability was significantly associated with high decision accuracy $( p \textless 0 . 0 1 )$ and task dificulty was negatively related to decision accuracy $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Although not formally hypothesized, we included visualization format as an independent variable in Models 2 and 3. Graphic visualization format was found to be significantly and negatively related to decision accuracy at $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1$ level in both models.

The results for decision confidence are presented in Table 4. Similarly, Model 1 only included control variables. Male participants were found to be significantly more confident about their answers than female participants. The efects of gender diference on decision confidence remained significant in all three models. Model 2 examined the efects of spatial ability and task dificulty on decision confidence. The efect of spatial ability on decision confidence was not significant, so H4b was not supported. ## 6. Discussions and conclusion

One of the purposes of this study is to empirically investigate how users would choose a visualization format for information-intensive decision tasks. The results showed that users tended to choose the graphic visualization format for more dificult tasks and the table format for less difficult tasks, indicating that they did consider task characteristics when making the selection of visualization format. According to the cognitive fit theory, for users to efectively utilize the information presented in a decision aid, the visualization format in the decision aid should fit with the mental model required by the task characteristics. Studies in cognitive fit theory have shown that there is a limitation in the symbolic (table) information representation in support of complex tasks. It is suggested that decision-makers tend to switch from symbolic processes to perceptual processes as tasks become more dificult and complex [36]. Our findings provide empirical support for this proposition. In addition, while previous studies stressed the importance of system designers and their roles in selecting and presenting the information that fits with task characteristics, our results suggest that users, for the most part, can make the appropriate choice. It is perhaps more important to ofer users various visualization choices and provide clear guidance on how to interact with visualization format than to decide on an optimal visualization format during design.

Users' cognitive style was found to have significant efects on how they chose the visualization format. Specifically, users with verbalizer cognitive style were more likely to choose the table format while those with visualizer cognitive style were more likely to use the graphic format. Engin and Vetschera [14] found that a match between cognitive style and information representation format led to better task performance. Although we measured cognitive style diferently, the underlining reason for the observed relationship between cognitive style and visualization format is the same. That is, users develop their cognitive style over time by building unique cognitive resources and expertise. It is reasonable to expect that they would choose the visualization format that matches with their available cognitive resources and expertise.

Given that both task dificulty and cognitive style were found to impact the choice of visualization format, it is important to recognize that, from a user perspective, the visualization format selection is a multicriteria decision. On one hand, they need to choose a visualization format to fit the task characteristics so that required cognitive resources are reduced. On the other hand, the chosen visualization format needs to match the cognitive style in order to take advantage of available cognitive resources. Other factors may be considered as well. Our study demonstrated that it might not be enough to just provide a variety of choices for the users. It would be valuable to provide complete workflow support for the selection process, where users could interactively explore diferent visualization formats before deciding on their choice [28].

Our study shows that spatial ability did not have significant efect on the choice of visualization format, but it had significant efects on decision accuracy. The latter result supports the notion that spatial ability is a part of one's intellectual capability and high spatial ability can lead to better problem-solving performance, e.g., in the STEM fields [48]. In the same study, Wai et al. [48] noted that, unlike math and verbal abilities, students were hardly got evaluated on their spatial ability. An explanation for the lack of significant relationship between spatial ability and visualization format choice might be because many users did not know their own spatial ability level. Therefore, it is not one of the factors in consideration when choosing a visualization format. As spatial ability gets incorporated into general education and get recognized more often, users are likely to understand spatial ability better and apply it more efectively.

One unique aspect of this study was that participants were asked to choose the graphic or the table visualization format for each task. Therefore, participants were not randomly assigned to expected matching-mismatching conditions with respect to cognitive style, spa tial ability, and task dificulty. The resulting breakdowns of observations in various matching conditions are shown in Table 5. The observations of visualization formats matching with task dificulty, cognitive style, or spatial ability were 66.2%, 63.9%, and 60.6% respectively. Although participants chose matching conditions most of the time, there were several reasons why participants' choice of visualization format might not always result in expected matches. First, as discussed earlier, participants might overestimate or underestimate their spatial ability. Second, we defined task dificulty objectively in terms of the amount of information to be acquired and processed. Participants, however, might not be able to accurately estimate the level of task dificulty until they started working on the problem. Third, participants might not be able to choose a visualization format that matches all three variables simultaneously. For instance, when a participant with visualizer cognitive style encountered a less dificult task, no visualization format would have matched both cognitive style and task dificulty. In these cases, users are likely to employ heuristics to decide which variable is more important to match. As shown in Table 5, 28.2% of observations had a match with task dificulty but a mismatch with cognitive style and 25.9% of observations had a match with cognitive style but a mismatch with task dificulty. Only 25.5% of observations had matches with all three variables. Further research is needed to understand the strategies and heuristics behind the user choices.

Our study found that the expected matches between visualization format and cognitive style or spatial ability had no significant efects on decision accuracy or decision confidence. Expected matches between visualization format and task dificulty had no significant impact on task accuracy but they did have an impact on task confidence. The results seemed to confirm that users were able to choose the “best” visualization format for the overall task environment. Even if the chosen visualization format did not match one of the variables, their performance was not afected. This reinforces the notion that we can rely on the users to choose the appropriate visualization format for the task.

The findings of this study contribute to both research and practice. First, our study extends the cognitive fit theory to interactive data visualization by focusing on individual diferences and user choices. In addition to task characteristics, we have shown that users tend to choose a visualization format that fits with their cognitive style. One implication is that the optimal visualization format for a task can vary based on in dividual diferences. The other implication is that users weigh multiple factors in choosing a visualization format that fits with a task. Although sometimes these factors may not agree on the same visualization format, users seem to be able to choose a satisfying option based on heuristics. Further research may identify additional variables and explore how users weigh the relative importance of matches between visualization format and various task and individual characteristics.

Second, our study informs us about how to design interactive business intelligence applications. Instead of foreseeing the optimal visualization formats, our findings suggest that users should be provided with many options so that they can explore and find the visualization formats that fit with their cognitive style and task characteristics. Several design features may be incorporated into an interactive application to facilitate user choices. For example, it should be amply clear to users what options are available to them. It should also be easy for users to switch from one visualization format to another, preferably with just one click. Advanced machine learning techniques may be employed to track individual user's preferences and habits.

Third, our study highlights the need for research in data visualization training. Our results show that both decision accuracy and confidence were lower when the graphic visualization format was used. We believe the lack of training in data visualization is at least part of the reason. Despite being frequent users of graphs and charts, most people do not have formal education or training on data visualization. As a result, data presented in graphs and charts may be interpreted inconsistently and incorrectly. Data visualization should be a significant portion of any data literacy program. Further research is needed to identify the topics and methods to be included in such programs.

There are several limitations to this study. First, business students were used as subjects in this experimental study. Students are often less experienced in the problem domain or with the technology under study than professionals. In this study, the tasks of evaluating financial information were appropriate for this group of senior undergraduate and graduate business students. With respect to interactive data visualization, it can be argued that student subjects should be more comfortable with technology than older generation professionals. Nevertheless, our results should be generalized with caution. Second, the tasks used in the study are information acquisition tasks. As discussed earlier, information acquisition tasks requiring precise numbers as answers put the graphic visualization format at a disadvantage, which may skew the results. Future studies may explore diferent types of tasks. Indeed, one potential benefit of interactive data visualization is its ability in supporting exploratory data analysis where there are no predefined questions or problems. Research is needed to examine how users can gain insights from data through a process of interactions with business intelligence applications.
