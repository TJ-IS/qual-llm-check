# Transitioning to a business rule management service model: Case studies from the property and casualty insurance industry

## A B S T R A C T

As the volume, sources and types of business rules continue to grow, so do the needs of organizations to accurately, consistently and effectively manage rules. Increasingly, firms seek business rules management systems (BRMS) for assistance. Although studies have examined BRMS technical considerations, few have examined management considerations of deploying and managing BRMS projects. This study addresses this gap through examination and comparative analysis of BRMS deployments. Qualitative case study methods are employed and findings suggest a common deployment methodology and emergence of tightly structured services model. Participant’s adoption drivers, consequences of deployment, IT alignment and transitions to IT service-orientations are provided.

## 1. Introduction

Effectively managing business rules across systems, processes and business units are becoming a higher priority in many firms [25]. Organizations have found it necessary to develop ways of managing business rule churn due to new or changed rules from government legislation, business competition, regulatory agencies, industry norms and others. Adding to these challenges include increased web-based and global competition making it essential for firms to continuously monitor and rapidly respond as competitive threats emerge. Furthermore, the sheer number of channels to manage business rules continues to rise, be it legacy, web, wireless systems or otherwise thus necessitating consistent, accurate and synchronized rule updates across families of related systems. When one considers the volatility to which business rules are added or changed in businesses that transcend time zones, seasons, statutory boundaries and channels, the complexity of business rule management grows on an exponential basis.

Fortunately, research and development has been conducted in academia and industry towards the development of automated business rule tools and techniques. For example, rule mining tools sweep through legacy program code, user manuals and other business documents to assist in the discovery and externalization of business rules. Business rule management systems (BRMS) have also been developed to assist with implementing and integrating business rules across information systems. In addition, methodologies have been developed to assist in business rule modeling, authoring, updating and maximizing rule reuse. More recently, studies have been published that advocate the establishment of business rules as a distinct layer in a services orientated architecture (SOA) [11,30]. Collectively, this research and development has begun to pay-off for organizations. Common benefits of effective business rules management have included firm-level agility, rapid rule updates, improved multi-channel management, greater control of business rule updates by the business staff, reduced system development time, in addition to significant improvements in rule consistency, accuracy and reliability [20].

Despite these significant technical orientated developments, little has been published regarding managing business rule projects and more specifically viewing business rules from a service science perspective. For example, how do organizations manage their BRMS development and deployment efforts? How are organizations structuring their support units for BRMS and what is the balance between IT and business staff? What benefits can be gained from viewing business rules from a service science perspective? Business rule management solutions are a quintessential illustration of an intersection between technology, people and business processes. Yet, with so much emphasis towards the technological aspects, we can lose sight of the management of information system considerations. As with many developments in the IT industry, it is the management of the technology and not the technology itself that presents the most significant challenges. Evidence is growing in the business press that this is occurring as it relates to developing and deploying BRMS [21,23].

To address these research questions, this study will examine five implementations of business rules management solutions in different firms from the same industrial group. We examine the underlying business drivers, scope changes, support roles, staffing levels, project management techniques, business–IT alignment and consequences of deployment. Although relevant differences between the firms are noted, the thrust of the findings suggests the use of a common implementation approach of business rule management solutions by the participating firms. This synthesized implementation approach is diagrammed, defined and referred to as the business rules deployment maturity model. The authors contend that this implementation approach is illustrative of the approach that firms may consider as they add business rule (BR) service delivery lines in their IT support units.

The paper is organized as follows. A literature review is presented in Section 2 that reviews key related research streams, including business rules management, application integration, IT alignment and offers a view of business rules management from a service science perspective. Section 3 provides the research methodology and setting and Section 4 outlines key findings from the study. Section 5 discusses the results and provides comparisons of our findings to the literature review. Finally, the study’s conclusions and recommendations for future research are provided in Section 6.

## 2. Literature review

The modern-day understanding of business rule management solutions has their derivation from the artificial intelligence and expert systems arenas [27]. Researchers and developers alike began to realize the practical real-world applications of the technology as a simple rules-based interpreter, led prominently through the RETE algorithm [9], and by the mid to late 1980s the business rules segment began to distinguish itself. In fact, it was during this timeframe that initial development of the now dominant modern-day BRE commercial products began in earnest: Gensym Corporation’s G2 in 1986, Haley Systems, Inc., in 1989, ILOG SA in 1985 and Blaze Advisor<sup>TM</sup> in 1988.

Since these early days, a ground swell of research, development and standards setting has emerged in the business rules management area. The BRMS market is expected to grow to a half billion U.S. dollars in annual product sales with more than 40 vendors worldwide by 2010 [14]. Furthermore, organizations like the Business Rules Group and the Business Rules Community have been formed to provide cross-industry support, lessons learned, enduser forums and conferences. Higher order standard setting organizations have taken notice with the charter of the Rule Interchange Format Working Group by W3C in 2005 and the release of the Semantics of Business Vocabulary and Business Rules (SBVR) standards in 2006 by Object Management Group.

From a research perspective, distinct research streams have emerged that provide insights ranging from BR authoring, engines, rule mining, and others. Fundamentally, a business rule is defined as ‘‘a statement that defines or constrains some aspect of the business. This must be a term or fact (described as a structural assertion), a constraint (described as an action assertion), or a derivation. It is ‘atomic’ in that it cannot be broken down or decomposed further into more detailed business rules. If reduced any further, there would be loss of important information about the business.’’ Halle defines the business rules approach to systems development as that which ‘‘allows the business to automate its own intelligent logic better, as well as to introduce change from within itself and learn better and faster to reach its goals’’ [13].

Business rule authoring is one such stream widely researched, with studies examining rule meta-modeling [36,28], authoring [4], authoring interfaces [12], and several applied rule analyst manuals. Business rule engines (BRE) is another research stream, including BRE effectiveness [15], performance and extensive commercially developed business rule engine literature available from vendors such as ILOG, Blaze, Pega Systems, Mindbox and others. Harvesting and the mining of business rules have also been examined [7], as well as their similarities to knowledge management [22], data mining [26] and artificial intelligence. Collectively these provide significant insights into business rules, but the studies largely focus on technical issues, providing little examination of management of business rule implementations such as support levels, scope management, deployment consequences, and effective project management. Similarly, the underlying business drivers of firms to adopt the business rules approach has been studied, including select case studies for build-to-order, customer order scheduling, but they often ignore management of the technology considerations, including IT to business staff alignment, maturity models, development methods, transitioning to a services-orientated IT support organization and others.

Business–IT alignment, for example, has proven to be an important consideration in BRMS projects. Since legacy systems traditionally have business rules buried directly in their application code, the ownership and control over administering BR updates between the IT and business staff if frequently a point of contention. The execution of BR updates and maintenance has historically required the use of the IT staff over prolonged time periods. In effect, this handed control of BR maintenance to IT since the business staff would be forced to wait days (and sometimes weeks depending on the size/complexity of the program) for the IT staff to make the changes, test, and re-deploy the remediated application. This lag time resulted in the business staff viewing IT as an obstacle in BR maintenance, and would give rise to end-users re-prioritizing, disengaging or even circumventing established BR maintenance procedures for more time-sensitive, but sub-optimal, work around solutions. This loss of control by the business staff and a lengthy BR update process are frequently cited drivers of firms to adopt BRMS. The degree of top management support from both the business and IT staff is an important consideration as well. In a recent survey of CIOs (Chief Information Officers) from 2004 to 2005 through 2008, the percent of CIO organizations that reported directly to the company CEO (Chief Executive Officer) has risen from 40% to 47%, while the level of IT spending by large corporations increased from 4.0 to 6.6 as a percent of annual revenues from 2002 to 2006 [6]. This combination of higher corporate visibility and greater capital responsibility may affect the IT organizations ability to deliver BRMS project success. Studies such as the Business–IT Alignment Maturity Model outlines five levels (ad-hoc, committed, focused, managed to optimized) and numerous metrics (ranging from skills assessments to competence and communication levels) and provides a useful tool for overall measurement of this business–IT staff alignment and maturity [17]. Furthermore, studies have been conducted that provide needed insights into the ideal IT leader, including effective IT governance techniques and approaches [29] could also provide insights into managing this balance of control during BRMS deployments.

Another important consideration is examining the development and systems integration methods utilized by firms when deploying BRMS. According to a recent survey, the top priority of CIOs (Chief Information Officers), has remained much the same during the five years from 2002 to 2006, which is integrating systems and business processes. Case studies that examine the techniques used, organizational transitions, lessons learned and effective practices can certainly provide needed insights. Moreover, if rules reveal and constrain business assertions and BR technology provide an automated means for this business behavior, then the use of BRMS to centrally manage externalized business rules and serve them to the applications in need of the rules (from across the enterprise) offers a prime illustration of integrating applications. Also, the literature from application integration studies can be useful in this context, such as the enterprise application integration (EAI) maturity model with five progressive integration levels beginning with pre-integration to point-to-point, to structural, to process to external integration [31]. This can provide a useful framework for comparing BRMS deployment levels with system integration standards and expectations. An extensive set of measurable critical success factors has also contributed to this model for each of the five EAI stages [19].

A third area of consideration when examining BRMS deployments is the transition to service orientated architecture (SOA) and its’ related impact on IT management and support structures. Intuitively, establishing a central business rules repository as a distinct layer in an SOA environment seems logical. This would permit business rules to be centrally stored, maintained and distributed to the systems that need the information and improve the firm’s ability to consistently manage and control the rules. One researcher has gone so far as to state, ‘‘SOA and BRMS; do both, or don’t bother with either’’ [11]. Also important for this study’s purpose, however, is considerations surrounding the ensuing transformation of a firm’s CIO (Chief Information Officer) support organization as they move further towards service-based IT delivery. This shift from a traditional technology-orientated IT support organization to a service-centric approach poses unique transformational challenges with the people, data, customers and processes [16]. Examples include shifting the IT unit’s focus from application support to services centric, changing IT performance metrics from hardware related (server up-time) to service related (‘customer’ satisfaction levels), or adding a service level management perspective in core infrastructure support as opposed to a strict technology management perspective [2,3]. These are examples of elements that can be examined in a BRMS case study research setting, in addition to the broader notions of viewing BRMS from a services science perspective [5].

Collectively, we can begin viewing business rules management from a service science perspective, where service systems are defined as ‘‘value-creation networks composed of people, technology and organizations. Interventions taken to transform state and coproduce value constitute services’’ [18]. By viewing business rule management in a broader service system context (see Fig. 1), we can identify key actors (service providers and service clients) that transcend traditional organizational boundaries, their forms of responsibility and ownership on the service targets (codified business rules and BR technology) and their forms of value coproduction.

## 3. Research methods

To address the research questions and accomplish the study’s objectives, a case study methodology was utilized in the research design. Case study approaches are appropriate when addressing the how and what type of research questions [35] and are particularly useful in gaining a greater depth of understanding in the phenomena under study. Case study research excels at helping us understand complex issues within its real-life context. They emphasize detailed contextual analysis of a limited number of events or conditions and their relationships. Researchers have used the case study research method for many years across a variety of disciplines. Social scientists, in particular, have made wide use of this qualitative research method to examine contemporary reallife situations and provide the basis for the application of ideas and extension of methods. Case studies have been a popular research method in the area of information systems. This research method has enabled researchers to benefit from the knowledge of practitioners and has resulted in theories that are empirically valid [8]. Finally, case studies help researchers identify good and bad practices of problem–solution process in the field [34].

Consistent with this study’s objectives, we identified different firms from a single industrial group. Although this approach may limit generalizability, it did permit us to gain a greater depth of understanding while increasing the reliability of the findings through corroborating and synthesizing the results across multiple participants. The particular industry sought was one that is known for higher adoption rates of BR solutions and commonly regarded as a leader in the business rules approach. In conducting the study, each of the participating firm’s development approach from inception to enterprise wide deployment of the business rules approach was documented (with respect to the business domain of the targeted implementation area). We examined the firms business rule support levels, roles, project scope, consequences of deployment, key management techniques, while paying particular attention to the business–IT staffing alignment. We intentionally avoided more technical aspects of the development such as system performance, testing procedures, product evaluations and other detailed technical considerations.

Data was collected during the third quarter 2006 through structured interviews via phone and video conference interviews (see Appendix A). Most firms required a minimum of two 3-h working sessions to complete the structured questions. Business documents were also requested and provided by the participating firms including organizational charts, sample reports, industry white papers, detailed job descriptions, and others. A follow-up session was arranged one month after the initial data collection for any remaining Q&As and to solicit the respondents’ validation of their responses. Three members of the research team were present during each session. The research team’s principal investigator was the same individual throughout data collection and led all interview sessions. Typical representatives from the participating firms included BR project leader(s), an IT Director, the CTO (Chief Technology Officer) or CIO (Chief Information Officer), and two end-users. The primary project leader(s) from each of the participating firms who oversaw the BR deployment project from inception through enterprise wide deployment participated in the data collection. Furthermore, four of the five project leaders were still employed by the participating firm. With the firm’s permission, the project manager that was no longer employed by the firm was contacted and agreed to fully participate in the data collection and study.

The insurance industry was chosen as an excellent candidate industry to seek participating firms. The insurance industry has been identified as a leading adopter in business rules management technologies due to the intense competition, increased legislation and multi-channel management strategies [24,33]. After selection of the industry, a list of candidate firms to participate in the case study was prepared based on the firm’s demonstrated willingness to discuss their BR deployment project. Reviews of commercial business publications, industry white papers, vendor recommendations, and company websites were conducted to identify potential firms. The candidate list was then prioritized and ranked based on diversity in firm size, geography, time since BR deployment, and the availability of key actors involved in the BR deployment. The top three firms were solicited and expressed a willingness to participate in the study. After data collection was complete with the top three firms, the next two firms were solicited and agreed to participate in the study. This enabled the research team to compare results and corroborate findings. A total of five firms were solicited and all agreed to participate in the study. Due to the sensitivity of the level of detail shared by participating firms, all company names have been withheld and would provide little value to the content of the discussion.

Analyzing the case study evidence was conducted using a technique referred to as explanation building. As outlined by Yin this technique involves the comparing of findings of an initial case and then systematically integrating results on a case-by-case basis. Appendix A provides the initial topics and questions discussed during interview sessions. Responses from each of the participating organizations were systematically documented and coded (as distinct cases) from one respondent firm to the next. Triangulation was also incorporated into the research design [1]. The use of triangulation in qualitative research can be especially useful towards increasing the study’s validity and reliability [10]. Consistent with the multiple perspectives from multiple observers [32], participants from respondent firms were sought from their IT organization, the business (operations) staff and BR end-users. We insured that at least three members of the research team were present during interview sessions and post-interview meetings were systematically conducted with research team members to compare and consolidate notes. Company documents were used to corroborate (and supplement) data collected during interview sessions, in addition to follow-up informal communications (via email and phone calls) to verify detailed human resource and financial information. The one-month post-interview meetings were used to formally present (and validate) the consolidated data collected with participants of respondent firms and to address any remaining questions. As provided in the findings section, the analysis is categorized into the three larger areas of business–IT alignment, BR development methodology and services-orientated IT structures. For purposes of this study, the BRMS implementation domain for each of the respondents encompassed their property and casualty insurance underwriting systems (in legacy and web environments), including systems for auto, home and related applications.

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. Adoption drivers and respondent firm profiles

The participating organizations are US-based firms that provide property and casualty (P&C) insurance, in addition to other value added services such as travel assistance and financial services. From an organizational structure perspective, the participating firms are vertically structured along lines of business (LOB) (homeowners, automotive, etc.), with distinct horizontal support units for IT, corporate, financial and others, indicated in Table 1. Company A is an insurance and financial services provider throughout the United States and Canada through a traditional brick-n-mortar agency network. Study participants from Company A included their BR project leader, their Chief Technology Officer (CTO) and two end-users. Company A has the longest duration since the time of their BR deployment, which was completed in 1998 (pre-Internet era). Company A coupled their BR implementation with their Y2K (Year 2000) readiness project, since most o their systems needed Y2K remediation. Company A’s primary driver towards adopting the BR approach was cost savings and to maintain rule consistency between various systems. Company B is a financial services and insurance provider to the United States through predominantly Internet (web-based) channels. Study participants from Company B included their BR project leader, an external BR consultant, their Chief Information Officer (CIO) and an end-user. The company had completed their BR project primarily to insure compatibility with their parent corporation who was in the process of purchasing the company. Company C is an insurance provider to the mid-west United States and Canada through a traditional brick-n-mortar agency network. Study participants from Company C included their BR project leader, an IT Director and two end-users. Company C determined the BR approach was essential to combat false and misleading information (including price quotes) about their company in the marketplace. Company D is an insurance and financial services provider throughout the United States through a balance network of traditional brick-nmortar and web-based channels. Study participants from Company D included their BR project leader, their director of financia systems development, an external BR consultant, and three endusers. Company D is the largest participant in the study and determined that the BR approach was essential to remain competitive in the industry and to maintain BR consistency with their balanced, multi-channel strategy. Company E is an insurance provider predominantly in the eastern United States through a traditional brick-n-mortar agency network. Study participants from Company E included their BR project leader. their Chief Information Officer (CIO), an IT vice president and two end-users. This is the smallest participant in the study with the shortest duration since the time of their BR deployment project. Company E determined it was essential to complete the BR project to better manage their rapidly growing business.

## 4.2. Deployment methodology

Despite these underlying motivational differences for the firms to adopt a business rules approach, one of the most significant findings is the similarity to which the participating firms approached their BR development and deployments. For example, all of the participants formed a central business rules group (CBRG) to guide and direct BRMS development, rule maintenance, systems integration and to coordinate implementations. The participants also made use of a business rules repository, that centrally stores active, inactive and in development business rules that have been captured and formally authored by a business rules analyst(s). Furthermore, all participants initiated their BRMS effort with a narrow scope (a single application) and gradually expanded the scope across applications, lines of business and eventually to enterprise wide (for the targeted implementation domain). None of the participants sought to automate 100% of their business rules (nor did they believe it to be possible). Rather they focused on business rules with high volatility, impacting multiple applications and where automation would assist the organization in meeting their business objectives (including their agility needs). [Note: some industry analysts that we interviewed estimate the target volume of rules to automate ranges from 30% to 35% of the organization’s business rules. Others are beginning to advocate a change in the commonly accepted definition of a business rule, such that a BR would be merely considered a ‘‘business policy’’ until such time that the policy could be authored codified and formalized for entry into an application as a business rule. Thus, proponents of the latter definition advocate for 100% automation of an organization’s business rules]. Consistency between the firms was to such a significant degree that we synthesized the findings from across the participants into a business rule deployment maturity model (Fig. 2) and a business rule services model (Fig. 3). The BR deployment maturity model (Fig. 2) is broken into five stages, with comparisons across these dimensions organizationa scope, ownership of the CBRG, structure of the CBRG activities, development and implementation responsibility. With scope along the vertical axis and time across the horizontal axis offers a usefu way of depicting the respondents’ approach, since the firms broadened the scope of their BRMS deployments over time, thus increasing the level of applications integration. The stages are briefly described below.

## 4.2.1. Stage 1

The respondent firms indicate that initial BR projects begin small with a single or narrowly defined group of applications. The IT support unit is the typical ‘‘owner’’ responsible for the project during this initial stage. Although the individuals working on the initial project are considered the firm’s de facto CBRG, they are a loosely coupled, informal group with an emphasis towards developing technological feasibility and compatibility of the particular BRMS solution with the underlying application(s). Likewise, the BRMS implementation and business rules repository are limited in scope to the initial application, with the CBRG taking the lead. This ‘‘starting small’’ insight was one of the most important effective practices emphasized by participating firms. Several respondents noted project failures and costly mistakes that they witnessed from across the industry when a firm began a BRMS initiative on an enterprise wide or large-scale basis.

## 4.2.2. Stage 2

If successful implementations occur and the underlying business drivers are met, the informal CBRG seeks to leverage the approach and success of the initial project by replicating it for additional rule set automation. The informal CBRG becomes less temporary with a larger percentage of employees’ time dedicated to BR activities and they become ‘dotted-line’, best described as a ‘‘virtual CBR group’’ by respondents. The CBRG project leader(s), typically one from IT and one from business staff, will likely be considered full-time dedicated to the project. Although the CBRG begins stage 2 reporting up through the firm’s IT unit, it will conclude stage 2 reporting to the business side once successful BRMS deployments occur and technical feasibility and viability are established. The reasons cited are due to the control needed by the business staff over authoring, implementing and maintaining new and revised business rules. Similarly, the BR repository scope grows in stage 2 expanding from a single to a group of applications (typically a family of related applications).

## 4.2.3. Stage 3

The BR project grows in scope (possibly encompassing an entire LOB) within the firm and beginning to assume an enterprise focus. The core set of individuals who have worked on BR activities since the start become the de facto BR experts in the firm. The CBRG is formally established with dedicated full time leadership, staff and ownership of the team shifting squarely to the business side of the firm. This shift is essential since it enables greater visibility in the firm, easier access to funding, and forces the group to focus on the underlying business value of the BR approach (rather than technological considerations). Stage 3 is also where a split in responsibilities begins between those responsible for BRMS development versus BRMS implementations. The CBRG group begins to handle BRMS implementations through a ‘coordinated’ approach via tracking implementations, leveraging lessons learned, capturing issues, resolutions and coordinating and communicating to the various business rules activities and pockets of expertise from across the firm. The actual BRMS implementation are now the responsibility of the business process owners (BPO) within the LOBs and local support units, with the CBRG acting in an advisory and center of expertise capacity. In effect, IT’s focus begins to shift to technical enablement and infrastructure and the business staff focus begins to shifts to interpretation of the business rules for implementation. At this stage, the initial business case and tentative plans of deploying the business rules approach across the enterprise (where appropriate) are prepared. The business rules repository also assumes a larger enterprise focus. The CBRG is pivotal in this transition of the rule repository through encouraging greater rule re-use and the development of an over-arching business rule meta-model that is synchronized with similar corporate level initiatives such as the enterprise data model, web semantic projects and longer-term corporate strategy and initiatives.

## 4.2.4. Stage 4

This stage is the peak of BR related activity in the firm which respondents often referred to as a ‘‘Business rules factory model’’ for churning out remediated applications that are interoperable with an online repository that centrally stores the firm’s business rules. The model is highlighted in Fig. 3 business rules services model and briefly described below. It identifies three areas within a firm relevant to a business rules approach (the central business rules group, the IT support unit and the local business process owners) and their overlap areas. The model provides high level services and functions that evolve during the maturity and expanded scope of BR deployments across an enterprise. Although some participants had subtle differences in the alignment of low-level activities in one area versus another, the basic structure, intent and functions provided in the model were consistent among all respondents. For example, beginning in the lower right corner of Fig. 3, business process owners (BPO) are responsible for BR creation, retrieval, updating, and deleting of the rules that are within scope of their LOB. This includes responsibility and control of BR authoring, maintaining and activating their segment of the enterprise wide rule repository. Moving counter-clockwise in Fig. 3, the BPOs will seek standards and direction from the CBRG concerning rule architecture, authoring and maintenance of the enterprise wide rule repository The non-overlapping areas of the CBRG provide enterprise-wide BR guidance, liaisons to top management, governance, championing and ‘‘evangelizing’’ of the business rules approach to the firm. The IT unit within CBRG will provide the most technical services including development of business rules tools and standards, API interfaces (for the firm and trading partners), establishing business rule architecture and integration standards, providing interoperability labs, and making key integration decisions. Core BRMS development wil continue to be the combined responsibility of all three organizations (BPO, IT, and CBRG). This combined group will also maintain the BRMS implementation schedule, establish formal mechanisms for knowledge sharing of lessons learned, effective practices and outward liaison to the industry and technology standards setting bodies. Finally, as established in stage 3, implementations will continue to be the responsibility of the BPOs with the assistance from the local IT support unit. The business rules repository begins to grow beyond an enterprise focus and include elements from the larger industrial group.

## 4.2.5. Stage 5

Once the ‘‘factory’’ is built in stage 4, the emphasis shifts to expanding the BR deployments to the next implementation business domain. That is, respondents not only emphasized the importance of starting BR endeavours with a small number of related applications, they also emphasized the importance of managing the project’s scope with a relatively narrow, manageable and logical domain of related business rules. The selection of which domain(s) to begin with, and the prioritization of those domains, is largely determined by the firm’s original adoption drivers for pursuing a business rules approach. Company C, for example, chose P/C price quoting business rules as their initial domain, due to their perceived competitive threat of misinformation across a variety of websites regarding their pricing policies. Thus, Company C captured the BR within this domain, formally authored the rules in their BR repository, identified the systems impacted by these rules and worked through their BR deployment accordingly. Note Company C started with an examination of their key business drivers and then proceeded to author business rules (rather than first focusing on the particular type of technology or tool). Similarly Company A closely tied their wider deployment strategy with their Y2K (Year 2000) remediation schedule and Company B’s deployment priorities were tied to those systems most likely impacted by an ensuing corporate merger. Company E, the smallest respondent firm, had the most aggressive schedule to a full enterprise deployment of all relevant mission critical systems. Other factors that influenced BR domain selection(s) and their prioritization include its’ alignment with similar corporate level initiatives (e.g. knowledge management, business intelligence programs, business process management), firm-level strategic competitive priorities, and compliance with regulatory legislation and standards. Overall, the aim of stage five is to operationalize the ongoing aspects of the BR approach, including identifying and delegating responsibilities, establishing the new ongoing services of the CBRG and in the IT support units and institutionalizing business rules management across the firm.

Overall, all five respondent firms did progress through the five stages depicted in the Business Rules Deployment Maturity Model (see Fig. 2). The model identifies maturity along five dimensions, including organizational scope (from a narrow group of related applications to an enterprise wide focus), ownership (from the IT to the business side), structure (from an initial project team to the formal BR Services Model), development responsibility (from an initial project team to a coordinated model between the CBRG, BPO and IT), and implementation responsibility (from an initial project team to a delegated model to the LOBs and BPOs with CBRG oversight). The key triggers from one stage to the next begin with the various adoption drivers (defined in Section 4.1 and in summarized in Table 1) which drove the formation of an initial project team in stage 1, the transfer of BR ownership from IT to the business staff in stage 2, the launch of a formal Central Business Rules Group in stage 3, the construction of the Business Rules Services Model (a.k.a. the factory) in stage 4 and the institutionalizing integration of an ongoing BR mindset in stage 5.

## 4.3. Consequences of deployment

The respondents indicated favorable consequences as a result of adopting and deploying BRMS. For example, all respondents indicated improvements in managing business rule churn, including improved rule accuracy, reliability, rule consistency across channels and greater control of entire BR process by the business staff. They also unanimously indicated significant reductions in the time required to deploy new rules, updates to rules, as well as cost and time reductions in the development of new systems. Overall, these improvements in the firms’ rule deployment, multi-channel management, and system development efficiencies are essential to remain competitive in today’s global, real-time environment and are consistent with benefits identified elsewhere. Additional consequences emphasized by Company A include their ability to more quickly deploy and manage several web-based systems (versus their competition), since they had completed a major BR project prior to the wide commercialization of the Internet. Company B emphasized their improved ‘marketability’ as a result of BR deployments, since their parent organization was seeking to sell the company and the successful BR deployments were viewed to ease any transition to new owners. Company C emphasized overwhelming improvements in their ability to combat false and misleading information rampant across various websites, and strategic improvements of leveraging data across the firm on a near real-time basis. Company D emphasized improvements in regulatory compliance, tracking and reporting as a result of BR deployments within niche insurance lines of business. Company E emphasized their improved ability to more quickly launch new systems and to enter new statutory markets with their rapidly growing business. In a nutshell, the consequences of effectively deploying BR described above go well beyond basic financial ROI benefits. Many can be considered longer-term and competitive in nature, providing profound strategic advantages for the respondents.

## 4.4. Traditional BR roles and deployment length variation

Two additional noteworthy findings include the business–IT roles and the variations in the deployment length between respondents. First, Table 2 lists the most frequently cited job titles (roles) that emerged during business rule deployments by respondent firms. The roles are split between those in the IT support unit, versus the business staff. From the IT side BR developers, BR tools support services, rule architects, and prototyping/testing specialists were the most frequently cited. From the business side business rule analysts, business rule developers, end-user support to (LOB and BPO), business analysts and domain/LOB experts were the most frequently cited. Overall, 62% of the personnel directly involved in the BRMS initiatives were from the business staff, versus 38% from the IT unit. Company A had the greatest level of business support staff (70%), citing reasons due to the extensive business rule authoring and a larger scoped project compared to other respondents. Furthermore, compared to the time period when Company A’s initial BR deployment was launched, many firms can now reduce their initial BR authoring efforts with the use of pre-built rule templates from BR consulting firms, trading partners, their vertical standards consortium, or other stakeholder firms.

Second, in comparing the time length required to complete BR deployments, some large variations exist, ranging from 30 months for Company A to six months for Company E. Company A’s greater size, volume of applications, and complexity of environment were attributed to its’ longer initial deployment length. When comparing two similarly sized respondents, Companies A and D, however, Company A still required 12 additional months. Further review of this variation revealed two primary causes. First, Company A coupled their BR deployments with their Y2K legacy application remediation project, which would often delay the BR project (since the Y2K took priority). Second, the scope of Company D’s initial BR project was smaller than that of Company A. Since Company D’s key adoption driver was consistency between their web-based price quote systems and their internal legacy systems, they narrowly defined their initial BR deployments to a handful of applications that were affected by these rules. Company A’s initial scope, on the other hand, encompassed a majority of their legacy applications in their P/C insurance segment. Other factors noted by respondents that could reduce the length of initial BR deployments included making use of temporary contractors for the lower-level detailed tasks, hiring consultants experienced in BR deployments, and more research studies such as this present study.

## 5. Discussion

Our discussion extends the results of the literature search survey, in light of the research findings, along three areas of business–IT alignment, BR deployment methods, and transitions to IS service structures.

## 5.1. Business–IT alignment

Prior to the BRMS deployments, the IT to business staff relations in the participating firms was strained. The business staff felt the IT unit had too much control over the business rule update process. In fact, representatives in the participating firms indicated wait times of several days to several weeks for the IT unit to implement, test and ‘go live’ with new or updates to business rules. As one business representative from Company C stated, ‘‘Although the business representatives accused the IT staff of having too much control, it was clear that the IT staff had no desire for this apparent control’’. That is, the IT staff did not intentionally seek to cause excessive delays in the implementation of BR additions/updates, but their hands were tied. The IT staffs were forced to follow strict company procedures surrounding information system remediation efforts or risk placing the entire legacy system (many of which are considered mission critical) in a vulnerable state. The business representatives viewed this as too controlling over the BR update business process, whereas the IT staff viewed this as compliance with corporate security policies and procedures.

After the BRMS implementations, however, the business staffs impression of the IT unit was significantly improved. The contributing factors were identified as follows. First, all respondents aligned their CBRG through the business side of the firms by no later than stage 2 of their deployments. Second, leadership of BRMS development and deployments were directly tied to the business side at multiple levels (from CBRG leadership through local LOB deployments). Third, responsibilities and tasks were assigned, defined, communicated and managed as they emerged throughout deployments (see Fig. 3). Fourth, the level of business staff participating in the project was much greater than that of the IT staff, averaging 1.5 business staff for each IT representative. Collectively, these actions gave the project greater visibility, quicker access to funding, broader access to internal and external resources, and a higher sense of priority, while keeping a check on the firms’ overall IT spend rates. The IT unit was not leading this project, but rather providing a well defined set of services and support throughout the project. Consistent with the Business–IT Alignment Maturity Model, several of the techniques used by respondent firms were anticipated as enablers for firms to move from one level to the next in the model, such as increased communications, competency of IT and the renewed partnership between IT and the Business representatives [17]. As one end-user respondent from Company E indicated, ‘‘The BRMS project was not viewed as another thing that IT was ramming down our throats’’.

## 5.2. Deployment methodology

The BRMS development was led and owned by the business staff much earlier in the development effort, when technical feasibility and compatibility were established, as opposed to waiting until system development was complete. Although BRMS development started small with a narrowly defined group of processes/ systems, the scope grew to multiple systems (and across LOBs) where interoperability and systems integration was the ongoing focus. Rather than a Systems Analyst serving as liaison to IT functional areas, the various IT units are directly aligned with their business counterparts at all levels (CBRG, LOB, development, business unit). New long-term service responsibilities have been established in these IT units (such as those listed on left side in Fig. 3) that must be provided to the business staff who are leading the development effort. As new information systems are developed and launched, many will need some degree of interoperability with the central business rules repository. In effect, a new business rules layer of infrastructure is added that needs to be encompassed in future systems development initiatives and ongoing management.

We can compare the BRMS development approach with the enterprise application integration maturity model (EAI) [31]. Overall, the two approaches align fairly well. BRMS development and EAI are similar in that they both begin with the pre-integration phase (a stand-alone system with extensive manual re-entry) and grow in scope from a point to point system integration solution, to establishing a central service(s) architecture and then expanding the scope of integration across the enterprise and to the external industrial group. Both approaches emphasize the importance of data and technology standards as pivotal towards advancing from one level of integration to the next. Although EAI also emphasizes the role of workflow management towards advancing to the process integration level and beyond (which is not necessarily advocated by business rule experts), the fact remains that business rules are at a lower, more atomic level than workflows. Some have argued that before you can focus on workflow management, you must first get your business rules automated [11,20]. From this standpoint, business rules automation can certainly complement EAI initiatives. EAI advocates extensive preliminary analysis of discovery, assessment, identification and planning towards the inter-relationships of data, systems and emerging technologies towards evaluating a course of action for the firm. These similar steps are taken with business rule discovery and externalization efforts and can act as a conduit to enabling and supplementing EAI efforts.

Despite the complementary nature of BRMS development and the EAI model, the top critical success factors (CSF) associated with each highlight sharp contrasts between the two. In one such study, the top CSF with EAI were determined to be cost/benefit justification of the project, careful implementation planning, making use of standards in data modeling and documentation, developing an effective training plan and effective project leadership [19]. Listed below is the top CSFs identified by the participants in our cases (listed in order of importance).

## 1. Start small and let it grow organically.

2. Remain focused on the BRMS project’s scope. These projects open the doors to many other opportunities such as legacy modernization, process reengineering, BPM integration, cost reductions, and other competitiveness considerations. Although these opportunities may be appropriate as follow-up items, attempting to conduct them in parallel with BRMS could ultimately derail the initial BRMS project and business objectives.

3. The business staff must own the business rule deployment process.

4. The business rules mindset should become part of normal ongoing systems development considerations in an organization.

5. Leverage pre-built data and business rule models whenever possible.

One clear similarity between the two is associated with the use of standard business data models and templates. The building of a central rules repository and the ongoing authoring of business rules are manually intensive and time consuming efforts, but essential for successful systems integration and necessary for meaningful, accurate and consistent data integration. It is interesting to note that none of the respondents in our cases identified financial cost justification of BRMS as a critical factor towards the project’s success. Respondents indicated that the financial benefits and, more importantly, strategic advantages of BRMS were so well understood at the project’s outset, that little attention was paid to this issue after the project’s inception.

We can also examine these critical success factors in a Business Rules Deployment Maturity Model context (Fig. 2). For example, the first ‘‘Start small and let it grow organically’’ and the second ‘‘Remain focused on the BRMS project’s scope’’ factors underpin the staged nature of the maturity model with a controlled (but progressive) project scope over time and a tightly managed organizational structure of BR team(s) with increasing corporate visibility, responsibility and clearly defined services over time. The third critical success factor emphasizes the importance of insuring ownership of BR related initiatives by the business staff (stage 2) and the fourth emphasizes the importance of institutionalizing a BR mindset across the firm (stage 5).

## 5.3. Services orientated IT structure

Three areas raised in the literature review regarding service orientated IT structures included SOA migration, IT centric support structures and viewing BRMS from a services science perspective. First, the case study results revealed there are alternative middleware solutions, rather than a strict SOA solution, such as with the use of a stand-alone BRE, or a ‘‘go direct’ solution where there is no formal BRE but rather a dedicated interface between the rule repository and the application [15]. Although these alternative middleware solutions may not be fully SOA compliant, they can certainly provide firms many of the practical benefits and advantages inherent in a services orientated architecture approach (rapid rule change deployment, automated and centralized rule management and others). Second, as depicted on the left side of Fig. 3, numerous service-centric roles throughout the IT support units emerged in the participating firms, such as the launch of interoperability labs, BRMS testing services, integration solution services, BR tools support, and expert insights into enterprise BR deployment scheduling and technology architecture guidance. As anticipated in the service orientation literature, this service-centric model saw the IT unit transform itself from being labeled the ‘‘bottle-neck’’ in BR deployments, to operating as technology consultants in well defined IT service centers and offering technology expertise to the business operations staff. Although the IT support unit’s will make use of a more complex hybrid organizational structure, it is one that is service based, rather than functionally based along antiquated IT support lines (mainframe support, resource pools, etc.).

Finally, from a service systems perspective (see Fig. 1) the case illustrations in this study focused on BRM implementations in well-defined domains, across a handful of high-value and related applications. But let’s assume organizations began developing performance objectives of automating and refreshing a full 20% of their business rules from across their entire organization on an annual basis. To accomplish such a feat, of course, would require extensive cooperation and participation from throughout the entire BR supply chain. Service providers such as BRMS and BR mining vendors would have to be prepared with a broad suite of ready-made middleware solutions that are scalable (from price and performance perspectives) for the variety of environments to be encountered. In particular, an independent rules repository in conjunction with tools that permit fluid movement from the rules repository to any implementation environment need to be developed. Legislative and regulatory agencies would likely be compelled to move beyond simply passing legislation, and begin issuing new (and changed) legislation in standard BR templates that are compliant with BR authoring norms and make use of standard business-orientated terms and definitions to expedite BR development and facilitate compliance tracking. BR domain SMEs from legal firms, consulting firms or municipal agencies would need to contribute as well by offering business rule interpretation directives, BR codification services, BR compliance and enforcement services. Since the largest manual-intensive effort in the BR supply chain is currently in rule authoring (and the inherent inefficiencies associated with every firm redundantly re-coding every business rule affecting their firm), much of the costs associated with build-out of a BR supply chain could likely be offset by the savings in centralized BR codification alone. Additional value propositions of a BRMS Service System would be profound; consistent rule interpretations, rapid rule deployment (with new and changes to rules), and greater opportunities for BR compliance tracking, enforcement monitoring, and improved BR change control. Furthermore, stream-lining the BR authoring and development phases, would enable a shift to more strategic and highervalue areas in the BR Service System such as closer examination of the cost/benefit (qualitative and quantitative), consequences, social impact and implementation expenses of deploying new and changes to BRs.

## 6. Conclusions

Business rules are emerging to have a profound impact on organizations of all types. As the need for real-time decision making, across multiple channels, on a consistent, accurate and timely basis, the need for firms to utilize a formal business rule management systems will continue to rise. This study closely examined the BRMS development and deployment practices of five firms, with an emphasis towards management of IT considerations. The results strongly suggest the use of a common deployment methodology and business rules services model have emerged within a leading, early adopter industrial group. An effective business–IT alignment with well-defined service-centric roles were found to be important factors contributing to BRMS project success. The consequences of their approach provided substantial benefits to the firms including improved firm-level agility, a return of control over BR updates from IT back to the business staff, improved perceived value of the IT support unit, rapid rule deployments, decreased systems development time, improved statutory/regulatory compliance, and enhanced ability to combat false and misleading information. The BRMS development and deployment practices do closely align with other widely researched MIS methods such as the enterprise application integration literature and the business–IT maturity model.

As noted earlier in the paper, one of the study’s limitations is the use of a single industrial group. Thus readers should be take caution in generalizing the findings beyond this setting. The selected industry, however, is regarded as a leader and early adopter in BRMS and the use of multiple firms from a single industry in case study research of this type is often preferred to enable greater depth of understanding and better control for crossindustry exogenous influences. Second, although the success or failure of the BRMS project was not part of the selection criteria of potential respondent firms, the selection process is likely biased towards firms with successful BRMS implementations. Indeed, closer examination of BR project failures and cross-industry comparisons of BRMS deployments represent possible extensions of this research. Other considerations for future research include more in-depth analysis of an organization’s selection and prioritization process of business domains to implement BRMS across their enterprise, business rule compliance and building upon the business rules view from a services science perspective. As noted in the discussion, there are substantial opportunities for removing inefficiencies out of the BR supply chain while simultaneously enabling significant societal gains.

Dr. Ravi Sen is an associate professor in the departmen of Information and Operations Management at the Mays Business School, Texas A&M University. He received his Ph.D. in Business Administration from the University of Illinois at Urbana, Champaign in 2003. His research interests include economics of electronic commerce, open source software, and software security. He has published in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Communications of the AIS, Electronic Markets, Journal of Electronic Commerce Research, and others.
