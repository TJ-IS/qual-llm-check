## A B S T R A C T

The loss and theft of mobile devices is a growing risk that has yet to be addressed in detail by the IS community. This study extends the literature significantly by examining the roles of key information sources in coping and threat appraisal development. This is accomplished by integrating protection– motivation with social learning theories. Through an email interview (n = 12) and two surveys (pilot, n = 115 and main, n = 339), the study identifies and shows how key information sources (knowledge regarding countermeasures, social influences, and experience of the threat) influence users’ coping and threat appraisals and, ultimately, coping intentions

## 1. Introduction

Given the growing severity and prevalence of information security risks, a growing body of research on users’ intentions to apply security measures to cope with such risks has developed [15,16,25,30,31,59,62]. These works largely focus on the emotional (e.g., fear) and rational (e.g., threat severity and likelihood and ability to engage in coping behavior) drivers of users’ intentions to engage in coping behaviors. These behaviors include, for example, changing passwords frequently, complying with organizational security standards, and installing software against malicious attacks [2]. Protection motivation theory (PMT) addresses the information sources that are used as a basis upon which threat and coping appraisals are developed, and the Information Systems (IS) literature is relatively silent about such factors. An understanding of the factors and learning processes that lead to threat and coping appraisals is important because it (1) extends the current and partial view of the PMT process in the IS literature and (2) can point to useful practical recommendations. Specifically, by manipulating such antecedents of PMT assessments, companies can improve their users’ security compliance or reduce their security deviance.

This study, therefore, extends the literature significantly by examining the roles of key information sources in coping and threat appraisal development.

To accomplish this objective, our study integrates protection motivation theory (PMT, [53]) with the social learning theory (SLT, [55]) aspect of social cognitive theory [8]. This integration relies on the premise that coping and threat appraisals are, at least in part, rational and are based on cognitive deliberation that takes into account various information sources. This assumption is reasonable because PMT explicitly relies on such sources of information in the appraisal process, and research has shown that information sources can be a basis upon which coping assessments are formed [65]. The use of SLT for explaining learning processes is also reasonable because it describes learning processes across situations [55], presumably also in the case of mobile device loss and theft. As per PMT, such processes inform the development of the appraisals that people use as a basis for their coping decisions. The viability of this integrative view was supported in the results from a limited set of email interviews, which we describe in Section 4. The results show that users rely on various sources of information (e.g., personal experiences of the threat, which in many cases serve as wake-up calls, and social influences from colleagues, such as co-workers) when developing threat and coping appraisals. In addition, users learn about coping strategies from magazines, media coverage, IT departments, and colleagues.

Consequently, in our model, we argue that coping and threat appraisals are based, in part, on such information sources. Specifically, we posit that users’ coping appraisals (self-efficacy

and response-efficacy) are based, in part, on accumulated knowledge regarding the response and that threat appraisals (perceived threat) are based on past personal experience of threats and the social influence of peers. Consistent with theories that explain IS coping behaviors, we further argue that social influences can also directly drive coping intentions [40]. The remainder of the model replicates past research and posits that coping and threat appraisals increase coping intentions.

The proposed model is tested in the context of mobile devices and focuses on the threat of device loss and theft. These unique contexts and threats differ from those typically examined in the IS literature. This is a distinctive feature of the current study that adds to its contribution. These unique foci were selected for practical and research reasons.

First, from a practical standpoint, the loss and theft of mobile devices is a growing risk that has yet to be addressed by the IS community. Mobile devices are much more likely to be lost or stolen than are desktop computers. Approximately 70 million smartphones are lost each year, and one laptop is stolen every 53 seconds [34]. In the U.S., 113 cell phones are lost or stolen every minute [4]. One in every six users experiences loss, theft or damage to mobile devices such as laptops, smartphones and tablets in a period of 12 months [33]. Theft or loss of mobile devices can also lead to the loss of valuable data assets (e.g., personal information or critical files) and access to vital applications (e.g., email or organizational applications) [43]. Consequently, lost and stolen mobile devices are a great security concern for users and IS professionals [50]. Nevertheless, this threat has not yet received much attention in IS research. Hence, we see an opportunity to examine this issue and to offer practical solutions that may reduce the harm caused by this severe threat.

Second, the unique combination of IT artifact and threat can provide fertile ground for examining the integration of social learning information sources with PMT. The context of mobile device use is significantly different from the context of organizational, stationary information system use [59] and from stationary home environments [2], which have been the foci in past IS research. Mobile devices are mainly owned by individuals, contain much personal information, often allow access to organizational systems, and can be used anywhere. Thus, they present a unique risk profile and, thus, threat severity and coping strategies that may differ from those common in stationary work and home environments. The threat is not only organizational but also very personal, and it can manifest itself anytime and anywhere. In addition, when devices are personally owned, coping may require more self-reliance than reliance on technical support provided by an organization.

Lastly, social factors, including pressures from the work environment, can play an important role in users’ security protection in this context. When devices are personally owned, users typically do not receive formal security education and training. Their learning is therefore mainly based on informal information sources such as colleagues, personal experience, and popular media. This effect may be augmented due to the visibility of mobile devices—people can easily see what others have or do on their devices. Consequently, social learning can be salient in mobile environments and arguably represents a highly relevant and unique characteristic of this setting. Moreover, because personally owned mobile devices can be used for both work and non-workrelated purposes and we spend a large portion of our waking hours at work and/or addressing work issues, it is reasonable to assume that social influences from the work environment (e.g., colleagues, IT departments) are salient.

Ultimately, the integration of PMT and SLT extends our understanding of the learning mechanisms that drive users’ threat and coping assessments and, ultimately, their security behaviors.

Moreover, the unique focus on mobile device loss and theft risks extends the breadth of risks and user populations that are examined in the IS literature and offers practical implications for the acute, yet understudied, issue of mobile device loss or theft. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section, the theoretical background is given, followed by the development of hypotheses. Next, the paper describes the methods and results. Finally, discussion and conclusion sections are provided.

## 2. Conceptual background

This study suggests that protection motivation theory (PMT, [53]) and social learning theory [55] can be combined and used as a theoretical lens for explaining users’ intentions to employ measures to reduce or prevent damage from the loss or theft of mobile devices. Thus, in this section, we provide background information regarding the context (mobile device loss or theft), followed by a detailed description of the abovementioned theories.

## 2.1. Mobile device loss or theft

Users carry mobile devices, such as smartphones, laptops and tablets, with them nearly everywhere they go. While such devices are small in size and easy to carry, they can also be easily forgotten, mislaid, or stolen if left unsupervised. Consequently, such devices are vulnerable to a unique risk of loss or theft, which may result in a range of adverse outcomes, as follows: (1) loss of the device (i.e., the potential for physical property loss), (2) loss of data (i.e., the potential of being unable to access needed data and giving others potential access to such data), and (3) loss of service access (i.e., losing the ability to remotely access needed applications and potentially allowing unauthorized access to such applications).

All of these adverse outcomes can cause a major inconvenience. For example, when a mobile device is lost or stolen, a user may be unable to address urgent work. Moreover, replacement costs could be high. In addition, large volumes of very personal data can be lost or fall into the wrong hands. These data may include personal photos and videos (76%), personal email correspondence (64%), passwords to social network and email accounts (22%), work emails (32%), business documents (20%), and financial information, specifically, passwords to online banking accounts (10%) [33]<sup>3</sup>. If sensitive personal or corporate data are stored in a device without any protective measures, the data could be misused. This could lead to tarnished reputations, loss of competitive position, and potential litigation [42]. If the device has remote access to other systems, such as banking services, social networks, or enterprise networks, the lost or stolen device may allow unauthorized access to such systems. Consequently, mobile device loss and theft have become a severe security threat to individuals and organizations.

Given the above threat severity, many solutions for preventing or alleviating such damage have been developed. First, some measures are targeted toward avoidance. These measures include actions or tools that can eliminate the possibility of mobile device loss or theft (e.g., storing devices in safe areas, not carrying them around, restricting the storage of sensitive information on devices through policies or technologies, and not enabling remote access to sensitive services or networks). Second, if the threat of loss or theft cannot be avoided, prevention countermeasures can be used to reduce the likelihood that mobile devices might be lost or stolen and to prevent other people from accessing and maliciously using the devices, data, and remote services. Passwords and data encryption are the most widely applied prevention countermeasures. Many vendors can provide other countermeasures, such

Third, remedy countermeasures refer to tools and activities that attempt to cope with the negative consequences of mobile device loss or theft and to compensate for any resulting damage. Remote data wipe services, access blocking, remote access to built-in cameras, and GPS tracking of devices can also be useful in this regard.

While the behavioral countermeasures that are normally used, such as avoiding the carrying of devices, are typically useful, they may not always be effective, at least in the areas of prevention and remedy. Thus, this study focuses on technical countermeasures, such as remote device wipe, data encryption, and password protection, which have the potential to be effective in protecting not only one’s device but also the data that it contains and remote access to its applications. Problematically, although there are a variety of technical countermeasures that can be used to cope with mobile device loss and theft and that are readily available in many devices, many users do not activate or use them. It is therefore important to analyze the motivation for using such technical solutions, and this study integrates PMT and SLT to perform this analysis.

## 2.2. Protection motivation theory (PMT)

Protection motivation theory explains the formation of individuals’ intentions to cope with a potential threat (protection motivation) and the consequent coping behaviors (coping mode) [53,54]. It argues that people’s motivation to protect themselves stems from their assessments of the threat and the efficacy of potential responses. Threat appraisals involve a determination regarding the personal relevance of facts pertaining to threatening events. People subjectively assess the severity of the threat and their own susceptibility to the threat. Therefore, threat appraisals encompass the individual’s estimation of the probability of contracting the threat (perceived vulnerability) and the severity of the threat (perceived severity). For example, when taking a new medication, a person may assess the likelihood of experiencing side effects (susceptibility) and the consequences of such effects (severity). The likelihood of an adaptive response (e.g., avoiding taking the new medication) is increased when perceptions of severity and vulnerability are high.

Simultaneously with threat evaluation, people assess proposed responses. They specifically assess their abilities to cope with and to avert the threat through such responses. These assessments pertain to the efficacy of the proposed response to the threat and one’s self-efficacy regarding the protective actions required to mitigate the threat. The likelihood of an adaptive behavior is increased when high levels of self- and solution-efficacy beliefs are present. For example, if a person believes that following an exercise regimen is within his or her capabilities and that it can reduce the risk of heart disease, he or she will be more likely to follow this regimen.

The roles of threat and coping appraisals as drivers of protection actions have been validated in information systems contexts (e.g., [40]). These appraisals, however, are based in part on external sources of information [53]. The current study also examines such sources of information and, thus, extends prior research in this realm. As per PMT, sources of information can include verbal persuasion, observational learning, and prior experiences with similar threats, all of which can be very relevant in the case of mobile device loss and theft and perhaps with regards to other information security threats. In addition, applications of PMT to security-related behaviors have traditionally focused on stationary computing environments in organizational settings [31] and emphasized threats, such as spyware, and compliance to security policies as a key coping mechanism [41]. This study therefore extends the existing view in the following two ways: (1) it focuses on the learning mechanisms that drive threat and coping appraisals rather than on validating the well-established roles of these assessments in driving coping behaviors and (2) it emphasizes an emerging and severe, yet understudied, threat. The insights gained from this study can therefore inform both research and practice.

## 2.3. Social learning theory

Social learning processes are prevalent and, consequently, have been the focus of much research [6,20,55]. The common thread in existing theories is that learning from the social environment can inform one’s cognitions, which, in turn, can drive behavioral choices. Among the different theories, Bandura’s [6,8] social learning theory has been viewed as the most comprehensive and influential [23,28,57].

Bandura’s theory explains human behavior in terms of a continuous reciprocal interaction among cognitive, behavioral, and environmental determinants. According to this theory, human behavior is stimulated by external influences, by internal processing systems and regulatory codes, and by reinforcing response-feedback systems [6]. The theory emphasizes the role of self-reactive influences in motivating and guiding one’s behaviors. In essence, it posits that self-observations and feedback from the environment are the basis upon which situational assessments (judgments) are developed, which, in turn, drive intentions for adjusting one’s behavior.

In the social learning component of this theory, learning is situated firmly in its social context and is portrayed as a dynamic interplay between the person, the environment, and the behavior. Consequently, humans can learn through observation without the need for imitation. Learning from the social environment can be either direct (e.g., by personal experience) or indirect (by observing others’ behaviors and the consequences of those behaviors) [6]. The theory can therefore explain people’s learning and how it affects their judgments [46]. It posits that people do not merely react to external influences; rather, they select, organize, and transform stimuli that impinge on them. This theory has been successfully applied to the study of IS training [14,32] and IS adoption [29,67] and has been used to explain the development of self-efficacy regarding privacy protection [65]. The current study posits that such social learning mechanisms apply to protection– motivation assessments, including threat and coping appraisals. That is, people receive cues regarding the threat and the technical measures to cope with the threat from various sources. They are also influenced by personal experiences, such as losing a device in the past. As per SLT, they then logically process this information to develop and adjust their judgments regarding the threat and coping approaches, which serve as inputs for the development of coping intentions.

## 3.1. Threat appraisal effects

A threat is an external stimulus that exists regardless of whether an individual perceives it [31]. The perception of threat refers to the anticipation of a psychological, physical, or sociological violation or harm to oneself or others, which may be induced vicariously [66]. People assess threats differently according to their experiences and their levels of threat vigilance [36]. Threat appraisal is often examined through two dimensions, namely, perceived vulnerability and perceived severity. Perceived vulnerability refers to the subjectively estimated conditional probability that the threatening event will occur, provided that no adaptive behavior is performed or that there is no modification of an existing behavioral disposition [37]. Some people may believe that they are more vulnerable to losing their mobile devices because they are naturally careless about keeping track of them or live in an unsafe neighborhood. Other people may perceive lower vulnerability to mobile device loss because they are always cautious and take good care of their devices. Therefore, if users perceive that a loss or theft may occur, with resulting damage or disturbance to their normal behaviors, they are more likely to be motivated to mitigate such events. Perceived severity refers to the degree of physical harm, psychological harm, social threat, economic harm, and danger to oneself and others [37]. When users perceive a threat, they tend to adjust their behaviors in response to the extent of the damage that the threat may cause [66]. The perceived severity of a threat will lead users to behave in a more cautious manner and to employ countermeasures. Many mobile device users perceive the loss or theft of their devices to be a severe threat to themselves [50] and are, hence, more likely to consider protective actions.

This view received support in past research concerning the use of anti-malware software [37] and information security behaviors [40,66]. In this study, we combine the perceived vulnerability and perceived severity into one assessment that reflects an overall threat perception. This is appropriate because threat often connotes risk [41], and risk is typically calculated by multiplying the probability of occurrence with the cost of potential damage from the event [9,10]. Thus, we can define the overall perceived threat as the multiplication of perceived vulnerability and perceived severity. This suggests that the perceived threat will disappear if either of the variables is zero. For example, when a user perceives that his or her mobile device has no chance of being lost or stolen, he or she will not feel threatened, even if the loss or theft may result in serious damage. Similarly, people who use cheap cell phones may feel that the severity of loss or theft will be insignificant. In essence, users’ coping intentions toward harms resulting from mobile device loss or theft depend on the joint effect of their perceptions of the likelihood of the occurrence (perceived vulnerability) and severity (perceived severity) of the threat rather than on their additive effect. Users are expected to develop stronger coping intentions when the perceived threat is high. A similar view, although it operationalizes perceived threat as severity and vulnerability assessments, has been supported in extant research [31,37,40,41,66]. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1. The perceived threat of device loss or theft is positively associated with users’ intentions to cope with this threat through the employment of technical countermeasures.

3.2. Coping appraisal effects

Coping appraisal involves assessments of the efficacy of countermeasures to eliminate or mitigate the threat and its harms (response efficacy) and assessments of one’s own efficacy to properly employ such countermeasures (self-efficacy) [40].

Self-efficacy assessments influence behaviors through social cognitive theory (SCT) mechanisms. This theory posits that when people perceive that they have the capability to perform an act that benefits them, they will undertake substantial efforts to accomplish that act. Self-efficacy relates to whether people feel they have the needed skills and resources to accomplish a goal. If people are highly confident in their ability to conduct a desirable action, they are more likely to take the action [6]. Applied to the current context, when users believe that they are capable of performing a needed coping behavior to prevent the loss or theft of mobile devices, they will be more motivated to take the coping action. For instance, if a user has high (low) confidence in using data encryption to protect data on his or her smartphone, he or she may be motivated (demotivated) to employ this measure. Such effects of self-efficacy on intentions to perform protective actions have been validated in various information security behavior contexts [27,37,41,66]. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H2. Self-efficacy regarding the use of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft damage is positively associated with users’ intentions to cope with this threat through the employment of such countermeasures.

In this appraisal mechanism, people also consider the effectiveness of coping actions [41,66]. These considerations capture the response’s efficacy, i.e., the extent to which a behavior, if executed, is believed to lead to specific outcomes [7]. In the current context, it refers to subjective assessments of the ability of a safeguarding measure to avert a security threat [40]. Given available information about countermeasures for coping with security threats, users assess the effectiveness of employing such measures and then decide whether they want to use them. For instance, users may assess how efficacious a PIN code is in preventing unauthorized use of their devices and process this information for making informed decisions regarding the use of this countermeasure. Such effects of response efficacy on intended coping behavior have been supported in numerous studies [27,37,41,66]. Replicating the results of these studies, we hypothesize the following:

H3. The perceived effectiveness of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft damage (response efficacy) is positively associated with users’ intentions to cope with this threat through the employment of such countermeasures.

## 3.3. Sources of information effects

Mobile device users are often exposed to various behaviors, pressures and cues in their environment that are related to the use of countermeasures against the harm of mobile device loss or theft. For example, their friends or peers may convince them to employ such measures or indirectly signal to them that such measures are needed by installing them and sharing this information. Users may also be exposed to news and professional materials regarding these issues, or they may have experienced device loss or theft, which should help them learn about the severity and susceptibility of such events. These elements can serve as sources of information that are used in the coping and threat appraisal processes. PMT implies that such sources of information can include knowledge regarding potential responses (technical security measures), prior experience of the threat (device loss or theft), and social influences. Through email interviews (see Section 4), we verify that these are indeed key sources of information upon which users of mobile devices rely when forming threat and coping appraisals. Given that these are key information sources, in the next paragraphs, we explain how they can inform threat and coping appraisals.

Typically, individual users are affiliated with various social and professional groups. The behaviors of such social groups can help individuals learn about and choose desirable and socially approved courses of action [51]. This happens through social learning processes, namely, verbal persuasion and observational learning, as described by SLT [6]. Observational learning captures individuals’ natural tendencies to imitate the actions that they observe others perform [28]. Social influence is particularly important in the mobile context due to the ubiquitous and conspicuous nature of the devices and their use for social interactions. Thus, possible mobile device risks and solutions will likely emerge in social situations and be mentioned by at least some peers. In the case of measures against device loss or theft, individuals may simply imitate their peers’ actions because they may assume that this is a desirable and socially acceptable behavior. Humans tend to develop such imitation capabilities since their infancy [13]. Similarly, people may be verbally convinced by peers to take recommended actions [26].

Arguably, such verbal and observational social influences also operate in the case of countermeasures against device loss or theft. When others in one’s social and professional circles use such countermeasures or recommend their use, one is socially cued to employ such countermeasures. Similar social cuing has been observed in multiple contexts [49,56,61], including in the cases of IT user threat avoidance [40] and adoption of antimalware software [37]. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4. Social influences regarding the use of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft damages are positively associated with users’ intentions to employ such countermeasures.

The social environment can also drive behaviors indirectly by providing information regarding an activity and driving the learning related to this activity. Consistent with SLT, learning is assumed to be largely a social process that synthesizes direct and indirect information from peers and self-observation of the environment and other sources in the environment that may be directly or indirectly related to the acquired knowledge. First, by observing others’ behaviors, one can gain knowledge regarding such behaviors [60]. Thus, when countermeasures against device loss or theft are employed by an individual’s important others or work colleagues, he or she may increase knowledge regarding such tools through observation. Second, when important others recommend such countermeasures, or when a company provides such countermeasures, a person may be prompted to learn about such countermeasures as a means to be able to comply and to decide whether, how, and the extent to which he or she should use them. As such, social influences can facilitate knowledge acquisition [68]. This happens because individuals attempt to bridge the gap between their current levels of knowledge and the levels needed to execute the socially cued behavior. Indeed, when users lack knowledge and experience regarding IT threat avoidance, the social environment can provide them with information regarding malicious IT risk and possible safeguard measures [40]. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5. Social influences regarding the use of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft damages are positively associated with users’ knowledge of such countermeasures.

Social learning theory postulates that human thought is influenced by observations. By observing the behaviors of others, an observer can acquire knowledge of predictable reinforcement contingencies and discern cues for the consequences [5]. In the context of device loss or theft, this learning can pertain to the countermeasures (as posited in H5) or to the threat itself. When social cues regarding the use of countermeasures against device loss or theft exist, they may signal to individuals that the threat is real and that they may be susceptible to severe consequences. Otherwise, their important others and work colleagues would not employ (indirect influence) or recommend employing (direct influence) such tools. In other words, direct (e.g., through persuasion) and indirect (e.g., through actions) social pressures will likely be applied only to threats that are deemed important by others, and the perceived risk of a threat can be manipulated through social pressures [39]. Thus, severity and susceptibility assessments of device loss or theft may be based in part on direct and indirect social pressures applied by others. Indeed, people utilize social cues from their environment for threat appraisals [48]. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H6. Social influences regarding the use of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft damages are positively associated with users’ perceived threat of device loss or theft.

Lastly, people update their perceptions and situational assessments in response to events and direct experience with a behavior [8]. This view is also advanced by SLT, which emphasizes the saliency of direct experiences in judgment development [35]. In the case of protection motivation, prior experiences with similar threats are intrapersonal sources of information, which can inform individuals’ threat appraisals [54]. This idea has been validated with regards to various events and risks, including myocardial infarction [63] and terror attacks [38]. Applied to the current context, it is likely that a user who has experienced mobile device loss or theft will have clearer perceptions of the vulnerability and severity of the threat of loss and theft. He or she will rationally see such events as more likely, and potentially as more severe, than others who have not experienced such a traumatic event. Hence, the following hypothesis is posed:

H7. Users’ prior experience of device loss or theft threat is positively associated with their perceived threat of device loss or theft.

Coping efficacy assessments can be influenced by individual differences [54]. Users’ knowledge of security measures is one such individual difference variable; some users learn and know more than others about countermeasures against device loss and theft. This knowledge can be a basis upon which people assess their efficacy to employ the countermeasure [8], and self-efficacy is both learned and modified via vicarious learning [11]. Users develop self-efficacy by determining what they should attempt to achieve, what they know, and how much effort they put into their performance in a given situation, i.e., how or whether they put their knowledge into action [24]. Applied to the current context, when users have more knowledge of countermeasures against the threat, they will likely feel more confident in employing these countermeasures. For example, a user who knows how remote device wipe applications work is more likely to feel efficacious in using these countermeasures than a person who may not even know that they exist. Therefore, we pose the following hypothesis:

H8. Users’ knowledge of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft is positively associated with their self-efficacy in using such countermeasures.

Knowledge regarding countermeasures can also serve as the basis for assessing the efficacy of the countermeasures. This follows the logic of SLT [8], according to which beliefs regarding an object or behavior are updated in part based on knowledge regarding the object or behavior. This also follows the logic of PMT [54], according to which response efficacy assessments are developed based on various considerations, presumably including one’s state of knowledge regarding the response. Hence, the efficacy of countermeasures should be assessed by considering, among other things, what they can do. At one extreme, if no such knowledge exists, the efficacy of the countermeasure will be unclear and diminished. At the other extreme, if an individual is familiar with the various functionalities and features of a solution, and assuming that these are useful and efficacious, he or she will likely develop stronger efficacy beliefs regarding the countermeasure. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H9. Users’ knowledge of technical countermeasures against device loss or theft is positively associated with their response efficacy assessments.

## 4. Methods

This study included three phases: an email interview, a pilot study, and the full study. First, an email interview (n = 12) was performed to discern key information sources upon which users build when developing threat and coping appraisals. These are the SLT inputs, which we then used to inform the PMT considerations. Next, a pilot study was conducted (n = 115) to refine and validate the measurement scales. Lastly, the research model was validated with SEM techniques applied to data from U.S.-based mobile device (smartphones and laptops) users (n = 339).

## 4.1. Survey instrument

The survey instrument captured the model’s constructs as well as demographic and descriptive statistics. Whenever possible, initial scale items were taken from previously validated measures and reworded to relate to the studied context. The survey items were presented to several academics and were refined based on their inputs. Because individuals may use multiple mobile devices, they were asked to choose the mobile device that was the most important to them and to reflect on this device when responding to the survey.

Coping appraisal was measured by two constructs: self-efficacy and response efficacy. These constructs were based on Lee and Larsen [37] and Workman et al. [66] and adapted to the context of this study. Threat appraisal was assessed with a perceived threat construct, which is consistent with the risk assessment literature and amalgamates vulnerability and severity aspects of mobile device loss or theft. The literature review indicated that the loss or theft of mobile devices represents three threats: loss of device, loss of data, and loss of services. Therefore, users’ threat (vulnerability and severity) perceptions should relate to these three dimensions, and the perceived threat can be conceptualized as the sum of products of the relevant threats’ vulnerability and severity. This is consistent with how perceptions and beliefs are operationalized in main theories of human behavior, such as the theory of reasoned action or planned behaviors (e.g., behavioral beliefs are often operationalized as the sum of products of the likelihood of outcomes and their level of desirability) [1]. The specific threat items in this study were derived from the IS security literature [37,40,66] and adapted to the study’s context.

Social influence was operationalized using a formative composite based on Johnston and Warkentin [31] and using the formative measurement specification technique outlined in Diamantopoulos et al. [18]. The logic of this measure is that social influence can be direct (an individual recommends that you use a new app), but it can also be indirect (an organization endorsing the use of an app signals to users that it is a recommended step); hence, the measure included two corresponding items. Threat experience was operationalized as a binary response question capturing whether individuals had previously experienced the loss or theft of their mobile device (1) or not (0). Knowledge is a context-specific construct that cannot be easily generalized across domains [12]. It was therefore operationalized using items capturing knowledge of domain-specific responses, such as data encryption and device blocking. While the items are technology-specific, the underlying construct encapsulates individuals’ overall knowledge of countermeasures. In addition, it was assumed that knowledge about the response is inclusive of knowledge of the threat; hence, the latter was not measured separately. For example, it is unlikely that an individual would know about anti-virus without knowledge about viruses and their risks. The coping intention scale was based on a behavioral intentions scale [17]. In addition to the model’s constructs, the survey captured age, gender and mobile device type (mobile phone or laptop), All of these measures are listed in Table 1.

## 4.3. Full study

Data for this study were collected using an online survey that was administered by a commercial survey company to 400 respondents who met the inclusion criteria of U.S. residency and usage of a laptop or mobile device. This approach was taken because it allowed us to reach a broad cross-section of the different age and professional segments of the population, making the sample possibly generalizable to a large segment of the population. This step yielded a sample of 339 usable records (85% response rate). Of this sample, 48.4% were women. The average age was approximately 39 years, and the modal level of education was college or university degree. Approximately 38.6% focused on their laptop/ notebooks as their most important devices in the survey, and the remainder focused on their mobile/smartphones. Twenty six percent had previously experienced loss or theft of a mobile device. The majority used the device for personal purposes (59.9%), and the remainder used it for a combination of work and personal purposes (2.1% used it primarily for work, and 38% used it for both work and personal purposes). These data agree with the usage pattern we previously discussed for mobile devices and the prevalence of mobile device loss or theft.

## 5. Analysis and results

## 5.1. Preliminary analyses

Three preliminary analyses were conducted before the hypothesized model was examined. First, descriptive statistics and reliability scores were calculated for the constructs. They are presented in Table 2 together with the intra-construct correlations. Table 2 demonstrates that all multi-item constructs have Cronbach’s alpha scores and composite reliability scores above 0.7 and average variance extracted (AVE) scores over 0.5, and the square root of AVE (on the diagonal) exceeds the corresponding intra-construct correlations. It was therefore concluded that the scales were reliable and presented sufficient convergent and discriminant validity.

It is interesting to note that older individuals perceived themselves to have less social influence, reported lower levels of knowledge of potential responses, and reported fewer incidents of loss or theft of mobile devices. These correlations seem reasonable given that age and gender can influence various aspect of mobile device use [52]. At the same time, device type seems to be moderately associated with the outcome variable. Laptop or notebook users seem to have stronger coping intentions compared with those of cell/smartphone users. In addition, the type of primary use of the device (only personal vs. work and/or personal) seems to be associated with some of the model’s constructs.

Next, common method variance (CMV) risk was assessed. A latent common method factor that draws equal variance from all observed indicators was included in a CFA model. The model fit the data well (x<sup>2</sup>(133) = 243.20, CFI = 0.97, IFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.050 with PCLOSE = 0.52, and SRMR = 0.046), but the loadings of this latent factor were not significant (p < 0.30). Moreover, contrasting this model with a CFA model with no latent method factor $\overset { \cdot } { ( } \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 3 4 ) = 2 4 3 . 4 5 )$ produced a nonsignificant chi-square statistic $( \chi ^ { 2 } \mathrm { d i f f } ( 1 ) = 0 . 2 5 )$ . This indicates that adding the method factor fails to significantly improve the chi square statistic of the model and that the model with no method factor is superior. We, therefore, concluded that CMV is unlikely to be an important source of variation in the data.

Lastly, while survey data were collected over a period of fou days and presented a fairly high response rate, there may still be a risk of non-response bias. To mitigate this risk, we compared the earliest third of the responses collected with the final third of the responses using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). The results (Pillai’s trace = 0.092, F(25) = 0.8, p < 0.74) indicated that there were no omnibus differences between early and late responses, mitigating any risk of non-response bias.

As shown, social influence has a broad set of impacts on coping intentions. It influenced coping intentions not only directly, as demonstrated in past research [31], but also through the information signals that it provides to mobile device users. ## 6. Discussion

How do IS users learn to develop threat and coping appraisals? How do they use such appraisals in making decisions about counter-actions? The findings, albeit in the specific context of mobile device loss or theft, indicate that this may concern protection considerations, which are partly informed by social learning processes. As per PMT [54], users of mobile devices simultaneously appraise the severity and susceptibility of the threat of device loss or theft (threat appraisal). They also develop assessments of potential technical countermeasures against the harms of device loss or theft (coping appraisal). These threat and coping appraisals serve as the basis upon which intentions to employ such countermeasures (coping intentions) are developed. Specifically, when users perceive the threat of device loss or theft to be strong or when they strongly believe in their ability to use the countermeasures and in the efficacy of such countermeasures to reduce harm, they develop stronger intentions to employ countermeasures against the threat and its harms.

As per SLT, threat and coping appraisals are developed in part through social cues and vicarious learning. When important others and organizations support or encourage the use of countermeasures against device loss or theft, it signals to users that the threat is serious and imminent. It also provides them with information regarding potential countermeasures and drives them to seek knowledge regarding such solutions. Moreover, in line with other applications of PMT [37,40], social influences directly increase intentions to employ countermeasures against device loss or theft.

Consistent with social cognitive theory and PMT, knowledge regarding countermeasures against device loss or theft is an important source of information for efficacy assessments. It increases self- and response-efficacy beliefs. That is, when users are well aware of the features of such solutions, they feel more efficacious in using them and believe that the solutions are better able to reduce the harms of device loss or theft. Lastly, learning through experience [44] also applies to cases of device loss or theft. Users who have experienced such an event perceive the threat to be more severe than those who did not have such an experience, presumably because they perceive themselves to be more susceptible to device loss or theft and are well aware of the potential harms of such events.

Altogether, the findings indicate that PMT and social learning processes can be intertwined and influence users’ intentions to employ countermeasures against device loss or theft. The implications of this view for research and practice are discussed below.

## 6.1. Implications for research

During this research, we analyzed a special security issue in the mobile device use context and built a user behavior model that combines both self-protection and social learning perspectives. The findings have several theoretical implications.

First, this study extends the view of PMT employed by past research by adding the social learning perspective. Prior studies have mostly illuminated the importance of threat and coping assessments in the coping behavior development process (e.g., [31]) and have done so in organizational systems contexts. That is, while prior research has mostly focused on cognitive mediation processes (i.e., coping and threat appraisals) as antecedents of coping intentions in organizational systems contexts, this study extends this view by including sources of information as predictors of cognitive mediation processes. Hence, the findings imply that more research should be conducted on such information sources because they have the potential to be the basis upon which important threat and coping assessments are developed. Unlike threat assessment, these informational sources can be directly manipulated by companies and service providers. Hence, findings regarding informational sources can also have practical implications.

Second, the findings of this study illuminate the relevance of social learning processes and the applicability of social learning theories to the case of mobile device security behaviors and perhaps other IS security behaviors. The findings suggest that users learn from their environments through observation, imitation, and social cues, as prescribed by the social learning aspects of the social cognitive theory. While such effects of social cues and pressures on one’s threat and coping appraisals have been demonstrated in non-IS contexts (e.g., [48]), this study is among the first to articulate and empirically support such broad effects in the case of technology-related threats<sup>4</sup>.

Arguably, the informational sources used in our model, while especially relevant and salient in the mobile device context, may be relevant to other contexts as well. That is, the boundary conditions for the theory, which the current study set to include mobile device usage settings, can possibly be extended to other IS use situations. For example, past incidents of computer virus infection or pressures from peers or organizations regarding the use of virus defense software may signal to users that such threats are imminent and, thus, influence users’ threat appraisals. Similarly, knowledge regarding virus protection applications or privacy regulation mechanisms may drive users’ assessments of their abilities to use tools or comply with such policies. Ultimately, this study can serve as a platform for extending our understanding of user security-related behaviors using a broad PMT view that includes the information sources on which threat and coping appraisals are partially based.

Third, our results support and extend current findings regarding protection–motivation in IS contexts. This study extends the generalizability of the protection–motivation framework to a relatively unexplored, yet important context, i.e., coping with the harm of mobile device loss or theft. Personally owned mobile devices can be used for both personal- and work-related purposes anywhere and anytime, and as such, their risk profiles differ from those of commonly studied technologies. This unexplored risk is relatively prevalent and severe, as demonstrated in our study, and can serve as fertile ground for further examination of factors that can help to mitigate the risk by changing users’ behaviors. Furthermore, given the prevalence of individual device use, both for personal and work settings (e.g., the Bring-Your-Own-Device trend, see [47,69]), research on such protective behaviors regarding one’s own device is warranted.

Lastly, this study has shown that it is feasible to treat threat appraisals as the product of threat severity and susceptibility. This view is consistent with the operationalization of risk [9,10], which often includes a product of the likelihood of the event and the severity of the outcome. Considering threat appraisals as risk assessments opens the door for expanding the body of work on protection motivation in the IS context by relying on the vast literature on risk [3,19,45]. We thus call for future research to add risk analysis aspects, drivers, and outcomes to protection– motivation models.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

The findings point to several important implications for mobile service providers, organizations, and mobile device users. The results of this study showed that threat and coping appraisals are important determinants of intentions to employ protective countermeasures against the threat of device loss or theft and that these assessment are influenced in part by knowledge of the response, past experience of the threat, and social influences. Interestingly, many of these antecedents can be manipulated through stakeholders’ actions.

First, this study illustrated the importance of self-protection for personal IS users. Many technologies that people use at work are consumer products that are owned by employees. Social learning, therefore, should be encouraged by organizations to make selfprotection more proactive. However, given the dual purpose use of many of these devices (e.g., they can store work documents and personal pictures), the responsibility for improving users’ coping abilities shifts to the end-users, who should be motivated to protect their personal assets, learn about loss and theft risks, and act upon their threat appraisals.

Second, mobile service providers should give users access to countermeasures against device loss or theft. This would serve a triple purpose. As per our model, it can increase coping intentions directly, increase users’ threat assessments through social cues, and increase their knowledge and awareness of the countermeasures. Social cues can be amplified by informing users of how many of their friends or co-workers use such countermeasures. This can be done on company portals or by emailing such information to users. Presumably, such social pressures have the capacity to drive conformity [21] and, ultimately, the abovementioned triple effect.

Lastly, it is not sufficient to provide access to such countermeasures. Stakeholders need to increase users’ knowledge regarding such tools to increase users’ coping appraisals. Organizations can increase users’ knowledge regarding coping mechanisms by providing mandatory training or providing users with incentives (e.g., more remote access privileges, higher pay) if they undergo the required training. Organizations can also increase employee knowledge regarding device loss and theft countermeasures by offering online discussion forums that are devoted to such issues and encouraging participation [64]. Mobile service providers can employ similar means—provide online training to users or discuss such issues in forums to increase mobile device users’ knowledge of countermeasures against device loss or theft. Users can also actively seek knowledge in forums, websites and countermeasure applications vendor materials. These actions should help them advance their levels of knowledge and, consequently, their self- and response-efficacy assessments.

## 6.3. Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, our cross-sectional data may not support the causality implied by our model. In addition, our model focused on a limited set of information sources, as implied by SLT and the responses to the email interviews. These, however, may differ in other contexts and include more specific or other sources. Future research can extend the set of sources that we utilized in this study and explore a broader set of information sources that can be used for threat and coping appraisals. Moreover, we employed PMT in this study due to its consistent successful application in other contexts. However, there are many other rational behavioral IS security models that can be adapted to examine the current phenomenon [2]. Future research may compare and integrate rational behavioral IS security models.

Second, because our model was tested in a specific and unique context, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. We focused on general theory integration and, consequently, did not include a large number of context-specific factors. Hence, future research may extend our model and examine the possible effects of such factors. Moreover, it is possible that the results may be different in other cultures or when applied to other coping behaviors in different IS use contexts. Furthermore, the measures that we used were tailored to the current context (mobile devices). Thus, caution should be exercised when comparing our results to those presented in past research, which focused mostly on stationary organizational contexts. In addition, the experience of threat was measured in a binary fashion. Future research can extend this view and examine the effects of the severity and recency of the threat experience. Furthermore, the focus on personal (nonorganizational) users poses new challenges. For example, peer influences may not be substantiated or positive, and past incidents may be attributed to external sources rather than to the self. Future research can tackle these newly imposed issues.

Lastly, the sample in this study may be somewhat heterogeneous, as it taps into different types of devices and uses. On the one hand, this allowed us to view the forest; i.e., the big picture. On the other hand, it prevented us from viewing the trees, i.e., the specifics of each situation. While we controlled for these effects and ruled them out, it is possible that a larger sample will reveal that users’ behaviors differ according to the device that they employ and their primary uses of the device. The sample mixes those who use mobile devices purely for personal purposes and those who also use them for business purposes. As the line between pure personal and personal/business users is increasingly blurring, the latter group is very interesting to study. However, given that this study is a very early, if not the first, study of personal users’ employment of countermeasures, we are unable to determine whether the same theoretical model may be equally applied to both groups. This can be addressed in future research.

## 7. Conclusion

This study sought to inform behavioral IS security research regarding how people develop coping and threat appraisals regarding mobile device loss or theft and, consequently, why they intend to use technical countermeasures against such threats. To this end, it applied a combined protection–motivation and social learning lens of analysis in a relatively unexplored, yet important, context. The findings indicated that users’ intentions to employ such countermeasures are influenced by their assessments of the threat, beliefs regarding their ability to employ the countermeasures, and assessments of the effectiveness of the countermeasures. These appraisals were informed through social and vicarious learning processes, in part, by available sources of information—social influence, knowledge regarding the countermeasures, and past experience of the threat. Ultimately, this study depicts threat and coping appraisals as mediational mechanisms that are informed by various internal and external sources of information. By demonstrating the viability of such informational sources in forming threat and coping appraisals, this study paves the way for further expansions of the protection–motivation framework and points to several theoretical and practical implications. Future research is encouraged to further expand this view to help organizations fight the dangerous epidemic of mobile device loss or theft.
