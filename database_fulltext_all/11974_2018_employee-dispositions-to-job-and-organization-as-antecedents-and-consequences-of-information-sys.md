# Employee dispositions to job and organization as antecedents and consequences of information systems use

Hillol Bala<sup>1</sup> | Akshay Bhagwatwar<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Operations and Decision Technologies Department, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, USA

<sup>2</sup> Operations Management & Information Systems, College of Business, Northern Illinois University, DeKalb, Illinois 60115, USA

Correspondence Hillol Bala, Associate Professor, Operations and Decision Technologies Department. Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, USA. Email: hbala@indiana.edu

## Abstract

Organizations implement information systems to improve employee productivity and engender favourable organizational outcomes. Although there is evidence of positive outcomes of system use, research has suggested that system use may lead to negative consequences for employees and organizations. There has been limited research that focuses on how employees' use of information systems in the workplace is associated with their positive and negative dispositions to job and organization. We develop and test a model that posits that dispositions to job (ie, job satisfaction, job security, job anxiety, and emotional exhaustion), and organization (ie, organizational commitment and organizational trust) will play a dual role of antecedents and consequences of system use. We conducted 2 longitudinal studies in the context of 2 different systems—a functional system and an enterprise system—and found support for our hypotheses (N = 257 and 181, respectively). We found that preimplementation job and organizational dispositions significantly predicted both lean and rich measures of system use. Further, we found that rich measures of system use (ie, cognitive absorption use and deep structure use) had differential impacts on postimplementation employee dispositions—functional system use had a positive impact and enterprise system use had a negative impact. Overall, our findings offer a comprehensive understanding of system use, and its antecedents and consequences for employees in organizations.

## KEYWORDS

cognitive absorption use, deep structure use, emotional exhaustion, job anxiety, job outcomes, job satisfaction

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Organizations implement information systems (IS), such as enterprise resource planning, supply chain management, customer relationship management, knowledge management and collaboration systems, and other functional systems to support and enable business functions and processes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Lyytinen & Newman, 2015; Tian & Xu, 2015). Gartner Research, a market research firm, reported that the worldwide spending on IS in 2016 was \$333 billion and is projected to grow to \$355 billion in 2017 (Forni & van der Meulen, 2017). These systems are expected to help employees improve productivity and engender favourable organizational outcomes (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Sykes, 2015). However, such positive outcomes can materialize only if systems are used by employees to perform their tasks. Indeed, system use has been considered a key dependent variable in research (DeLone & McLean 2003; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). Recently, researchers have operationalized sys tem use beyond the traditional lean measures, ie, duration, frequency, and intensity of use (Venkatesh, Brown Maruping, & Bala, 2008; Venkatesh et al., 2003). Burton‐Jones and Straub (2006), for example, used 2 constructs— cognitive absorption use and deep structure use—to operationalize system use beyond lean measures. Lean and rich measures cumulatively provide a comprehensive understanding of individuals' system use

Notwithstanding such rich conceptualizations and operationalizations of system use, there has been limited research that focused on the consequences of system use. In particular, there has been a dearth of research that pro vides a comprehensive understanding of the influence of system use on employee dispositions to job and organiza tion, ie, employees' feelings toward their job and organization. Prior research has offered numerous cases in which system implementations had significant negative consequences for employees (Sarker, Chatterjee, & Xiao, 2013 Srivastava, Chandra, & Shirish, 2015). In some cases, system use had both positive and negative consequences concurrently—ie, some employees considered a new system an opportunity, whereas other employees considered it a threat (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Srivastava et al., 2015). For example, Ou and Davison (2011) and Gupta, Li, and Sharda (2013) have found that instant messaging, a key feature of many collaboration systems, can concurrently lead to increasing work interruption and communication performance. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a deep understanding of how system use influences employees' job and organizational dispositions

In addition to postimplementation issues (ie, impacts of system use on employees' job and organizational dispo sitions), influence of preimplementation dispositions on system use is also crucial, yet understudied (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Raghu‐Nathan et al., 2008). Given that systems are not implemented in a vacuum and employees already have dispositions toward their job and organization before they experience a new system, it is likely that employees' preimplementation feelings toward their job and sense of association to their organizations can play an important role in motivating them to use a new system (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) For instance, employees who have a strong sense of commitment and trust toward their organizations might agree with the decisions made by the management regarding a new system implementation and try to get accustomed to the new system (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Fang & Neufeld, 2006; Susarla, Barua, & Whinston, 2003). Similarly, employees who are overall happy and satisfied with their job may show less resistance to the changes in their work processes introduced by a new system (Fang & Neufeld, 2006). Overall, preimplementation employee dispositions to job and organizations might thus play a crucial role in influencing postimplementation system use. Although prior research has examined various antecedents of system use (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016: Venkatesh et al. 2003, 2008) there has been limited research that focused on the influence of preimplementation dispositions to job and organization on employees' system use.

Against this backdrop, we seek to accomplish 2 research objectives: (1) to examine how system use is associated with employee dispositions to job and organization (postimplementation) and (2) to investigate how these dispositions measured prior to a system implementation predict system use. We develop and test a model that posits that dispo sitions to job (ie, job satisfaction, job security, job anxiety, and emotional exhaustion) and organization (ie, organiza tional commitment, and organizational trust) will play a dual role (ie, antecedents and consequences of system use in the system implementation process. Preimplementation dispositions will predict how employees will use a system, and these dispositions will be significant consequences of system use in the postimplementation phase. We incorporate multiple operationalizations of system use into our model (ie, lean measures, such as duration, frequency, and intensity, and rich measures, such as cognitive absorption use and deep structure use; Burton‐Jones & Straub 2006). We base our hypotheses on the widely used expectation disconfirmation theory (EDT), which explains perspectives and reactions of employees as they become accustomed to a new system. Expectation disconfirmation the ory is particularly suitable as a theoretical foundation, given its focus on the process of confirmation/disconfirmation and the emotional/behavioural outcomes of system use. We found support for our model and hypotheses in 2 longitudinal studies conducted in the context of 2 different systems—an enterprise system and a functional system. Our findings provide insights on the association between employee dispositions (both preimplementation and postimplementation) and their use of a system

The paper is organized as follows. First, we discuss the theoretical foundations for our research. Next, we discuss the research model and develop hypotheses. We then discuss the methodology, analysis approach, and results. Next we discuss the maior findings and provide insights on the theoretical and practical contributions of this research Finally, we conclude with a discussion of limitations of this study.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

We first discuss EDT, the theoretical foundation for our research model, and its relevance to this research. We then discuss system use with the goal of justifying the use of both the lean and rich measures in our model. Next, we discuss the literature on employee dispositions to job and organization, focusing on specific constructs that we examine in this study. Finally, we discuss the classification of systems in organizations based on the work by McAfee (2006) with a focus on 2 types of systems—enterprise and functional systems—that represent th context of our study.

## 2.1 | Expectation disconfirmation theory

At its core, EDT posits individuals' affective reactions (eg, satisfaction) toward an object or an event as a function of prior expectation and disconfirmation (Oliver, 1980; Oliver & DeSarbo, 1988). EDT has been used in IS research to identify how users follow a process of expectation formation, trial, and disconfirmation to form satisfaction and con: tinuance intentions toward a system (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). Users develop preusage beliefs (expectations) and attitudes based on the information they get about a system from various sources (Lankton, McKnight, & Thatcher, 2014). These sources could be their colleagues who discuss information about the system among each other, workshops to learn the system, or documents such as information brochures or other online material on the system. Next, during the usage stage, disconfirmation and satisfaction are mechanisms that modify employee beliefs and attitudes toward the system (Lankton et al., 2014). Meeting or exceeding the pre‐usage expectations (confirmation) can lead to positive emotions or attitude toward the system. In contrast, not meeting those expectations (disconfirmation) can lead to negative emotions and attitude. As employees continue to use a new system, the process of confirmation‐disconfirmation continues till the magnitude of disconfirmation perceptions decreases or wears off, indicating that the employees have a clear understanding of what to expect from the system— an equilibrium stage (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). EDT suggests that achieving an equilibrium stage is critical, as it is an indication of employees having a clear understanding of a new system, its functionalities, and its utility for their work (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). In summary, EDT provides a framework to explain how expectations can influence the feelings that users develop toward a system, depending on whether the system meets, exceeds, or falls short of their expectations (Lankton et al., 2014).

In the context of this research, EDT provides important theoretical foundations to explicate why users of a system might develop and/or modify certain dispositions to job and organization based on their experiences with a new system. Since the focus of this research was not on the reactions (eg, expectation and disconfirmation) of users toward a new system, we leverage the insights provided by EDT on the process of confirmation/disconfirmatio (instead of the factors themselves). We expect that the preimplementation dispositions to job and organization would act as a precursor for employees to form a set of expectations with a new system. These expectations would the drive attitude toward and subsequent actual use of the system, which would in‐turn influence postimplementation dispositions to job and organization. We base our theorizing on research in organizational behaviour, which suggests that employees with negative perceptions toward their job and organization are not likely to exhibit positive attitude for any new changes introduced by the organization (Chang, Rosen, & Levy, 2009; Koys, 2001; Saari & Judge, 2004) Consequently, a new system implementation that would lead to substantial changes to their work routines would not be perceived positively by such employees.

The confirmation/disconfirmation process in the context of technology use (Lankton, McKnight, Wright, & Thatcher, 2016: Venkatesh & Goval. 2010) is important to understanding why emplovees develop dispositions to job and organization after using a system. Specifically, we build on prior research (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010) that sug gests that the confirmation/disconfirmation process in context of technology use follows a causal flow involving three steps: (1) pre‐use exposure to information regarding the characteristics and/or components of a technology leading to formation of expectations, (2) post‐use cognitive comparison between the expectations and actual experiences lead ing to confirmation/disconfirmation, and (c) development of positive/negative reactions based on a combination of expectations and confirmation/disconfirmation. Cumulatively, the confirmation/disconfirmation process provides an understanding of emotional or mental reactions employees might have toward a new system. We build on this con firmation/disconfirmation process to develop our research model and hypotheses.

## 2.2 | Operationalization of system use

In the IS literature, the widely used system use measures are frequency, duration, and intensity of use, cumula tively referred to as lean measures of system use (Burton‐Jones & Straub, 2006; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008). An employee's work processes consist of a sequence of activities to accomplish particular tasks (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). The activities can be scheduled along a definite temporal continuum and their frequency can vary depending on the nature of the employee's work (Ancona, Okhuysen, & Perlow, 2001). Frequency of use therefore depends on the way these activities are scheduled. For example, an employee whose work activities involve using queries from a system for data entry and data extraction must use the system frequently compared to another employee whose work activities involve meeting clients and project partners. Duration of use, which represents the amount of clock time spent in using the system (Venkatesh et al., 2008), is not only influenced by external factors such as the nature of the activity, skill levels in using the system, and job role, but also depends on internal factors such as employees' motivation to perform an activity. Similar t frequency of use, intensity of use depends on the demands of activities being performed by an employee. For complex activities, an employee is likely to use a system with greater intensity as compared to a routine activity

Burton-Jones and Straub (2006) suggested that lean measures are incapable of capturing the rich spectrum in individual's system use. They noted that literature on system use lacks a theoretical basis and empirical evidence about these lean dimensions, as well as antecedents and consequences of system use. They offered 2 rich operationalizations of system use—cognitive absorption use and deep structure use—that capture the richness of how individuals may use a system. Cognitive absorption use represents the extent to which an individual is absorbed and involved in task execution using a system (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Deep structure use represents the extent to which an individual uses the features of a system that relate to his/her tasks (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Burton‐Jones and Straub (2006) empirically showed that the relationship between an individual's system use and his/her task performance can be better explained by rich measures than lean measures of system use. Lean measures of use do not provide insights on how employees use a new system and whether they struggle with the use of its features. In contrast, rich use measures capture whether employees are exploring the breadth of system features and whether they are feeling cognitively absorbed in their work

## 2.3 | Employee dispositions to job

We focus on 2 widely used positive, ie, job satisfaction and job security, and 2 negative, ie, emotional exhaustion and job anxiety, job dispositions. Job satisfaction is a positive emotional state resulting from the appraisal of one's job or job experience (Locke, 1976; Mobley, 1977; Porter, Steers, Mowday, & Boulian, 1974). Spector (1997) defined jo satisfaction as the extent to which employees like (are satisfied) or dislike (are dissatisfied) their job based on thei evaluation of whether the job provides them with what they consider important. Job security represents an employee's confidence about the continuity of his/her job irrespective of any internal or external organizationa circumstances (Clark & Postel‐Vinay, 2009). Job satisfaction and job security represent positive feelings toward jo and work environment.

Emotional exhaustion has traditionally been considered a job stress construct that results from workplac stressors, such as negative events in the workplace or supervisory performance evaluation concerns (Lee & Ashforth, 1996; Wright & Cropanzano, 1998). This condition represents a chronic state of emotional and physical depletion that can predict job performance, organizational citizenship behaviour, and turnover intentions (Cropanzano, Rupp, & Byrne, 2003). Job stress represents “the feeling of a person who is required to deviate from normal or self‐desired functioning in the work place as the result of opportunities, constraints, or demands relating to potentially important work‐related outcomes” (Parker & DeCotiis, 1983, p. 165). Job anxiety is defined as a feeling of fear and/or an apprehensive mental state of an employee regarding certain components of work (Srivastava, 1977). Both emotiona exhaustion and job anxiety represent a negative psychological emotion that employees might experience due to significant changes in their routines following a new system implementation. As it is common for a newly implemented system to introduce significant changes in how employees perform various tasks and how business processes operate, employees might feel apprehension and anxiety about anticipated changes in their job during a system implementation.

In recent years, IS have become functionally sophisticated and capable of processing information at a much faster pace and greater volume than before (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011; Fang & Neufeld, 2006; Ragu‐Nathan et al., 2008; Wang, Shu, & Tu, 2008). Consequently, employees are required to process continuous stream of information available through systems in addition to challenge of dealing with newly implemented systems every few years (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). With the need to continuously invest efforts to get accustomed to new systems and consequent changes in work, employees feel frustrated and overwhelmed (Ayyagari et al., 2011; Tarafdar, Tu, Ragu‐Nathan, & Ragu‐Nathan, 2011). Although research has focused on employee resistance to new systems and project failures as a consequence of such resistance, consequences of employee reactions to a new system extend beyond resistance. Research has examined consequences of negative reactions to new systems, such as politica infighting between management and employees, and increased level of stress among employees, as well as negative work outcomes, such as decreased productivity, and longer work hours, and increased turnover intentions (Ayyagar et al., 2011; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013, 2016; Ragu‐Nathan, Tarafdar, Ragu‐Nathan, & Tu, 2008; Tarafdar, Tu, & Ragu‐Nathan, 2010). In this regard, we take a nuanced approach by considering employee's dispositions to job (both positive and negative). Our research builds on and extends prior work by focusing on the dynamic interpla between system use and employee dispositions to job during preimplementation and postimplementation phases

## 2.4 | Employee dispositions to organization

We focus on 2 widely used employee dispositions to organization—organizational commitment and organizationa trust. Commitment denotes a notion of being emotionally or intellectually bonded to an entity or a course of action (Mowday, Steers, & Porter, 1979; Steers, 1977). Organizational commitment has been studied extensively in organizational behaviour and social psychology research. Employees with a high level of organizationa commitment show motivation in performing tasks relevant to organizational benefits (Eisenberger, Fasolo, & Davis‐LaMastro, 1990). Behaviour such as active engagement in organizational activities, changes, and decision‐making instances is also an indication of strong organizational commitment (Meyer & Allen, 1991). Employees with a high level of organizational commitment would likely possess a positive attitude toward organizational changes and confidence about the positive outcomes from decisions made in their organizations (Meyer & Allen, 1991; Williams & Anderson, 1991).

Organizational trust refers to an employee's belief in the functioning of his/her organization and a set of positive expectations about the intent and behaviour of organizational components such as departments, interdependencies, and organizational roles (Mayer, Davis, & Schoorman, 1995; Schoorman, Mayer, & Davis, 2007; Shockley‐Zalabak Ellis, & Winograd, 2000). Employees with a high degree of organizational trust are expected to be vulnerable or open‐minded to decisions made within the organization with the expectation that these decisions would be beneficia to them, irrespective of their ability to monitor or control them (Hart, Capps, Cangemi, & Caillouet, 1986; Mayer et al., 1995). Employees with strong organizational trust have a favourable sense of belief in decisions made within the organization (Driscoll, 1978; Hart et al., 1986). These employees generally believe that the decisions made are going to be beneficial to them at an individual level, resulting in improvements in their productivity. Further, employees with a high level of organizational trust would more likely show positive beliefs in organizational practices across various business processes, resulting in a strong motivation to perform their routine work (Mayer et al., 1995; Shockley Zalabak et al., 2000).

## 2.5 | Classification of ISs

McAfee (2006) classified information technologies (ITs) into 3 types on the basis of their core functionalities and use: function IT, enterprise IT, and network IT. The function IT represents systems that make execution of discrete work tasks efficient and effective (McAfee, 2006). These systems focus on individual tasks rather than functioning of the organization. Consequently, they are critical to the core work tasks that they are designed for but might not be critical to the business of the organization. For example, organizations may implement specialized functional systems for designing and modelling products (eg, software engineering and computer‐aided design tools). Word processors, spreadsheets, and statistical software are other popular function ITs that employees use in the workplace

Enterprise IT encompasses systems that control execution of business processes, which are often interrelated (McAfee, 2006). Consequently, use of these systems in one area of business mandates the use in related areas. With these systems, it becomes important to ensure data compatibility and integrity. Examples of enterprise IT includ enterprise resource planning, supply chain management, and customer relationship management systems that ar commonly implemented in organizations. Network IT represents systems or software that enable communication or collaboration and support data of different formats (McAfee, 2006). These systems are often not mandatory for employee use and may not be part of the sequence of work tasks or business processes. Examples of network IT include knowledge management and collaboration systems, and enterprise social media systems

We focus on 2 types of systems in this research—a function IT and an enterprise IT. The function IT, henceforth functional system, that we focus on is a plant safety management (PSM) system. A PSM system is integral to workplace occupational health and safety management activities in a manufacturing plant. The enterprise IT, henceforth enterprise system, that we focus on is a product lifecycle management (PLM) system. A PLM system is an important part of core business processes of a manufacturing organization since it keeps track of core activ ities of a manufacturer—to design, develop, and manufacture products. They support enterprise business processes related to creating, reviewing, approving, and tracking all product specifications included in the product record throughout different functional areas of an organization, such as procurement, product development, and production.

Figure 1 presents our research model. Consistent with EDT's theoretical assertion regarding preimplementation an postimplementation reactions toward a system, we posit that preimplementation employee dispositions to their jo (ie, job satisfaction, job security, emotional exhaustion, and job anxiety) and organization (ie, organizational commit ment and organizational trust) will influence both lean and rich system use measures. Further, system use will influ ence postimplementation employee dispositions.

In this section, we offer theoretical explanations for the hypotheses. We distinguish between the two systems—a functional system and an enterprise system—in hypotheses development. We do not have any theoretical reason t expect that preimplementation dispositions will have differential impacts on use of the 2 systems because potentia users of the systems would have no prior experience with them preimplementation. Consequently, the preimplementation hypotheses are not different for the 2 systems.

For postimplementation hypotheses, we consider the differences between functional system and enterprise system. We focus on the shakedown phase, a critical phase in determining success and acceptance of a system (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013; Morris & Venkatesh, 2010). Shakedown phase is the period from the point the system is functiona and accessible by employees (going live or rollout) to the point when normal operation or routine use has been achieved (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013).

First, regarding influence of lean and rich measures of system use on job dispositions, we suggest that there will be no differences for the 2 systems for constructs related to positive dispositions toward the job (ie, job satisfaction and job security) since these constructs relate to an employee's perceptions about their job and not emotional reactions. This is consistent with prior research that focused on emotional reactions of employees at work (eg, Halbesleben & Bowler, 2007; Wright & Cropanzano, 1998). However, given that negative dispositions (ie, emotional exhaustion and job anxiety) are related to employees' core emotions, we expect differential impacts on these 2 dispositions for the 2 systems. Further, building on social exchange theory, Cropanzano et al. (2003) sug gested that the sense of commitment and trust toward an organization are affective reactions of individuals that are influenced by work situations. Employees exposed to stressful work situations tend to emotionally withdraw from work. Eventually, such employees lose commitment toward organization as well as their trust in the organization to make favourable decisions for them. Functional systems affect peripheral work functions in an organization and are expected to cater to individual tasks (McAfee, 2006). In contrast, enterprise systems affect core business functions and processes of an entire organization. Consequently, while these 2 systems may lead to similar feelings of job satisfaction and job security, they are likely to engender different emotional or affective reactions (ie, emotional exhaustion job anxiety) and organizational dispositions (ie, organizational commitment and organizational trust) for employees

## 3.1 | Linking preimplementation dispositions to job and system use

We suggest that employees with higher levels of positive job dispositions, job satisfaction, and job security are likely to welcome challenges in work and are open to changes in work environment that might positively influ ence their job outcomes (Fang & Neufeld, 2006; Spector, 1997). They would perceive the implementation of a new system as a change that improves productivity, leading to a positive set of expectations from the system and a positive attitude toward the system (Susarla et al., 2003). Consequently, these employees would b excited about exploring functionalities of the new system and become accustomed to changes in the work pro cesses introduced by the system.

After implementation, these employees would be interested in learning its features and how those can be leveraged for their tasks. Per EDT this represents the usage stage where the emplovees adiust their expectations with the system and develop attitudes and use intentions. As noted earlier, employees who have positive dispositions toward their job would have a favourable expectations with the new system and would consequently use the system for a greater duration, with higher intensity, and more frequently (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004; Susarla et al., 2003). To learn different aspects of the system, these employees would explore features and understand how these features would make their work efficient. As employees explore the system, however, they will adjust their expecta tions and attitudes toward the system on the basis of whether the system met their expectations (Venkatesh & Goyal 2010). Employees who feel that their job is secure would spend more time gaining knowledge about the new system. This will help them realize how the system can help perform their tasks. Such employees are likely to remain engaged and get absorbed in performing tasks using the new system (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). Overall, these employees will attain an understanding of how their work processes function on the new system, helping them quickly reach th equilibrium stage (per EDT) using the system in various ways.

In contrast, employees who are not satisfied and/or feel insecure about their jobs would not feel excited abou changes introduced by a new system (Mount, Ilies, & Johnson, 2006). Research in organizational behaviour suggests that employees with high level of job dissatisfaction exhibit negative attitude toward any changes in work (Chang et al., 2009; Mount et al., 2006). Since these employees were unhappy with their work before system was imple mented, the challenge of getting accustomed to a new system would be a hindrance to performing tasks. As EDT sug gests, these employees would experience negative cognitions and eventually have negative use intentions. These employees might feel that the new system is of limited help for their work. They might avoid using the system alto gether and look for workarounds (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Boudreau & Robey, 2005). Employees who feel that they could soon lose their job because of performance inadequacies would not find it beneficial or expend much effort to learn the new system. Consequently, these employees would exhibit lower levels of system use.

Hypothesis 1A,B Employees' preimplementation positive job dispositions (ie, job satisfaction and job security) will be positively associated with (A) lean or (B) rich measures of system use.

We suggest that employees with high degrees of negative job dispositions, ie, emotional exhaustion and jo anxiety, are likely to want to avoid using a new system since they may feel that using the new system frequently and for a long duration will increase their exhaustion and anxiety from work (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005). Building on EDT, such employees are likely to avoid using the system because they will need substantial time to evaluate if the system is useful (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). Since these employees are unaware of new system's functionalities, the will not be able to develop the right set of expectations from the system. EDT suggests that such employees will eventually feel dissatisfied with the system and develop a negative attitude toward it. They will not reach the equilibrium stage where they have an understanding of the functionalities of the system and if the system is useful for their work. Since using a new system requires additional efforts to learn the new features and perform tasks in a different way, these employees are likely to minimize their use of the system fearing that they might make mistakes while using the system leading to an increasing level of exhaustion and anxiety. As a result, these employees will exhibit low levels of system use.

Further, given the extent of changes introduced by a new system and the apprehensive mental state it may create, employees are not likely to be involved in high degree of cognitive absorption and deep structure use. For instance, even though employees might use the system for extended periods, the time would mostly be spent in understanding the changes in their work rather than using the system to accomplish tasks (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Given the level of stress, employees might spend a significant amount of time in learning a few features of the system. They would not be able to explore all features of the new system for their work benefits, thus implying a low level of deep structure use. Per EDT, such employees might not be able to develop the right set of expectations with the system and would not engender positive attitudes toward the system (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004) Given the level of fear and apprehensiveness, they might not feel cognitively absorbed while working with the new system. They might not be able to concentrate on their tasks because of the extent of changes in their work tasks Thus, they would not likely to be engaged with the system (Burton‐Jones & Straub, 2006)

## 3.2 | Linking preimplementation dispositions to organization and system use

We suggest that employees with a high level of organizational commitment would show an overall positive atti tude toward a new system. Given their favourable disposition to their organization, employees would believe that the new system is going to be beneficial for the organization and for them. EDT suggests that such employees would be proactive in getting the right information about the system and developing the right set of expectations. Consequently, once a system is implemented, these employees would want to use it to understand if it meets their expectations, leading to the use of system for a longer duration of time, more frequently, and with more intensity (Venkatesh & Goyal, 2010). Employees would be excited about the changes and would show a high level of concentration while using the system. They would adjust their expectations and realize th value of system for their work. These employees would also spend time in exploring system features and how those could benefit them at work (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016). Being motivated about their work, employees would try to become comfortable with the new system by being focused in their work and staying cognitively absorbed, and would try to achieve a high level of proficiency in using the system (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016 Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

Employees who exhibit high organizational trust would believe that a new system implementation will benefit them, their colleagues, and the organization. As noted earlier, research in management suggests that employees with high organizational trust have positive perceptions about changes in the organization (Koys, 2001; Saari & Judge 2004). These employees are likely to develop a positive attitude toward the system and believe that its use wil improve productivity. Such employees would spend time and effort in using the system. Trusting that the decision made by management to implement a new system is going to make them efficient at work, employees would sta focused while working on the new system. They would approach colleagues and supervisors for assistance on any issues. They would be motivated to get any difficulties related to system use resolved and become accustomed to changes introduced by new system. Instead of appraising the new system use as an imposing challenge, employees would invest effort to learn functionalities of the system that could benefit their work (Beaudry & Pinsonneault 2005).

In contrast, if employees are not committed to organization, they might not perceive changes introduced positively and hence might not think that the new system is beneficial (Chang et al., 2009; Mount et al., 2006). Instead they might view the system as an unnecessary investment and believe that it might fail. Consequently, suc employees might avoid using the system and would not reach the equilibrium between their cognitive beliefs abou the system. Such employees might not spend much time in learning the components of the system that are required to execute their work tasks. Per EDT, such employees would develop a negative attitude toward the system, as they might not be able to conceive any benefit from it. Given that they might not believe in the success of the system, the might avoid using the system and find workarounds. Consequently, they would not be cognitively absorbed while using the system and might also avoid exploring features that the system offers

## 3.3 | Linking postimplementation measures of system use and positive dispositions to job

An increase in lean measures of system use implies that employees are using a system regularly. This would indicat that they perceive the functionalities of the system as necessary and are maximizing its use. Per EDT, such employee would quickly reach the equilibrium stage and understand what to expect from the system (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). If employees perform their work efficiently using the system, they would feel happy about thei ability to complete their tasks and perceive the system to be beneficial in helping them execute their work. Consequently, employees are likely to feel satisfied with their job. Further, if employees can complete their tasks efficiently and achieve desired outcome using the system, they would feel secure about their job. Overall, they will have a pos itive attitude toward the system and would see the system as an asset for their work.

In contrast, if employees do not use a system frequently, for an appropriate duration, and intensely (ie, reduced lean measures of use), they are either not comfortable with the functionalities of the system or are not finding th system useful for their work. Employees might feel that they will not be able to accomplish their tasks in an efficient manner. Such employees might not understand what they can expect from the system. Per EDT, such employee would not reach the equilibrium stage with their use of the system and would be dissatisfied with the system (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). Consequently, they might feel a sense of dissatisfaction with their job for not being able to execute work processes efficiently using the system. Further, being unable to accomplish their tasks employees would feel that they might lose their job. Overall, we hypothesize that an increasing level of lean measures of system use will have a positive relationship with job satisfaction and job security for functional and enterpris systems.

An increase in cognitive absorption use indicates that employees are engaged in using a system (Burton‐Jones & Straub, 2006). This focused use will lead to increased work efficiency (Burton‐Jones & Straub, 2006), resulting in favourable performance appraisals from managers. Increased deep structure use implies that employees are exploring and employing useful system features. This will lead to a reduction in time required to perform tasks and will make employees proficient in using the system. Consistent with EDT, we posit that employees would get comfortable with the new system and know what to expect from it (Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004). They would reach the equilib rium stage quickly and would develop a positive attitude toward the system. Consequently, employees would feel sat isfied with their job and will feel a sense of security about their job.

If employees are less engaged during system use (ie, low cognitive absorption use) or not using features of the new system for tasks (ie, low deep structure use), they are either using inefficient means for performing tasks or not performing their tasks at all. As a result of not using the system, employees would experience a decrease in work productivity, resulting in dissatisfaction toward the job. Employees might also face negative reactions from supervi sors. As a result, they might feel that they have become inefficient at work and might lose their job, leading to increased job insecurity.

Hypothesis 3A,B During the shakedown phase of functional and enterprise systems implementation, measures of system use are positively associated with (A) job satisfaction and (B) job security.

## 3.4 | Linking postimplementation measures of system use and negative dispositions to job

For a functional system, increased lean measures of system use indicates that employees are comfortable with a new system and using it to accomplish tasks related to the functional areas that the system is supposed to support. Since a functional system is designed for specific work contexts (McAfee, 2006), as employees start leveraging the system for their work, they would feel that their work stress has decreased (because the functional system helped them accom plish tasks). Building on EDT, employees would feel that their expectation for the new system are met, leading to favourable reactions toward the system (Lankton et al., 2014). In the context of PSM system that we investigated, instead of using a paper‐based approach, the system facilitated capturing plant‐level incidents and ensured occupa tional safety. Consequently, employees did not have to spend time on paper work for capturing plant‐level incidents and instead got their tasks executed quickly using the new PSM system, leading to reduced fatigue and stress. As a result, they were likely to sense a decrease in apprehension toward using the new system and eventually got comfort able with their new job routines (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016), which would lead to a lower levels of job anxiety. In contrast, reduced frequency, duration, and intensity of use would imply that the employees are feeling frustrated with a new system and are not able to complete their tasks using it. This would lead to increasing stress, avoiding work, or applying workarounds to avoid the system use (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016). We hypothesize that this would lead to an increase in employee emotional exhaustion and job anxiety during the shakedown phase

Increase in rich measures of system use would lead to a decrease in the level of emotional exhaustion. With focused system use, employees would develop a level of comfort with a new system. Although a functional system such as PSM is peripheral to the core business functions of an organization, it still has significant implications for indi vidual employee at the plant who need to use the system to document safety‐related issues. Since the system is geared toward the employees working at the plant, they would try to make the system a routine part of their job rather than being a challenge in performing their tasks. They would not look for workarounds and thus explore the system features and how those could benefit their work. Consistent with EDT, such employees would develop th right set of expectations with the system and would be overall satisfied with how the system assists them in thei work. Consequently, we hypothesize that their emotional exhaustion and job anxiety would decrease

For an enterprise system such as the PLM, employees have to execute critical business processes of an organization using the system such as product design, development, and manufacturing. As a result, using an enterprise system involves working under the pressure of not committing any errors in the work. In addition, PLM systems incorporate capabilities that allow managers or supervisors to continuously monitor the work done by the employees using the system. We posit that in such a context, an increase in lean measures of system use might not always be a positive indicator. We suggest that increase in lean measures of use might be a consequence of the employees not being able to do their work efficiently using the new system. While the employees might spend more time on the system, use th system frequently, and work on the system with higher intensity, it might not necessarily lead to efficient task execution. Enterprise systems are core to the efficient execution of organizational business processes. PLM systems for example, play a critical role in managing the entire product line. As a result, employees using these systems feel constant pressure of executing their work efficiently and with minimal errors. Because of the added audit trail and supervision capabilities of a PLM system, the employees would more likely perceive a sense of fear of receiving negative reviews from their managers/supervisors if they commit minor errors (Lee & Ashforth, 1990, 1996) Consequently, the employees might feel anxiety and emotionally drained at the job (Lee & Ashforth, 1996; Wright & Cropanzano, 1998).

Because of the work pressure resulting from the fact that an enterprise system caters to core business functions we speculate that even with increased rich measures of system use, employees would feel emotionally drained and frustrated with the new system. As enterprise systems such as PLM incorporate monitoring capabilities for management, this feature would likely make the employees feel that they are under constant scrutiny for job performance Consequently, even though employees show a high level of cognitive absorption use and deep structure use, they may feel dissatisfied with the system. Per EDT, this represents a scenario where the system does not meet the expectations of employees since it includes capabilities that increase employees' work pressures, leading to negative emotions (Lankton et al., 2014). The expectation to execute time‐intensive business processes with minimal errors on a new system would make them feel exhausted and frustrated with the new system. The audit trail capabilities that allows management to track specific work tasks using the system may create employee fear that they might get negative reactions from management even if they commit a minor error during work execution.

Further, if employees are not engaged in using a new system, it could mean that the system is acting as a source of frustration for employees instead of facilitating their tasks. Also, if employees do not explore the features of the new system, they will remain unaware of the support that the system offers for their work. Employees will keep using traditional ways of doing work rather than adopting the enhanced functionalities of the new system (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). This will lead to a feeling of disappointment among employees due to their perception that the system is of no use to them or that it poses challenges they cannot meet. Overall, employees would be unhappy with the system and would be dissatisfied with aspects of their job that requires use of the system. Further, employees might become tensed if their tasks are not accomplished, leading to an increasing pressure from their managers and peers. Employees will more likely develop a sense of apprehension that they will not be able to get accustomed to the system and will not be able to perform their tasks. This would lead to an increase in emotional exhaustion and job anxiety. Similar to the positive influence of lean measures of enterprise system use on emotional exhaustion and job anxiety, we posit that increase in rich measures of enterprise system use would lead to high levels of emotional exhaustion and job anxiety.

## 3.5 | Linking postimplementation system use and dispositions to organization

After a functional system implementation, if employees exhibit high levels of lean measures of use, they would start realizing the benefits of the system immediately. Functional system is designed for individual tasks within a specific work context, Conseguently, emplovees are able to realize the benefits quickly (McAfee 2006) Through cognitive absorption and deep structure use, employees would explore the functionalities of the system and leverage them for work. Plant safety management is the functional system that replaced the paper‐based system that was used pre viously for incident management at a plant in our study, Conseguently, the PSM system is a significant improvement in the capability to capture plant‐level incidents and ensure occupational and health safety. Realizing this potential of a system could lead to a sense of trust in the management's decision making and toward the organization. Wit increased lean and rich measures of system use, employees would realize that the functional system improves thei work efficiency. Consistent with EDT, these employees would develop an appropriate set of expectations with the system and would develop a sense of satisfaction with it. During the usage stage, employees would be able to adjus their cognitive beliefs toward the system's capabilities and understand how the system can be used for their work Through consistent use, employees would be able to achieve high levels of productivity and would thus develop positive attitude toward the system. Consequently, they would see the management's decision to implement a new system as beneficial to them (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), a belief that would positively influence their commit ment toward the organization.

Similarly, through continued use of the system, employees would be able to concentrate on their tasks and accomplish them in less time than before. Consequently, they would receive positive reviews from their managers, leading to an increased trust that the organization makes decisions that are beneficial to them. Through deep structure use, employees would understand the features of the system that could be beneficial for work. This would convince them that the system has been carefully designed to suit their work requirements (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). On the basis of EDT, such employees would reach the equilibrium stage quickly and would feel an overall sense of satisfaction since the system meets their expectations. As a result, employees would be convinced that the management makes decisions keeping their interests in mind. Consequently, they would develop a high level of trust toward the organization.

Hypothesis 5A,B: During the shakedown phase of a functional system implementation, measures of sys tem use are positively associated with (A) organizational commitment and (B) organizational trust

Enterprise systems are integral to core business processes (McAfee, 2006) and are consequently used by employees who are responsible for those processes. As noted earlier, PLM system in the context of this study is used by employees responsible for product design, development, and manufacturing. Because of the system's audit trai capabilities that allow for close monitoring of employee work by management, we posit that employees would work on the system more frequently, for longer hours, and with greater intensity. Because of monitoring capabilities, employees might feel that they are under constant supervision of the management. On the basis of EDT, such employees would feel that their expectations with the system are not met since it includes capabilities that they did not anticipate to be part of system, leading to negative emotions (Lankton et al., 2014). Employees may also show increased rich measures of use implying that they are working on the new enterprise system with a high level of engagement and using the various features for their work. This will be a significant change in their work routines as compared to old system that lacked audit trail capabilities. Consequently, employees might feel that the management does not trust them in executing work independently. Employees might feel less committed to their work and to organization. They would feel that decisions made by management increase their work pressure. They would perceive that the organization is not making decisions keeping their interest in mind.

## 4 | METHOD

We conducted 2 longitudinal studies in the context of 2 different system implementations in a large Fortune 500 manufacturer in the United States The company has over 60 000 emplovees in locations in the United States and aroung the world. Three waves of data were collected from users of the systems. Study 1 was conducted in the context of PSM system, a functional system for managing plant level occupational health and safety. Study 2 was conducted in the context of a PLM system, an enterprise system that supported business processes related product design, development, and manufacturing. We introduce these systems in earlier sections and provide more details in the next sub section. Our methodology was suitable for investigating the research questions related to the relationships between both the preimplementation and postimplementation constructs. Since our model captures employee dispositions to job and organization prior to and after the implementation, we captured these dispositions through 3 waves of surveys, using 3‐ and 6‐month periods of implementation stages (see Figure 2).

## 4.1 | Research sites, participants, and data collection

In study 1, the PSM system was implemented as part of the workplace occupational health and safety management process. It had functional modules related to hazard identification, workplace safety incident investigations, inspections and audits, and health and safety action management. We received a list of 494 plant‐level employees who would participate in the training related to the PSM system. These employees were responsible for managing safety at the plant level and were the intended users of the system (ie, plant supervisors, production line leaders, nurses, and plant managers). In study 2, the PLM system offered a complete view of product records, including finished products, manufactur ing processes, bills of materials, ingredients, and packaging materials. Product lifecycle management enabled collaborative business processes to create, review, and approve all product specifications included in the product record throughout the organization. The system also supported business processes related to product development, sales and inventory management. We received a list of 386 employees who were going to participate in training related to the PLM system. These employees were from business units related to product design, research and development, product testing, production management, and inventory management. We invited employees from all levels (ie, managers and line employees) to participate in the ${ \sf T } _ { 1 }$ survey before the training. ## 5.2 | Structural model results

## 5.2.1 | Predicting system use

We hypothesized that preimplementation job and organizational dispositions would have a direct effect on postimplementation system use. Results indicate that controlling for behavioural intention and facilitating conditions, disposition constructs were significantly associated with system use (Table 4). Positive effect of job satisfaction on cognitive absorption use was supported in both studies (Hypothesis 1B). Similarly, negative effect of emotiona exhaustion on lean use and cognitive absorption use was supported in both studies (Hypothesis 1C,D). Effect of orga nizational trust on cognitive absorption use was supported in both studies while the effect on deep structure use was supported in study 1 (Hypothesis 2B). Employees' preimplementation organizational commitment had no significant association with system use in both studies. Our model explained 26% and 25% of the variance in lean use, 18% and 17% of the variance in cognitive absorption use, and 27% and 22% of the variance in deep structure use in studies 1 and 2, respectively (see Table 4).

## 5.2.2 | Predicting job outcomes

We hypothesized that postimplementation system use would have direct effects on postimplementation job and organizational dispositions. Results indicate that most hypothesized effects were supported for both studies (Table 5). For example, effect of cognitive absorption use and deep structure use on job satisfaction was significant in both studies (Hypothesis 3A). Similarly, effect of cognitive absorption use on job security was supported in study 2, and effect of deep structure use on job security was supported in Study 1 (Hypothesis 3B). ## 6 | DISCUSSION

We accomplished 2 objectives—(1) we examined how employees' preimplementation dispositions to job and organization influenced system use and (2) we showed that system use influenced employee dispositions to job and orga nizations postimplementation. We conducted 2 longitudinal studies and found support for our research model Next, we discuss theoretical contributions, practical implications, and limitations.

## 6.1 | Theoretical contributions

This study makes at least 3 theoretical contributions. First, it offers theoretical explanations to known organizationa challenges related to new system implementations that span across preimplementation and postimplementation phases. We focus on individual‐level constructs relevant to employee dispositions to job and organization, and postimplementation system use. Organizational and job disposition constructs are relevant to preimplementation and postimplementation phases. Although the constructs in our model have been previously studied in IS, there is limited research that offers insights on interrelationships among these constructs across phases of system implementation. Our research model captures employee dispositions at different points of time during a system implementation. Our studies suggest that employee dispositions to job and organization are key to motivating employees to use a system. Broadly, our findings suggest that employees who are happy with their job and feel a strong sense of association with the organization are willing to face challenges introduced by a new system. Also, it is important that employees thoroughly use the newly implemented system for their work to achieve overall benefits in terms of work efficiency performance evaluation, job satisfaction, and security. Such employees are likely to have positive dispositions to jo and organization after the implementation of a new system

Second, we contribute to 2 separate streams of literature on system use: (1) interrelationships between job and organization‐related constructs and system use constructs, which have not been studied extensively to date (Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), and (2) literature of rich use measures, cognitive absorption use, and deep structure use (Burton‐Jones & Straub, 2006). Our model focuses on determinants and consequences of lean and rich measures of use. While lean measures of use capture how employees are using a new system and whether they are struggling with its features, and rich use measures capture whether employees are exploiting the new system and feeling absorbed in their work, rich measures are stronger indicators of an employee's use of new system. When employees explore features of new system and get engrossed in work, they get their work done efficiently. This leads to an increased level of positive dispositions to job and organization and reduced level of job stress and anxiety. By proposing rich measures as antecedents of employee dispositions to job and organization, we integrate rich measures into the nomological network of system implementation characteristics. We also extend the work by Bala and Venkatesh (2013) and Morris and Venkatesh (2010) on job‐related constructs that addressed the influence of system implementations on job characteristics and job outcomes. We focus on a different set of variables relevant to employees' reactions to a new system implementation.

This work leverages EDT by focusing on process of confirmation/disconfirmation driven by an emplovee's disposition to job and organization. In this regard, we contribute to existing research by suggesting that in addition to expectations and perceptions toward a new system, factors that might not be related to the system (such as the job and organization dispositions that we considered) are also important determinants of use. Recent research has focused on investigating external factors (termed as situational context factors), which in addition to individual expectations from a technology, can influence technology use (Lankton et al., 2016; Song et al., 2016). W contribute to this stream of work by suggesting specific individual factors that influence system use. Specifically, the individual dispositions we incorporated in the model highlight individual emotions that drive individua expectations and resultant use of system. Similarly, we also incorporate organizational dispositions that capture individual attitude toward the organization as another set of factors that influence the confirmation/disconfirmation process.

Finally, our findings show that system use may not necessarily lead to favourable employee dispositions. For instance, we found that lean measures of use had positive influence on job anxiety and emotional exhaustion, and negative influence on organizational commitment and trust in study 2 (PLM system). Since lean measures capture how system is being used by employees, findings suggest that employees may develop a heightened level of anxiety and exhaustion when they have to use features of a system frequently and for a long duration. Also, deep structure use had a positive influence on job anxiety and emotional exhaustion in study 2. These findings offer a paradox— although system use had a positive influence on job satisfaction and job security, it had a negative influence on other employee dispositions.

We suggest that the reason for the paradox in study 2 is related to the nature of the PLM system and its role in organization. This system is a key part of core business processes of a manufacturing organization since it keeps track of critical activities of a manufacturer—to design, develop, and manufacture products. Consequently, the system is central to what the employees do as part of their job. In addition, as these systems include audit trail capabilities, a key reason for organizations to implement these systems is to closely monitor how, product design and development projects are progressing and how employees are performing in these projects. It is likely that sinc employees could accomplish their work efficiently after a new system implementation, they were satisfied with their job (Venkatesh et al., 2010). However, because of audit trail capabilities, employees might have felt the pressure to perform the work under management's scrutiny, where concern over minor mistakes could increase technostress levels. Consequently, we suspect that increasing the level of system use in this study led to a heightened level of anxiety and emotional exhaustion in the first 3 months postimplementation. Our results also indicate that an increase in level of system use led to decreasing levels of organizational commitment and trust. We suspect that a possible reason is that employees who used the system eventually discovered its capabilities that helped management monitor their day‐to‐day work.

In contrast, the PSM system in study 1 was not central to core business processes. Although some employee used it regularly, the system was implemented to capture plant‐level incidents and offer a mechanism to ensure occupational and health safety. It had an overall positive impact because employees felt it made it easy to capture these safety incidents and associated responses. Further, our interviews revealed that prior to implementation of thi system, incident management was primarily done using paper‐based forms and documents. Therefore, the PSM system led to significant improvements in the management of health and safety issues and reduction of related risks Although peripheral to core business processes of the organization, the PSM system had significant positive implica tions for individual employees and their daily work practices at the plant.

## 6.2 | Practical implications

We suggest 2 types of system use interventions: preimplementation and postimplementation. Preimplementation interventions include close consideration and monitoring of employees' reactions to a new system. Organizations can hire trainers and consultants to conduct workshops for employees, which would benefit those who are concerned with negative work changes introduced by the system. The sessions/workshops should be carefully designed with th goal of making employees anticipate and understand changes in their job role, work schedules, and the overall business processes to ensure that they prepare themselves for upcoming changes. In addition, as new systems may be complex and difficult to master in a short period depending on employees' skill levels, managers should talk to their employees t ensure that they are not undergoing undue work stress or are not worried about their postimplementation job role.

Organizations should take measures to encourage employees to set appropriate expectations from a new system. It is critical for supervisors to talk to employees and provide information about the new system, its core func tionalities, and impact on day‐to‐day work activities. In addition, employees should be provided timely updates via emails, meetings, or newsletters regarding the progress of implementation and encourage them to ask questions about it as needed. This will allow employees to prepare themselves for the change in their work processes. It is also important to organize workshops or symposiums that will facilitate interactions between employees regarding the system and help form appropriate expectations. Eventual goal of such programs should be to facilitat exchange of useful information and to create a sense of shared workplace community, in that employees dealing with the system are experiencing some of its challenges together and are made to feel important part of a company's improvement process

Similarly, preimplementation interventions such as having a well‐structured incentive program for participation in system development activities and management support during ES training could be effective in reducing negative reactions. To obtain useful employee feedback before implementation, the system development team can invite future users to evaluate modules/components of a system such as the interface, critical functionalities, and integrated tools in relation their job processes. Importantly, preimplementation testing phase of a system coul incorporate employee input, thus assuring employees that the system is being developed per their expectations It is vital that users get to interact with the preimplementation version (prototype) of the system, understand the goals new system is being designed to achieve, and realize potential benefits by evaluating the preimplementation prototypes. These activities could thus develop a sense that the new system is being designed for employees' ben efit and with their input, and that challenges related to implementation will be anticipated, addressed, and shared

Postimplementation interventions could incorporate activities such as monitoring of system use and checking whether employees perceive the system as beneficial. Managers can monitor system use behaviour to ensure quick issue resolution. Managers should keep in touch with employees and track any unusual behaviour, such as high absence rates or decrease in productivity. For employees that are stressed about job changes, organization should hire consultants that can help such employees and ensure that their job security will not be affected due to stress. In addition, managers should be concerned that employees are not spending excessive time at work and get time to relax during work hours. Organization can also arrange social events and workshops so that employees can share concerns with colleagues. For example, suggestions employees can share about using the system with colleagues in a similar job role are more valuable than usual training sessions, since they can relate to concerns of those colleagues.

## 6.3 | Limitations and future research directions

Limitations of this study should be addressed in future research. First, we did not incorporate characteristics of th new IS that could play a role in predicting employee outcomes. Future research should consider individual‐level per ceptions a new system and employee work processes, such as complexity and rigidity (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Such perceptual characteristics measured at the individual level could moderate the influence of system use on disposi tional constructs that we examined herein. Factors such as characteristics of new system, employees' skill levels wit a new system, and external factors such as training and management support could offer insights on relationships that we hypothesized. Consequently, considering such factors could provide new understandings on the role managers and trainers play during preimplementation and postimplementation stages.

Second, we collected data from a single organization. Although the data were analysed in the context of 2 sys tems, it is possible that employees in other organizations will have different experiences with these systems. Future research should examine the influence of system use in other organizations and in the context of other systems. Third, in addition to the job and organizational disposition constructs. future research should examine the influence of team constructs, such as a team's shared mental model about tasks and their information‐sharing behaviours. Empirica measurement of these constructs could provide insights on how team performance is influenced by the introductio of a new system. Although individual factors are important to the success of a new system, it is common for employees to collaborate with their teams/colleagues. System use in relation to employee management thus plays the role of a crucial communication vehicle in an organization that serves to facilitate collaboration. Understandin team‐level constructs in this context can provide insights on how social, psychological, and organizational cultur influences negative reactions toward a new system.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

In this paper, we developed and tested a model that postulates that employee dispositions to job and organization before implementation of a system will explain employees' use of a system. Further, employees' use of a system wil influence job and organizational dispositions after the implementation. Our model incorporates lean and rich conceptualizations of system use and a set of important employee dispositions from the organizational behaviour literature. We found strong support for our research model and hypotheses. Our findings offer insights on relationships between employee dispositions and system use, and thus address the call for research to link system use and its antecedents and consequences. We also contribute to the organizational behaviour literature on work outcomes by offering an in‐depth explanation of how system use is associated with these outcomes. Further, our research offers insights on potentia sources and consequences of underuse of a system and on developing intervention strategies to minimize such underuse
