# All Are Not Equal: An Examination of the Economic Returns to Different Forms of Participation in Open Source Software Communities

Il-Horn Hann

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, ihann@rhsmith.umd.edu

Jeffrey A. Roberts

Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Palumbo–Donahue School of Business, Duquesne University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15282, robertsj1503@duq.edu

Sandra A. Slaughter

Ernest Scheller Jr. College of Business, Georgia Institute of Technology, Atlanta, Georgia 30308, sandra.slaughter@scheller.gatech.edu

pen source software (OSS) communities live and die with the continuous contributions of programmers who often participate without direct remuneration. An intriguing question is whether such sustained participation in OSS projects yields economic benefits to the participants. Moreover, as participants engage in OSS projects, they take on different roles and activities in the community. This raises additional questions of whether different forms of participation in OSS communities are associated with different economic rewards and, if so, in which contexts. In this paper, we draw upon theories of signaling and job matching to hypothesize that participants who possess “proof” of their skills in OSS projects are financially rewarded for their activities in the labor market. More specifically, we distinguish between participation in OSS communities that is associated with a signaling value for unobserved productivity characteristics and an additional value that accrues to participants whose OSS roles and activities match those in their paid employment. Following a cohort of OSS programmers over a six-year period, we empirically examine the wages and OSS performance of participants in three of the foremost OSS projects operating within the Apache Software Foundation. Controlling for individual characteristics and other wage-related factors, our findings reveal that credentials earned through a merit-based ranking system are associated with as much as an 18% increase in wages. Moreover, we find that participants who have OSS project management responsibilities receive additional financial rewards if their professional job is in IT management. These findings suggest that rank within an OSS meritocracy is a credible and precise signal of participants’ productive capacity and that participants’ roles and activities in an OSS community have additional financial value when aligned with their paid employment.

## Introduction

The capacity of the Internet to make information flow more efficiently has given volunteering-based organizations unprecedented reach to both their members and sponsors and has greatly facilitated the coordination of their activities. Indeed, new forms of organized volunteering have emerged that take advantage of the Internet as an inexpensive and effective communication vehicle. A case in point is open source software (OSS) communities. In OSS, communities are formed by participants who engage in public software development in which they read, modify, and redistribute the software source code using the Internet (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). OSS communities have increasing significance to commercial activity (Hyatt 2008). Driven by a large base of online contributors, OSS projects such as Linux, Apache, and Mozilla represent a significant competitive challenge to industry leaders in their respective software categories.<sup>1</sup> Vendors of proprietary software products such as Microsoft, therefore, perceive OSS products as a significant and growing source of competition (Lohr 2007).

Given the increased commercial interest in OSS software, a widely debated question is whether there are any economic returns to OSS participants who contribute their efforts to these projects. Some researchers have documented a variety of social benefits to OSS participation such as enjoyment and social interaction (David and Shapiro 2008). Other researchers, most prominently Lerner and Tirole (2002), have argued conceptually that OSS participation may yield subsequent economic rewards relating to contributors’ future marketability.

While researchers have posited the economic benefits of OSS participation, to our knowledge, there is no empirical evidence of the existence and scope of these economic benefits. Also unknown is what type of involvement is rewarded. Specifically, it is not known whether there are different economic rewards associated with different forms of OSS participation. This is an important gap in knowledge of OSS participation because participants vary widely in the nature of their activities in OSS communities. Some participants have a single or limited encounter with an OSS project, whereas others choose to get involved at a deeper level and to make sustained contributions (Setia et al. 2012). Some participants take on primarily technical roles in OSS projects, fixing problems in the software, extending existing functions, or adding new features to the code. Other participants have technical management responsibilities for one or more projects and have the authority to make technical and managerial decisions that determine project content and direction as well as to maintain project plans and release schedules.

If different forms of OSS participation are rewarded differently, this raises an additional question: in which contexts would each form of participation be most valued? Some participants work in jobs directly related to their roles and activities in OSS communities, but other participants work in unrelated jobs. Would employers recognize and reward the alignment between OSS participation and participants’ paid employment? To our knowledge, there is no research that has addressed this question. Given the different forms of participation in OSS communities, it is important to develop a more nuanced theory of the relationship between OSS roles and activities and economic rewards. This study sets out to address this gap in our current knowledge. We draw upon two theories from labor economics—signaling theory (Spence 1973) and job matching theory (Hartog 1981, 1986)—to hypothesize the relationship between economic rewards and different forms of OSS participation. These theories are extensions of human capital theory (Becker 1975), which posits a relationship between workers’ marketable skills and wages.

We empirically evaluate our hypotheses by examining the wages and OSS experience of a cohort of participants in three prominent projects in the Apache OSS community over a six-year period. Using panel data models, we find that achieving a higher status in the merit-based ranking within the OSS community is associated with up to an 18% increase in participants’ wages, depending on the rank attained. This suggests that holding a rank within the OSS community serves as a credible signal of the productive capacity of a programmer. In addition, we find that highly ranked OSS participants who have taken on OSS project management obligations are further rewarded by their employers if they also occupy IT management positions within their firms. Our results suggest that OSS communities provide a venue for evaluating and demonstrating technical leadership skills, skills that are especially valuable in the IT labor market for participants who hold leadership roles in their paid employment. We conduct a variety of analyses to rule out alternative explanations for our findings such as simultaneous discovery of ability by the OSS community and the employer and self-selection. We also estimate different specifications of our model to demonstrate the robustness of our results.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to provide empirical evidence of the financial benefits associated with participation in OSS communities. Our study makes a new contribution to the literature by theorizing and empirically demonstrating that different forms of OSS participation are rewarded differently and by identifying the contexts in which certain forms of OSS participation are more valued. In the next section, we develop theoretical explanations and hypotheses of how participating in OSS communities can enhance wages.

## Explaining the Relationship Between Wages and Open Source Participation

Financial returns to participation in OSS communities can be viewed from a labor economics perspective. From this perspective, participation in OSS communities can provide financial returns to participants through two complementary venues: signaling and job matching. We draw upon these theories to develop our hypotheses of economic returns to OSS participation.

## A Signaling Explanation of Economic Returns to OSS Participation

Signaling or sorting theories argue that there are worker traits that employers cannot directly observe that relate to workers’ marginal productivity and that are known to the worker but not the employer. Such qualities are at times seemingly banal but may be important to employers. For example, individuals who demonstrate perseverance (such as graduating from high school) are more likely to exhibit both lower quit rates as well as lower levels of absenteeism (Weiss 1995). Generally, employers pay higher wages for workers who have such traits as workers with these traits are more productive. However, employers are unable to distinguish among workers given the intangible nature of such traits. Signaling or sorting theories further assume that such desirable intangible traits are correlated with credentials that can be observed, such as an educational credential (Spence 1973). For example, a worker’s completion of an educational degree implies the existence of an unobservable attribute (say, perseverance) that relates to the worker’s productivity. Employers may therefore use credentials as proxies for desirable intangible traits to “sort” or otherwise differentiate among workers in hiring and setting wages. Signaling is especially salient in occupations where screening of workers is important and difficult (e.g., where there are high costs to the firm of training, hiring, absenteeism, and quits; Weiss 1995). In such occupations, employers seek valid information about individuals’ abilities and can use individuals’ accomplishments or certifications as signals of quality to sort higher- from lower-ability types.

Software engineering is an occupation in which signaling is likely to be pertinent. Programming tasks require significant autonomy and creativity where the behaviors that transform inputs to outputs are not well understood (Kirsch 1996, Weinberg 1998). As several research streams have noted, assessing software development performance is extremely challenging. Certain aspects of software, such as complexity, task interdependence, and the inability to visualize the product and to articulate requirements, make it difficult to control, monitor, and assess the software development process (Brooks 1995). The software contracting literature has used these characteristics to model information asymmetries that result in agency problems (Whang 1992). Control and coordination theory (Kirsch 1996) further argues that software development is a nonroutine, complex task where different stakeholders have different extents of knowledge about the process. These characteristics of the software task make it very difficult for employers to discern developers’ abilities.

Participating in OSS projects affords a venue in which such abilities can be discerned by highly skilled peers and rewarded with a higher rank or standing within the project (Lerner and Tirole 2002, von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). Software development requires skills (traits) that can often be transferred (valuable) to other software projects (Boh et al. 2007). This implies that the capabilities relevant to success in OSS projects are also likely to be relevant to a participant’s professional employment. In this case, OSS participation serves as a signal of desirable, although imperfectly observable, productive capacities (Lerner and Tirole 2002, Weiss 1995).

The information conveyed by OSS participation is especially valuable as there is a wide range of productivity among programmers. One study of “star” programmers found that the top 1% of programmers were 1,272% more efficient than the average (Goleman 1998). Indeed, controlled experiments of software developers suggest that the very best developers are anywhere from 4 to 28 times as productive as the very worst developers (e.g., Sackman et al. 1968, Prechelt 2000). Although it may be relatively easy to identify the few “star” programmers, it could be much more difficult for employers to identify the above average programmers who have a good understanding of the problem and often develop an efficient solution for the problem at hand. Yet at the same time it may be valuable to employers to distinguish the “next best” tier of developers who are far more productive than the average.

Further, measures of OSS experience, such as the level of effort invested in participation or the level of code contributions, per se, might not be the best indicators of productive capacity. OSS projects represent large-scale, distributed development projects involving thousands of contributions from hundreds of developers (Mockus et al. 2000). High-ability participants typically make many submissions to the code base, but it could be the depth of their understanding, the efficient design of their solutions, or their ability to organize and manage others and get them “on board” with their ideas and strategies that represent the true quality of their contributions (von Krogh et al. 2003). Although possible, as a practical matter, it may be difficult for employers to efficiently evaluate these desirable productive qualities based on measures of OSS experience. Thus, employers may value a reliable proxy that is correlated with these characteristics and is indicative of successful OSS project participation. If employers can use OSS participation as a sorting mechanism, then the existence of a “credential” or observable measure of successful participation would allow them to make inferences about a developer’s productive capacity.

Many OSS communities are structured as meritocracies and reward their best performers with a higher status or rank within the community. Indeed, Eric Raymond, in his influential OSS manifesto “The Cathedral and the Bazaar,” was perhaps the first to acknowledge the role of merit and meritocracy in OSS projects: “In the hacker community, by contrast, one’s work is one’s statement. There’s a very strict meritocracy (the best craftsmanship wins) and there’s a strong ethos that quality should (indeed must) be left to speak for itself” (Raymond 2001, p. 89). Since Raymond’s writing, the use of meritocratic governance models has gained prominence as the explicit governance approach to many important OSS projects such as Linux (Ubuntu), Linux (Fedora), Mozilla, Python, and Apache, among others. In OSS projects with meritocratic governance models, status, responsibility, and benefits are commensurate with contribution. Ranks or roles in these OSS meritocracies are granted based on the intensity of the participant’s involvement in and contributions to the community. For example, in Mozilla, “leadership roles are granted based on how active an individual is within the community as well as the quality and nature of his or her contributions. This meritocracy is a resilient and effective way to guide (Mozilla’s) global community.”<sup>2</sup>

The existence of ranks or roles in OSS meritocracies makes them particularly appropriate to evaluate a signaling explanation of economic returns to OSS participation. As observed by Tyler et al. (2000), an empirical analysis of signals in labor markets requires exogenous variation in the signal status among individuals with similar levels of human capital. Participants in OSS meritocracies possess such a variable or credential—their rank within the community. Employers can then use such a “signal” of success as a sorting mechanism in the IT labor market.

As we have noted, OSS meritocracies use increasing levels of status or rank to recognize participants who have distinguished themselves through the sustained quality and quantity of their contributions. Such recognition suggests that these participants may possess qualities that are difficult to detect but valuable to employers. In the Apache Software Foundation (ASF), for example, there are five observable levels of recognition or rank—“developer,” “committer,” “project management committee member,” “ASF member,” and “ASF board member.” Each level in the ASF hierarchy represents an increasing degree of participation, involvement, responsibility, and authority in the community (Mockus et al. 2000). Comparable rank hierarchies can be observed in other OSS meritocracies.

Such hierarchies of roles in OSS meritocracies make it possible to examine whether participants holding higher ranks in the meritocracy are rewarded more than participants holding lower ranks. As we have described earlier, signaling theory (Spence 1973) argues that employers rely on signals such as educational credentials as proxies for valuable but difficult to observe qualities of workers, like ability, character, motivation, and perseverance. Subsequent to Spence’s seminal work, researchers have refined signaling theory to pose differences among levels of credentials, discriminating signals of higher quality from signals of lower quality (Belman and Heywood 1991, Hungerford and Solon 1987). From this view, there is a correspondence between the level of the signal and the level of the worker’s (unobserved) productivity. That is, a signal of higher quality (such as completing a bachelor’s degree) is thought to reflect a higher level of worker productivity than is a signal of lower quality (such as completing high school), all else equal. Empirically, studies on the returns to schooling have found support for this more nuanced perspective of signaling, (e.g., Belman and Heywood 1991).

In OSS meritocracies, progression to a higher rank requires a significant and deep level of involvement in the community as well as numerous and consistently high quality contributions. Participants who achieve a higher rank receive increased responsibilities and decision-making rights in the community. To illustrate, in the Mozilla hierarchy, module owners lead the development of a module’s code, super-reviewers review the code and approve it for checking into the code base, release drivers provide project management for milestone releases, and ultimate decision makers are trusted members of the community who have the final say in the case of disputes. Given the requirements for advancement to a higher rank in an OSS meritocracy, achievement of a higher rank could operate as a signal of higher quality compared to achievement of a lower rank. In effect, similar to levels of educational credentials, ranks in OSS meritocracies could help employers to sort among qualified workers because those holding higher ranks could be considered to possess higher ability and marginal productivity. This leads us to posit the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Participants with a higher rank in an OSS community earn higher wages than participants with a lower rank.

## A Job Matching Explanation of Economic Returns to OSS Participation

We have hypothesized that a participant’s rank in an OSS community can provide a signal of desirable but difficult-to-observe skills and abilities and that employers can use such information to infer and financially reward the participant’s anticipated higher marginal productivity. A participant’s rank in an OSS community can also provide information about the participant’s specific type of skills and abilities. If so, then employers can use this information to assess the relatedness of those skills and abilities to the participant’s paid employment. Job matching theory (Hartog 1986) provides an explanation of why information about relatedness of skills and abilities would translate into higher economic returns.

Like signaling theory, the theory of “job matching” or “job assignment/sorting” (e.g., Jovanovic 1979;

Hartog 1981, 1986; Woodcock 2010) assumes that workers are heterogeneous in their knowledge, skills, and abilities. Job matching theory also assumes that the levels of these attributes affect workers’ marginal productivity at work and therefore their wages (Becker 1975). In addition, jobs are assumed to have particular requirements for knowledge, skills, and abilities. Jobs are heterogeneous in this regard because some jobs are more complicated or difficult than are others. Jobs will, therefore, differ in complexity and their skill requirements (Hartog 1986, Jovanovic 1979).

According to job matching theory, a worker’s marginal productivity is determined by the quality of the match between the job’s skill requirements and the worker’s skills (Hartog 1986). That is, for a given job, a worker whose skills fit the requirements of that job will perform at a higher level than a worker whose skills do not. A higher marginal productivity due to a good job match is rewarded with a positive wage differential (Hartog 1981). The basic idea is that if a worker with a certain level or type of skills has a job that requires a different level or type of skills, there is a limitation to the utilization of the worker’s skills and a “ceiling” on the worker’s productivity, resulting in lower wages than for a worker whose skills are properly matched to job skill requirements.

Empirical studies have found support for job matching theory (e.g., Allen and Van der Welden 2001, Robst 2007). A recent study based on surveys of college graduates conducted by the National Science Foundation found that graduates who reported working in their major field of study earned more than those who were partially or completely mismatched (i.e., working in an occupation different from their college major). Mismatched workers also earned less than other workers in the same occupation (Robst 2007). Another study of Dutch workers found that worker-job mismatches affected wages, job satisfaction, turnover, and on-the-job search (Allen and Van der Welden 2001).

Given the benefits of a good job match, ideally, workers and jobs would “sort” so that workers’ skills would match the skill requirements of their jobs. However, it is not easy to match workers to jobs. This is because there is much uncertainty about whether the worker’s skills and abilities fit the required skills and abilities for the job (Hartog 1981, Woodcock 2010). Therefore, a central issue in the literature on job matching is how to optimally match workers to jobs.

In the context of OSS meritocracies, a participant’s rank or role in the community reveals information about the types of activities in which the participant engages and excels, and this information could reduce uncertainty about the match between participants and their paid job. Of the levels of rank in

OSS meritocracies, typically participants in the lowest ranks have predominantly technical responsibilities. In the ASF, for example, developers and committers create software code, and committers have the additional responsibility to commit the code to the code base. In Mozilla, similar activities are performed by module owners and super-reviewers, respectively, and in Python by developers and core developers.

Participants in succeeding ranks have distinctly different roles, typically with managerial responsibilities. Project management committee members (in the ASF) and release drivers (in Mozilla) have technical management responsibilities in actively managing their respective projects and in maintaining project plans and release schedules. Some OSS meritocracies (such as Fedora and the ASF) have elected board members who make important governance and strategic decisions for the community such as overseeing the creation and dissolution of projects; allocating of funds, equipment, and other resources to projects; and resolving issues regarding intellectual property and trademarks.

Given the discernable delineation of activities and responsibilities across OSS meritocracies, employers may utilize the information about the specific roles and activities of participants associated with particular ranks. Specifically, different ranks may suggest the existence of particular skills (such as technical skills or managerial skills) that are extremely advantageous for certain IT job positions. First, participants with technical roles in OSS communities may be well suited to IT technical jobs such as software engineering. Individuals who work in IT technical jobs and who also serve in technical roles in OSS projects may therefore earn an additional premium in their paid employment. This is because attaining a technical rank in an OSS meritocracy suggests that the individual possesses a high level of programming knowledge and productive technical abilities and because these attributes are especially applicable to IT technical jobs.

From the perspective of job matching theory, a technical OSS rank held by a participant can therefore serve as an indication of a “match” when the participant holds an IT technical job, and evidence of the match should provide valuable information to the employer. Recall our earlier discussion that software development is a challenging activity to evaluate and that it is difficult for employers to discern the skills and abilities of their workers. Although employees may demonstrate their productive capacity on a day to day basis in their paid job, there still may be uncertainty about the match (Woodcock 2010). A participant holding a technical OSS rank provides external validation of the participant’s match to an IT technical job. Given there is a match between OSS participation and the paid job, from job matching theory, these technically skilled OSS participants are expected to have a higher marginal productivity in their paid IT technical jobs than those OSS participants working in paid IT technical jobs who do not hold an OSS rank involving technical responsibilities, and this match should be reflected in a positive wage premium.

Hence, in addition to the direct effects of OSS rank on wages and controlling for other wage-related factors, we hypothesize:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> OSS participants working in IT technical jobs and holding an OSS rank that involves execution of the project’s technical responsibilities will earn even higher wages compared to OSS participants working in IT technical jobs and holding an OSS rank that does not involve such responsibilities.

Participants who progress beyond technical OSS ranks are recognized for exhibiting leadership and organizational skills in the OSS community. Because OSS projects are self-governed, participants who hold managerial OSS ranks must be able to effectively communicate software design decisions and marshal the requisite support to accomplish project goals. Such “soft” skills are very desirable for individuals in IT management jobs (Joseph et al. 2010, Lee et al. 1995) but are often difficult to discern. Thus, employers may further reward highly ranked participants working in IT management jobs. Similar to the logic of H2A, from the perspective of job matching theory, OSS participants holding managerial ranks in an OSS community can be inferred to have IT managerial skills. The rank can therefore suggest a “match” when the participant holds an IT managerial job for which those managerial skills are relevant, and evidence of the match should be reflected in a positive monetary premium because of the expected higher marginal productivity of those participants in their paid employment.

Hence, in addition to the direct effects of OSS rank on wages, and controlling for other wage-related factors, we expect the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> OSS participants working in IT managerial jobs and holding an OSS rank that involves execution of the project’s managerial responsibilities will earn even higher wages compared to OSS participants working in IT managerial jobs and holding an OSS rank that does not involve such responsibilities.

Finally, we consider the relative value of a match between OSS technical roles and technical IT jobs compared to the match between OSS managerial roles and managerial IT jobs. We expect that the wage impact of fit between an OSS participant’s management-oriented OSS rank and IT management job (posited in H2B) will exceed the wage impact of fit between an OSS participant’s technically oriented

OSS rank and IT technical job (posited in H2A) for several reasons.

The first reason has to do with the relative value and scarcity of technical leadership skills in the IT labor market. Project leaders are crucial to the success of software projects. Indeed, a common characteristic of failed software projects is the lack of effective project management (Schmidt et al. 2001). Software projects are often complex, risky, and difficult to manage (Barki et al. 2001). The challenge of leading a team of programmers arises not only from the nature of the project but also from the personalities of programmers, who are often characterized as “introverted” and preferring to work autonomously when possible (Weinberg 1998). In OSS communities, leadership may be especially challenging because the leader has no positional authority over other participants in the traditional employment sense (Markus et al. 2000). At the same time, the leader must also have high technical competence: “Programmers, being people who tend to value creative work and professional competence, tend to put their stock in people whom they perceive to be good at the things they do0 0 0” (Weinberg 1998, p. 80). The difficulty is that programmers who possess both technical and managerial capabilities are relatively scarce. Individuals entering the IT profession tend to be oriented more toward the technical skills and often lack the interpersonal skills necessary to manage and motivate others (Lee et al. 1995).

Second, while it is difficult to evaluate the technical capabilities of programmers, it is even more difficult to determine whether technically competent programmers have high levels of soft skills (Joseph et al. 2010). Therefore, information about a match between technical leadership skills and IT managerial jobs should be significantly more valuable in the IT labor market than information about a match between purely technical skills and IT technical jobs. This implies the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2C</sup> <sup>(H2C).</sup> OSS participants working in an IT management job and holding an OSS rank that involves execution of the project’s managerial responsibilities will earn even higher wages than OSS participants working in IT technical jobs and holding an OSS rank that involves execution of the project’s technical responsibilities.

## Research Setting and Data

## Research Setting: Apache Software Foundation Open Source Software Projects

We investigate the wages and the OSS roles and activities of participants in three prominent OSS projects under the control of the ASF.<sup>3</sup> The ASF is a not-forprofit corporation that provides the legal, organizational, and financial infrastructure for projects that develop a full-featured Web-server product offering. Apache is an ideal OSS community in which to test our hypotheses. First, as described earlier, Apache has a defined meritocracy with distinct roles or ranks (i.e., developer, committer, project management committee member, ASF member, and ASF board member). The main contribution of developers is the creation of software code, which typically entails fixing a problem in the software, extending existing functionality, or adding a new feature. Many participants have a single encounter with the project; however, some developers choose a deeper level of involvement and continue to make contributions. If developers’ contributions are significant and consistent over a period of time, they may be nominated for a promotion from developer to committer. Committers have greater responsibility because they have write access to the code repository and hence in effect make short-term decisions for the project.

Committers may be promoted to become project management committee members. Promotion to project management committee member is a consensus decision by the existing project management committee and is confirmed by the ASF board of directors. Project management committee members have the authority to make technical and project management decisions that determine project content and direction. Promotion to ASF board member is achieved via an annual election by existing ASF members. While project management committee members and board members govern the ASF, ASF members are legally the shareholders of the foundation. New ASF members are elected by current membership after demonstrating a meritorious contribution to the evolution and progress of the foundation. Attaining ASF membership requires nomination by an existing ASF member and election by secret ballot.

The ASF is well suited to evaluate our research questions and hypotheses. First, Apache ranks provide the hierarchical structure required to test our signaling hypotheses. Second, the variations in the activities and responsibilities associated with each of the ranks allow us to test our job match hypotheses. ASF projects enjoy wide acceptance both in the marketplace, where Apache maintains a dominant share of the Web server market (Netcraft 2010), and in the OSS developer community, where ASF projects consistently attract and retain the large number of participants that is vital for OSS project success (von

Krogh et al. 2003). For the six-year period under study, the projects examined experienced more than 150,000 changes from more than 1,300 different OSS developers. Apache is a high profile, successful OSS community. If OSS participation is to result in an economic benefit for the participant, we would expect to see this benefit especially for a popular and well known OSS community because the value of the signal from holding an OSS rank in such a community should be high.

## Data Source: Archival Data on Apache Participants Rank and OSS Experience

In addition to source and binary codes of the actual programs, Apache products include project websites, change logs, documentation, and developer communications in the form of email archives. From these products, we extracted information about each participant’s progression in the Apache meritocracy as well as source code contributions used to derive objective measures of OSS experience. Before actually joining an OSS project, potential developers typically observe the “lay of the land” regarding both the form and substance of project membership (von Krogh et al. 2003). In our study, a participant’s first source code contribution is considered a consummation of the joining decision, signaling entry into the meritocracy and the beginning of one’s Apache career. During the time period prior to making an actual contribution, any latent contributors are considered to be outside the Apache meritocracy. To determine each individual’s Apache career progression, we used archival data from three sources: the Apache developer website, contribution meta-data from the Concurrent Versioning System (CVS) revision control software, and minutes from the Apache Board of Directors meetings. Each Apache subproject maintains a separate developer website that includes a list of contributors and project management committee members. By observing changes to the archives over time, we are able to construct a time line for the promotion of individuals within each project.

Progression up the Apache career ladder is captured as a series of discrete transitions. The first transition is from latent contributor to an Apache developer. The second transition occurs when a developer is granted commit access to the project source code archives (i.e., becomes a committer). Both changes in rank are derived from CVS meta-data. We observe initial contributions and hence when developer status begins. Similarly, the transition from developer to committer occurs when contributors first exercise their newly acquired CVS commit privileges. Promotions from committer to the rank of project management committee member or to ASF member are announced each year at the annual board of directors meeting. Thus, in our panel, the transition to either project management committee member or to ASF member occurs on the date of the announcement. Lastly, transition to the rank of ASF board member occurs on the date of recognition of new board members as recorded in board meeting minutes.<sup>4</sup>

To establish objective measures of OSS experience, we collected data on individual contributions to Apache OSS projects. A submission to an OSS project is known as a “patch,” similar to a modification request in traditional software development environments. We follow the method introduced by Mockus et al. (2000) to reconstruct patches from source code archives. For each patch we extract and retain common software metrics including lines of code added and deleted, the date of submission, the names and number of source code files affected by the change, change log entries, and the list of patch authors. To account for differences in the lines of code metric between programming languages, we normalized each contribution to the function points metric using industry standard language conversion factors for the respective programming languages (Boehm et al. 2000).

## Data Source: Survey Data on Apache Participants’ Demography and Job History

To augment the archival data, we collected demographic and job history data using three secure Webbased surveys of a cohort of Apache participants. A cohort or panel design follows a group of people over a period of time. The primary advantage of a longitudinal cohort design (over a cross-sectional design) is that it reduces the influence of confounding variables because repeated measures are collected on the same individuals over time; this can provide stronger evidence of the causal association between the factors of interest and outcomes (Hakim 2000). In our study, we used a cohort sampling strategy to measure participants’ activities and roles in Apache over time and relate them to the participants’ corresponding wages. In each survey round, the respondent was asked to report the current year’s job and wages and the prior year’s job and wages; thus, there were six years of job and wage data collected. Dr. Roy Fielding, the chairman of the ASF at the time, introduced the first survey to 1,301 respondents via email in November 2001, and the first survey concluded in January 2002. Two hundred thirty-three email invitations were undeliverable. Of the remaining 1,068 respondents, 325 completed the instrument, yielding a response rate of 30%. The second and third waves of survey data collection targeted 237 respondents from the first survey who agreed to participate in additional rounds of data collection. The second survey concluded in January 2003. Eleven email invitations were undeliverable. Of the remaining 226 respondents, 122 completed the instrument for a response rate of 54%. The third survey concluded in January 2005. Thirteen email invitations were undeliverable. Of the remaining 224 respondents, 96 completed the instrument, yielding a response rate of 43%.

Our full sample includes wages reported in 25 different currencies. Labor market characteristics can vary widely across countries (Levina and Xin 2007). An econometric model that accounts for these crossnational differences would be difficult to implement, requiring many currency and purchasing powerrelated transformations. Accordingly, we limit our analysis to respondents who reported income in U.S. dollars. For all missing variables we implemented a list-wise deletion strategy. Applying these constraints results in a repeated measures cohort sample consisting of 147 developers each having at least two years of reported wages, for a total of 459 observations for any of the years 1999 through 2004.

Finally, we examine whether there is attrition bias in our sample because inference based on balanced or unbalanced panel data may be subject to bias if the nonresponse within the panel is endogenously determined (Wooldrige 2002). Recall that although we do collect our data in three phases, our sample construction is that of a cohort. That is, only respondents from our first survey appear in subsequent survey results. As our cohort of respondents “matures” within the Apache project, we cannot expect the respondents to remain similar to the population at any point in time after the initiation of survey. Thus, we must consider the threat of cohort attrition bias (Hakim 2000). Attrition from the original sample represents a potential threat if those who leave the study are systematically different from those who remain. If, however, there are no systematic differences between the “leavers” and the “stayers,” then there is no attrition bias. One approach to detect the presence of attrition bias is to test for differences in characteristics between the leavers and the stayers via logistic regression (Miller and Hollist 2007). The results of two logistic regressions comparing the characteristics of respondents who did not participate in surveys 2 and 3 to the respondents in survey 1 indicate that there are no systematic differences between those who remain in our study and those who do not $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 9 . 1 8 , p > 0 . 1 0 ; \chi ^ { 2 } =$ 140311 p > 0010 respectively). This suggests that cohort attrition bias is not a problem in our sample.

## Empirical Model, Estimation, and Results

By constructing a panel that combines ASF participation data extracted from project archives with job history and career experience data obtained from our surveys, we can explore the relationship between measures of OSS participation and the change in wages over time. The panel data approach also allows us to control for time-invariant participant endowments such as intelligence.

## Empirical Model and Measures

Following the extant empirical literature on labor market signaling, we use a Mincerian wage model as the model of choice for assessing the capacity of OSS participation to serve as a credible signal of productive capacity. Such a model has been first used to test the impact of human capital attributes on logearnings (Mincer 1974) but has become the standard labor market model. The basic model specifies log wages as the dependent variable, and the independent variables include characteristics such as schooling, experience, and other variables of interest to the researcher (Weiss 1995). Following the literature, we extend the general labor market model to include variables related to OSS participation such as the participant’s Apache OSS experience, Apache rank, and job history.

A common concern is the potential correlation of an unobserved person-specific variable, say $\alpha _ { i } ,$ with one or more of the regressors. If $\alpha _ { i }$ contains some time-invariant heritable characteristic and is correlated with one of the other regressors, both OLS and GLS will yield biased and inconsistent parameter estimates (Chamberlain 1984). In our study, it is important to account for unobserved skill, ability, or quality differences across OSS participants. Such differences might include inherent software design capabilities; the ability to explain complex technical issues; or the ability to work in an unstructured, often chaotic, environment. Measures of work or programming experience may not adequately reflect such abilities. Ideally, one would like to directly control for these individual effects. If, however, such abilities are rooted in the individual and thus constant over time, then a fixed effects (FE) model solves this omitted variables problem (Wooldridge 2002). By differencing away time-invariant variables, whether observed or unobserved, the FE model produces consistent parameter estimates that are purged of heritable individual effects. Hence, we estimate the following FE regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} L W A G E _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D E V _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} C O M _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} P M C + _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} E X P _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} E X P S Q _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {6} O S S E X P _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {7} L E D U _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {8} J S W C H _ {i, t} + \beta_ {9} F P U B _ {i, t} \\ & + \beta_ {1 0} F S W I N _ {i, t} + \beta_ {1 1} S T D N T _ {i, t} + \beta_ {1 2} P D A P C _ {i, t} \\ & + \beta_ {1 3} I T M G T _ {i, t} + \beta_ {1 4} T E C H _ {i, t} + \alpha_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \\ & (i = 1, \dots , N; t = 1, \dots , T) \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where i represents cross-section (participant) observed at time t. The individual fixed effect $\alpha _ { i }$ is assumed to be an estimable cross-section-specific constant term. The dependent variable, LWAGE, is the natural logarithm of each participant’s total annual wages and bonuses at time t. To normalize wages to a common basis, each year’s wages are expressed in constant 1998 U.S. dollars using Consumer Price Index deflated values.

Signaling variables. The dichotomous variables DEV, COM, and PMC+ operationalize the observed levels of a participant’s rank at time t − 1 that occur within the Apache meritocracy; that is, developer, committer, and project management committee member or above, respectively. These variables reflect the relative wage differences for each rank compared to the base (i.e., LATENT) for the prospective participant. As hypothesized, we expect our rank variables to be positively related to LWAGE.

Labor market variables. In the labor market literature, experience is commonly measured as the length of time spent participating in one’s vocation. Software engineering research has shown that with experience, developers gain familiarity with the software application domain and increase their understanding of the structure and architecture of the modules, files, and code within the system (Banker et al. 1998). Thus, we operationalize OSS experience as the cumulative amount of work-product (measured as function points) contributed to the Apache project as of time t − 1. All else equal, we expect higher levels of OSS experience to be positively related to LWAGE.

Job titles reflecting the computer and information technology occupations listed in Occupational Information Network O\*NET 3.1 database of worker attributes and job characteristics are used to classify respondents into technical and nontechnical job roles. Nontechnical jobs are coded as other and are absorbed into the intercept as part of the base case. Based on job description, technical roles are further classified as being primarily managerial or nonmanagerial in nature. ITMGT and TECH are binary variables that assume the value of 1 if the respondent reported being employed, respectively, as an IT manager or in a technical role at time t, and 0 otherwise. EXP and LEDU are the traditional human capital variables. EXP is the years of work experience of a participant at time t − 1. Consistent with the labor market literature, wages should increase with work experience; however, the percentage increase should decline with higher work experience. Thus, we expect EXP to be positively related to wages and EXPSQ (the square of EXP) to be negatively related to wages. To mitigate collinearity, EXP is grand-mean-centered prior to squaring. LEDU represents the number of years of schooling for a participant at time t − 1. Education is typically represented as time invariant in studies of human capital accumulation; however, the presence of students in our sample makes it possible for us to infer accurate levels of schooling within subject by tracking a respondent’s full-time student status within each period. Returns to schooling are expected to be positive.

Control variables. The variable PDAPC is a binary variable that assumes the value of 1 when a participant’s paying job at time t involves contributing to any one of the Apache projects and 0 otherwise. If the act of participation in an OSS project is valuable in and of itself, then participants may be willing to forgo some amount of earnings for the opportunity to work on the project, and PDAPC would be negative and significant. If, on the other hand, OSS participation represents a specialized or rare skill, then employers would pay a premium for such skills, and PDAPC would be positive and significant. The variables STDNT, FPUB, FSWIN, and JSWCH assume the value of 1 if the respondent is a student, works for a publicly traded firm, works for a firm in the software or e-commerce industry, or has experienced a job switch at time t, and 0 otherwise. Students are frequent participants to OSS projects (David and Shapiro 2008). Because, ceteris paribus, we expect students to earn low wages, STDNT should be negatively correlated with LWAGE. In addition, firm-level characteristics, such as firm size and sector, and job type (managerial versus technical) can significantly affect the earnings of employees in the IT industry (Ang et al. 2002). Following prior research, we expect that respondents working in publicly traded firms and firms engaged in the production of software to have higher wages, all else equal (Ang et al. 2002, Levina and Xin 2007). Lastly, IT labor market participants are among the most likely to switch jobs for the purpose of advancement (Schafer 2003); hence, respondents that switch jobs should have higher wages.

However, contrasts can also be used to test more complex linear arrangements, including the pattern required by H1. In the present case, a significant linear trend would support the hypothesis that wages tend to increase as rank increases (Thompson 2006). Estimating the contrast, we reject the null hypothesis that no linear trend exists between rank and wages $( F = 4 . 2 9 , p <$ 0005). We now examine the individual rank variable coefficients. Also as expected, increases in LWAGE grow more slowly over time as EXPSQ is negative and significant $( p \dot { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). We next turn to the other control variables in our model. Again, referring to column 3 of Table 2, respondents who were paid to develop Apache software (PDAPC) were observed in every observation period, and there is a moderately significant relationship between being a paid Apache developer and LWAGE $( \beta = 0 . 0 7 5 2 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ . In our sample employers pay a modest wage premium to employees who are compensated for their Apache participation. We find that some firm-level factors are associated with higher LWAGE. Respondents working for publicly held firms earn 5.9% higher wages $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ than do those in private firms. Conversely, average wages for respondents who work for software firms are not significantly different from those who do not. As expected, being a student is significantly and negatively associated with LWAGE as students earn 21% less than nonstudents $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . Lastly, JSWCH is not significant.

Robustness and Alternative Explanations of Results We conducted several analyses to assess the robustness of our results. These include (i) the potential for a relationship between rank and OSS experience, (ii) the potential endogeneity of rank, (iii) whether differences in wages may reflect differences in learning rates among OSS developers, (iv) the possibility of simultaneous discovery of productive ability by the IT labor market and the OSS community, and (v) labor market demand effects as possible alternative explanations for our results.

Relationship between rank and OSS experience. We investigated a potential relationship between the rank variables and the OSSEXP variable. Note that the pairwise correlations between rank and OSSEXP (shown in Table A2 in the appendix) are relatively low (the highest correlation is 0.35), and collinearity diagnostics for our primary model suggest no issues of multicollinearity as the variance inflation factors and condition indices are well below cutoff levels with all variance inflation factors below 3.97 and all intercept adjusted condition indices less than 4.11 (Belsley et al. 1980). We further evaluated the potential impacts of collinearity on our results by re-estimating our models, excluding the rank variables, and then excluding the OSSEXP variable. Columns 1A and 1B in Table 3 report the results of the extended model excluding the rank variables and then excluding the OSSEXP variable, respectively. In all estimations, our results are robust and the estimated coefficients on rank or OSSEXP in the respective models when either is excluded are essentially the same as when all variables are included. The coefficients on the other variables in the model also do not change significantly when rank or OSSEXP are excluded. When the rank variables are not included, the coefficient on OSSEXP remains near zero and when OSSEXP is not included, the coefficients on both COM and PMC+ remain positive and significant. These analyses suggest that our results are not influenced by a relationship between rank and OSSEXP.

Therefore, it is possible that unobserved abilities (like managerial skills) drive both the rank in OSS and the raise in wage. By relating wages to prior (lagged) values of rank, we have decreased the possibility that rank is endogenously determined (Wooldridge 2002). We further evaluate the possible endogeneity of rank using an “event study” type of approach, comparing wages immediately before and after acquiring the signal (i.e., promotion in rank). From this perspective, a significant one-time change in Apache rank would serve as a more conservative test of the signaling hypothesis. We evaluated a model where the change in rank from DEV to COM is modeled as a one-time event. Column 3 of Table 3 shows the results of implementing this rank change event. Coefficient estimates are close to those in the standard model in both size and significance. The rank-event variable is also positive and significantly associated with wages $( \beta = \bar { 0 } . 1 3 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 \bar { 1 } )$ , yielding a 14.37% increase in wages. These results further support the exogeneity of rank.

As a final check, if the signaling mechanism for rank holds, a one-year lead of rank changes should have no impact on wage. Thus, following Wooldridge (2002, p. 285), we also estimate Equation (1) with the addition of the leading values of the rank variables suspected of violating the assumption of strict exogeneity. The Wooldridge test fails to reject the null hypothesis that all such leads of rank are zero (p > 0010), providing additional evidence that rank is indeed exogenous.

Different learning rates. Some developers could learn more quickly than others from their participation in OSS projects (Singh et al. 2011), and therefore would benefit more quickly in terms of wage premiums. That is, differences in wages may reflect differences in learning rates among developers. We evaluated this possibility using a random coefficients model. The random coefficients model includes not only a random intercept but also a random coefficient on the OSS experience variable; i.e., the coefficient on OSSEXP is allowed to vary randomly across the Apache developers in our sample. Random coefficients on OSSEXP allow for the potential that each developer accumulates and benefits from increased OSS experience at a different rate (Singh et al. 2011). We estimated this model and report the results in Column 4 of Table 3. As shown there, the results from this estimation are similar to those of the standard model, suggesting that our results are robust to different learning rates.

Simultaneous discovery of ability. Another alternative explanation for our findings is that individualspecific quality is learned over time by the OSS community and the developer’s employer and the simultaneous discovery of this quality would correlate with a rise in rank. In this situation, the identification of rank as a signal of productivity may not establish causality because rank would not be a signal but rather a simultaneous outcome of the developer’s previously identified productivity potential. We considered this possibility via an exploratory propensity score analysis. The use of propensity scores allows us to compare two groups of OSS participants who are similar with respect to all covariates but differ in their rank. If a participant’s capabilities are learned by the market through observation, one would surmise that these two groups would have similar wages; i.e., the market would take account of the productive capacities of the OSS participant irrespective of the rank. Instead, our propensity score matching exercise suggests the existence of a causal link between promotion in rank to committer or above and above average wages. In addition, sensitivity analysis reveals that the treatment effect on wages of being promoted to the rank of committer or above is robust to unobserved selection bias of as much as 200%. Thus, we do not find that the market discovers the productive capacities of OSS participants irrespective of rank. The appendix provides a detailed description of the propensity score analysis and results.

Demand effect. Our study investigates contributors to the Apache projects at a time when OSS software programmers were in high demand. Hence, it is possible that wage increments may be due to the demand situation. However, we note that the increase in wages associated with Apache rank credentials persists in subsequent years. Our data collection effort spanned a period of six years. Although it is possible that the market demand temporarily rewards those who through luck or foresight can document a specific skill, it is less likely that such an effect will persist over time. We further tested for “demand effects” using the Apache market share as a labor market proxy. If the Apache market share is growing, the demand for Apache skills would increase and lead to a higher salary. However, although the coefficient on Apache market share is positive, it is not significant $( p > 0 . 1 0 )$ . Moreover, the substantive interpretation of all focal variables is unaffected. This suggests that labor market demand effects are not salient.

## Discussion and Conclusion

This paper examines the economic returns to different forms of participation in OSS projects. Overall, our results indicate that employers pay significantly higher wages to participants who attain a higher rank in the OSS community. This finding is consistent with a signaling hypothesis that OSS rank conveys information about sought after, but typically hard to observe, characteristics that may distinguish above average programmers. Our results also support the job matching hypothesis of a wage increase for alignment between a participant’s rank and paid job for participants holding an IT management job and whose rank in Apache involves managerial responsibilities. The estimated coefficient implies that highly qualified individuals who have an Apache rank of PMC or above and who are IT managers enjoy wages that are 37% higher, on average, than IT managers with no rank. In contrast, we find no evidence of increased wages attributable to a job match for participants of rank COM who are employed in technical jobs. It appears that sustained OSS participation that resulted in a rank of committer led to a wage increase through signaling difficult to observe productivity characteristics rather than through a match of OSS participation and job skill. Conversely, participants holding an OSS managerial rank are much more broadly qualified because they have succeeded not only at the committer level but also beyond. This suggests that participants holding an OSS managerial rank possess qualifications and skills that are viewed as a complement to their paid IT managerial jobs and are thus rewarded with above average wages.

To further understand the signaling value of rank COM to employers, we examined (1) the desired characteristics of committers as described in ASF governance documents and (2) individual candidate characteristics emphasized during rank promotion deliberations. The ASF is very specific in describing the attributes a committer should possess. Candidates for rank COM should exhibit so called “CoPDoC” properties. These are (1) (Co)mmunity—one must interact with others and share vision and knowledge; (2) (P)roject—a clear vision and consensus are needed; (3) (Do)cumentation—without it, underlying rational remains only in the minds of the authors; and (4) (C)ode—discussion goes nowhere without code. As potential leaders within the ASF community, candidates for rank COM are vetted for characteristics that are consistent with these principles. Examination of the ASF listserv archives reveals that supporters of a candidate for rank COM make statements that echo these characteristics. Often noted are technical qualities such as high quality contributions, deep technical understanding, and elegant software design. But equally prominent are statements about the contributor’s level of engagement. These include the willingness to sign up for challenging feature work; persistence and reliability; sustained project commitment; active participation in developer and user discussions; ability to serve as a mentor; and willingness to perform less glamorous tasks (documentation, scripts, etc.) for the greater good. Such characteristics are likely to be equally attractive to employers independent of specific occupational requirements.

In our data, attaining the rank of PMC is associated with higher wages, indicating the importance of this credential in signaling desirable albeit difficult to observe characteristics of participants who achieve it. Moreover and as previously mentioned, when considering the nature of the tasks performed in the job match context, PMC members, unlike committers, also enjoy higher wages if a job match exists. To further understand this finding, we investigated the nature of the responsibilities of PMC members. Consistent with our job match results, the responsibilities of PMC members closely resemble those of IT managers. In general, PMC members are responsible for the active management of the ASF projects and developer communities in their charge. As such, the tasks undertaken by a PMC member encompass responsibilities related to (1) project governance, such as the establishment of rules and procedures for the day-to-day management of their developer communities; (2) project oversight, such as resolving interpersonal disputes, addressing security concerns and legal issues, serving as veto of last resort, and maintaining the quality, stability, and robustness of both code and long term social structures—including the recruitment of new developers and committers; (3) product scheduling and release management, such as taking oversight of all official releases of the project; and (4) product strategy, such as advocating a technical direction for the project and approving new subprojects. As these responsibilities are broadly consistent with those of professional IT managers, it is not surprising that we find support for our job match hypothesis related to OSS technical management. This suggests that taking on management roles in OSS projects demonstrates to employers the existence of valuable technical management skills that are also useful in a participant’s paid employment.

Our study contributes by refining the concept of OSS participation to distinguish the particular roles and activities that participants perform. While researchers (e.g., Lerner and Tirole 2002) have posited the benefits of OSS participation, to our knowledge ours is the first study that empirically demonstrates the value of OSS participation as a signaling mechanism for employers. While the general insight may be intuitive, little is known about the rewards associated with different forms of participation. OSS participants differ in the role that they play: some thrive in primarily technical roles, whereas others have technical management responsibilities. Our study draws on signaling theory to argue that specific roles in OSS meritocracies act as credentials or signals of valuable underlying characteristics of OSS participants such as ability, motivation, and perseverance. We argue for and demonstrate the existence of a more nuanced relationship between OSS signals and economic rewards, revealing that participants holding ranks higher in the OSS meritocracy earn more than those with lower ranks.

Our study subsequently advances knowledge in investigating the degree to which a form of participation is more valued in specific contexts. Some participants work in jobs directly related to their activities in OSS communities, whereas other participants work in unrelated jobs. We draw on job matching theory to posit how taking on different roles in OSS projects can reflect the presence of technical or managerial skills, which, when aligned with roles in paid work, can positively affect a participant’s wages. Our results reveal that employers recognize and reward the alignment between OSS participation and participants’ paid employment for those in technical management roles.<sup>6</sup>

Our results have important implications for research. Specifically, in order to fully evaluate the economic returns to OSS participation, our study suggests that activities must be analyzed (i) over time and (ii) in a specific context. A key strength of our research is the depth of information we have gathered over a six-year period on the specific roles and activities of participants in the Apache software projects. Our findings reveal that participating at a limited level has no effect on wages. Only those participants who garner peer recognition through advancement in rank benefit from their activities. We believe this result reflects the nature of the software development context in which it is difficult to monitor, assess, and measure performance and in which there are dramatic differences in abilities among programmers. In this opaque environment, the online community of participants provides very precise (and valuable) information about performance of individual software developers.

The importance of rank as a signal to employers has an interesting implication for OSS communities. The original purpose of ranks and the resulting meritocracy within the ASF were driven by the need to define roles and to characterize the status of participants within the community. However, our work shows that the formalized process of evaluating contributors is potentially useful to employers as well. This suggests that OSS communities should consider the adoption of a merit-based peer evaluation system similar to that used by the ASF and other OSS meritocracies. The trade-off here is the value of such a system for internal and external purposes and the potential draw that such a system can add to the participation rate of an OSS project against the cost of implementation in terms of required acceptance by organizational members and the required commitment and resources of its most active members.

This study has strengths and limitations. We were fortunate to be able to exploit data sets from multiple sources, including objective data on contributions and rank progression, which we match with survey data on demographics and wages. Such an approach mitigates common method and source biases. The panel data allow us to control for unobserved heritable characteristics of an individual such as cognitive ability that are endowed and fixed over time. Moreover, pursuing an in-depth study of OSS participants within one community rather than a cross-section of a few participants from many communities allows us to obtain a representative sample and increases the internal, external, and statistical validity of the results (Campbell and Cook 1979). Strictly speaking, our results are only applicable to the ASF. However, we believe that our insights on the value of participating in OSS projects as a signaling mechanism should generalize to other OSS projects structured as meritocracies. Since Eric Raymond (2001) drew attention to the importance of merit in OSS projects, the use of meritocratic models has gained prominence as the explicit governance approach for many prominent and commercially viable OSS projects including, but not limited to, Mozilla, Python, Linux Ubuntu, and Linux Fedora. From a signaling perspective, a credential should be most meaningful when it is from a respected OSS community. We would therefore expect that our results would generalize to such OSS projects.

The use of self-reported income gathered via survey is familiar in research on salary in human resources, labor economics, management, and IS (see, for example, Levina and Xin 2007, Mithas and Krishnan 2008). In survey-based research, there is a legitimate concern that respondents consciously misrepresent information of a private nature in an effort to see themselves in a favorable light. This misrepresentation can produce a systematic bias referred to as social desirability bias (SDB) (Klassen et al. 1975). SDB has been shown to exist across a broad range of self-reported measures from food consumption (Hebert 1995) to sexual behavior (Guest et al. 2005). However, regarding income, a review of the literature on bias in income measurement in surveys concludes that “wage and salary income response bias estimates from a wide variety of studies are generally small and without consistent sign and indicators of unreliability (random error) are quite low” (Moore et al. 1999, p. 343).<sup>7</sup>

Going forward, we see several interesting directions for future research. It would be valuable to examine the implementation and effectiveness of merit-based peer evaluation systems like that used by the ASF in other OSS communities and in commercial organizations. More generally, our study may spur further research on how participating in OSS communities can influence participants’ performance and wages in their regular employment. For example, a strong OSS participation record could be particularly helpful as a signal early on in a career, whereas such a signal may be rewarded less at a later stage in the career. In addition, it would be interesting to make a comparison between participants in OSS projects and nonparticipants. As we discussed above, attaining a career-relevant rank in OSS communities such as Apache often requires prolonged effort. It is possible that such an effort directed at different activities (such as obtaining other credentials) may compare more favorably than engaging in OSS communities.
