# IS BEST ANSWER REALLY THE BEST ANSWER? THE POLITENESS BIAS<sup>1</sup>

Shun-Yang Lee Department of Operations and Information Management, School of Business, University of Connecticut, Storrs, CT 06269-1041 U.S.A. {shun-yang.lee@uconn.edu}

Huaxia Rui Simon Business School, University of Rochester, Rochester, NY 14627 U.S.A. {huaxia.rui@simon.rochester.edu}

Andrew B. Whinston McCombs School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78705 U.S.A. {abw@uts.cc.utexas.edu}

Popular knowledge management platforms such as community-based question answering sites (CQAs) and electronic networks of practice (ENPs) rely on accurate quality assessment of user-contributed content to ensure effective knowledge creation and exchange. However, quality assessment is subjective by nature. Based on the politeness theory, we hypothesize that answers written more politely are more likely to be perceived as high quality answers by the question asker due to the low face threat. We first test our hypotheses through a random coefficient logit model with data obtained from Stack Exchange, a popular CQA platform. We then conduct a randomized experiment where we exogenously manipulate the politeness level of otherwise similar answers. Our analyses, based on both the Stack Exchange dataset and the randomized experiment, lend strong support to the existence of a politeness bias, which affects question askers’ subjective evaluation of answer quality. This study contributes to the literature in knowledge management, cognitive bias, and behavioral issues in information systems.

Keywords: Knowledge management, community-based question answering, politeness theory, cognitive bias

## Introduction

Knowledge is an important asset for individuals and organizations alike. The need for knowledge is fundamental to one’s career perspective and many other facets of life; organizations rely on knowledge to maintain successful operation and stay competitive (see Nonaka 1991, 1994). Over the past few decades, information systems have been a key enabler for individual’s and organization’s knowledge management processes; therefore, the issues of knowledge contribution, knowledge exchange, and knowledge sharing have also been a major focus of research in the information systems community (see Alavi and Leidner 2001; Grover and Davenport 2001; Sambamurthy and Subramani 2005). In recent years, the proliferation of electronic networks of practice (ENP) have received attention from academics (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2005). ENPs are defined as self-organizing networks where a potentially geographically distributed group of users exchange knowledge through a computer-mediated communication system; these users often share some common practice or interests, and user participation is voluntary (Brown and Duguid 2001; Wasko and Faraj 2005). A particularly interesting type of ENP is community-based question answering sites (CQA) on which anyone can post questions or provide answers to others’ questions. Popular CQAs such as Quora, Yahoo! Answers, and Stack Exchange are convenient platforms for users to exchange knowledge with others.

Since the majority of ENPs and CQAs are open platforms, anyone can contribute to the knowledge exchange process, and, as a consequence, the quality of the user-generated content varies and can be vastly heterogeneous (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Quality indicators can help CQA users locate high quality content more efficiently; meanwhile, the use of these indicators themselves can significantly affect the usefulness and effectiveness of the CQAs (Meservy et al. 2014; Poston and Speier 2005). Therefore, the choice of a good quality measure is crucial for CQAs to be effective.

Theoretically speaking, any user associated with a CQA platform can provide his/her own quality assessment of a given answer: (1) question asker-based measure: the original question asker can evaluate proposed answers; (2) viewer/ audience-based measure: some platforms implement a content quality assessment mechanism, which allows all users to rate CQA content through voting or assigning numerical ratings (Poston and Speier 2005); (3) answerer-based measure: another way to assess content quality is to allow answer providers to place bets on their own answers based on how confident they are in their own answer quality (Fang et al. 2007; Savage 1971); (4) platform-based measure: the CQA platform can designate a group of moderators to evaluate answer quality for all answers. Since the number of answers on CQAs can be very large, it is generally not feasible to implement a platform-based measure. Incorporating a betting mechanism where answer providers place bets on their answer quality is also infeasible because it would require the platform to eventually know the true objective quality of each answer. As a result, the most popular quality measures on CQAs have been the question asker-based best answer and the audiencebased most popular answer, both of which come from users’ subjective evaluation of answer quality. Note that both the question asker-based and audience-based quality assessments reflect the perceived quality, which is conceptually distinct from the unobserved objective quality. For example, from the community’s perspective, the objective quality of an answer should depend not only on whether it helps solve the question asker’s problem, but also whether it can help solve others’ problems with same or similar symptoms but potentially different causes. The purpose of devising different assessment methods of perceived quality is to help users identify answers that might be of high objective quality.

Since an answer on CQA platforms is written to address a question raised by some question asker, the original question asker would seem to be the most qualified judge of the answer quality. Indeed, popular CQA platforms seem to follow this intuition. For example, Stack Exchange, one of the most popular CQA platforms today, displays the question-askerchosen answer first, followed by all other answers ordered by the number of votes they each receive. When a user hovers the pointer over the green check mark displayed next to the question-asker-chosen answer (see Figure 1), Stack Exchange shows the following message: “The question owner accepted this as the best answer,” and displays the best answer selection time. This arrangement is intuitively appealing because the question askers themselves should know best whether their questions have been satisfactorily answered. Therefore, it seems appropriate to use the question asker’s choice of best answer as the primary quality measure.

However, since the true, objective quality is unobservable, the evaluation of answer quality, either through question askers best answer choice or through the audience-based voting mechanism, is fundamentally a judgment and decisionmaking task, and will only reflect answers’ perceived quality. Research in psychology, behavior economics, behavior finance, and other related areas has shown that people are not always fully rational and are susceptible to numerous cognitive biases. For example, the classic paper by Tversky and Kahneman (1974) listed common heuristics people tend to employ in making judgments under uncertainty and discussed the resulting systematic and predictable biases; Rabin (1998) discussed areas where findings in psychology can inform the studies of economics; Barberis and Thaler (2003) offered a comprehensive overview of how psychology affects investors’ behaviors; in the information systems literature, Poston and Speier (2005) studied the anchoring and adjustment heuristics and the resulting biased judgment in content search tasks in knowledge management systems. Inspired by these studies, we investigate a new type of cognitive bias in CQA platforms, which we call the politeness bias, and study how this bias affects users, particularly question askers’ evaluation of the perceived answer quality.<sup>2</sup> The finding of our study has important theoretical and practical implications and will improve our understanding of the effect of cognitive biases on CQAs and knowledge exchange platforms in general.

Our study draws upon the social psychology and linguistics literature and analyzes linguistic features of answers on CQA. Specifically, we consider certain linguistic features that could reflect the impoliteness level of a given answer, and examine how these features might be correlated with the perceived quality of answers. Since the knowledge transfer process depends crucially on communication (Ko et al. 2005), it is reasonable to expect that on CQAs, where content quality is evaluated by community members, the way answers are written and communicated through natural language will affect their perceived quality.

Politeness theory, a conceptual framework widely studied by linguistics and communication researchers, describes how speakers and addressees communicate with each other to retain politeness while achieving their respective communication goals (see Brown and Levinson 1987; Goffman 1955; Goldsmith and MacGeorge 2000; Holtgraves 1986; Morand 1996, 2000). On CQAs, users who provide answers address issues raised in questions and convey ideas through textual responses, and politeness theory explains what type of answers are more likely to preserve the face (Brown and Levinson 1987; Goffman 1955) of addressees—that is, question askers—and will thus be received more favorably than answers that are more threatening to the addressee’s face. We conjecture that, everything else equal, answers that preserve the addressee’s face will likely be perceived as high quality answers by the addressee.

We develop a set of hypotheses based on the politeness theory and empirically test these hypotheses in a data set obtained from Stack Exchange, a collection of community-based question answering sites with Stack Overflow being its largest child forum. Stack Exchange is an ideal platform for our analysis because (1) Stack Exchange sites contain a large number of textual content contributed by community users, and (2) Stack Exchange implements two mechanisms (best answer decision and community votes) to reflect the perceived content quality, which allow us to examine the relationship between linguistic features and different measures of content quality. Figure 1 shows a sample question as well as an answer addressing this question. We first specify a random coefficient logit model to examine the relationship between linguistic features and the first measure of perceived quality—whether or not a given answer will be chosen as the best answer. For the second measure of perceived quality— the number of votes a given answer receives—we specify a question-specific fixed-effect negative binomial model to conduct a semi-placebo test. Such a test is similar to but not exactly the same as a placebo test because we expect the general audience to be less susceptible but not necessarily immune to the politeness bias. Going beyond the observational study, we further conduct a randomized experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), where we exogenously manipulate the politeness level of otherwise similar answers to study how it affects the perceived quality of answers and how this effect is moderated by the role of the evaluator (i.e., question asker or general audience).

Overall, our observational study and experiment results lend strong support to the existence of politeness bias: question askers have inherent bias of favoring polite answers while assessing answer quality. We believe this is because polite answers are more face-preserving and thus less facethreatening. This implies that the design of answer display rules in current CQA platforms, if relying too much on the question asker’s own answer assessment, might be inadvertently sacrificing quality for politeness, which would negatively impact the community’s collective knowledge-building process. As a consequence, even though question askers seem to be the most qualified individuals to determine whether the answers have successfully addressed their questions, the CQA platform should reconsider to what extent question askers’ choices of best answers should be promoted so as to improve the effectiveness of the platform as a whole. Generally, through the identification and a better understanding of the politeness bias, we hope to raise both researchers’ and practitioners’ awareness in studying how this type of cognitive bias could affect people’s decision-making processes.

Our research contributes to the study of knowledge exchange in ENPs and CQAs, as well as the study of cognitive bias in the context of quality assessment. We believe this new direction of research should be of interest to the knowledge management research community in the study of IT-enabled knowledge exchange, as well as to behavioral researchers in the information systems community.

## Literature Review

## IT-Enabled Knowledge Exchange and CQAs

The ability to manage knowledge effectively is crucial to an organization’s success (Nonaka 1994). Several IT-based systems such as knowledge management systems (KMS) have been designed to enable the creation, storage, transfer, and application of knowledge (Alavi and Leidner 2001). A detailed review of KMS and how they enable the knowledge management processes can be found in Alavi and Leidner (2001). Grover and Davenport (2001) described a process framework for the study of knowledge management.

Electronic network of practice (ENP) is a popular type of KMS. Distinct from traditional KMS, ENPs are selforganizing, and participation is voluntary. They allow users of similar professional practice or interests to exchange knowledge online, and these users do not necessarily need to have any offline connection or relationship with one another. Similarly, community-based question answering sites (CQA) provide an ideal medium for the voluntary communication and knowledge creation among users with similar interests, often in the form of question–answer pairs. Several studies in the information systems literature have looked at the user’s contribution and sharing behaviors (Wasko and Faraj 2005), the platform’s social structure and characteristics (Wasko et al. 2009), and the user’s evaluation and adoption of knowledge (Meservy et al. 2014). More recently, Beck et al. (2014) studied enterprise social media-enabled ENP using a multilevel model of knowledge exchange. Broadly speaking, the process of posting questions in expectation of receiving useful answers is also likened to the concept of crowdsourcing (Huang et al. 2014; Tausczik and Pennebaker 2011).

## Content Quality Assessment

Several studies adopted a cognitive perspective to examine how individuals evaluate and adopt knowledge. Zhang and Watts (2008) listed truthfulness, relevance (e.g., Xu and Chen 2006), and helpfulness, among others, as factors that might affect the perceived argument quality, and dual-process models such as the elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986; Meservy et al. 2014; Sussman and Siegal 2003) and the heuristic–systematic model (Chaiken 1980; Zhang and Watts 2008) have been applied to study the role of argument quality in information adoption decisions. Several communication studies analyzed ways in which arguments are constructed, particularly in conversations and persuasive communication. For example, the conversational argument coding scheme (CACS) has been used extensively to analyze conversational arguments in areas such as persuasion, conflict, decision making, and many other contexts (Aakhus 2010; Canary and Seibold 2010).

The issue of argument quality assessment is especially important for ENPs and CQAs. This is because both ENPs and CQAs allow for open participation and voluntary knowledge contribution, often without a centralized screening process. Therefore, while there might be a plethora of interesting content, the quality level can be vastly heterogeneous. As a result, knowledge seekers often have to rely on their own judgment to evaluate content and filter useful information (Meservy et al. 2014). Since users do not need to provide any proof of their capabilities or other credentials, it is difficult to assess a given user’s expertise level other than relying on some types of reputation scores the platform generates for each user, often based on his/her past activities. In addition to reputation scores, most CQAs also provide some type of content quality measure to help knowledge seekers evaluate the quality of the content. Poston and Speier (2005) studied how content ratings affect users’ search, evaluation processes, and decision performance. A number of computer science researchers have studied the problem of content quality prediction through social structure, user reputation, and texts, among other observable features (e.g., Adamic et al. 2008; Harper et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2008; Shah and Pomerantz 2010; Tian et al. 2013).

As mentioned in the introduction, the two most frequently used quality measures implemented in CQAs are (1) best answer and (2) most popular answer. While both measures reflect the perceived quality of answers, the main difference between these measures is that the best answer is determined solely by the question asker, whereas answer popularity is determined collectively by community members. To make this distinction clear, we refer to question askers’ best answer choice as question asker acceptance and refer to answer popularity as general audience reception. Notice that both quality measures are generated by users and are inherently subjective. In fact, mismatches between the true content quality and the given rating are common (see Poston and Speier 2005). In this paper, we study how different quality measures would provide different evaluation for the same piece of content and aim to explain this discrepancy through empirical analyses based on observational data and a randomized experiment. We also point out that, while CQA question asker acceptance and general audience reception can themselves be characterized as information adoption decisions, in this study we focus on their roles as (perceived) quality level indicators designed to assist general CQA users in finding helpful answers.

## Politeness Theory

Knowledge exchange is inherently a communication process. Factors such as source credibility and both the sender’s and recipient’s communication competence all affect the effectiveness of knowledge transfer (Ko et al. 2005). The use of language largely determines how effective the communication would be. Several information systems researchers have studied computer-mediated communication (CMC), such as those taking place on ENPs and CQAs, through textual and linguistic analysis (e.g., Abbasi and Chen 2008; Ludwig et al. 2014).

Since an important function of language is its ability to facilitate communication, one’s choice of words is also closely dependent on the specific communication goals one hopes to achieve. Brown and Levinson (1987), among a stream of sociolinguistic and communication research, proposed the politeness theory to explain how individuals choose appropriate words during a conversation to satisfy each other’s face wants, where (1) a positive face refers to an individual’s desire to have his/her identity approved by others, and (2) a negative face refers to the desire to have one’s autonomy respected and actions unimpeded (see also Goldsmith and MacGeorge 2000). The study of face can be traced back to Erving Goffman’s (1955) study on face-work. More recently, several politeness theory studies have been conducted in the domain of organizational studies (e.g., Morand 1996, 2000). For example, Morand and Ocker (2003) studied the implication of politeness theory in the context of computer-mediated communication.

Harper et al. (2008) studied strategies that question askers on question answering sites can use in order to receive higher quality answers, such as providing higher payment to elicit answer provision on for-fee platforms and using rhetorical strategies such as showing gratitude. They found that the use of “thank you” contained in questions received differing appreciation levels, depending on the specific forum culture. Similarly, Burke and Kraut (2008) found that the level of politeness associated with questions can positively or negatively elicit the number of answers responding to those questions, depending on the forum-specific group norms. Both studies relied on experts to manually evaluate the politeness level of the questions, and therefore the data sets they used for analysis are considerably smaller than ours. Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. (2013) recruited Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) workers to manually annotate messages and used the annotation results to train a politeness classifier. Using the trained classifier to analyze much larger data sets, they found that users’ requests (questions) become less polite as their reputation increases.

It is worth noting that the aforementioned politeness studies focused primarily on the politeness level associated with questions, while we aim to study how the politeness level of answers would affect the answer quality level perception. More closely related to our research objective, Trees et al. (2009) studied how face-threat mitigation strategies used by teachers can increase students’ judgments about the quality and usefulness of teachers’ instruction. However, being polite might come at a cost: Bonnefon and Villejoubert (2006) studied how politeness can lead to a severity bias in a medical context; Brummernhenrich and Jucks (2013) studied how the politeness strategies used by tutors in educational instructions can hinder effective tutoring and negatively affect the student’s learning process. In this paper we investigate how the politeness level of an answer to a question affects the question asker’s evaluation of the perceived answer quality.

## The Politeness Bias Hypotheses

As noted earlier, an effective assessment of content quality is critical for CQAs. In addition to measures such as reputation score, question asker validation of an answer, and overall answer popularity, the politeness theory suggests that certain linguistic features might influence the perceived quality of an answer, which is subjective by nature. According to the politeness theory (Brown and Levinson 1987), actions that threaten one’s face wants are called face-threatening acts (FTAs). For example, during communication, the speaker’s negative evaluation, insults, and criticism of the addressee are FTAs that threaten the addressee’s positive face because they might be perceived as a disapproval of the addressee’s current effort; the speaker’s suggestions, recommendations, and advice for the addressee can be perceived as FTAs that pose threats to the addressee’s negative face because these suggestions might impose constraints on the addressee’s future behavior (Wilson et al. 1998). Notice that not all FTAs are equally face threatening; Brown and Levinson (1987) suggested that the degree of threats are moderated by the (1) power/status difference and (2) social distance between the speaker and the addressee, as well as (3) cultural specific ranking of impositions. For instance, the less power the speaker has compared with the addressee, the more threatening the FTA would be to the addressee: an employee’s giving advice to his or her manager is likely to be more face threatening to the manager than the CEO of the company giving the same advice to that manager.

Recall that CQA users ask questions and provide answers, which can be categorized as advice-seeking and advice-giving behaviors. According to the politeness theory, advice-giving is perceived by addressees, particularly the direct recipient of the advice—that is, the question askers—as FTAs that threaten the negative face, and different word use and different ways of describing an answer could result in different degrees of threats. FTAs that pose a higher level of threats are more likely to be perceived as impolite and are thus less likely to be accepted by the addressee. In other words, the politeness level associated with a given answer will likely affect how the addressee—particularly the question asker in the case of CQA platforms—perceives the answer. Therefore, we hypothesize that the higher an answer’s face threatening intensity is, the less likely it will be chosen as the best answer by the question asker.

Hypothesis 1 (FTA on Question Asker): Controlling for answer informativeness, the more impolite an answer is, the more negative the question asker’s acceptance will be.

Meanwhile, the politeness theory suggests that the degree of face threat is moderated by the power difference between the speaker and the addressee. The higher the speaker’s status, the lower the perceived face threat would be. In our context, if the answer provider’s reputation level is much higher than that of the question asker, then the perceived face threat coming from an impolite answer might be smaller.

Hypothesis 2 (Power Distance and Question Asker Acceptance): The higher the answer provider’s reputation level is, the weaker the negative effect of impolite answer on the question asker’s acceptance will be.

In the current research, we first conduct an observational study to empirically test these hypotheses using a dataset from Stack Exchange. We then conduct a randomized experiment where we exogenously manipulate an answer’s politeness level to strengthen the causality of our empirical analysis. We describe our analyses in the following sections.

## Study 1: Stack Exchange Analysis Data and Variables

We obtain a rich dataset from Stack Exchange, a popular community-based question answering platform. Stack Exchange is a network of more than 140 diverse question answering sites, each focusing on a specific topic ranging from computer programming and mathematics to philosophy and physical fitness. Users on Stack Exchange sites can post questions, and others can provide answers to these posted questions. The question asker can then choose among different answers the one that s/he thinks best addresses the question and mark it as the best answer; this best answer will be displayed before all other answers. Similar to other CQAs, Stack Exchange contents are generated and moderated by users. Specifically, any user can upvote any post, question, or answer if s/he thinks the post is of high quality; similarly, the user can downvote any post if s/he thinks the post quality is low. For a given question, Stack Exchange sites order the corresponding answers by first displaying the best answer determined by the question asker and then displaying the other answers according to the number of votes these answers received. Stack Exchange sites also maintain a reputation system where users can accumulate reputation points based on the number of posts they have generated, the number of votes their posts have received to date, and their other activities on the site. As stated on Stack Overflow, Stack Exchange’s largest child site, reputation points reflect a user’s expertise and how much the Stack Exchange community trusts the user.<sup>3</sup> The reputation points can also serve as a signal of one’s expertise to potential employers, as employers also use Stack Overflow to find talents (Xu et al. 2018).<sup>4</sup>

## Data Collection and Reconstruction

All historical Stack Exchange contents, including all posts, votes, and user public information, are distributed under the Creative Commons BY-SA 3.0 license, and the sites prepare and regularly release data dump for public downloads. We downloaded a subset of eight Stack Exchange sites from the data dump released in September 2014 for our empirical analysis. These eight sites cover diverse topics and are representative of the overall Stack Exchange community. An overview of these sites is shown in Table 1. The downloaded dataset for each site includes post, vote, and user information; the post information contains post type (question or answer), post author, post title, post body in raw text format, post date, and number of votes received, for each post on a given site; the vote information contains vote date, vote type (upvote, downvote, etc.), and the corresponding post being voted; the user information contains user name, age, location, a list of all questions and answers posted by this user, and his or her reputation score as of September 2014, etc.

The Stack Exchange data dump does not include the time series data of user reputation scores; instead, it only provides a snapshot of all users’ reputation scores extracted in September 2014. Since we would like to obtain a user’s reputation score at the time of posting questions or answers, it is critical that we reconstruct this measure from the information available to us. We use Stack Exchange’s rules of gaining and losing reputation points to reconstruct, for each post, the corresponding post user’s reputation score at the time of posting by counting the number of questions asked, the number of answers provided, the number of votes received, the number of answers being chosen as best answers, as well as other measures that would affect the user’s reputation score, for all posts in all eight Stack Exchange sites. We extend the original dataset by including this retrospective reputation score information for each post. We evaluate the reliability of this procedure by comparing the reconstructed scores with those retrieved through the Stack Exchange API. The details of the evaluation are provided in Appendix D.

Similar to reputation score, the Stack Exchange data dump does not include any time series data on the number of votes a given post receives up to a specified time. This information is important for our analysis because, due to the design of Stack Exchange sites, answers that are chosen as best answers will appear first on the webpage, immediately below the associated questions. As a consequence, the webpage’s answer placement might make it easier for users to see and vote on best answers than on other answers, and thus the number of votes received by the best answer might be biased upward. To alleviate this concern, for each post, we recompile the votes information and recount the number of votes received immediately before the best answer is chosen for the corresponding question. In other words, we only consider the number of votes each answer receives up to when the best answer is chosen. Through this approach we are able to measure the popularity of answers prior to the website’s exogenous answer placement, which only takes place when the best answer is chosen.

## Main Variables: Impoliteness Proxy

The key challenge in studying the effect of politeness on perceived answer quality is to quantify the politeness level of an answer. Our instrument of quantifying politeness level is through a combination of linguistic markers that are likely associated with impoliteness in an advice-giving context. In particular, we construct our impoliteness measure by checking the occurrences of the following markers: sentences beginning with a second-person pronoun (i.e., you, your, yours, yourself), sentences beginning with question markers (i.e., why, how, what, where, which, when), negations (e.g., can’t, cannot, don’t, do not, etc.), informal titles (e.g., dude, bud, etc.), and agency-giving (e.g, you can).

While you is the only second personal pronoun in modern English, in many other languages, there are multiple secondperson pronouns to choose from when one needs to address another person. Brown and Gilman (1960) identified two types of second-person pronouns: “T” pronouns (after the French tu) and “V” pronouns (after the French vous). The T form is more likely to be used if the solidarity between the speaker and the addressee is greater or if the speaker has marked dominance of power over the addressee. For example, in German, du belongs to the T form of second-person pronoun and Sie belongs to the V form of second-person pronoun. Similarly, different second-person pronouns are used in Chinese for its T form and V form. According to social linguistic theories on personal reference, the V form is generally considered a more polite form of second-person pronoun. In modern English, you, as the only second-person pronoun, is often perceived as rude and impolite (Murphy 1988). As an alternative to using this ambiguous secondperson pronoun in modern English, Brown and Ford (1961) showed that intimacy engenders the use of the first name in address, and addressees of higher status are more likely to receive a title and last name. Indeed, Brown and Levinson (1987) proposed several redressing strategies including (1) replacing the second-person pronoun (you) by the impersonal pronoun (one), or (2) avoiding using you altogether—known as pronoun avoidance.

The sociolinguistic theories and the redressing strategies discussed above both suggest that the use of second-person pronoun is likely to be perceived as less polite by the addressee (i.e., the question asker). In our impoliteness measure, we specifically consider second person pronouns that occur in the beginning of a sentence. This is because these second person pronouns are likely associated with imperatives and direct requests, whereas second person pronouns appearing elsewhere might not necessarily be considered impolite (e.g., “Thank you”). A related marker is agencygiving phrases such as “you can” as the use of such a phrase in answers may be related to the act of advice-giving, which threatens the question asker’s negative face.

In addition, negations are also often perceived as impolite (e.g., Voigt et al. 2017), as negation might mean disagreement with the question asker’s statement. Questions contained in an answer text can also be perceived as impolite because when an answer text contains questions, the answer provider might be expressing doubts, or seeking more information (i.e., imposing a request on the question asker, which is perceived as face threatening) (Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil et al. 2013). The use of informal titles can be perceived as impolite because it might be interpreted as showing a lack of respect (Voigt et al. 2017).

Operationally, we separate sentences in each answer text using the python natural language processing library spaCy, which enables accurate sentence boundary detection. Once texts are broken down into sentences, we count the occurrences of the impoliteness markers. We then divide the count of the impoliteness markers by the answer length to obtain the density of impoliteness markers contained in an answer text. We use this normalized value as our measure of impoliteness, which allows us to study hypothesis H1.

In order to test hypothesis H2 regarding the relationship between politeness, reputation/power distance, and the perceived answer quality, we include the interaction term, Impoliteness<sub>qa</sub> @ log(Reputation<sub>qa</sub>), where Reputation<sub>qa</sub> is the answer provider’s reputation score at the time they posted answer a in response to question q.

We acknowledge and point out a related but distinct concept in communication studies: you-attitude (e.g., Rodman 2001). The you-attitude can be understood as a style of writing that takes into account the reader’s perspective, including respecting the reader’s intelligence and ego, referring to the reader’s request (e.g., Locker and Kienzler 2013; Shelby and Reinsch 1995), often used in business communications.<sup>5</sup>

While related, this concept is generally different from the mere use of second-person pronouns (Rodman 2001) and is not the focus of the present study.

## Control Variables

We include several linguistic features in our regression analysis: words belonging to each of these linguistic categories are based on the LIWC 2007 dictionaries. LIWC (Pennebaker et al. 2007) is a state-of-the-art psycholinguistic package used to analyze linguistic features of texts by calculating percentages of words that belong to different word categories, each of these categories reflecting different language and psycholinguistic dimensions of a written text. Rigorous studies were conducted during the building of the LIWC dictionaries to ensure the reliability of these generated word categories.

First, we include YOUWEI, which is the sum of secondperson, first-person plural, and first-person singular pronouns, measured in percentages, to control for the inherent need for answerers to use personal pronouns. This is an important control because differenet answers might have different needs for the use of pronouns, which also depends on the specific question being addressed.

Second, we include the variable WE, defined as the proportion of first-person plurals among all first-person pronouns, or, mathamatically, WE=(%we)/(%we+%I), where %we and %I are the percentages of first-person plural pronouns and first-person singular pronouns, respectively, in an answer. The reason to include WE is because the relative use of firstperson plural pronouns (we, us, our) and first-person singular pronouns (I, me, my, mine) has been shown to reflect the relative status of two people in an interaction (Chung and Pennebaker 2007), which might correlate with the perceived quality of answers.

Third, we include in our empirical analyses the following variables: SHEHE, THEY, IPRON, and ARTICLE, defined as percentages of third-person singular pronouns, third-person plural pronouns, impersonal pronouns, and articles, respectively. These words, as well as first-person and secondperson pronouns described earlier, belong to a class of words called function words. Several psycholinguistic studies have shown that the use of function words reflects a person’s otherwise unobservable attributes and underlying psychological processes (e.g., Chung and Pennebaker 2007; Ludwig et al. 2014; Pennebaker et al. 2003; Tausczik and Pennebaker 2010 ). For example, Pennebaker (2011) suggested that the use of articles (a, an, the) is related to concrete nouns and concrete ideas, which might be positively correlated with the perceived answer quality; in addition, Chung and Pennebaker (2007) summarized findings that relate the use of function words to social status, attention, negative affective states, and deception, among others. We therefore include these function words in our empirical analyses.

Many Stack Exchange sites are related to computer programming and many posts contain mathematical derivations or code snippets. We carefully remove these math segments and code snippets and only retain verbal descriptions and explanations of these snippets for our linguistic analysis, while generating a dummy variable indicating whether an answer contains a code snippet. This is because mathematical derivations and code snippets follow mathematical and programming syntax and are less informative in reflecting users’ linguistic styles, but the presence of code snippets is likely to be positively correlated with perceived answer quality. Specifically, HasCode is defined as a dummy variable which equals 1 if the answer contains a code snippet and 0 otherwise.

We include the variables Vote, which reflects the number of votes an answer receives immediately before the best answer associated with the corresponding question is chosen, calculated as (#Upvotes – #Downvotes); log(Reputation), defined as the answer provider’s logarithmic-transformed reputation points at the time of posting the given answer; WordCount, defined as the number of words contained in the answer. These variables are likely to be related to the perceived quality of an answer. We also include log(TimeDiff), which is the logarithmic-transformed time difference between the question post time and the answer post time measured in minutes, to account for a potential duration effect.

Several studies have shown that the perception of politeness and the usage of politeness strategies might differ significantly between native and nonnative speakers (e.g., Carrell and Konneker 1981; Maier 1992), which might confound our analysis of politeness and quality evaluation. Therefore, we only consider textual data written by high-proficiency English speakers to ensure our results are not confounded by other aspects of language proficiency. We use a heuristic approach to determine whether a given user is likely a high-proficiency English speaker: the user information section of the Stack Exchange dataset contains a location field where users can provide their location information in their public user profile. We use this information as a proxy for their country of origin and language preference and identify users from Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, United Kingdom, and United States to be included in our main empirical analysis because users from these countries are more likely to be highproficiency English speakers.<sup>6</sup>

## Empirical Analysis

We construct a series of empirical models to test our hypotheses using the Stack Exchange dataset. Recall that our goal is to explore the relationship between politeness level, measured by the variable Impoliteness, and the perceived answer quality. As described in the data section, for each post in the data set, we obtain detailed information including post type, post text, post user information, and voting information. Using these variables, for each answer post we know (1) whether or not it is chosen as a best answer and (2) the number of votes it has received before the question asker chooses the best answer. Generally speaking, answers receiving a large number of votes are not necessarily chosen as best answers, although in many cases best answers are also voted the most. We first examine the relationship between politeness level and the question asker acceptance.

## Question Asker Acceptance Model

Recall that, for a given question, the question asker would choose from all candidate answers the one that he or she thinks best addresses the question. Therefore, the best answer decision is a multinomial choice problem. Also notice that, since questions are potentially very different from one another, it is highly likely that their corresponding answers will also be very different from one another, while exhibiting some level of similarity among answers that address the same question. We construct a random coefficient logit model, also known as the mixed logit model, to accommodate both the multinomial choice nature of best answer decisions and the potential similarity among affiliated answers. Formally, assume that answer a, which addresses question q, provides the question asker with perceived quality $\mathrm { U } _ { q a } ,$ defined as

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {q a} = \beta_ {q 1} \cdot (I m p o l i t e n e s s) _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 2} \cdot Y O U W E I _ {q a} + \\ \beta_ {q 3} \cdot W E _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 4} \cdot S H E H E _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 5} \cdot T H E Y _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 6} \cdot \\ I P R O N _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 7} \cdot A R T I C L E _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 8} \cdot V o t e _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 9} \cdot \\ \log (R e p u t a t i o n _ {q a}) + \beta_ {q 1 0} \cdot I m p o l i t e n e s s _ {q a} \cdot \log (R e p u t a t i o n _ {q a}) ^ {(1)} \\ + \beta_ {q 1 1} \cdot W o r d C o u n t _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 1 2} \cdot H a s C o d e _ {q a} + \beta_ {q 1 3} \cdot \\ \log (T i m e D i f f _ {q a}) + \varepsilon_ {q a} \end{array}
$$

where ε is an unobserved random term that is distributed according to a Type I extreme-value distribution. Recall that we use YOUWEI to control for the need for answers to use personal pronouns, as described earlier. As mentioned earlier, the variable Impoliteness measures the impoliteness level of the answer, and we use the coefficient of Impoliteness to examine hypothesis H1; the inclusion of the interaction term, Impolitenes $\delta _ { q a }$ @ log(Reputation ), corresponds to hypothesis H2.

The advantage of using a random coefficient logit model is that, through the random coefficient setup, each question asker is allowed to value each independent variable differently, which means that different question askers can have different preferences. Specifically, we assume that the coefficients $\beta _ { q 1 } . \beta _ { q 2 } , . . . , \beta _ { q 1 3 }$ in Equation (1) are question-specific and independently normally distributed.<sup>7</sup> The question asker is assumed to pick the answer that has the highest perceived quality to be the best answer—that is, for question q, BestAmswe $\dot { \mathbf { \eta } } _ { q } = a r g m a x _ { q } \mathbf { U } _ { q a } .$ Let $\mathrm { \bf P } _ { q a }$ denote the probability that answer a associated with question q is chosen as the best answer. Since we do not observe the question asker’s preference, we need to integrate over the distribution of all possible preferences the question asker could have. Then, $\mathrm { \Delta P } _ { q a }$ can be shown to be

$$
P _ {q a} = \int \frac {\exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta\right)}{\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta\right)} f (\beta | \theta) d \beta\tag{2}
$$

where $X _ { q a }$ is a vector of answer a’s characteristics as appeared in Equation (1), A(q) is the number of answers that are associated with question q, $\beta = ( \beta _ { q 1 } , . . . , \beta _ { q 1 3 } ) , f ( \beta | \theta )$ is the density function of $\cdot \beta ,$ which represents the question asker’s preference, and θ is a vector of parameters associated with the density function $f .$ Appendix A of this paper presents the details of the estimation procedure. Once we estimate the random coefficient logit model, we can compute the marginal effect of Impoliteness on the probability of a given answer being chosen as the best answer using the formula

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \frac {\partial [ \text { Prob } (\text { isBestAnswer }) ]}{\partial [ \text { Impoliteness } ]} & = \left\{E [ \text { Prob } (\text { isBestAnswer }) | \text { Impoliteness } = \text { Imp } + \Delta \text { Imp } ] - E [ \text { Prob } (\text { isBestAnswer }) | \text { Impoliteness } = \text { Imp } ] \right\} / \Delta \text { Imp } \end{array}
$$

where Prob(isBestAnswer) is the probability of an answer being chosen as the best answer.

The politeness theory predicts that answers posing higher threats to the addressee’s face are considered less polite, which we argue negatively affects their chances of being accepted by the question asker. These findings suggest that the politeness level of the answer is closely related to the question asker’s answer acceptance decision.<sup>8</sup> We acknowledge that these results do not necessarily reflect causal relationships because no matter how we use control variables for the latent objective quality, it is likely that there will still be residual components of the objective quality left in the error term. Rather, we argue that answers which are perceived by question askers to be of highest quality are associated with high politeness level (low impoliteness level). We defer the investigation of causality to the randomized experiment section later in this paper.

Recall the politeness theory also theorizes that factors such as power difference and social distance will moderate the perceived face threats. Our model explicitly includes the answer provider’s reputation score in the analysis, and the difference between answer providers’ reputation scores reflects the difference in the question asker–answer provider power difference across different answer providers. Hence, we examine the estimated coefficients for Impoliteness\* log(Reputation) to investigate the moderating effect of power distance on how politeness correlates with question asker acceptance. From the random coefficient logit estimation results shown in Table 4 we can see that the coefficient associated with the term Impoliteness\*log(Reputation) is significantly positive. This means that, controlling for Impoliteness, the higher the answer provider’s reputation, the weaker the answer Impoliteness’ negative correlation with question asker acceptance becomes. In other words, while Impoliteness is negatively correlated with question asker acceptance, this negative correlation is reduced when the answer provider’s reputation is high. Therefore, hypothesis H2 is supported.

Interestingly, if we consider the terms Impoliteness and Impoliteness\*log(Reputation) together, we can see that, for someone with a high enough reputation score, the higher Impoliteness is, the more likely it would be picked as the best answer. In contrast, for people with lower reputation, the higher Impoliteness is, the less likely it would be picked as the best answer. These results suggest that, for high reputation answer providers, the negative impact coming from the impolite answers can be countered by their high reputation: the question asker will find the impoliteness to be less of a concern or even as a signal of authority.

We also point out that the number of votes an answer receives prior to the time the best answer is chosen is positively correlated with this answer being chosen as the best answer; an answer is more likely to be chosen as the best answer if it contains code snippets; answers that are posted relatively late are more likely to be chosen as best answers, perhaps because answers posted later are more likely to have incoporated or corrected earlier answers to provide a more satisfactory response to the question asker.

We rerun the random coefficient logit model using 300 and 350 draws to see if the results are robust to the number of draws we use to compute the simulated likelihood, and the results, shown in Tables B1 and B2 in Appendix B, are similar to the results using 250 draws.

## A Semi-Placebo Test with General Audience Reception

Recall in the hypothesis development section we argued that the question asker prefers polite answers because s/he is the direct recipient of the answer and most susceptible to the face threat from the answer. In contrast, since the general audience (i.e., readers of the answer text) are not the direct recipient, they should face a much lower level of face threat from reading an impolite answer. To verify that the general audience is indeed less affected by the impoliteness level of an answer text, we conduct a semi-placebo test to explore the relationship between answer politeness and general audience reception. Notice that the general audience reception of an answer is measured by the number of votes (#Upvotes – #Downvotes) this answer receives up to the time when the best answer is chosen for the corresponding question.

Since the dependent variable here is a count variable taking only integer values, we specify a negative binomial count model where the dependent variable is the number of votes up to when the best answer is chosen, and independent variables include Impoliteness and control variables such as other linguistic features, whether the answer contains code snippets, the time difference between question post date and answer post date, and the answer provider’s reputation score at the time of posting the answer. Notice that, similar to the case of question asker acceptance, it is likely that answers corresponding to different questions will differ significantly from one another. Therefore, we specify a fixed-effect negative binomial model to address this issue. The likelihood of this model is as follows (Hausman et al. 1984):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname * {P r} \left(y _ {q 1}, y _ {q 2}, \dots , y _ {q A} \mid \gamma_ {q 1}, \gamma_ {q 2}, \dots , \gamma_ {q A}, \sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A} y _ {q a}\right) \\ = \frac {\Gamma \left(1 + \sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A} y _ {q a}\right) \cdot \Gamma \left(\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A} \gamma_ {q a}\right)}{\Gamma \left(\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A} y _ {q a} + \sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A} \gamma_ {q a}\right)} \prod_ {a = 1} ^ {A} \frac {\Gamma (\gamma_ {q a} + y _ {q a})}{\Gamma (\gamma_ {q a}) \cdot \Gamma (1 + y _ {q a})} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

$$
y _ {q a} \sim N B 1 \left(\gamma_ {q a}, \delta_ {q}\right), \quad \delta_ {q} = \frac {\phi_ {q}}{\exp \left(\mu_ {q}\right)}
$$

where $y _ { q a }$ is the number of votes given to answer a which is associated with question $q ,$ and $y _ { q a }$ follows a negative binomial distribution with the following probability mass function:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(y _ {q a} \mid \gamma_ {q a}, \delta_ {q}\right) = \frac {\Gamma \left(\gamma_ {q a} + y _ {q a}\right)}{\Gamma \left(\gamma_ {q a}\right) \cdot \Gamma \left(1 + y _ {q a}\right)} \left(\frac {\delta_ {q}}{1 + \delta_ {q}}\right) ^ {\gamma_ {q a}} \left(\frac {1}{1 + \delta_ {q}}\right) ^ {y _ {q a}} (\tag{4}
$$

Here, Γ(@) is the Gamma function; $\mu _ { q }$ and $\phi _ { q }$ reflect questionspecific fixed effects (Greene 2007; Hausman et al. 1984); and $\gamma _ { q a }$ is parameterized as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \gamma_ {q a} = \exp \left(\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot (I m p o l i t e n e s s) _ {q a} + \beta_ {2} \cdot Y O U W E I _ {q a} \right. \\ \quad + \beta_ {3} \cdot W E _ {q a} + \beta_ {4} \cdot S H E H E _ {q a} + \beta_ {5} \cdot T H E Y _ {q a} \\ \quad + \beta_ {6} \cdot I P R O N _ {q a} + \beta_ {7} \cdot A R T I C L E _ {q a} + \\ \quad \beta_ {8} \cdot B e s t A n s w e r _ {q a} + \beta_ {9} \cdot \log (R e p u t a t i o n _ {q a}) \\ \quad + \beta_ {1 0} \cdot I m p o l i t e n e s s _ {q a} \cdot \log (R e p u t a t i o n _ {q a}) \\ \quad + \beta_ {1 1} \cdot W o r d C o u n t _ {q a} + \beta_ {1 2} \cdot H a s C o d e _ {q a} \\ \quad + \beta_ {1 3} \cdot \log (T i m e D i f f _ {q a})) \end{array} \tag {5}
$$

where variables corresponding to linguistic features are defined similarly as in the question asker acceptance model; BestAnswer is a dummy variable which takes the value of 1 if the answer is picked as the best answer, and 0 otherwise. Therefore, it can be shown that

$$
E \Big [ y _ {q a} \mid \gamma_ {q a} \Big ] = \frac {\gamma_ {q a}}{\delta_ {a}} = \frac {\gamma_ {q a} \bullet \exp (\mu_ {q})}{\phi_ {q}}
$$

$$
\operatorname{Var} \left[ y _ {q a} \mid \gamma_ {q a} \right] = \frac {\gamma_ {q a}}{\delta_ {a} ^ {2}} = \frac {\gamma_ {q a} \cdot \exp (2 \cdot \mu_ {q})}{\phi_ {q} ^ {2}}
$$

Notice that another option is to use a fixed-effect Poisson specification to model the number of votes each answer receives. However, the Poisson regression model imposes the restriction that the conditional variance be equal to the conditional mean, known as the Poisson variance assumption (Wooldridge 2010), which might be overly restrictive. Our data exhibits overdispersion, which refers to the phenomenon that the conditional variance is greater than the conditional mean. Therefore, we believe the use of the fixed-effect negative binomial model is more appropriate for our empirical analysis. The fixed-effect negative binomial model we specified in the previous section is estimated through a maximum likelihood approach (Hausman et al. 1984). Notice that the outcome variable—in our case, the number of votes each answer receives—needs to be nonnegative. Therefore, we discard answers that receive more downvotes than upvotes, which corresponds to less than 1% among all answers, and only use answers with nonnegative votes for our analysis. The estimation results are shown in Table 5. Notice the number of observations is larger in the general audience reception analysis than in the question asker acceptance analysis. This is because in the case of general audience reception we are able to use all answers (with nonnegative number of votes) for the analysis, while in the case of question asker acceptance we had to remove answers whose corresponding question asker has not chosen a best answer.

The results in Table 5 suggest that Impoliteness does not seem to be correlated with the number of votes an answer would receive, which is consistent with our expectation: since the general audience is not the direct recipient of the answer, the answer’s politeness level should only marginally affect the reception of these readers.

Recall that our fixed-effect specification controls for any question-specific answer characteristics, and therefore our analysis reflects the likelihood of an answer receiving more votes than its competing answers. Similar to the question– asker acceptance model, our general audience reception model explicitly controls for the answer provider’s reputation score, and the difference between answer providers’ reputation scores should reflect the difference in the question askeranswer provider power difference across different answer providers. From Table 5, we see that the coefficient associated with Impoliteness\*log(Reputation) is insignificant, thereby suggesting no moderating role of power distance on the general audience reception. This is also expected because the impoliteness would only pose minimal face threats to the general audience, and hence the answer provider’s reputation level would not have made any significant difference. In summary, consistent with the politeness theory and our hypotheses, our semi-placebo test does not find any evidence of the general audience being susceptible to the politeness bias.

## Study 2: Randomized Experiment

In the previous section, we used observational data from several sites of Stack Exchange to show the negative correlation between impoliteness and the question asker’s best answer choice. We also conducted a semi-placebo test and found no evidence that answer impoliteness is correlated with general audience reception. To further evaluate the causality between the (im)politeness of an answer and the question asker’s quality perception of the answer, we conducted a randomized experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT). In this experiment, we created answers with similar content but differing levels of politeness. We then asked subjects, randomly assigned to the question asker group and the general audience group, to select their preferred answers. Our goal is to examine whether there exists any systematic difference among the choices made by the two groups. This setup allows us to exogenously manipulate answer politeness and the evaluator’s role (question asker or general audience), which collectively allow us to establish causal relationships between politeness and perceived answer quality. As shown in the subsequent sections, this experiment involves (1) answer generation, (2) informativeness assessment, (3) politeness assessment, and (4) the perceived quality assessment. We describe the experiment platform and each component of the experiment in detail in subsequent sections.

## Answer Generation

To simulate a question-answering environment and to ensure the experiment participants (i.e., AMT workers) have the expertise required to evaluate answer quality, we first selected 10 questions from AMT’s FAQ page.<sup>10</sup> These are frequently asked questions such that typical AMT workers should have sufficient knowledge to evaluate the corresponding answers. We used the official answers provided on the webpage as the basis to generate two versions of the answer with approximately the same information content, but phrased differently to reflect different politeness levels. The list of questions and their corresponding answers can be found in Appendix E. This setup allows us to establish, for each question, two comparable answers in terms of content informativeness with differing and exogenously assigned politeness levels, which enables us to causally examine the effect of politeness on the perceived answer quality.

## Informativeness Assessment

To ensure that each pair of answers created for a given question does contain roughly the same amount of information, we recruited 32 AMT workers, each evaluating all 10 pairs of answers. For each pair of answers, the worker was instructed to indicate from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) how much s/he agreed with the statement that “these two answers convey roughly the same amount of and similar information.” The worker was specifically instructed to “focus on the informativeness of the answers alone and ignore the tone, wording, phrasing, and politeness.” We randomized the order in which the two answers for each question were displayed, as well as the order in which different pairs of answers were displayed, to ensure the response was not influenced by the order of display. The informativeness assessment result is shown in Table 6. The t-test results show that workers generally agreed that each pair of answers provides roughly the same amount of and similar information, suggesting our generated answers are comparable.

## Politeness Assessment

Having established the similarity in terms of informativeness for each pair of answers, we next recruited another group of 80 AMT workers to evaluate the politeness level of each answer. For each answer, the worker was asked to “evaluate the politeness level of the answer, regardless of whether you agreed with the answer or not, and regardless of the answer quality.” Specifically, we pooled all 20 answers (2 answers for each of the 10 questions) together, and randomly assigned 5 answers (not necessarily corresponding to the same question) to each AMT worker. Therefore, each answer on average received 20 politeness level evaluations (from 1 = very impolite, to 7 = very polite). This setup implies that the worker generally did not see both answers corresponding to the same question, which ensures that the worker was not comparing the difference between the pair of answers and instead focused on the task of politeness assessment alone. The answer assignment and the order in which different answers were displayed were completely randomized to ensure that the politeness assessment was not influenced by the order of display. We calculated the average politeness rating for each answer, then conducted two-sample t-tests with unequal variances to compare each of the two answers corresponding to a given question. The average politeness rating for each answer as well as the t-test results are shown in Table 7, where we have labeled the answer with the lower politeness rating among the pair as Answer 1 and labeled the one with higher politeness rating as Answer 2. These results suggest that the politeness levels between answers corresponding to a given question are significantly different.

## Perspective-Taking Manipulations

Up to this point we have successfully demonstrated the similarity in terms of content informativeness, and the difference in terms of politeness level, for each pair of answers corresponding to a given question. Since the objective of this experiment is to examine the causal relationship between politeness and perceived answer quality moderated by the evaluator’s role (i.e., question asker or general audience), we need to be able to simulate a question asker and a general audience condition, where subjects assigned to a given condition will evaluate the answer quality from the assigned perspective. In other words, subjects assigned to the question asker condition should evaluate answers as if they were the original question askers; subjects assigned to the general audience condition will evaluate the answer quality as if they were reading questions posted by others. To this end, we adopted a perspective-taking strategy developed in the psychology literature to help us exogenously manipulate and assign subjects into the question asker perspective and the general audience perspective. See Ku et al. (2015) for a review of perspective-taking strategies and manipulations used in the management and social psychology literature.

## Experimental Design

According to Ku et al. (2015), perspective-taking involves simulating “what it would be like to be someone else and to see the world from that person’s viewpoint.” Several studies have operationalized perspective-taking through prompts that contain explicit instructions. For example, in a negotiation study, Galinsky et al. (2008) explicitly asked participants (hypothetical buyers) to understand what the sellers are thinking, and “try to imagine what you would be thinking in that role”; Batson et al. (2003) used a similar strategy to manipulate participants’ perspective in a study of moral actions. In our experiment we followed a similar approach. Specifically, we recruited another group of 123 AMT workers—none of whom was involved in the earlier informativeness or politeness assessments—and randomly assigned them into the question asker condition (62 workers) or the general audience condition (61 workers). Prior to reading the questions and the corresponding answers, AMT workers assigned to the question asker condition were given the following instructions:

When you evaluate these answers, imagine how the person who had asked the question would feel and evaluate these answers. Concentrate on how the person is struggling to understand how MTurk works, and how much the person would appreciate getting his/her questions answered. Try to visualize how the person would feel when reading these answers. Select the better answer.

## Experiment Results

Finally, we analyzed AMT workers’ answer selection decisions for both conditions. While the display order of answers was randomized during the experiment, in the analysis here we labeled the answer with the higher politeness rating as the “polite” answer for ease of exposition. Then, for each worker and each question, we determined whether the polite answer was chosen. We pooled all responses from both groups of workers together and ran a question-level logistic regression, where the outcome variable is 1 if the polite answer was chosen for a given question, and 0 if the impolite answer was chosen. We included question-fixed effects and worker-level clustering of standard errors. The result is shown in Table 9. This table shows that the coefficient associated with the variable “Is\_Question\_Asker\_Condition” is significantly positive, which suggests that workers assigned to the question asker condition are significantly more likely to select the polite answer as the better answer compared to workers assigned to the general audience condition, thereby establishing the causal relationship between the politeness of answers and the perceived answer quality, moderated by the evaluator’s role. This finding is consistent with the results from the observational study where we found that the question asker prefers polite answers more than the general audience does.

We also examined whether workers assigned to the question asker condition generally prefer polite to impolite answers: for each participant we computed the proportion of questions (out of a total of 10 questions) in response to which the parti cipant selected the more polite answer. For example, if a participant selected the more polite answer for 8 out of 10 questions, then the proportion is 8/10 = 0.8. We computed this proportion for each participant and conducted a t-test examining whether this proportion is different from 0.5 (which corresponds to the case where participants on average select as many polite answers as impolite answers). The result, shown in Table 10, shows that participants assigned to the question asker condition select polite answers much more frequently than impolite answers.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Content quality assessment for ENPs and CQAs is an important research topic in the IS literature. A suitable quality assessment method can help users locate high quality content. Since content provision on ENPs and CQAs is voluntary and open to anyone, the quality level is highly heterogeneous and its assessment at a large scale is often challenging.

We first conducted an observational study using data from Stack Exchange by examining how question asker acceptance is correlated with the impoliteness level of an answer. Estimation results from a random coefficient logit model suggest that an answer’s impoliteness level is negatively associated with question asker acceptance. On the other hand, our semiplacebo test based on general audience reception, another measure of perceived quality, shows that such a negative association disappears when answer quality is assessed by users who are not the direct recipient of the answer. These results are consistent with predictions from the politeness theory: since CQAs are by nature advice-seeking and advicegiving platforms and hence more relevant to the negative face, we hypothesized and empirically verified that impoliteness is negatively correlated with the quality perception of the direct recipient of the message (i.e., the question asker). One important implication of this finding is that content quality assessment appears to be sensitive to the particular evaluation measure being used, and the subjective nature of quality perception suggests that the politeness expressed in a message would influence the quality evaluation which leads to what we called the politeness bias.

We then conducted a randomized experiment to show that, controlling for answer informativeness, question askers consistently prefer polite answers more than the general audience does. This means answer evaluation on CQA platforms might be influenced by factors that are unrelated to objective quality, such as the politeness of the answer. As a consequence, the politeness bias might affect the overall effectiveness of knowledge exchange on CQA platforms.

Overall, this study integrates insights from the politeness theory as well as the knowledge exchange and ENP literature, and suggests a number of important implications for researchers and practitioners.

## Theoretical Contribution

The first theoretical implication of our study is that the degree of politeness expressed in a text plays an important role in quality perception and assessment. Specifically, since CQA is essentially an advice-seeking and advice-giving platform, the majority of exchange happening on CQA is face threatening (Brown and Levinson 1987), and the politeness theory suggests that the question asker is susceptible to face threats contained in an answer. Our study shows that this susceptibility to face threats affects the question asker’s evaluation of answer quality: answers that exhibit a lower level of face threats are more likely to be perceived by question askers as better answers than other answers. In contrast, the general audience would perceive a minimal level of face threats from the same answer than the question asker because they are not the direct recipient of the answer. Although the politeness/ face threatening level of an answer should not be directly related to its actual quality level, the agents evaluating its quality can be significantly affected by it and hence provide different quality assessments. This politeness bias reveals the drawback of assessing answer quality based on the perception of the question asker.

The subjective nature of quality evaluation, as reflected in our results, suggests that ENP and CQA researchers should carefully examine if any quality-independent factors (such as the politeness level) might be present in their research contexts which could confound the quality assessment process. Similarly, a CQA’s choice of quality assessment methods should be carefully considered to facilitate effective knowledge exchange.

Several social science disciplines such as economics and finance have started to incorporate psychology theories and findings into their core theory building process to better reflect human behaviors (e.g., Barberis and Thaler 2003; Rabin 1998). Politeness bias can play an important role in interpersonal communication processes, and hence computermediated communications such as those taking place on ENPs and CQAs might all be influenced by it. Researchers are encouraged to explore more into the extent to which the politeness bias affects communication as well as the knowledge exchange process.

The study of politeness and its effect on CQA quality assessment is a relatively new research area. Although knowledge exchange, ENPs, and CQAs have been studied extensively in the IS literature, the intersection of the politeness theory and the knowledge exchange process is unique in the literature and provides opportunities for future research. We believe our paper explores an exciting gray area of IS research (Whinston and Geng 2004) where diverse disciplines such as IS, psychology, communication, and economics are integrated to provide an interdisciplinary understanding of the IT artifact.

## Managerial Implication

Our empirical investigation suggests that quality assessment methods are subjective and can be affected by qualityindependent factors such as politeness. This has important implications for practitioners.

We believe there are at least two drawbacks of adopting the question asker based “Best Answer.” First, users are more reluctant to contribute answers once the “Best Answer” is selected due to the incentive mechanism designed by Stack Exchange.<sup>11</sup> Second, as we have shown in this paper, the “Best Answer” is really the best answer perceived by the question asker. While the best answer selection can signal to other users that the selected answer was able to help resolve the question asker’s issue, we have shown that the question asker is susceptible to the politeness bias. In addition, many users often face the same problem raised by the question asker but with different underlying causes or contexts. Eliciting a variety of answers from a large community is thus very important. By placing the “Best Answer” above all other answers, the “Best Answer” necessarily attracts more viewers and thereby more votes, and its name also suggests that it has the best objective quality. As a result, viewers may be biased prior to assessing the objective quality of each answer. These exogenous design features could distort the natural sorting process of answers that is based on collective voting, leading to certain high-quality answers buried among answers or even some contributors being discouraged. Ultimately, the inefficiency and distortion risks increasing search cost for viewers and reducing contributions by answer providers.

Meanwhile, removing the question asker’s option to approve an answer also has negative effects. For example, question askers might be less engaged and answer providers could also be less motivated because they certainly want to see their inputs help solve problems.

Considering the trade-offs discussed above, we recommend that platforms display the number of votes in a more prominent position compared with the answer selected by the question asker; we also recommend changing the name “Best Answer” to “Asker Accepted Answer” or other more neutral and accurate names to reduce the bias introduced by the name “Best Answer.”<sup>12</sup> The platform can also provide question askers with alternative ways to express their appreciation to the answerers, such as awarding bonus reputation points, so that the question asker would not feel compelled to select any answer as the best answer. If each user can assess the quality of an answer independently without bias, the collective voting outcome should become more informative and thus more useful to users. Moreover, since users tend not to post answers to questions that already have their corresponding best answer selected, platforms might benefit from requiring question askers to select their preferred answer only after a sufficient amount of time has passed or after a sufficient number of answers have been posted. The platform can also intentionally delay the display of the question-asker-selected answer until a sufficient amount of time has passed or a sufficient number of answers has been posted. Such design changes can potentially motivate more users to contribute their knowledge.

Brown and Levinson (1987) summarized some common strategies, known as redressing, to communicate negative facethreatening FTAs while also preserving politeness in advicegiving situations: instead of making one’s point directly and posing face threats to the addressee, the speaker or writer can use strategies to lower the perceived face threat. Therefore, we recommend that answer providers compose answers with minimal abrasiveness so as to pose little face threat. After all, the goal is to help question askers. In addition, CQAs might also automatically suggest some level of redressing when a user posts an answer, similar to the idea of automatic grammar check. In this way, a minimum level of politeness can be maintained in the CQAs, and the impact of politeness bias on the question asker will be reduced or even eliminated. As a result, the overall quality assessment mechanism will work more effectively.

Overall, the easier it is for users to locate high quality content, the more valuable the CQA would be to users. Since CQAs such as Stack Exchange have become increasingly popular and are expected to become a major player in the technology and education scene, it is imperative that CQAs understand the politeness bias and its consequence to ensure effective knowledge exchange and successful commercialization. A careful design of quality assessment methods can be highly beneficial to the growth of these platforms.

## Limitation and Future Research

Our research is not without limitations. First, in the current study we focused on the politeness analysis of the question asker/addressee’s negative face. In reality, the answer provider/writer would also be facing certain degrees of face threats, which requires further examination. In addition, we only studied politeness bias in the context of CQAs. It is unclear how and to what degree the politeness bias might affect other types of knowledge management systems. That said, we believe our econometric analysis and randomized experiment based on the politeness theory in the context of CQAs is an important first step toward understanding the role politeness bias plays in influencing user behavior and decision making, and we hope researchers will continue exploring the impact of politeness bias in different contexts.

## Details of Random Coefficient Logit Model Estimation Procedure

In this section, we briefly describe our estimation procedures of the random coefficient logit model used to study the question asker acceptance. As described in the main text, we specify a random coefficient logit model to study the relationship between politeness and best answer choices. Recall that Equation (2) is the probability of a given answer being chosen. Let $y _ { q a }$ be the indicator which takes the value 1 if answer $^ { a , }$ which addresses question $q ,$ is chosen as the best answer, and 0 otherwise. Then the likelihood function for question q can be written as

$$
S _ {q} = \int \prod_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \left[ \frac {\exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta\right)}{\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta\right)} \right] ^ {y _ {q a}} f (\beta | \theta) d \beta\tag{3}
$$

Since the integral in Equation (3) does not have a closed form solution, we approximate it through simulation (Train 2009). Specifically, for question q and a given θ, draw $\beta$ from f(β|θ) a total of R times and mark them as $\beta ^ { 1 } , \beta ^ { 2 } , . . . , \beta ^ { R }$ . Then we can use these draws to approximate the likelihood of a given question, and the integral in Equation (3) becomes

$$
\widehat {S _ {q}} = \frac {1}{R} \sum_ {r = 1} ^ {R} \prod_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \left[ \frac {\exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta^ {r}\right)}{\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta^ {r}\right)} \right] ^ {y _ {q a}}\tag{4}
$$

Our goal is to estimate the parameters, $\theta ,$ of the density function f(β|θ). Recall that β is normally distributed. Assume $\beta \sim \mathrm { N } ( \mu , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ . The mean, $\mu ,$ and variance, σ², of β can be estimated through maximizing the joint simulated log-likelihood function of all questions:

$$
S L L = \sum_ {q = 1} ^ {Q} \ln \left[ \widehat {S _ {q}} \right] = \sum_ {q = 1} ^ {Q} \ln \left\{\frac {1}{R} \sum_ {r = 1} ^ {R} \prod_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \left[ \frac {\exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta^ {\gamma}\right)}{\sum_ {a = 1} ^ {A (q)} \exp \left(X _ {q a} ^ {\prime} \cdot \beta^ {\gamma}\right)} \right] ^ {y _ {q a}} \right\}\tag{5}
$$

The maximum simulated likelihood estimator (MSLE) maximizes Equation (5). Note that during the model estimation, questions that received only one answer and questions that did not have a corresponding best answer chosen will be dropped. This is because answers corresponding to these questions do not have within cluster variations in their outcome variable and are thus uninformative to the model estimation.

## APPENDIX D

## Evaluation of the Reputation Score Reconstruction Procedure

Recall that the data dump provided by Stack Exchange does not include the time series data of users’ reputation score history, and therefore we had to reconstruct such data. To verify that the reconstructed reputation scores are indeed reliable, we compare the reconstructed scores with those retrieved from Stack Exchange’s API.<sup>1</sup> Specifically, we randomly select 500 answers and use the Stack Exchange API to retrieve the answer providers’ approximate reputation score at the time of posting their answers. We then compare our own reconstructed reputation score with the API-returned reputation score. In the graph below (Figure D1) we plot each answer provider’s reconstructed reputation score on the x-axis and the API-returned reputation score on the y-axis. We also plot the 45-degree line (i.e., y = x) for ease of interpretation. If the reconstructed reputation scores are similar to the API-returned reputation scores, then the points should lie on or close to the 45-degree line. As shown in Figure D1, almost all points lie along or close to the 45-degree line, with very few exceptions (those exceptions are mostly users whose reconstructed reputation score is 0 while the API-returned reputation score is nonzero, or vice versa). This shows that our reconstructed reputation scores are reasonably close to the approximation provided by the official Stack Exchange platform, suggesting our reconstruction mechanism is satisfactory.
