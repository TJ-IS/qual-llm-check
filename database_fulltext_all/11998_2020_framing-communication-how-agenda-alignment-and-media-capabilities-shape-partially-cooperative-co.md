# FRAMING COMMUNICATION: HOW AGENDA ALIGNMENTAND MEDIA CAPABILITIES SHAPE PARTIALLYCOOPERATIVE COMMUNICATION<sup>1</sup>

Andrew Harrison and Jaime B. Windeler

Department of Operations, Business Analytics and Information Systems, Carl H. Lindner College of Business, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, OH 45221 U.S.A. {Andrew.Harrison@uc.edu} {Jaime.Windeler@uc.edu}

People frame their communications to suit their personal agendas, even when they are collaborating. How ever, most information systems (IS) literature assumes people working together have completely aligned agendas. The assumption of agenda alignment in prior research motivates our examination of how agenda and media capabilities influence communication performance. By relaxing the assumption of complete agenda alignment, we reconceptualize how media capabilities interact with communication processes to influence communication performance. We empirically test our model using a lab study with 712 participants interacting face-to-face or via virtual worlds on tasks with differing degrees of agenda alignment. We used a hierarchical linear modeling approach to test our hypotheses. Our results support the role of media synchronicity for explaining communication performance when agendas are aligned. However, our study shows that relaxing the assumption of agenda alignment changes communication in profound ways. We find that media synchronicity is insufficient for describing how media capabilities influence performance in partially cooperative communication contexts. Our findings reveal the importance of anonymity and communication framing when communicants’ agendas differ.

Keywords: Computer-mediated communication, synchronicity, media capabilities, anonymity, virtual world, virtual reality, agenda alignment, partial cooperation

## Introduction

Communication is critical to business; it is important for planning, motivating, socializing, controlling, and negotiating, and is a cause of, and solution to, conflict (Barki and Hartwick 2001; Fisher 1981). Communication is increasingly important in today’s digital world where technology enables broader reach, faster diffusion, and greater connectivity than any time in human history. In light of the prominent role of communication in the digital world, communication performance is a key concern—organizations and individuals alike want to understand how to communicate well (Lewis 2019).

Communication research has generally addressed this subject by evaluating the needs, motivations, and goals of communicators. An important phenomenon in communication research that encapsulates the needs, motivations, and goals of communicators is communication framing. Communication framing is the strategic presentation of a message with the intention to influence an audience’s interpretation of the message (Matthes 2009; Scheufele 1999; Shah et al. 2002). For example, exaggerations and the strategic withholding of information are communication frames commonly used to achieve personal agendas in day-to-day communications (George and Robb 2008). How a message is framed is based on two factors: (1) the communication processes needed to induce a particular interpretation of a message and (2) the communicators’ agenda alignment (Chong and Druckman 2007; Price et al. 1997). When agendas are closely aligned, less framing is needed, but when agendas are misaligned, communicators may engage in more effortful communication processes to influence how their message is received.

While communication research has explored how communicators deliberately frame messages, IS research has focused on how technologies affect how messages are exchanged. In early computer-mediated communication (CMC) research, theories of communication performance revolved around the importance of a technology’s rich set of features and its ability to support “natural” modes of human expression, such a visual and audible cues (Daft and Lengel 1986; Kock 2004). More recent theories have shifted focus away from media richness and naturalness, to focus on the fit between the capabilities of the medium and the needs of the communicators (Dennis et al. 2008, Fuller and Dennis 2009).

This shift has led CMC research to incorporate communication processes into models of computer-mediated communication performance. This prior research identifies how the fit between a medium’s capabilities and communication processes drives communication performance (Dennis et al. 2008; Fuller and Dennis 2009). Two fundamental communication processes, convergence and conveyance, help communicants resolve equivocality and uncertainty by “in essence, ‘framing’ the situation” (Dennis and Valacich 1999, p. 4). During communication, a relative emphasis on convergence or conveyance can result in different communication frames, which in turn affect communication performance (O’Neill et al. 2016).

Although CMC research incorporates communication processes, the other aspect of communication framing—agenda alignment—has received less attention. CMC research typically assumes people share an identical set of prioritized goals with no need or desire to withhold information. Although some IS research does not assume complete cooperation, it tends to posit the opposite and explores deception and fraud (Carlson and George 2004). Such research is critical; however, there remain opportunities to explore a middle ground between complete cooperation and non-cooperation by considering how communication framing influences communication performance in CMC.

Exploring this middle ground is important for three reasons. First, if we ignore agenda alignment, our models will be bounded by cooperative assumptions that people always share identical goals organized in the same hierarchy of importance. This ignores the partially cooperative communication (George and Robb 2008) that occurs when people work together without sharing identical agendas (Sarangi and Slembrouck 1992). Some scholars argue that all organizational communication is partially cooperative and complete agenda alignment is impossible due to self-interest and opportunism (Simon

1945). However, even if we assume complete cooperation is possible, many common organizational activities involve communication with conflicting or partially overlapping agendas including, “compensation, regulation, leadership, impression management, whistle-blowing, vertical integration, [and] transfer pricing” (Eisenhardt 1989, p. 59). Navigating relationships with misaligned agendas is a common experience for information systems professionals, who often span boundaries across organizational functions (Bahli and Rivard 2003). Second, agenda alignment is critical in determining how we define communication performance. Knowing or assuming that you and your communication partner share similar goals sets a cooperative tone for your communication, where shared understanding is ultimately sought. However, when agendas are opposing or misaligned, the exertion of personal influence to change others’ views may be a better reflection of communication performance. Thus, incorporating agenda alignment into CMC research is critical to capturing the various outcomes sought during interpersonal communication.

Third, accounting for agenda alignment is critical from a technological perspective. It is problematic to assume completely cooperative orientations because it may induce biases toward technologies that enable a certain type of communication performance—shared understanding. IT capabilities that support shared understanding, such as a medium’s support for synchronicity, clearly have a role to play in partially cooperative communication. However, when personal influence is a desired outcome, other capabilities—such as anonymity— may take center stage and synchronicity may be less salient. Accordingly, we seek to understand: how does agenda alignment interact with communication processes and media capabilities to influence communication performance in computermediated environments?

Our study contributes to the IS literature by integrating the communication literature on agenda alignment and communication framing with the literature on CMC. Our research implies boundaries for theories that assume agenda alignment (George et al. 2013; Sarker et al. 2010; Valacich et al. 2009) and offers specific implications for understanding the relative importance of media capabilities and communication processes for communicants with differing agendas. We use these insights to develop a model of communication performance that is generalizable to widespread, partially cooperative interpersonal communication.

In this paper, we begin by exploring this research question against the backdrop of media synchronicity theory (MST), which evaluates how media capabilities, communication processes, and appropriation factors affect communication performance when communicants’ agendas are aligned.<sup>2</sup> Next, using the propositions within MST as a starting point, we explore how agenda alignment and communication processes interact, reconceptualizing communication performance in light of partial-cooperation, and evaluating additional media capabilities that may affect communication performance when agendas are misaligned. Specifically, we identify media capabilities that relate to anonymity and enable people to assert personal influence through communication framing.

We empirically test our model by drawing on game theory to develop a lab experiment that allows misalignment in individuals’ agendas. We then analyze data from our lab experiment using a hierarchical linear modeling approach to account for individual and group-level dynamics that affect communication performance. In our analyses, we compare the outcomes of the experiment against a second experimental task in which participants were encouraged to align their agendas. Finally, we use these comparisons to evaluate how well various models explain partially cooperative and completely cooperative communication performance, and discuss the implications of our results.

## Theoretical Background

We take a three-step approach to develop this research project. First, we reconceptualize communication processes in terms of agenda alignment. When agendas are unaligned, there is greater need to control the information that is conveyed and to influence how it is interpreted during convergence (Chong and Druckman 2007), suggesting an interaction between agenda alignment and communication processes. This perspective presents communication framing as a combination of communication processes and agenda alignment (Chong and Druckman 2007), and directs us to incorporate communication framing into our model predicting communication performance. Second, we reconceptualize communication performance. We assert shared understanding—the outcome specified by many models of CMC—may not be the best reflection of communication performance when communicants’ agendas are not aligned and do not wish to share all information they possess. Third, we identify media capabilities that are important when we relax the assumption of agenda alignment. Extant theories of CMC posit that communication performance is driven by an interaction between communication processes (or task needs) and media capabilities (Dennis et al. 2008; Maruping and Agarwal 2004). When we alter our conceptualization of communication processes and communication performance, we posit that the media capabilities that support this relationship may also change. Following the development of our model and hypotheses, we test the hypotheses using a multilevel analytical approach incorporating individuals’ interests and group-level influences related to communication.

## Conceptualizing Communication Processes via Communication Framing

Theories that assume agenda alignment are insufficient to explain partially cooperative communication because they do not account for the way differing agendas affect communication (Rigotti 2005). When communicants strive toward different agendas, they must control their information transmission and influence information processing (Price et al. 1997). This need for control and influence impacts how people coordinate, and suggests that agenda alignment interacts with communication processes. Most CMC theories do not account for these needs, and by extension, they do not capture which media capabilities are suitable for supporting partially cooperative communication.

To illustrate this issue, consider a situation with completely unaligned agendas—an advanced fee scam, where a victim is persuaded to pay money in expectation of receiving something for greater value (Edelson 2003). CMC theories like MST would suggest that these scams, which typically require convergence, create greater shared understanding when using media with greater capabilities for synchronicity. But when one communicant is deliberately manipulating messages to benefit from information asymmetry, more convergence and synchronicity may not improve shared understanding. Skillful manipulators may even prefer synchronous media that present a more convincing facade in order to diminish shared understanding (Carlson and George 2004).

To account for how differing agendas impact communication, we leverage the concept of communication framing to conceptualize an interaction between communication processes and agenda alignment. Communicants’ agendas, and their preferences for convergence or conveyance, are manifest in the way they choose to frame communication (Drake and Donahue 1996; O’Neill et al. 2016). People use communication framing to influence the way messages are interpreted in order to pursue their personal agendas (Scheufele 1999). When communicants have aligned agendas they engage in less communication framing; when their agendas are unaligned they engage in more framing (Rigotti 2005).

Although communication framing supports a person’s agenda, it also reflects the communication processes that person uses during communication (Chong and Druckman 2007). Communicants’ differing communication frames tend to drift toward each other (converge) to promote agreement (Drake and Donahue 1996). Similarly, when using conveyancebased messaging, people are more likely to accept appeals that are framed as personally relevant, are more likely to argue against messages when their agendas differ (Rothman and Salovey 1997). Accordingly, we incorporate the concept of communication framing in our model as an interaction between communication processes and agenda alignment (Nisbet et al. 2013). By considering how communication processes and agenda alignment together frame communication we address a broad spectrum of communication ranging from to antagonistic, such as spoofed websites and advanced fee scams, to cooperative, such as helpdesk support and security alerts, to every-day interpersonal interaction that is partially cooperative (Eisenhardt 1989).

As we relax the assumption of agenda alignment, other media capabilities that reveal or suppress communicants’ agendas become increasingly relevant. Media capabilities such as synchronicity may affect the coordination of communication (i.e., convergence and conveyance), while other media capabilities may elucidate or obscure communicants’ agendas. Although synchronicity explains how media capabilities and communication processes affect shared understanding, it is not sufficient to address how communicants’ differing agendas may affect other desired communication outcomes. Accordingly, we must consider how communication performance changes when agendas differ to determine relevant media capabilities.

## Conceptualizing Communication Performance: Shared Understanding and Personal Influence

When agendas do not align, shared understanding may not be desired by one or more parties (Carlson and George 2004). The ends to which each person strives may be partially or completely unaligned and consequently, people may conceal or distort the information they share with others to garner beneficial outcomes (McCornack 1992). Such behaviors are part of communication framing, in which people try to influence the way others receive and interpret a message (Katz et al. 2017). Thus, when people have unaligned agendas, they try to influence one another to maximize their self-interest and achieve their agenda (Schmitz and Fulk 1991). Exerting influence through the framing of communication is a primary mechanism for changing others’ behavior (Scheufele 1999). Asserting personal influence (e.g., through persuasion, deception, and coercion) also represents one of the most common ways unaligned agendas influence communication (Sarangi and Slembrouck 1992; Tam and Ho 2006; Thomas and Wareing 2004), and is a primary goal of communication when agendas are unaligned (Toth 2000).

Following this perspective, we conceptualize partially cooperative communication performance in terms of personal influence, which reflects a “change in a person’s cognition, attitude, or behavior, which has its origins in another person” (Raven 1964, p. 371). Broadly speaking, performance represents a goal-directed outcome (McGrath and Hollingshead 1993). When agendas are unaligned, the goal of each communicant—and thus their communication performance—will be to influence their communication partner. The extent to which a person’s cognition, attitude, or behavior are changed to conform to one’s agenda reflects the effectiveness of their communication.<sup>3</sup>

According to MST, communication performance is influenced by an interaction—conceptualized as fit—between communication processes and media capabilities (Dennis et al. 2008). At the same time, other research on communication framing indicates communication processes and agendas interact (Nisbet et al. 2013). This suggests there may be a three-way interaction between communication processes, agenda alignment, and media capabilities. To understand how these interactions occur, we next consider the role of media capabilities in partially cooperative communication (Dennis et al. 2008). We start with a review of media capabilities, then identify our extensions.

## Conceptualizing Media Capabilities: Media Synchronicity and Anonymity

Media capabilities are “structures provided by a medium which influence the manner in which individuals can transmit and process information” (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 583). MST identifies media synchronicity as the proximal media capability supporting communication performance for cooperative communication. Media synchronicity is “the extent to which the capabilities of a communication medium enable individuals to achieve synchronicity”—that is, a shared pattern of coordinated behavior (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 581). Tasks requiring an emphasis on convergence need media that enable faster information transmission with more feedback and verification—higher media synchronicity. In contrast, media with capabilities that support rehearsing and reprocessing a message—lower media synchronicity—are a better fit for conveyance (Dennis et al. 2008). Media capabilities interact with communication processes; media synchronicity enables shared focus between communicants, and media with low synchronicity impedes shared focus (Münzer and Holmer 2009). We use the same logic to deduce that there may be other relevant media capabilities when we integrate agenda alignment into our model of partially cooperative communication. Thus, we sought a media capability that affects partially cooperative communication performance in a similar manner as media synchronicity affects cooperative communication performance.

To identify a relevant media capability, we looked at how Dennis et al. (2008) identified media capabilities relevant to cooperative communication. MST is based on a seminal model developed by Shannon and Weaver (1949) that breaks communication into distinct entities: sender, receiver, message, medium, and environment. We selected Shannon and Weaver’s model as our starting point for a bottom-up structured literature review due to its prominence and use as a foundation for many communication theories; Appendix A details our structured review process.

We began our review by selecting 15 seminal studies<sup>4</sup> that were rooted in Shannon and Weaver’s model and addressed partially cooperative communication contexts. We built from these exemplars to evaluate 120 papers that included 154 variables, some of which were highly similar or identical to one another. Our review of these papers suggests partially cooperative communication performance is affected by the ability to identify other communicants. For example, DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) described how a lack of identifiability improved creativity within groups, but masked relevant political information used for negotiating solutions. Similarly, Nunamaker et al. (1991) found that anonymous group decision support systems encouraged broader participation but increased conflict. In their research, Schmitz and Fulk (1991) noted that individuals may intentionally choose less revealing media in order to create confusion to achieve their own goals. Our review identified anonymity, auditability, complexity, confidentiality, editability, empowerment, extensibility, persistence, privacy, social presence, transmissibility, transparency, and verifiability as media capabilities related to the ability to conceal or detect agendas. We arranged these constructs according to Shannon and Weaver’s model and whether the authors of each work attributed the construct to the communication sender, receiver, message, medium or environment. Our arrangement of these constructs allowed us to integrate the prior literature with MST by organizing similar constructs.

The concept that deals with the relative difficulty of discerning identities of communicants is frequently referred to as anonymity, perceived anonymity, social anonymity, or visibility. Anonymity represents the extent to which a message or information about its sender or receiver is perceived as inaccessible to others (Griffith and Northcraft 1994). When it is conceptualized as a socially derived media capability, anonymity is a continuous, subjective, perceptual attribute, rather than an objective, binary trait of the medium (Pinsonneault and Heppel 1997). This definition of anonymity relates to the sender and receiver’s interpretations of the message, rather than the technical availability of information. Research shows that these conceptualizations of anonymity are distinct; it is possible for people to identify the author of a message that is “technically” anonymous—that is, it contains no identifying information (Hayne et al. 2003).

Research demonstrates anonymity is highly relevant to partially cooperative communication performance. Anonymity lowers social inhibitions, and encourages participation, resulting in a wider range of innovative ideas during group tasks (Carte and Chidambaram 2004; Connolly et al. 1990; Sarker et al. 2010). However, individuals are less likely to consider the interests of others when anonymity is high (Nunamaker et al. 1991). Additionally, anonymity influences how senders frame messages, and how people interpret the framing of messages they receive, in CMC settings (Scott 2007). Lee (2016, p. 203) posits that, “The very process of having to identify oneself … is likely to alter the choices made about the framing of the message.” Such outcomes of anonymity are highly relevant to research about interactions between people with misaligned agendas (Gavish and Gerdes 1998). Anonymity has also been a prominent issue for practice and has been described as the most important attribute of a medium during partially cooperative contexts including choosing to share or withhold sensitive data (Daries et al. 2014) and when falsifying or exaggerating information (Noonan 2007).

Anonymity is regarded as “a fundamental concept in group support systems (GSS) research” (Pinsonneault and Heppel 1997, p. 89), was one of the first constructs observed to influence computer-mediated communication (Jarvenpaa et al. 1988; Myers 1987), and has maintained relevance as Internetbased communication and commerce has proliferated (Chatterjee et al. 2015; Harrison 2018). Due to its prominent role in theory and practice for communication when agendas are not aligned, we incorporated perceptions of anonymity into our model for predicting communication performance.

## Hypotheses Development

Based on our conceptualizations of relevant media capabilities, we develop the research model shown in Figure 1. We first hypothesize the left side of the model, which involves consideration of anonymity, followed by the threeway interactions between media capabilities, agenda alignment, and communication processes. As a means to test these complex relationships, we construct our model within the following boundaries.

First, consistent with Dennis et al.’s (2008) theory, we focus on the impact of individual media capabilities rather than theorizing interrelationships between media capabilities. Second, we hold any appropriation factors<sup>5</sup> constant by controlling for their effects on communication performance rather than hypothesizing their effects on fit. To hypothesize their effects would require a seven-way interaction (e.g., media synchronicity × communication process × agenda alignment × familiarity × training × past experience × social norms), that would be both theoretically and statistically intractable. Third, we hypothesize the effects on the dependent variables at their highest levels. This illustrates the interactions in the context of outcomes most relevant to desired outcomes (high shared understanding and high personal influence) while minimizing redundancy in the hypotheses. Using this approach, we do not explore communication performance with low synchronicity, high anonymity, or conveyance, because we do not expect these to result in the highest levels of shared understanding or personal influence. Although we do not hypothesize the interactions at low levels of shared understanding and personal influence, we report and explore them in our analysis.

## Hypothesizing Communication Performance: Shared Understanding and Personal Influence

Following Dennis et al. (2008), we hypothesize that the medium’s capabilities will interact with communication processes to influence communication performance. Based on our preceding arguments about the importance of agendas, we add agenda alignment to this and model fit as a three-way interaction. Conceptualizing fit as an interaction between characteristics of the task (or communication process) and technology (media capability) is consistent with the tasktechnology fit literature and with MST (Dennis et al. 2008). First, we hypothesize when shared understanding will be highest according to a three-way interaction between communication processes, media synchronicity, and agenda align ment (H1). Then, we hypothesize the same interaction, but substitute anonymity for synchronicity (H2). Finally, we hypothesize the three-way interactions that predict personal influence (H3 and H4).

We expect shared understanding to be highest when communicants’ agendas are aligned and they are engaged in convergence processes using higher synchronicity media. Agenda alignment should enhance shared understanding because when communicants’ agendas are aligned, they should readily exchange information as they move toward their shared agenda. People with aligned agendas typically have more success communicating innovative ideas (Kiousis and McCombs 2004; Valente and Davis 1999). In contrast, when agendas are unaligned, communicants should be less likely to exchange information beneficial to developing shared mental models; it is more difficult for them to focus on the same topics and agree on meaning (Entman 2007; Schildkraut and Muschert 2014). Thus, agenda alignment may eliminate inefficiencies that arise when people are working toward different outcomes.

Convergence includes the mutual agreement of meaning on both sides of an exchange, while conveyance represents the collection and distribution of information to generate individual understanding. Convergence typically requires backand-forth communication to reach shared understanding, whereas conveyance relies on information processing to analyze and make sense of a message. Convergence involves development of a shared mental model of one another’s interpretation of information. In other words, the two-way nature of convergence involves sharing and development of understanding on both sides of an exchange, while conveyance represents a one-way sharing of information. Dennis et al. (2008, p. 580) explain that “Conveyance processes … enable the receiver to create and revise a mental model of the situation” (emphasis added). Based on our preceding arguments, this two-way exchange should be more efficient and effective when agendas are aligned and we expect shared understanding to be higher for agenda-aligned convergence compared to agenda-aligned conveyance.

Dennis et al. (2008) propose that shared understanding is higher when communicants are converging on meaning using higher synchronicity media. We follow their logic, which suggests that back-and-forth sharing of preprocessed information, as well as verifying one others’ interpretation of information, enhances convergence (Dennis et al. 2008). Higher transmission velocity and higher symbol set variety are two physical media capabilities linked to media synchronicity (Dennis et al. 2008). Transmission velocity makes coordination more efficient by reducing lags that can lead to information loss, while symbol set variety provides more avenues through which verification and coordination can occur. Thus, high synchronicity supports coordination and verification important for convergence and facilitated by greater information exchange due to agenda alignment.

H1: Shared understanding will be highest when communicants with aligned agendas are converging on meaning using higher synchronicity media.

By similar rationale, we expect media with higher capabilities for anonymity to diminish shared understanding during convergence processes when communicants’ agendas are aligned. When anonymity is high, communicants will have greater difficulty verifying the accuracy of information shared (Davenport 2002; Drouin et al. 2016); this should lower the exchange of information and shared understanding. In contrast, media with capabilities for lower anonymity provide communicants with more information about the identity of communicants and the messages they send. This should make it easier to verify understanding during convergence and more efficient to ascertain agenda alignment (Bialaszewski and Giallourakis 1985; Jarvenpaa and Leidner 1999; Wang et al. 2016). Low anonymity media supports coordination and verification important for convergence when agendas are aligned (Wilson et al. 2015).

H2: Shared understanding will be highest when communicants with aligned agendas are converging on meaning using lower anonymity media.

We expect personal influence to be highest when communicants’ agendas are unaligned and they are converging on meaning using higher synchronicity media. Recall that personal influence is defined as a change in a person’s cognition, attitude, or behavior. The extent of change will be highest when communicants have unaligned agendas. The more incongruent the agendas, the more influence will be required to change a person’s cognition, attitude, or behavior (Rigotti 2005). Thus, unaligned agendas will evoke more and stronger attempts at personal influence compared to aligned agendas (Nisbet et al. 2013).

Convergence involves fast processing of information which permits verification, adjustment of position, and negotiation (Dennis et al. 2008), which will confer more opportunities for personal influence. It should be easier to change a person’s behavior or attitude during convergence processes because you can adjust influence tactics as you receive information from the other person. Faster information processing also denies the other person resources (e.g., time) to deliberate, uncover information, and mitigate influence. Consequently, convergence involving unaligned agendas should increase personal influence.

Higher synchronicity media enables rapid back-and-forth processing and immediate feedback during convergence (Dennis et al. 2008). This not only puts greater time pressure on the exchange, but it allows communicants to send “installments” of messages that can help them assess a person’s agreement and understanding (Clark and Brennan 1991). Time pressure reduces motivation to processes information systematically (De Dreu 2003). The pressure afforded by convergence with high synchronicity media should further increase the likelihood that a communication partner will disclose information useful for devising influence tactics. Conversely, media lower in synchronicity permit reprocessing of information and rehearsing of responses (Dennis et al. 2008). This should lower personal influence by allowing more opportunity to deliberate on willingness to change position and craft counter-influence tactics.

H3: Personal influence will be highest when communicants with unaligned agendas are converging on meaning using higher synchronicity media.

Turning to anonymity, we expect personal influence to be highest when communicants’ agendas are unaligned and participants are converging on meaning using low anonymity media. We expect reduced normative pressures and deindividuation associated with higher anonymity media to diminish personal influence. Deindividuation is a psychological state of decreased self-evaluation that is fostered by computermediated environments (Chatterjee et al. 2015) and causes anti-normative and disinhibited behaviors (Diener 1979). Lower social inhibition and accountability to others diminishes one’s willingness to succumb to normative influence (Nunamaker et al. 1991; Postmes et al. 2001). Feelings of deindividuation negatively influence group performance when group members have differing goals (Jung et al. 2010). Deindividuation, when combined with opportunism in computer-mediated environments, reduces pressure to behave ethically (Chatterjee et al. 2015) and leads to antisocial behaviors (Jessup et al. 1990). In contrast, low anonymity environments promote social norms and curtail deindividuation (Bhattacherjee 2002). Consequently, we expect low anonymity media will be more useful for asserting personal influence when people have unaligned agendas and need to converge on meaning.

H4: Personal influence will be highest when communicants with unaligned agenda are converging on meaning using lower anonymity media.

## Methodology

To test our research model, we conducted a laboratory experiment that allowed us to vary both media and task. We varied the media to manipulate media capabilities and varied the task to manipulate communication framing. This permitted us to examine the interaction between media capabilities, communication processes, and agenda alignment. We organized participants into groups of four and instructed them to work on two tasks: a puzzle-solving task and a game theoretic investment task. Each group collaborated on the tasks using one of three mediums: (1) face-to-face (F2F), (2) a virtual world with a traditional interface (VWTD), or (3) a virtual world with a virtual reality interface (VWVR). We chose VWs because they allowed us to compare CMC to face-toface communication. Face-to-face is the most natural communication medium and a “gold standard” against which scholars often compare technology, and VWs were a good fit for the media capabilities we studied. We varied anonymity by allowing participants to customize the look of their avatar, their display name, and providing one-on-one messaging. Figure 2 summarizes the experiment.

Our sampling frame was 1,171 undergraduate business students recruited from courses at a large university in the midwestern United States. Thirty-seven percent of participants identified as women. Of the 1,171 people recruited for the study, 736 participated. The remainder either did not show up for their lab appointment or were scheduled as reserve participants to ensure there were four participants per group. We dropped 6 groups due to incomplete data; the remaining 712 participants comprised 178 groups of 4 people; 208 participants (52 groups) in F2F, 260 participants (65 groups) in VWTD, and 244 participants (61 groups) in VWVR. Groups of four provided a collective, rather than dyadic experience, and the investment task was designed for four-person groups (Camerer and Ho 2015).

## Experimental Procedure

When participants registered for the study, we emailed them a link to an online survey. The first page of the survey informed them of the study procedures and obtained their consent to participate. The first online survey assessed demographics and some appropriation factors. Participants completed this survey before their lab appointment. When participants arrived at the lab, they took a second online survey to assess perceptions of media capabilities. Participants in F2F completed the second survey in a computer lab. After finishing the survey, a research assistant led participants to a room where they collaborated on the group tasks. In F2F, group members sat together at a table; in VWTD and VWVR, a research assistant seated participants in separate rooms as they arrived to the lab.

To preserve perceptions of anonymity, we took care to ensure all group communication in VWTD and VWVR was computer-mediated. We seated participants in their individual rooms immediately upon their arrival to the lab and closed the doors. A white noise machine suppressed ambient noise and we staggered participant room assignment. The participants remained isolated in these rooms for the duration of the experiment and only communicated with other participants through their avatars. Finally, we asked participants if they knew the identity of any group members. This variable— familiarity with group members—was an appropriation factor and was non-significant, suggesting it did not influence our results.

Once participants in VWTD and VWVR were isolated in a room, a research assistant helped them customize their avatar and practice using the VW controller. They could customize their avatar’s gender expression, skin color, hair, eyes, body shape (i.e., weight), clothing, and display name. A research assistant told participants they were not required to use their real name or appearance for their avatar and that their actual identity would not be known to their group. Next, they performed a 5-minute training to become familiar with the VR controls while participants in F2F moved directly to the first task.

When participants were ready begin, they watched a video explaining the rules of the first task. The scripts for the videos were identical across conditions; however, we demonstrated the tasks using the relevant medium. For example, actors in the video shown to participants in F2F performed the tasks face-to-face. After watching the video, participants completed the first task with their group. On completion of the first task, participants watched a video explaining the rules of the second task, then completed the second task. We varied the order in which groups completed the tasks to control for order effects. After both tasks, participants completed a final survey to assess agenda alignment and communication processes. As each participant finished, a research assistant debriefed, paid, and dismissed them.

## Tasks

We had three criteria in selecting tasks for the experiment. First, we wanted the tasks to have objective, non-perceptual performance metrics. This was important in diminishing common method variance. Second, we wanted to ensure the media capabilities were relevant to the tasks and media we chose. Third, the tasks we chose are widely used in social science research (Camerer and Ho 2015; Kotovsky et al. 1985). To induce variance in communication framing, we chose two tasks: the Tower of Hanoi puzzle (i.e., tower task) and the public goods with punishment game (i.e., investment task).

The Tower of Hanoi puzzle involves three rods in a straight line (labeled rod A, B, and C) and four disks of different sizes. At the start of the puzzle, the disks are stacked in a pyramid on rod A with the smallest disk on top and largest on bottom. Participants move only one disk at a time and cannot place a larger disk on a smaller disk. The objective is to move the disks to rod C in the fewest number of moves, restoring them to their original order. The minimum number of moves is 15. Participants took turns deciding how to move the disks and getting feedback from their group; their goal was to complete the puzzle in the fewest turns. Thus, the task required participants to coordinate actions to reach a shared agenda.

The investment task is the “public goods with punishment” game (Camerer and Ho 2015; Fehr and Gächter 2000). It is a prisoner’s dilemma game designed to elicit partial cooperation between self-interested players. The goal is to maximize individual returns on an investment. Although groups can cooperate, this task constrains cooperation because it involves independent decision-making, mechanisms for punishment, and a goal of maximizing individual returns. Before the task, we motivated participants by explaining they could earn an Amazon gift certificate based on their individual performance.

The task comprises six rounds and each round has two phases: investment and punishment. At the start of each round, the investment phase begins and each person has one dollar in nickels. During the investment phase, group members simultaneously decide how much money they will contribute toward the group (i.e., the “public good”) versus keep for themselves. Participants earn money based on their choices and are encouraged to discuss their investment strategies with their group members. Any money invested with the group yields a 40% return to all players, regardless of whether a player invested their own money in the group. Any money invested individually yields a 10% return.

For example, imagine a group has three members who each decide to contribute \$1.00 to the group pool (for a total of \$3.00) and a fourth group member who decides to act selfishly and keep their dollar to invest individually. The group pool is split evenly among all members, even those who did not contribute. Thus, all four group members earn 40% of the \$3.00, or \$1.20. The member who acted selfishly earns an additional 10% return on the dollar they invested, or \$1.10. Thus, three members earn \$1.20 in profit while one member earns \$2.30 in profit at the end of the round. The payouts reward groups that act cooperatively; the average return for groups acting cooperatively will be higher than groups that do not work cooperatively. However, individuals who act selfishly and invest individually while their other group members contribute toward the public good can reap greater personal rewards at their group’s expense. While there are incentives for individuals to act selfishly, there are also mechanisms to enforce social norms through the punishment of selfish behaviors.

The punishment phase allows players to punish group members through monetary penalties. A participant may “pay” nickels to penalize other participants after each round. For each nickel a participant pays to penalize, the person being penalized loses three nickels. Thus, the penalty is costly for both parties, but more costly for the person being punished. Punishment is only economically beneficial when it influences the punished player to change their behavior in subsequent rounds. Participants who want to enforce norms of reciprocity (i.e., pressure others to invest with the group) can trade short-term losses for long-term influence. To allow players to recognize punishment as normative pressure rather than wanton aggression, the players are encouraged to share their reasoning for punishment. Thus, players can attempt to increase their own earnings by influencing the behavior of others in subsequent rounds. During the game, participants make investment and punishment decisions independent of one another and simultaneously reveal their decisions to the group. They place nickels on a game board with spaces representing investment and penalty decisions. A divider hides the game board from other players until the research assistant instructed players to reveal their decisions to the group. Given the experiments’ constraints, players must anticipate their group members’ investment strategies, and eventually settle on a trust-based equilibrium though observation and communication (Fehr and Gächter 2000). At the end of six rounds, participants received an Amazon certificate for the larger of \$10 or their earnings in the game. The average payout was \$10.00, which participants were informed of when they completed the first survey.

## Technology

The three conditions, illustrated in Figure 3, were designed to vary perceptions of anonymity. The technologies used in VWTD and VWVR were similar in several ways. The virtual environments used the Unity platform and the design of the VW and avatars in each condition were identical. In both VW conditions, participants used a game controller and could speak to others through a headset with an integrated microphone. VWTD and VWVR also had key differences. Participants in VWTD used two 22-inch monitors, a keyboard, video controller, mouse, and headset for input/output. Participants in VWTD viewed their avatar and those of their group members in a third-person perspective. In contrast, participants in VWVR wore a head-mounted virtual reality display for all group interaction. Participants saw “through the eyes” of their avatar. When they turned their head, scenes in the virtual world changed.

Besides differences in perspective and display, participants in VWTD used a text-based chat system; one monitor showed the virtual environment and the other monitor had the chat window active. Participants in VWTD could send one-onone, direct messages using the chat system. This enabled us to vary anonymity by allowing “invisible whispering,” which has been shown to affect group communication and performance outcomes (Dennis et al. 2010). Only the senders and recipients could access these private messages; the research assistant could not see them or access them later. In contrast, for F2F and VWVR it was difficult to conceal private communication with others.

## Manipulation Check

We performed a manipulation check by assessing perceptions of the media capabilities in our model. We tested differences between the means for each condition using ANOVA. The results demonstrated that our manipulations were successful. The mean values of agenda alignment were significantly different for each task (p < .001); the mean of agenda alignment was 5.80 for the tower task and 4.22 for the investment task. The mean values of media synchronicity were significantly different (p < .001) and varied from 5.67 in the F2F condition, 4.45 in the VWVR condition, and 3.68 in the VWTD condition. The mean of anonymity was 2.52 in the F2F condition, 3.98 in the VWVR condition, and 4.68 in the VWTD condition. These values were also significantly different between the three conditions (p < .001), and the differences were consistent with extant theory (Chatterjee et al. 2015). Because our manipulation was successful, we could have used binary measures of these variables, but opted for participants’ perceptions of both media capabilities and agenda alignment in order to have more variance with which to account for communication performance.

## Results

As described previously, we evaluated our model using perceptual measures for media capabilities, agenda alignment, communication processes, and communication performance. For measures of gender diversity and national diversity, we used Blau’s (1977) index to operationalize differences in group composition. Task order was also measured as a grouplevel variable, while all other variables were measured at the individual-level. For variables measured at an individual level, it was important for our analysis to account for group membership because an individual’s perceptions may vary as a result of interactions with other group members during communication (Gallivan and Benbunan-Fich 2005; Walczuch and Watson 2001). To evaluate the influence of group membership on our data, we tested the interclass correlation coefficients for communication process (.08), agenda alignment (.62), media synchronicity (.84), anonymity (.60), shared understanding (.63), and personal influence (.47). While most of the interclass correlation coefficients demonstrated reliability at a group-level, our theoretical position that individuals may have independent, and perhaps conflicting, agendas suggests that we should collect our data at the individual level and use a multilevel analysis to account for both individual and group-level phenomena rather than exclusively use group-level aggregation (Klein et al. 2001). Similarly, Gallivan and Benbunan-Fich (2005) recommend this approach for “Cluster 4” models, like ours, where data collected at an individual-level of analysis may be affected by group-level interactions. This multilevel approach is wellsuited to group settings where individuals within a group may have differing perceptions that would be obfuscated by grouplevel aggregation (Bliese 2000).

To evaluate the data, we used a random effects hierarchical linear modeling approach that uses a combination of individual and group-level measurement to control for group effects (Preacher et al. 2006). We compared two models, one which predicted shared understanding, and the other which predicted personal influence. We tested the models using the multilevel package in R. This package uses maximum likelihood estimation to evaluate the model. This multilevel modeling approach is recommended for testing causal hypotheses when using data collected from individual members of a group (Gallivan and Benbunan-Fich 2005). In scenarios where group interactions are analyzed using individual responses from the group’s members, this method is necessary to control for possible group-level effects (Klein et al. 2001). Hierarchical linear models account for error that may be correlated between observations due to the grouping of measurement (Garson 2013; Raudenbush and Bryk 2002). Besides controlling for correlated error, hierarchical linear models also allow the specification of group-level variables that may influence individual-level perceptions and behaviors (Preacher et al. 2006). For example, we control for whether the group participated in the tower or investment task first because starting with a more cooperative task may increase cohesion that could affect the second task (Carte and Chidambaram 2004). Similarly, group differences in gender or nationality may affect group agreement (Ahuja et al. 2015).

## Discussion

Our research objective was to understand the implications of partial cooperation during communication and answer the question: how does agenda alignment interact with communication processes and media capabilities to influence communication performance in computer-mediated environments? We explored this question by integrating research about how people frame their messages to exert their personal agendas with a model of CMC where cooperation has been assumed. Our results support the efficacy of MST, a leading theory of CMC, for explaining completely cooperative communication. However, our results showed the interaction between the need for coordination and a medium’s capabilities was insufficient to predict partially cooperative communication performance. Instead, to predict communication performance in partially cooperative contexts, we incorporated communicants agendas and another media capability, anonymity. Our data indicate shared understanding is highest when people with aligned agendas are converging on meaning using media with capabilities for higher synchronicity. Personal influence is highest when people with unaligned agendas converge on meaning using lower anonymity media. Given the prevalence of partially cooperative communication in organizational settings in particular, our findings bring important extensions to CMC literature.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our primary theoretical contributions lie in our conceptualizations of partially cooperative communication and agenda alignment, which have broad implications for IS theories of computer-mediated communication. First, we introduced the concept of framing from the communication literature. Communication framing provides a vehicle for integrating agenda alignment and communication processes and has broad implications for research because it highlights the interaction between agenda alignment and communication processes on communication performance. Future research might build from this by examining agenda alignment and other communication processes, such as information manipulation (McCornack 1992) and persuasion (Schmitz and Fulk 1991). This interaction may shed light on other forms of interpersonal communication, such as complimenting, convincing, and coercing. These other forms of interpersonal communication are common in organizational settings and may be of interest to researchers studying impression management, negotiating, boundary spanning, and security.

Communication framing also helps us expand the role of communication processes proposed in MST. We find that agendas can range from aligned to unaligned, and these differences may affect communication outcomes. Communicants with different needs for information transmission and processing coupled with differing agendas frame their communications differently (Shah et al. 2002). Accordingly, we find interactions between communication processes and agenda alignment occur as people frame their communications to suit their agendas. This interaction represents a means to extend research about media effects to everyday interactions where agency and opportunism abound (Eisenhardt 1989). For example, we expect the concept of framing to be useful to emerging IS research on the role of technology in highly framed “fake news” and “filter bubbles.”

Our secondary theoretical contributions lie in extending MST to explain a broader spectrum of communication behaviors. We contribute to the literature in this area by uncovering interactions between media capabilities and communication frames. When communicants’ agendas are highly aligned and communicants are attempting to converge on meaning, media that offer greater capabilities for synchronicity lead to the greatest shared understanding. However, our findings uncover why media synchronicity is less useful in explaining interactions when communicants’ agendas may conflict. In these scenarios, we find that a three-way interaction between anonymity, communication processes, and agenda alignment drives communication performance. This finding contributes to the literature by highlighting the role of agenda alignment among other multiple communication contingencies in determining media fit.

This finding also sheds light on the premise that media offer additive and reductive capabilities that are preferred at different levels of group maturity (Carte and Chidambaram 2004). Our experiment involved newly formed groups, which could led to expectations that reductive media capabilities would be useful (Staples and Zhao 2006). However, we observed through interactions with communication processes and agenda alignment that anonymity, which is a reductive capability, was linked to lower communication performance; media synchronicity was linked to higher communication performance. Consequently, our findings suggest agenda alignment, rather than group cohesion, may be a better determinant of when reductive or additive capabilities are beneficial for groups.

Third, we contribute to the literature by showing how agenda alignment alters communication performance. The implications of this research question assumptions of complete cooperation and lead us to consider how agenda alignment changes communication processes, outcomes of communication, and the media capabilities that drive communication performance. For most extant CMC research, communication performance is conceptualized in terms of shared understanding. But by incorporating agenda alignment into the model, communication performance morphs from an emphasis on shared understanding to an emphasis on personal influence. This reconceptualization is important because it has implications for which media capabilities are important for communication performance. When shared understanding is the goal, media capabilities that help coordinate communication are key. If we relax the assumption of agenda alignment, coordination may be less important, and the ability to discern another person’s agenda is critical. In our structured literature review, we highlighted many media capabilities that could be used to uncover agendas, ultimately focusing our attention on anonymity. Our results show that anonymity, among other media capabilities, may be more important for predicting personal influence, while media synchronicity is more important for predicting shared understanding.

Our contribution lies in demonstrating that complete cooperation is an assumption that bounds CMC theories, rather than attempting to “complete” MST, or any other CMC model, with consideration of additional media capabilities. Relaxing this assumption can change CMC theories in profound ways—altering which media capabilities and communication processes affect various outcomes of communication. In our study, H2 and H3 were not supported, indicating that media capabilities have varying salience depending on agenda alignment. These results emphasize the effects of agenda alignment on communication performance, and offer promise for resolving inconsistencies in opposing theoretical perspectives; for example, the opposing effects of anonymity hypothesized by deindividuation theory (Chatterjee et al. 2015) and the social identity model of deindividuation effects (Postmes et al. 1998). Our findings indicate that increased capabilities for anonymity can be beneficial but they depend on agenda alignment and communication processes. The strong two-way interactions between communication processes and agenda alignment indicate that individuals deliberately and strategically frame their messages.

Finally, our study has implications for research derived from MST. First, we encourage future research to consider different ways to model fit. Extant research has modeled fit as an interaction or measured it directly via user perceptions. In the latter approach, perceptual measures of fit represent a manifestation of the interaction between media capabilities and communication framing. Although we believe the interactive approach is stronger and provides more empirically defensible results, the perceptual approach has its benefits. It eliminates interactions in the model, which could make it possible to hypothesize more antecedents, such as more media capabilities, appropriation factors, and communication framing constructs. The perceptual approach can also reduce the theoretical density of the model and the statistical limitations (i.e., power) imposed by interactions. We included a perceptual measure of fit in our survey and we ran our model with this variable in place of the three-way interactions. We provide the details of this secondary analysis in Appendix C. Our results were highly stable, suggesting our results are robust and offering evidence in favor of using perceptual measures of fit.

## Practical Implications

Although our research is primarily focused on enhancing theories of communication performance, our results have implications for practice as well. First, our findings are relevant for organizational leaders and members, particularly those in distributed and virtual teams, who wish to improve communication in cooperative contexts. A useful heuristic that we offer teams and team leaders is, if their communication task involves convergence processes and aligned agendas, such as an IT help desk, they will want to consider media that enhances synchronicity. However, if their communication task involves convergence processes and partially aligned agendas—for example, negotiating a bid or compensation—they may want to consider low anonymity media. This is especially important in group settings where consensus requires compromise and reciprocity. In situations involving more adversarial exchanges—such as whistle blowing or conflict management—low anonymity can help communicants probe for intent, verify authenticity, and build trust.

The practical implications of our research also provide guidance on how to design CMC systems to improve communication. Our results suggest that for cooperative communication, virtual environments media should clearly identify communicants (Connolly et al. 1990; Nunamaker et al. 1991), and ensure representations of communicants reflect the people communicating (Yoo and Alavi 2001). For systems used in situations where communicants’ agendas may differ, the ability to vary information and identities may increase personal influence. This recommendation diverges from the design of many online platforms, particularly social media sites, which typically offer capabilities for anonymity. These platforms may not be designed for cooperative interaction, but if they want to facilitate personal influence, they may consider designs that provide some greater flexibility for identity protection and anonymity, such as verified accounts, as Twitter provides, or seamless transition between identifiable and anonymous modes of interaction, as Reddit provides.

Our work may also have implications for virtual teams and other forms of geographically dispersed work that embody agency tension, such as teleworkers and online “gig workers.” Research shows that, if a person believes their interaction partner is in a distant city, they initially cooperate less, are less willing to be persuaded, and more willing to deceive their interaction partner (Bradner and Mark 2002). Other research supports a link between geographic distance and interpersonal closeness in work teams, and suggests that technology is critical to bridging this divide (O’Leary et al. 2014). Our work provides a point of departure for building bridges that can bring agendas, or at least understanding of agendas, closer together—by calibrating media capabilities for a team or workers’ agenda alignment and communication processes.

Another practical application of our research reflects the use of virtual reality as a communication medium. Virtual reality has been available for decades, but has seen tremendous recent growth in consumer markets, business operations, and medical treatment (Sheikh 2016). Our results suggest the ability to represent others through virtual reality can deter undesirable behaviors. In our study, groups were less likely to engage in reckless punishment or retaliation in virtual reality than when using a traditional monitor display. These results suggest introducing virtual reality into business and personal interactions may improve communication performance.

## Limitations to Theory and Future Research Directions

Just as the implications of this study will stimulate additional research, so too will its limitations. One limitation is the complexity involved in empirically testing the propositions of MST. This study did not contain an exhaustive list of media capabilities, communication processes, or appropriation factors and still evaluated 20 relationships between variables. For parsimony, it may be necessary to trim peripheral, nonsignificant paths from the model in future studies depending on research relevance. For design science researchers, it may be useful to evaluate the physical media capabilities in the model and drop downstream variables, such as socially derived capabilities. In contrast, for behavioral researchers, it may be useful to drop physical media capabilities and focus on socially derived capabilities, as we did in this project.

The complexity of our interactional model precluded us from hypothesizing interrelationships between synchronicity and anonymity. While much of the IS literature focuses on direct effects of media capabilities, some research suggests there may be relationships between them (Tang et al. 2013). Additional research can help IS scholars better understand the distinctions and commonalities between media capabilities. Our structured literature review revealed a nebulous network of media capabilities in need of synthesis and clarification.

Model complexity also limited our exploration of appropriation factors. We found two of four appropriation factors (experience with a medium and social norms) proposed in MST influenced communication performance for completely cooperative communication. Surprisingly, for partially cooperative communication, none of the appropriation factors were linked with communication performance. This is an opportunity for future researchers to explore the appropriation factors that are meaningful for partial cooperation. We also encourage future researchers to explore alternative ways to model appropriation factors in CMC research.

Our study used survey assessments based on extant research to measure perceptions of media capabilities. Studies of MST have used expert assessment to measure media capabilities or do not directly measure them (DeLuca and Valacich 2006; George et al. 2013; George and Robb 2008; Löber et al. 2006;

Muhren et al. 2009; Murthy and Kerr 2003). The original expert ratings of media capabilities are now 20 (Dennis and Valacich 1999) and have changed over time (Dennis et al. 2008). In response, several authors have assessed media capabilities using survey-based measures (Ahuja et al. 2015; Harrison 2018; Sarker et al. 2010; Tang et al. 2013). This suggests a need for further refinement and standardization of scales for measuring media capabilities. Our study is a step in that direction.

Finally, our study was designed as a controlled lab experiment and this choice limits its generalizability, however, our aim was to provide a test of our theory rather than generalize a theory to a specific setting. Lee and Baskerville (2003) describe how experimental research is meant to determine if the theory being tested applies to the lab setting and evaluate if the results conflict with the theory. They explain that once experimental results are observed to support to a theory, then it would be appropriate to see if the theory (not the experimental results) generalize to other settings such as organizations. Accordingly, we believe that future empirical research about the role of agenda alignment in an organizational setting would be a useful extension of this work.

## Conclusions

In this study, we wanted to understand how media capabilities that improve communication performance in completely cooperative exchanges differ from those that improve communication performance in partially cooperative contexts. We identified how media capabilities, communication processes, and agenda alignment affect communication performance. First, we provided evidence of the importance of agenda alignment in communication. Our findings illustrated that interactions between agenda alignment and communication processes play a critical role in determining how messages are framed. We also identified anonymity as a media capability that affects communication performance. Our study challenges the notion that media synchronicity alone is the primary means by which media influences communication performance. Instead, we show agenda alignment and additional media capabilities are necessary to capture how media affect partially cooperative communication performance.

## Appendix A

## Review of Media Capabilities Related to Identifiability

We selected 16 exemplar studies in IS research that were rooted in Shannon and Weaver’s (1949) model and addressed partially cooperative communication contexts. First, we evaluated this group of exemplars seeking conceptual patterns. This research described how a medium’s capabilities allowing the identification of group members affected communication performance outcomes including creativity, negotiation, participation, conflict, and confusion (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987; Nunamaker et al. 1991; Schmitz and Fulk 1991). Accordingly, we chose to narrow our focus to research on media capabilities that offer cues of identity during partially cooperative communication. After narrowing our conceptual focus, we built our review by incorporating papers that cited our exemplars and included media capabilities related to the concept of identifiability. This process resulted in the identification and review of 120 papers and 154 variables, some of which were highly similar or identical to one another, listed in Table A1. As we incorporated these new papers into our review, we synthesized them to extract additional themes that would help clarify the role of media capabilities in partially cooperative communication. We identified several important themes. We also observed that some media provide greater cues about an individual’s agenda (Chong and Druckman 2007), and individuals seeking to take advantage of others may choose media to limit cues of their duplicity (George et al. 2013). This research posits that media capabilities provide contextual information about exchanges that is critical for verifying, probing, and understanding another person’s agenda (Davenport 2002; Thomas and Wareing 2004). Media capabilities related to an ability to conceal or detect agendas (George et al. 2013; Nunamaker et al. 1991; Rigotti 2005) or that affected how people exert personal influence (Gavish and Gerdes 1998; Scheufele 1999) included anonymity, auditability, complexity, confidentiality, editability, empowerment, extensibility, persistence, privacy, social presence, trans missibility, transparency, and verifiability.

After identifying these media capabilities, we focused our attention on improving conceptual clarity and selecting constructs suitable for our model. Many media capabilities in these studies capture a similar conceptual phenomenon, but use different aliases. To make sense of thi collection of media capabilities, we organized the constructs according to Shannon and Weaver’s model and whether authors attributed the construct to the communication sender, receiver, message, medium or environment. We then mapped these attributions, along with prototypica construct definitions, and aliases. We found that similarly described media capabilities affected the availability of cues about communicants identities and agendas, but there was not consistent agreement about the names of the constructs.

By arranging conceptually similar constructs according to Shannon and Weaver’s model we were able to differentiate between the perception that a message or information about its sender/receiver cannot be determined (i.e., socially derived capability) and the features of a medium that mask a message or information about its sender/receiver (i.e., physical capability) (Chatterjee et al. 2015; Gavish and Gerdes 1998). Media capabilities that referred to perceptions of the sender or receiver, or their interpretation of the message, reflected socially-derived media capabilities. Frequently, the socially derived concept was referred to as anonymity. Anonymity is the extent to which a message or information about its sender or receiver is perceived as inaccessible to others (Griffith and Northcraft 1994). This conceptualization of anonymity represents a continuous, subjective, perceptual attribute, rather than an objective trait of the medium (Pinsonneault and Heppel 1997).

## Alternate Operationalization of Fit

Our findings support the proposition that media capabilities, communication processes, and agenda alignment interact and influence communication performance. However, it is not feasible to test an exhaustive list of media capabilities and appropriation factors using an interaction model. The number of variables in the interactions would exceed what is theoretically or mathematically feasible. Consequently, we tested an alternate model operationalizing fit as a manifestation of this interaction. Fit has been a central construct in models of IS adoption contexts (Dennis et al. 2001) and CMC (Fuller and Dennis 2009). Using this approach, a measure of fit represents the interaction between media capabilities, appropriation factors, communication processes, and agenda alignment. The measures are shown in Table C1.
