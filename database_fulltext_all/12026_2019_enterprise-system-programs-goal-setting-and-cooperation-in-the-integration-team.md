# Enterprise system programs: Goal setting and cooperation in the integration team

Jamie Y.T. Chang<sup>a</sup>, James J. Jiang<sup>b</sup>, Gary Klein<sup>c</sup>, Eric T.G. Wang<sup>d,⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, Tunghai University, Taiwan, ROC

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Enterprise systems Cooperation Program management Integration team Goal setting

## A B S T R A C T

Fully implemented enterprise systems (ESs) become valuable tools by enabling a variety of business functions critical to an organization. Adopting a program implementation approach improves the chances of delivering an ES in accordance with budget, resource, and scope considerations. The program approach is more likely to succeed in meeting expectations when united by an overarching goal, yet an integration team representing the multiple business functions in an ES implementation must ensure attainment of the overarching goal while stil retaining local goals for each project. We know little about the theoretical relationship that considers working toward goals across functional areas simultaneously with an overarching goal for the same system. In particular, the integration team must cooperate e<sup>f</sup>ectively under di<sup>f</sup>ering goals to deliver the expected product e<sup>fi</sup>ciently, which is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult to accomplish. We build a research model rooted in goal-setting theory to consider the unique context of an overarching goal for the system as a whole with additional goals set for cross-functiona purposes. We indicate achievement of essential cooperation and realization of expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with essential goal commitment. Survey data from key informants in cross-functional integration teams support the model anò indicate a direction to enable targeted business functions for an ES program implementation.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise systems (ESs) represent an important technology investment for <sup>fi</sup>rms to span functional areas through an integrated platform [54,104]. The functions and competencies of an organization are integrated by “linking core business processes internally and externally to make them more e<sup>f</sup>ective and e<sup>fi</sup>cient” [72, p. 392]. Historically, the IS literature considers the implementation of an ES to be a single project, but the reality is “<sup>fi</sup>rms that invest in ES <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves embarking on not just one, but a series of major business improvement projects” [107, p. 306]. These projects are managed as a program, where a program is a collection of projects and activities structured to achieve an expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t that typically requires organizational change or transformation [62].

In practice, an identi<sup>fi</sup>able team bears the responsibility for moving the separate, interdependent projects toward the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts an organiza tion expects of the program as a whole [31,50]. We call this the “in tegration team” as the independent projects require integration to complete the program similarly to integration teams in R&D or other cross-functional environments [9,18,30,55,115]. Committed agents from the individual projects, functional stakeholders with diverse agendas, and executive representation comprise the integration team to ensure breadth of interest [88.110]. The integration team assumes decision authority over the individual ES projects and the overall ES implementation to enact actions that achieve system speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. The ES integration team, therefore, must collaborate across business units and functions in the pursuit of achieving ES expectations [37,101]. Di<sup>fi</sup>- culties arise in this context involving cross-functional interests of the individual projects and potential con<sup>fl</sup>ict among the multiple goals represented on the ES integration team.

Often, the ES implementation is one of the biggest projects that an organization ever launches and is a strategic business imperative [114]. As a result, issues surrounding the implementation process have been one of the major concerns in research and practice. Each implementation has a number of diverse stakeholders that must be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed as shown in Fig. 1 [24]. Each added project in an ES contributes to the set of goals as each functional area tries to gain advantages from the new system initiative. Even when team members share common goals, crossfunctional harmony is di<sup>fi</sup>cult [84,85]. All of these stakeholder goals reside in the membership of the integration team and in<sup>fl</sup>uence directions, decisions, and eventual performance. We will call the entire collection of goals the goal set, to include the overarching ES goal and all individual project goals. To succeed in an ES implementation program, cooperation is crucial for managing the diverse and collective interests of all stakeholders, resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts, and minimizing resistance [27].

The establishment of an overarching goal to guide an ES toward prescribed bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is often the most critical step. Chang et al. [24] state “when the goal can be made relevant to the entire organization as a superordinate goal, a good deal of cooperation and coordination arises” [24, p. 474]. For an ES implementation, “high-level goals of integrating, optimizing, and providing improved access to information are translated into real functionality supporting real processes that help or hinder possibly thousands of individuals in doing their (possibly new) jobs” [107, p. 317]. Further, IT studies identify the importance of set ting proper goals to encourage achievement of purpose [60,78]. This advice derives from goal-setting theory (GST), which obliges commit ment to a harmonious goal containing no con<sup>fl</sup>ict. However, individual ES project goals in the program may con<sup>fl</sup>ict with each other in addition to the overarching ES goal. When this condition is present in an ES implementation, integration becomes problematic [9,122]. Because the integration team has the authority to resolve goal con<sup>fl</sup>icts among the individual ES projects to improve ES performance [37,101], each project’s goals should be set to achieve e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation. However, we know little about how these theoretical relationships derive from established understandings of GST in the single project context to apply to the ES program context illustrated in Fig. 1.

To enhance current knowledge, we consider the following questions: i) whether goals at the ES overarching level and at the project level both promote behaviors of cooperation; and ii) whether a commitment to the overarching ES goal increases the strength of the re lationship from goals to cooperation. Establishing the full goal set is critical because the <sup>fi</sup>nal ES product must satisfy strategic initiatives typically considered in the overarching goal but include the goals of lower management responsible for project implementation [64].

Contradictory goals at the project level lead individual projects to pursue improper actions. In the case of an ES implementation, this implies that the integration team, to include the individual project managers, must set each project’s goals for e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation. Thus, goal setting in the context of multiprojects ES implementation must consider the goals for each individual ES project to acquire cooperation among the ES integration team members [28]. Thus, we will rely on a model rooted in GST to explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the two components in the goal set and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of commitment to the overarching ES goal in the attainment of essential cooperation among the integration team members.

## 2. Background and hypotheses

An ES implementation represents a major strategic initiative for an organization [114]. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the importance of ESs to organizations, articles address numerous concerns of deploying an ES. Studies conside that six phases exist, each with unique problems: (1) adoption, (2) acquisition, (3) implementation, (4) use and maintenance, (5) evolution, and (6) retirement [36,42]. The ES implementation is one of the biggest and costliest undertakings an organization launches [36]. Thus, issues surrounding the implementation process are a major concern and are the focus of our study. Published studies on the implementation phase consider the following: (a) the implementation approach (e.g., Elbanna [38]), (b) organizational issues (e.g., Soh et al. [112]), (c) success factors (e.g., Dezdar and Ainin [34]), (d) knowledge management (e.g., Remus [100]), and (e) general issues (e.g., Chen et al. [25], Tsai et al. [119]). These studies establish the need to examine ES implementation issues from many perspectives. Chen et al. [25] examine how poor project management can imperil the implementation of an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system. Their results show that the breadth of functions served by an ES implies a much wider and more complicated integration with the processes, systems, and culture of an organization leading to larger management issues.

Implementation success deals with the issues of how to succeed through an ES implementation. It covers aspects such as ES project success and failure de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, problems and outcomes, and critical success factors [41]. Reported studies attempt to explain di<sup>fi</sup>culties of implementation and activities to achieve desirable results that include con<sup>fl</sup>ict and con<sup>fl</sup>ict management, user resistance and psychological contracts, and a need for continual improvement in knowledge management practices [87,100]. Some researchers generated lists of the critical success factors at di<sup>f</sup>erent implementation stages that include top management support, process change management, ERP teamwork and composition, cross-functional integration, training, and communication [29,35,63,74,86]. These studies provide valuable insight on ES implementation success but are almost entirely driven by a single project perspective and fail to address the need to integrate the ES across functional areas, with legacy systems, and the processes and culture of the organization. However, research on how to handle an ES implementation program is rare, with considerations of how to manage, govern, or control even rarer [36,38,107].

## 2.1. Team goal setting

In GST, goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city, behaviors of attainment, and goal commitment are among the critical components to facilitate team performance [82,83]. Characteristic of a high-performing team is a group “who must interact cooperatively and adaptively in pursuit of shared value objectives” [20, p. 222]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>city provides a common understanding of the goal for team members and a<sup>f</sup>ects the variability of team performance because speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals provide clear guidance. Goal commitment is a desire to reach the stated goals and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in having goals to be achievable. Bene<sup>fi</sup>cial actions and behaviors coalesce the conditions to promote eventual success. Fig. 2 illustrates a common model of goal setting in a team.

We situate our theorizing within the context of an ES program, considering the ES integration team as the focus of our study. Such a contextually focused approach to theoretical exploitation is appropriate as it generates context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c insights [59]. In particular, context shapes the meaning and interpretation of theoretical relationships, potentially changing functional form, directionality, and strength of relationships. The context <sup>fi</sup>ts the conditions of team goal setting shown in Table 1 [73,76]. Each team member has local goals and perspectives on how to implement achievement of the overarching ES goal. The completion of any single goal depends on the completion of the entire goal set, setting up cooperation as an essential behavior. Therefore, we consider relationships between cooperation within the ES integration team, the overarching ES goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city, the commitment toward the overarching ES goal, with the addition of consensus on individual project goals of the ES integration team members. We restrict the study to contextual conditions where the ES integration teams (i) have the decision authority to determine how each individual ES project goal should be set, and (ii) vary in the extent to which they commit to the overarching ES goal. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne our constructs in Table 2 and depict our research model in Fig. 3. We follow the primary concepts of GST but allow for the unique context of an ES program implementation in our discussion.

## 2.2. Cooperation in ES integration team

The centerpiece of our model is the promotive behavior of cooperation in the ES integration team. Cooperation is an essential component in the literature on communication and software development teams [32,75]. Organization studies identify di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives for conceptualizing cooperation. One perspective considers more the motivation of cooperation, emphasizing “the act of working together to one end” [89, p. 8]. The second perspective of cooperation in organizational research centers on cooperative actions. Here, cooperation is “a functional system of activities of two or more persons” [13, p. 17]. The behaviors for successful work groups include more coordination, more helping, more communication, more cooperation, and more division of labor [5]. For our model, we consider the actions of cooperation in the ES integration team as the primary concern. Thus, our cooperation captures the e<sup>f</sup>orts of the ES integration team members.

## 2.3. Overarching ES goal specificity

Managers at lower levels are not as likely to be aware of strategic ends as are the top managers [52]. Therefore, we consider the speci<sup>fi</sup> city of the assigned strategic goal is critical for e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation in a team that crosses functions [124]. First, goals serve as a directive function: They direct attention and e<sup>f</sup>ort toward goal-relevant activities and away from goal irrelevant activities. Direction regulates and sustains individual e<sup>f</sup>orts toward goal accomplishment; especially when e<sup>f</sup>orts are interdependent [26]. Second, goals have an energizing function [10]; they serve as a moving force entailing both physical and emotional e<sup>f</sup>orts toward attainment. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals enable collaboration strategies to achieve desired outcomes [64]. An overarching goal motivates cooperation among individual ES project leaders and provides a unifying direction [24]. In the ES implementation context, the executive(s) on the integration team embodies the overarching goal of the ES with the entire team responsible for the attainment of this goal. This overarching ES goal must be understandable to provide the direction and energizing force for the ES integration team members to cooperate. We follow the common line of understandability in the measurement of the construct at the team level [43,71,73,94,113]. The overarching ES goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city construct enables us to capture understanding by the ES integration team members, something not present in the single-project context.

## 2.4. ES integration team consensus of project goals

Each of the multiple projects in an ES implementation will have local goals addressing local interests. The integration team is responsible for ensuring local goals to align with the overarching goal and the objectives of the organization [120]. Local goals include aspects of usability, roles, controls, and culture that may vary from domain to domain or function to function. The ES integration team members should all understand these goals for the individual projects. These multiple ES projects have interdependencies rooted in organizational competencies for the pursuit of the global organizational objectives [51]. Eliminating the local objectives is not an option because projects implement di<sup>f</sup>erent functionalities and cross-geographic locations and apply unique processes with unique local needs. The construct captures the understanding among ES integration team members on the goals of the individual projects, something not considered in the single project context. A focus on understanding allows a more cogent comparison to the overarching goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city and is consistent with the current thought about consensus being a state of understanding [64]. In particular, a shared understanding allows managers to act independently, “but in a way that is consistent with the actions of others and consistent with the spirit of the decision” [3, p. 125].

## 2.5. Overarching ES goal commitment

GST conceptualizes goal commitment as a moderator between the goal and needed e<sup>f</sup>ort (e.g., cooperation) [83]. Essentially, participants in goal achievement must possess commitment as a bene<sup>fi</sup>cial attitude that can leverage the transformation of an established goal into action [61,83]. Thus, the integration team’s commitment to the overarching ES goal will bind the interdependent project teams to the achievement of the overarching ES goal. This does not exclude local commitment to independent goals set for the individual projects, but the integration team holds the responsibility for the overarching ES goal, and we focus on commitment at that level. The determinants of commitment fall into two broad categories: 1) factors that make goal attainment important and 2) factors that make an individual con<sup>fi</sup>dent that the goal can be attained [68,69].

## 2.6. Integration team performance

An ES implementation integrates various modules for di<sup>f</sup>erent business functions. It is important that each module is delivered during the implementation in order to achieve the promised scope [23,109]. In the completion of programs for IS products, schedule and budget con siderations are early indicators of successful performance [96]. The ES implementations target completion by delivering the ESs with expected scope, e<sup>fi</sup>cient resource usage, and schedule adherence, which serve as our <sup>fi</sup>rst dependent variable [25]. The responsible team may be evaluated on these performance concerns [97].

## 2.7. ES-enabled business functions

In addition to the performance characteristics, the integration team oversees the product of delivery. From a product perspective, an ES must target the organizational level by enhancing multiple functional areas [109]. Shang and Seddon [109] speci<sup>fi</sup>cally suggest that ES bene<sup>fi</sup>ts must consider operational, managerial, strategic, IT infra structure, and organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. To that end, the multiple projects in an ES must accomplish the local goals in order to accomplish the organizational objectives [107]. Otherwise, the ES cannot address global objectives of business process integration and data sharing. In this study, we examine the business functions enabled by the ES product as a second dependent variable [14].

## 2.8. Complexity as a control

The complexity of the implementation is a potential cause of delays, cost overruns, and failure to deliver the expected scope of features [62,70]. Controls are placed on the dependent variables to consider issues of complexity that account for alternate considerations of variation in the measures of performance and enablement. Complexity is considered as consisting of many varied parts and operationalized in terms of the number of components, longevity, or number of partici pants [7]. We use the number of independent projects in the implementation, the number of departments represented on the integra tion team, and duration as proxies for complexity, similar to other studies in IS [125].

## 2.9. Hypotheses

To achieve functional enablement of an ES, each team member must cooperate with others through formal and informal processes. The efforts of one individual have implications for the work and progress of others. Given that many team members represent individual projects or modules and oversee local goal achievement, what might improve the quality of one module could impede the functionality of another module in an ES implementation. Because of such an interdependent nature, all members must cooperate to integrate the cross-functional interests to achieve the full goal set. In the context of IT product management, e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation enhances program achievement [96]. Research in related disciplines <sup>fi</sup>nds that when individual interests are harmoniously managed as a cooperative and collective e<sup>f</sup>ort, de sired results are achieved [21,91]. In contrast, a lack of cooperation among separate interests hinders processes and joint tasks [102]. Thus, this study proposes the following:

H1a. Cooperation within the ES integration team positively impacts integration team performance.

H1b. Cooperation within the ES integration team positively impacts ESenabled business functions

With everything else being equal, people achieve objectives when they understand expectations based on goal-driven directives [81]. On the integration team, the goal set directs the entire implementation through an overarching goal and each local project with the remaining goal set. Allowing individual projects or modules to retain high levels of goal autonomy may engender con<sup>fl</sup>icting goals that create great di<sup>fi</sup>- culty in arriving at a truly integrated solution [1,9]. Thus, any individual goals must subordinate to the overarching goal [24]. A speci<sup>fi</sup>c overarching goal provides an overall picture to understand the direction and the targeted objectives for the ES implementation. More formally, based on GST, the goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city provides a shared cognitive basis or understanding to facilitate promotive behaviors among the team members [73]. Understanding is critical for constructively resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts or/and deviations [105], and explains how teams adapt their processes to varying local conditions [39,92,123]. Thus, we expect

H2a. Overarching ES goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city will positively a<sup>f</sup>ect the level of cooperation in ES integration team.

In the context of an ES implementation, success requires that each individual project be successful [24]. A variety of perspectives across multiple projects might lead to individual project teams failing to understand the goals of other projects [49,67]. Thus, reaching a consensus of understanding is critical to attaining cooperation when the projects are interdependent [96]. Goal interdependence leads group members to engage in promotive behaviors, such as collaboration, communication, and cooperation [118]. Project goals within the ES goal set should di rect actions of integration in the accomplishment of the collective goals through coordinated activities that manage the interdependencies of the projects [16]. As explained by Latham [77], the pursuit of in dividual goals will not be detrimental to an overarching goal as long as the team realizes that both are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and hence increase performance. As such, consensus on the individual ES project goals among the members of the integration team can direct each project team’s behavior toward the accomplishment of local goals as well as the overarching ES goals. Empirical studies demonstrated this e<sup>f</sup>ect. For example, Ko et al. [71] reveal that goal consensus among critical stakeholders encourages cooperation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals of each individual project guide actions to complete individual projects in a program environment successfully [96]. However, without a consensus of individual project goals among the ES integration team, the individual projects might con<sup>fl</sup>ict with what they attempt to accomplish. Functional areas can bene<sup>fi</sup>t at the expense of others in the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of an ES; without an understanding of how the responsibilities are allocated to the individual projects, the focus will only be on the individual projects instead of cooperation for the ES implementation in its entirety [24]. This study, therefore, proposes

H2b. ES integration team consensus of project goals will positively a<sup>f</sup>ect the level of cooperation in ES integration team.

Having goals alone might be insu<sup>fi</sup>cient for successful attainment [3,81]. People must be motivated to take the correct action and follow the set direction [10]. Such motivational factors play an important role in developing cooperation [61]. A high level of cooperation arises best when the individual project leaders have no doubt, as to whether the pursuit of the overarching ES goal undermines localized interests. In short, goal commitment to the overarching ES goal is likely to facilitate cooperative behaviors among team members because of the collective nature of the overarching ES goal [6]. Further, any action taken without commitment may be countered to the achievement of the overarching goal and result in negative actions [64]. In the ES implementation context, ES goal commitment has all key members of the integration team dedicated <sup>fi</sup>rst to achieving the overarching ES implementation goal. Therefore, we expect

H3a. Overarching ES goal commitment will moderate the relationship between the overarching ES goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city and cooperation in ES integration team, i.e., the magnitude of the impact will positively associate with the levels of overarching ES goal commitment.

Commitment binds individuals to a course of action relevant to a target [90]. As successful task completion requires know-how and cooperation, it is expected that individuals with a commitment to goal attainment will try to faithfully apply knowledge and work together to achieve success [33]. However, in the context of a multiproject ES implementation, the attainment of ES goal set requires each project also to be successful [107]. To ensure local goal success requires cooperation among all team members to keep all goals on track, avoid deviations from the consensus and minimize the potential for con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Commitment to the overarching goal should assist in keeping the local goals oriented to the consensus and enhance essential cooperation. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3b. Overarching ES goal commitment will moderate the relationship from the ES integration team consensus of project goals to cooperation in ES integration team, i.e., the magnitude of the impact will positively associate with the levels of overarching ES goal commitment.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Measurement

Existing measurements serve as the basis for our constructs, but modi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the ES program implementation context and investigated rigorously for validity. Reliability and validity are critical to the inter pretation of empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Neuman [95] provides explicit proce dures to improve construct reliability and validity: (1) clearly con ceptualize the construct, (2) ensure face validity and content validity, (3) improve convergent and discriminant validity, and (4) use a pilot test. To conduct the empirical study, all measurement items were translated from English into Chinese using the forward-back translation approach. We invited two bilingual researchers to perform the translation works independently and then evaluated their works together to ensure that both the English and Chinese versions of measurement items were comparable with high accuracy. To further ensure that the respondents can fully understand the measurement items, we invited two MIS professors familiar with enterprise system research to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the questionnaire and assure a meaningful match between the Chinese and English versions. Also, two senior ES managers, who had industrial experience, validated the Chinese versions. This procedure resulted in modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the questionnaires to clarify two items.

The draft questionnaire was pilot tested on a sample of students from executive MBA classes at a major Taiwanese university. The knowledge of business processes, structures, and procedures is high for this experienced group. They were asked to evaluate the measurement items in terms of applicability, clarity, and whether the described behaviors appeared on the measurement items were essential or not. The content validity ratio (CVR) and the content validity index (CVI) were computed for all measurement items. The CVR is determined by experts’ ratings of essential items and their applicability. The CVI is determined by experts’ ratings of item clarity. The results indicate that the mean CVR and the CVI of all measurement items are higher than the cut-o<sup>f</sup> value of 0.8, which support content validity [98]. A principal components analysis with varimax rotation of the items showed high loadings of items on the expected factors and no serious issues of crossloadings. The strength of the resulting reliability estimates suggested a high internal consistency among the measurement items. The <sup>fi</sup>nal items are in Table 3.

## 3.2. Sample and data collection

The target sample for this study includes top Taiwanese companies who have implemented enterprise systems. These organizations contract with a variety of ES vendors and represent a variety of organizations in terms of size and industry, providing a broad base to gain a representative sample. The sample was randomly drawn from “The 2010 Top 1000 Companies” issued by the China Credit Information Service, Ltd. We <sup>fi</sup>rst contacted the Chief Information O<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) in each company to introduce the purpose of this study and explain how the results of this study can help them improve ES implementation. For those willing to participate, researchers made an appointment for an advance visit with a contact person suggested by the CIO to further verify compliance with our context and identify appropriate informants. To secure a representative sample, we required the following: (1) the company implemented at least two projects within a recent ES implementation, (2) the ES had rolled-out, (3) an identi<sup>fi</sup>able team served the role of the integration team, and (4) two key informants within the company could respond (one functional manager who served as a project manager and one IT manager on the integration team). From each team in our sample, we required the completion of a survey instrument by the IT manager and one randomly chosen functional manager who also served as a project manager.

Questionnaires were administered to the groups of the functional project managers on the integration team and IT manager separately. In the introduction page of each questionnaire, we indicated that the respondents should focus on the team integrating the projects of the ES implementation, that all responses would be con<sup>fi</sup>dential, and that only the research team had access to the identi<sup>fi</sup>er matching the questionnaires. Correlations increase between constructs because of systematic errors if survey data for those constructs come from the same source. One way to reduce the e<sup>f</sup>ect of correlation due to systematic errors is to use di<sup>f</sup>erent informants for di<sup>f</sup>erent constructs [17]. ES integration teams are composed of an implementation manager, the individual project managers, and other key managers or executives in the organization. In consideration of the knowledge and skill of the informants when answering our questionnaire, two informants within an ES implementation assessed items of the di<sup>f</sup>erent constructs. The informants included one functional manager serving as the project manager and one IT manager within a <sup>fi</sup>rm serving as the technical manager on the integration team. Functional managers are often in charge of individual projects and suitable to evaluate items assessing project goals setting, program goal setting, cooperation, ES-enabled business functions, and integration team performance. IT managers assessed ES goal commitment, as they supervised the IT-related issues with each project and often serve as the ES technical manager. Furthermore, respondents were directed to consider the most recently completed ES implementation, with a focus on the early stages for the goals related constructs to avoid consideration of understanding acquired during implementation. Through the contact in each <sup>fi</sup>rm, paired responses totaled 183 from 183 <sup>fi</sup>rms. The characteristics of the respondents are in Table 4.

## 4. Data analysis and results

We employed partial least square (PLS) path modeling to perform estimates for the study. PLS uses bootstrapping to estimate empirically standard errors for its parameter estimates, which can avoid restrictive distributional assumptions [47]. Following the two-stage analytical procedure recommended by Anderson and Gerbing [4], a measurement model <sup>fi</sup>rst assesses the convergent validity and discriminant validity. Subsequently, structural relationships are examined, also with PLS.

The measurement model was assessed regarding reliability and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was demonstrated with composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE). AVE represents the ratio of the sum of the variance captured by the construct and measurement variance and is a measure of convergent validity [8]. Any AVE above 0.5 indicates that the explained variance is greater than the variance caused by a measurement error, which holds for the con structs of this study. Considering Cronbach’s alpha con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant item-total correlations, and AVEs that indicate the construct captures the majority of the variance, we are con<sup>fi</sup>dent of convergent validity. CR and Cronbach’s alpha for each construct exceeded the recommended value of 0.7, and the AVE for each construct exceeded the recommended value of 0.5 level, establishing convergent validity as seen in Table 5. For discriminant validity, the square root of AVE for each construct should be greater than its correlation with all other constructs because this indicates that the measurement items share more overall variance with their respective construct than with other constructs [46]. ## 5. Discussion

Management of an ES implementation has become an increasingly more important practice as information technology penetrates all functions of the organization and impacts ability to work in a network of organizations, suppliers, and customers. Organizations wishing to capitalize on an ES require a better understanding of how to develop essential cooperation. The literature establishes good practice for generating cooperation in a project environment. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmed practices consider the aspects of both organizational and process variables [117]. On the system development side, cooperation is enhanced through <sup>fl</sup>exible governance, culture and norms, communication, operative trust, team composition, and goals established for the project [32,80]. These crucial components identify with the single project context. In the context of an ES implementation program, however, multiple projects must be considered. We argue that, in the ES program context, the integration team must work with the goals set by top management as well as consider those of the functional areas impacted by the ES implementation. This study, therefore, explored the following two questions: i) whether goals at the ES overarching level and at the project level both promote behaviors of cooperation, and ii) whether the commitment to the overarching ES goal increases the strength of the relationship from goals to cooperation. In answering these questions, we extend our understanding of team GST to the context of ES programs and include distinct consideration of local and global goals in the same team.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we found that consideration of both ES integration team consensus of project goal and the overarching ES goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city had a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on cooperation in the ES integration teams (i.e., support H2a and H2b). The cooperation in an ES integration team, in turn, has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the integration team performance and ES-enabled business functions (i.e., support of H1a and H1b). However, the result that cooperation is a partial mediator indicates that further practices must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in future studies to make greater improvements in performance. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of ES integration team consensus of project goals on cooperation in ES integration team is positively moderated by overarching ES overarching goal commitment (i.e., support of H3b), while ES overarching goal commitment does not moderate the relationship between overarching goal speci<sup>fi</sup>city and cooperation (i.e., failed support of H3b).

The lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for any of the complexity variables might be surprising as complexity is well established as a complication to success at the project level [12,116]. However, the purpose of managing a major endeavor as a program instead of as a single project is to overcome many of the di<sup>fi</sup>culties including issues of complexity [45,62]. Segmentation of a major endeavor into smaller partitions, such as dividing a program into multiple projects, is an e<sup>f</sup>ective means to overcome issues of complexity [103]. Programs allow greater <sup>fl</sup>exibility in driving toward goals with greater planning e<sup>f</sup>orts that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally address issues of complexity and interdependencies of goals [24]. Program management, as structured through the integration team, can alter projects, add projects, and delete projects to meet changes to goals that would otherwise add unaddressed complexity [62]. Team beha viors not included in this study but considered in the choice of the in tegration team might also play a role, including adaptability, learning, and autonomy [12,15,45]. These features should be considered in future studies.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide new insight into better understanding the role of goal setting in the ES program context [126]. We <sup>fi</sup>nd the well-de-<sup>fi</sup>ned model to hold at the highest level. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c overarching goals for the ES implementation as a whole do promote positive behaviors and improve success. In the IS and ES project management literature, studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between the speci<sup>fi</sup>c objectives of an IS project and project outcomes in the context of single project implementation [24,53,78]. In the context of programs, however, the unique nature of multiple interests adds to the complexity of attaining desired cooperation. Without <sup>fi</sup>rst achieving an understood consensus on the many functional goals within each project team, the individual members of the integration team will pursue goals not targeted to the desired outcome. Local goals might drive each project in a con<sup>fl</sup>icting direction. Successful completion of each project proves essential. Future studies on ES program implementations should consider the entire goal set. Progress toward goals divergent from the overarching goal would be counterproductive, but ignorance of the in dividual project goals can also be just as problematic.

Furthermore, this study adds evidence on cooperation behaviors but in the unique setting of the integration team, thus generalizing the application of cooperation in explaining certain IS phenomena. An ES program represents a social system whose members must cooperate to complete the collective goals [96]. The need for cooperation stems from the complex interdependences among the individual projects working together toward the entire ES goal set. IS project management studies have shown the importance of intrateam cooperation on IS project completion [48,127]. For example, the cooperative norms presented in development lead to improved processes and actions necessary for success [80]. This <sup>fi</sup>nding further supports empirical studies that argue the importance of helping implementation teams e<sup>f</sup>ectively manage collective knowledge, expertise, and skills [121] and associate their actions to the demands of the projects and the expectations of other members [79].

The results of this study have important implications for practi tioners. For organizations implementing an ES, the existing literature promotes the importance of alignment between overarching ES objectives to gain competitive advantages. As a result, the main focus is on a primary goal for the integration team. The results of this study, however, indicate that the commitment of the integration team members also plays an important role and that the members of the in tegration team must have set project goals. From a practical standpoint, it would seem e<sup>fi</sup>cient to set goals and means for the multiple projects in conjunction with other members of the integration team. To have the desired levels of commitment from integration team members, organizations must consider the impacts of the ES goal for all the major stakeholders involved in addition to the superordinate goal suggested by Chang et al. [24]. In organizing and directing local goals, however, project managers and key stakeholders on the integration team should all understand each project’s goals and the goal interdependence structure to facilitate the necessary cooperation in the integration team. These key players should be brought into partnering relationships, and rewards must be established that promote the full goal set.

## 6. Conclusion

This study addresses a critical issue for the management of ES program implementations. The management of goals for building cooperation to reach scope requirements e<sup>fi</sup>ciently is essential for organizations. Consequently, organizations arrange ES implementations in program environments governed by integration teams. The growing use of such structures as a management device requires that we examine factors that contribute to the management process and eventual success. To this end, based on goal-setting, we con<sup>fi</sup>rm setting an overarching goal for the ES implementation along with reaching an un derstanding of individual project goals is essential in fostering desired cooperation within the integration team. Commitment to the overarching ES goal increases the cooperation on the integration team generated through the many goals for the functional projects that comprise an ES implementation. On the whole, the ES program context requires that goals be considered at both local and global levels.
