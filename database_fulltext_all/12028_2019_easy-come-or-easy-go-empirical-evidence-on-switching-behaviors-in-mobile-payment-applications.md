## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Mobile payment application Push-pull-mooring framework Switching behavior Privacy concerns Inertia Technological self-eficacy

## A B S T R A C T

Switching behaviors of mobile payment application received scarce attention. This study investigates the key factors influencing the switching behaviors of mobile payment application through the perspective of the push–pull–mooring framework. Privacy concerns, alternative rewards, and inertia are identified as push, pull, and mooring factors, respectively. The model was tested with 3785 valid responses among Alipay users. Inertia was found to attenuate the relationship between alternative rewards and switching behavior. This study sheds new light on the switching behavior of mobile payment application users. Our findings inform service provider to retain existing users as well as attract potential users

## 1. Introduction

The prevalent usage of mobile devices (e.g., smartphones and tablets) among the majority of online population has galvanized the dramatic growth of mobile commerce in recent years. In particular, mobile payment is one of the most critical drivers to foster the successful development of mobile commerce [99]. The worldwide mobile payment market is enormous, and the volume of mobile payment applications users is soaring. Its total revenue has reached \$450 billion in 2015 and is expected to surpass \$1 trillion in 2019 [83]. Attracted by its great potential, numerous firms have developed their mobile payment applications to reap the prominent profits and try to keep users to be loyal to them.<sup>1</sup> For example, Nokia invested \$70 million in Obopay to enter the mobile payment market and Google supplied \$100 million to develop its own mobile payment application [57]. Among these players, Alipay is now the largest mobile payment platform in the world, as shown in Fig. 1 [10,84].

However, the intensive competition among the mobile payment platforms is a typical example of winner-takes-all [35], a phenomenon in the market of strong network efects where merchants and potential users tend to adopt the mobile payment platform with a larger number of existing users, making the smaller platform smaller and the larger one larger [52,65,103]. Thus, vendors of mobile payment applications need to retain existing users and facilitate their continuance usage to achieve financial profitability [21], rather than making the users switch to other alternative applications.

The life cycle of a specific technology normally includes three phases, namely adoption, usage, and switching or termination [32]. As extant stu dies on mobile payment applications mainly focus on users’ initial adoption [38.51.56.64.99] and continuous usage behavior $[ 4 , 1 5 , 1 8 , 1 0 2 ]$ , little attention has been paid to the last phase of mobile payment application switching behavior. In essence, we conjecture that external stimuli and the internal beliefs that users hold to guide their behavior in the first two phases (i.e., adoption and usage) may not apply for the third phase, or the degree of influence may difer [32,55]. Although users’ switching behaviors have been investigated in various contexts such as social networking [55], blog service [36], mobile shopping [44], online auctions [52], e-commerce [16,48], and e-health care services [45], the unique characteristics of mobile payment applications may challenge the findings derived from other research settings.

Hence, this paper attempts to examine the user switching behavior in the mobile payment application usage through the theoretical lens of the push–pull–mooring (PPM) framework. The PPM model serves as a theoretical underpinning to unveil the antecedents of users’ switching behavior, consisting of push factors that drive users away from incumbent services, pull factors that attract users to alternatives, and mooring factors that either hinder or facilitate switching behavior [5]. The PPM framework has been adapted to explore IS switching behaviors with regard to a plethora of technological artifacts, such as instant messaging service [86], blog service [36], and mobile shopping [16,44].

However, extant literature suggests that there is a paucity of in vestigating switching behaviors utilizing PPM framework. First, most of the previous studies generally regarded dissatisfaction, alternative attractiveness, and switching cost as the push, pull, and mooring factors, respectively, leaving specific contextual variables that may influence users switching behavior yet to be fully identified. For example, mobile payment vendors require users to disclose highly confidential authentic personal information to complete transactions. Privacy protection strategies<sup>2</sup>, which are commonly adopted in other contexts, cannot be applied to mobile payment applications. Privacy concern may hamper users’ switching behaviors because users are unwilling to provide their personal information to new vendors. Privacy concern was, therefore, considered as a push factor of switching. Moreover, switching behaviors may be prevalent among users who are attracted by the monetary rewards (e.g., cash bonuses, coupons, discounts, and promotion deals) of the alternative platform, which forms the pull factor. Compared to other mobile services, there are more formalities involved in the set-up process of a mobile payment application. Users may stay with incumbent mobile pavment applications because of inertia. which refers to users’ conscious continuance of the status quo rather than switching to a potentially better alternative [42]. Inertia, thus, can be treated as the mooring factor.

Second, previous studies have primarily focused on personal-based mooring factors of switching, such as trust [44], past experience [36], and habit [100]. The institutional and relational factors that may keep users staying with incumbent service have been largely overlooked. This study provides a comprehensive picture of mooring factors based on a personal–institutional–relational framework. Specifically, we proposed three mooring factors, i.e., technical expertise of users, perceived economic of incumbent service, and past investments, which represent the personal, institutional, and relational forces, respectively.

Third, little is known about the specific mechanisms through which mooring factors influence switching behavior. A variety of mooring variables have been identified, and they were hypothesized to influence switching behaviors directly [17,44]. Unexpected results emerged from the model that assumes a direct relationship between mooring factors and switching behavior. For example, comparative attractiveness was hypothesized as a moor factor to negatively predict user’s switching intention [47]. This hypothesis, however, failed to be supported. Drawing on status quo bias theory, this study introduced inertia as a key mediating variable that bridges various mooring variables and switching behavior.

The results of the present study inform theory and practice in several aspects. We contribute to IS switching literature by incorporating contextual variables, such as inertia, privacy concerns, and the monetary rewards of alternatives, into the PPM framework. By proposing inertia as a key mediating variable, this study clarified the exact mechanism through which mooring factors influence users’ switching de cisions. Furthermore, our empirical findings will inform and enable both incumbent service providers and newcomers to retain existing users as well as to attract potential users. We appeal to mobile payment vendors to relieve users’ privacy concerns, as we found that the contribution of privacy concerns to shaping switching behavior outweighs that of inertia and the monetary rewards of alternatives. Our result also guide mobile payment vendors to cultivate the inertia of their users.

## 2. Theoretical backgrounds

## 2.1. Mobile payment application and its key characteristics

Mobile payment<sup>3</sup> refers to a process in which at least one phase of the transaction is conducted by a mobile device capable of securely completing a financial transaction over a mobile network, or by various wireless technologies [24,56,99]. Mobile payment services are achieved through mobile payment applications. Some studies view mobile payment applications and mobile banking systems as being equivalent [28], or believe that some of their characteristics may overlap [81]. There are quite distinctive systems in terms of the number of players involved. Mobile banking is a consumer–bank dual relation, while mobile payment is a three-party process between a customer, merchant, and bank [54]. The current study focuses exclusively on third-party mobile payments.

Compared with other mobile services, such as email, social networking, instant messaging, and so forth, mobile payment applications possess unique characteristics.

First, mobile payment applications require users to disclose highly personal information, such as their bank account, identity number, phone number, and home address, to process and complete their transactions. Mobile Internet users are increasingly sufering from privacy invasions because of vulnerable mobile networks [102]. Users commonly adopt several self-protective strategies to maintain the ser vice and protect their privacy. For example, users may provide false personal information while registering on social networking applications [1,93]. These privacy protection strategies cannot be applied to mobile payment applications because refusing to disclose or disclosing untruthful information might lead to the termination of this service.

Second, the competition among various mobile payment platforms presents a typical example of winner-take-all, a phenomenon in the market of strong network efects. When a network efect is present, the value of a service increases as the number of users increases. Studies have found that both merchants and users are more likely to accept payment platforms that have a large number of users [35].

Third, compared to other mobile services, there are more formalities involved in the set-up process of a mobile payment application. Energy, time, and eforts are warranted for the mobile payment application to function. Mobile payment application users are required to link their mobile payment accounts with bank accounts and establish login credentials. Because of the formalities of starting a new mobile payment application, users may continue using the incumbent service, even if it is not the best one.

## 2.2. The life cycle of mobile payment applications

The whole life cycle of mobile payment applications can be classified into three phases: adoption, usage, and termination (see Fig. 2). In the first phase, users develop the intention to adopt a mobile payment application, whereas users develop the intention to use a mobile payment app continuously in the second phase. The life cycle concludes with the termination phase, in which users stop using the incumbent mobile payment application and potentially switch to an alternative.

Diferent theoretical approaches have been employed to explain users’ behaviors in each phase. The stream of technology acceptance focuses on the intention to adopt a mobile payment application [25,40]. Drawing on the difusion of innovations theory, another stream of studies explored five innovation characteristics that afect mobile payment adoption [56,96]. In the usage phase, the expectation-con formation model is widely used to explain the continuous usage intention [7]. Several antecedents of the continuous usage intention, such as service quality, cost rationality, satisfaction, perceived value, and habit, have been identified [8,46,102].

Despite the abundance of extant studies focused on the first two phrases, the switching phase of mobile payment application has been largely overlooked [89]. The information system (IS) literature indicates that continuance and discontinuous usages are not opposite extremes on the same continuum [89]. In essence, studies have shown that continuous and discontinuous usage decisions are driven by dif ferent factors [55,66]. For example, users may adopt a mobile payment service because it ofers convenience and speed. Users may also use a mobile payment application just for the new user rewards. Users may discard mobile payment applications because of their greater uncertainty and risk compared with ofline and online payments. Drawing on the PPM framework, we developed a model to investigate the formation of users’ switching behavior within mobile payment applications. The following section provides an overview of switching behaviors and the PPM framework.

## 2.3. The push–pull–mooring framework and switching behaviors

The PPM framework is the dominant paradigm in migration literature [5]. Specifically, the PPM framework suggests that migrants’ decision to move from one geographic area to another is afected by push, pull, and mooring factors. This framework is selected to understand mobile payment application switching behaviors due to three reasons. First, the PPM framework explains why people move from one place to another for a certain period of time [61]. Mobile payment application switching can be conceptualized as the behaviors that lead users to migrate from incumbent services to alternatives. The analogy between human migration and mobile payment users’ switching behavior is straightforward and of theoretical relevance. Second, users’ decisions to switch mobile payment vendors are made based on a period of usage of the incumbent services, which is similar to human migration [9]. Third, although switching services may not involve physical movement, PPM framework has been validated in various IT contexts, such as instant messaging service [86], blog service [36], and mobile shopping [44] etc. As a typical example of IT services, we argue that PPM framework can be generalized to mobile payment applications.

Under the framework of PPM, the factors inducing users’ switching behaviors can be classified into push, pull, and mooring factors. Push factors, often referred to as stressors, are defined as the forces that drive people away from current products or services, while pull factors or attractors reflect the positive factors of the alternatives. Since switching is a complex decision, mooring factors represent the supplementary constraints that facilitate or hamper behaviors.

Among these prior studies on IS users’ switching behavior, most focused on dissatisfaction [17], weak connection with peers [36], inconvenience [44], and fatigue with incumbent services [86] as the push factors. Alternative attractiveness, subjective norms, and the availability of alternatives were identified as pull factors [11,36,86]. Tech nological self-eficacy, perceived switching cost, trust, and afective commitment were hypothesized as the mooring factors that intervene in the efects of both push and pull factors [16,44]. Considering the unique characteristics of mobile payment services (see Section 2.1), we propose the below unique critical factors that facilitate or hamper users switching behaviors.

## 2.3.1. Push factor: privacy concerns

Privacy risks can be a barrier for the rapid development of mobile payment application [27,38]. As discussed above, mobile payment vendors require users to truthfully disclose personal information to verify their transactions. In a study by Dewan and Chen [27], about half of respondents felt that mobile payment platforms collect users’ personal information far beyond the basic requirements to go through the transactions and using mobile payment applications put their personal information at risk. Mobile payment users become disadvantaged once they hand over their personal information. The increasingly sophisticated data analytics made the usage of user information often invisible. Mobile payment application users rarely have clear knowledge of how their personal information will be used. Mobile payment application users may switch to alternative platforms if they believe that the in cumbent vendor is unable or unwilling to properly protect their personal information. Previous studies on switching behaviors have used dissatisfaction and fatigue with incumbent service [55,86] to represent the push factors. Yet, little attention has been paid to users’ privacy concerns as a stressor. Considering the particular context of mobile payment applications, we, therefore, identified privacy concerns as a critical push factor that drives users away from the incumbent service and toward an alternative.

## 2.3.2. Pull factor: the monetary rewards of alternatives

The network efect is evident in the mobile payment applications. Network efects become significant after a certain subscription percentage has been achieved, thus establishing a critical mass. At the critical mass point, the value obtained from the service is greater than or equal to the price paid for the service [39]. The core function of various mobile payment applications is homogeneous. Compared with the users of other mobile applications (i.e. social networking, online game, and instant messaging), mobile payment users are more sensitive to monetary rewards. To quickly establish the massive user network, granting newly adopted users with monetary rewards has been found to be one of the most widely used mechanisms to attract potential users in the mobile payment market. For example, Alipay recently released an incentive policy through which each Alipay users may obtain a random cash bonus (up to \$15 each day from December 16, 2017 to December 31, 2017 [94]. In the same vein, Apple Pay users receive 10% cash back on each transaction in a given period [14]. Users may switch to alternatives if they are lured by monetary rewards [78]. We therefore identified the monetary rewards of alternatives as an im portant pull factor.

## 2.3.3. Mooring factor: inertia

Mooring factors refer to the supplementary factors that hold potential migrants to their original places [61]. Under the impacts of mooring factors, users are unwilling to switch, even if there are significant push and pull factors [5]. A possible reason is that people tend to perform behaviors automatically because of behavioral inertia [50], which refers to users’ conscious continuance of the status quo rather than switching to a potentially better alternative [42]. Inertia may bias the switching decision-making process in two ways. Switching behavior is facilitated if the benefits of alternatives outweigh that of the in cumbent service. Once inertia sets in, individuals tend to rely on their past behaviors, not current deliberations, to guide present and future choices [73]. Thus, inertial individuals avoid making accurate assessments about the merits of the new systems and rationalize continuing under the status quo. Moreover, inertial individuals are more likely to form the perception that the losses incurred by changing from their current situation seem larger than the gains [42]. As argued above, there are a lot of formalities involved in the set-up process for mobile payment applications, which may be viewed as potential suck costs if users chose to switch to another platform. We thus identified inertia as a dominating mooring factor to better understand the underlying me chanism of mooring effects.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

Based on the literature on mobile payment applications and the PPM framework, this study attempts to explore the factors that influ ence users’ switching behaviors, particularly the pull, push, and mooring factors that should be considered within the context of mobile payment applications. The PPM model hypothesizes that push, pull, and mooring factors influence switching behavior directly. Following Lai,

Debbarma and Ulhas [44] and Hsieh, Hsieh, Chiu and Feng [36], mooring factors were further hypothesized to moderate the main efects of push and pull factors on switching behavior. Therefore, we first investigated how privacy concerns and the monetary rewards of alternatives influence users’ switching behavior. Second, we examined the direct efect of inertia on switching behavior. Third, we explored how inertia moderates the relationships between 1) privacy concerns and switching behavior and 2) the monetary rewards of alternatives and switching behavior. At last, the antecedents of inertia were investigated. The overall model is illustrated in Fig. 3.

## 3.1. Privacy concerns and switching behavior

Privacy is defined as the right of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others [95]. Privacy concern refers to the subiective views of fairness within the context of privacy [53]. User’s activities on mobile payment platforms present rich business opportunities. Mobile payment vendors personalize their services by analyzing these usergenerated contents. On the other hand, the potential for consumer data to be abused is alarming. For example, Alipay (China’s largest mobile payment vendor) users protested that they unrecognizably accepted a service agreement while using Alipay’s annual statement function [68]. The service agreement allows Alipay to provide user’s financial activities to a third party and to keep user’s account information even if the service has been terminated.

As the use of mobile payment application increases, users’ concerns about privacy grow, as evidenced by the multiple measures taken by users to secure their personal information [83], while vendors of mobile payment applications require users to disclose highly sensitive information to process online transactions. Users may switch to alternative platforms if they believe that the incumbent service vendor is unable or unwilling to properly protect their personal information.

We thus propose the following hypothesis:

H1. Privacy concerns are positively associated with the switching behavior of mobile payment users.

## 3.2. Monetary rewards of alternatives and switching behavior

The perceived monetary rewards of alternatives refer to tangible benefits, such as cash bonuses, coupons, discounts, and promotion deals provided by alternative mobile payment platforms. Pertinent studies have suggested that any social behavior may result in both costs and benefits [23]. Mobile payment application users may compare the monetary rewards of the alternatives with the sunk cost of switching. The sunk costs of switching include the benefits of the incumbent service and the time and efort that have been devoted to the incumbent service, as well as afective commitment [74]. Users’ switching intention is formed as the monetary rewards of the alternatives outweigh the sunk cost of switching. Therefore, we posit that:

H2. The monetary rewards of alternatives are positively associated with the switching behavior of mobile payment users.

## 3.3. Inertia and switching behavior

## 3.3.1. The direct efect of inertia

Status quo bias argues that individuals prefer to maintain the status quo instead of switching to a new or potentially better alternative [75]. The concept of inertia, which is termed as a manifestation of status quo bias, is defined as “user attachment to, and persistence in, existing behavioral patterns, even if there are better alternatives or incentives to change” [73]. Polites and Karahanna [73] argued that the concept of inertia includes three dimensions, namely behavioral-, cognitive-, and afective-based inertia. One may keep using a service simply because of behavioral-based inertia. Cognitive-based inertia means that individuals may continue using the incumbent service even if they are aware that it is not the best one. Afective-based inertia forms as individuals enjoy using the incumbent service. As discussed above, inertia may result in the perception of resistance to change. Moreover, previous studies have found that inertia is negatively related to the perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use of an alternative service [3,49]. Inertia causes users to perceive an alternative as less attractive. We thus argue that inertial users prefer to stay with incumbent services, even if they have realized the advantages of alternatives. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H3a. Inertia is negatively associated with the switching behavior of mobile payment users.

## 3.3.2. The moderating role of inertia

Although privacy concerns should increase users’ switching behavior, we believe that this relationship is contingent on inertia, which may be the result of a psychological commitment to existing service providers [42]. This commitment may be an attempt to avoid the regret that might result from making a worse decision [77]. Inertial users perhaps have no hands-on experience trying a new service out, and their knowledge of alternative services is limited. Therefore, users with a high level of inertia are uncertain about the benefits of alternative systems because of their limited knowledge of alternative services [73]. Privacy concerns deliver a message that the privacy protection of the incumbent service is not competitive compared with the privacy protection of the alternatives. This perception can be weakened by inertia as inertial users may be uncertain about the privacy protection of alternative mobile payment applications. Although users have recognized that the incumbent service may not be the most eficient or efective, they stick to the incumbent service as a “known quantity.” We thus expect that the positive efect of privacy concerns on switching behavior decreases as inertia increases:

H3b. Inertia weakens the positive relationship between privacy concerns and switching behavior.

Cognitive misperceptions are more likely to occur among users with a high level of inertia due to loss aversion [73]. This means that users tend to weigh potential losses (however small) as being greater than potential gains in deciding whether to switch away from the incumbent service. Previous studies indicate that switching behaviors occur if the benefits of the alternative outweigh the sum of the benefits gained from the incumbent service and users’ sunk cost (or past investment in the incumbent service) [74]. Inertial users may overestimate the perceived sunk costs of learning to use the existing mobile payment application and treat them as huge loss if they shifted to another platform. Such perceived loss can be stronger than the monetary benefits they can get from switching to an alternative application. We thus expect that the positive efect of the monetary rewards of alternatives on switching behavior decreases as inertia increases. Taken together, we propose the following:

H3c. Inertia weakens the positive relationship between the monetary rewards of alternatives and switching behavior.

## 3.4. The antecedents of inertia

## 3.4.1. Economic value

Economic value is defined as the perceived fairness of the price for the level of service quality and the breadth of features ofered [91]. The perception of economic value forms in a comparative fashion. Verhoef [91] argues that the relative price and quality of competitors are prominent factors in the calculation of the economic value of the incumbent service. This comparative nature makes perceived economic value an objective and generalizable measure of service characteristics that influence inertia. High perceived economic value indicates that mobile payment application charges a relatively low service fee or ofers a high level of service quality than alternatives. Utility maximization theory suggests that consumer behavior is guided toward the maximization of total utility or total satisfaction received from consuming a goods or service within given budget [12]. Users may continuously use incumbent mobile payment service if it is believed to contain high perceived economic value. We thus expect that high economic value, be it from low pricing or good benefits, increases mobile payment users’ afectivebased inertia.

H4a. Perceived economic value is positively associated with inertia.

## 3.4.2. Past investment

Past investment refers to the time, efort, and money that users have devoted to their incumbent service. Users invest time and efort in a mobile payment application to create benefits, foster certainty, and establish brand relationships, all of which could be terminated when they switch to an alternative. Users initially adopt a service by evalu ating the merits of various service providers. Past investment is a major antecedent of cognitive-based inertia because it has the power to lock users into a service for relational and economic reasons [13]. First, users may sufer relational losses when switching to an alternative service. Long-term users have developed intimate relationships with their service providers [13]. Second, users may sufer benefit losses, such as accumulated points, premium services as a long-term consumer, and credits that users have already paid for, when switching to an alternative service [71]. Moreover, users of a mobile payment application may have established a user profile, automatic payment from their credit cards, or category preferences that cannot be transformed to alternatives [74]. We thus propose the following:

H4b. Users’ past investment in an incumbent mobile payment application is positively associated with inertia.

## 3.4.3. Technological self-eficacy

Technological self-eficacy captures users’ beliefs in their proficiency with an IT-related task [34]. Switching to a new mobile payment application requires abandoning the accounts associated with the former service, setting up features at the new service, and adapting the new interface. Experiential knowledge, such as previous knowledge about current technologies, is quickly obsoleted and out of fashion in a dynamic and rapidly developing area like the mobile Internet [2,34]. Therefore, switching-related tasks may be dificult among mobile pay ment application.

Behavioral-based inertia refers to those individuals who continue using a service simply because it is what they have always done [73]. Users with low technological eficacy are likely to be swamped with the technical issues relating to switching [74] and are more likely to con tinue using the service they are familiar with. For example, users trying to use a new mobile payment application might be dissuaded by the technical issues of creating personal profiles, setting up automatic bill payment, exploring additional features, and so forth. On the other hand, users with higher technical eficacy do not face any user-related switching barriers [90]. We can thus expect that technological self-efficacy negatively predicts behavioral-based inertia, such that users are more likely to try alternative services as their technological self-eficacy increases.

H4c. The technological self-eficacy of mobile payment users is negatively associated with inertia.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Survey deployment and data collection

Alipay is the largest mobile payment platform in the world [10,84] and was, therefore, selected to test our model. A pilot test was conducted to obtain feedback on the validity and usability of our instrument. Fifty student users of Alipay from a university located in Western China were invited, and forty-two valid responses were received. Respondents were asked to give open feedback on the comprehensibility of the items, the overall time required, and any other problems they encountered in completing the survey. Cronbach alpha values and factor analysis were used to obtain acceptable reliability levels. Hardto-understand items were modified without losing their construct meaning.

The snowball sampling strategy was adopted in the final survey. Two-thousand invitations were sent to Alipay users chosen randomly from a panel maintained by a marketing survey firm. At the same time, we asked the respondents to spread the survey invitation to their friend who also use Alipay. This survey consisted of two parts: the demographics and Alipay usage information and the scales of our constructs. Our survey was left open for 4 weeks, with a friendly reminder sent to non-respondents on the 15th day. We announced that respondents who completed and spread our survey would have a 50% chance of receiving a 10-yuan (approximately \$1, 50 USD) cash reward. After eliminating incomplete or rush responses, 3785 valid responses were used in our final analysis.

The demographics of the respondents are reported in Table 1. Because we were unable to obtain the oficial demographics of Alipay users, we compared the demographics of our sample with those of the recent empirical studies that also surveyed Alipay users [22,51,102]. No systematic diferences were found. Therefore, our sample is representative of the target subjects. We also compared the demographics of early respondents with those of late respondents [60]. The results of the Chi-square tests show that the demographics of the early respondents did not difer significantly from those of late respondents.

## 4.2. Measurement

The measurement items of our survey were largely drawn from validated scales in the pertinent studies. All these items were measured by 7-point Likert scales ranging from strongly disagree = 1 to strongly agree = 7. Appendix 1 lists the measurement scales and their sources.

We adopted the nine-item scale for inertia developed by Polites and Karahanna [73] that taps each of its three dimensions (e.g., afectivebased, behavioral-based, and cognitive-based). The items for privacy concerns were adapted from Xu, Dinev, Smith and Hart [98]. The construct of the monetary rewards of alternatives was measured using scales from Zhao, Lu and Gupta [101]. The perceived economic value of a service was derived from Sweeney and Soutar [87]. Using items derived from Taylor and Todd [88], we also developed a global measure of technological self-eficacy with mobile payment applications. Past investments were measured using items derived from Rokkan, Heide and Wathne [76]. Finally, the items used to measure switching behavior were drawn from Hsieh, Hsieh, Chiu and Feng [36].

A set of control variables was included to eliminate alternative explanations on the formation of switching behavior. The demographic characteristics of respondents, such as gender, age, education, and length of use, were controlled. Previous studies have confirmed that satisfaction and trust significantly predict switching behavior [58,71,102]. In addition, switching behavior can be influenced by network externality. Individuals were heavily influenced by their peers on a range of issues, including social participation, social leadership, and club membership [79]. Users may switch to an alternative mobile payment application and avoid being in a minority if the alternative service has a large number of users. Therefore, satisfaction, trust, and critical mass were controlled as well.

## 5.1.2. Common method variance

Because our dependent variable and independent variables were drawn from a single survey, the study results may sufer from common method variance (CMV). Several recommended procedurals were used to minimize CMV. We placed the filler tasks (basic information of the respondents) between the items of our constructs, creating a psychological separation between the measurement of the dependent variable and independent variables, which helps minimize CMV [31].

A two-step confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to detect the impact of CMV [37,72]. In the first step, a nine-factor model (with three first-order constructs of inertia) was estimated.<sup>5</sup> Each of the 30 items was restricted to serving as an indicator of the respective latent factor. The fit indices of the first model were: $\alpha ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 1 . 4 5 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 5 9 , \mathrm { G F I } =$ 0.923, AGFI <sup>=</sup> 0.877, RMSEA = 0.052. All these indices satisfied the recommended thresholds<sup>6</sup> and indicated a good data fit. In the second step, in addition to the nine factors examined in the first model, one additional factor was added to represent the unmeasured common method. Each of the 30 items was allowed to load on its respective theoretical factor construct. and all were allowed to load on the additional methods factor, which was constrained to be uncorrelated with the other nine factors. The fit indices $( \alpha ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 1 . 4 5 , \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 6 0 , \mathrm { G F I } =$ $0 . 9 2 3 , \mathrm { A G F I } = 0 . 8 7 8 , \mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 5 1 )$ of the second model were largely identical to those of the first one. In addition, the Chi-square test comparing the first model with the second model indicated that the diference between the two models was not significant (α $( 2 ) = 0 . 0 1 5 ,$ $\mathbf { p } = \mathbf { n } . s . )$ . These tests provide evidence that CMV was not a major concern in the current study. To detect potential multicollinearity, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIFs) for all variables used in the structural model. The highest individual VIF was 2.42, far below the critical value of 3.3 [63].

Fig. 4 presents the results of our structural model. Privacy concerns $( \beta = 0 . 5 5 1 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and the monetary rewards of alternatives $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 8 , \textrm { p < } 0 . 0 1 )$ have a significant positive efect on switching behavior, validating H1 and H2. As expected, inertia $( \beta = - 0 . 2 2 4$ $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ was negatively associated with switching behavior, supporting H3a. Inertia was found to exert a negative moderating efect on the paths between the monetary rewards of alternatives and switching behavior $( \beta = - 0 . 2 4 2 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 1 )$ ), supporting H3c. H3b failed to be supported because the coeficient of the interaction term (privacy concerns × inertia) was insignificant. Overall, our model explained 49.6% of the variance in users’ switching behavior. These three antecedents jointly explained 77.2% of the variance of inertia.

Contrary to our expectation, inertia’s negative moderating efect on the path between privacy concerns and switching behavior (H3b) was not supported. A possible explanation for this result is that inertial users do not make ongoing evaluations of their automatic behavior unless some circumstance triggers the need for conscious thought [70]. The misuse of user’s personal information may lead to financial loss. Privacy concerns serve as the trigger for this conscious thought. Therefore, the positive relationship between privacy concerns and switching behavior was not contingent on inertia.

The model results show that the absolute coeficient of privacy concerns was larger than that of inertia and the attractiveness of alternatives. The $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ of these three additional models was 45%, 34%, and 29%, respectively. Because the three explanatory variables were added separately, the $\mathrm { \dot { R } } ^ { 2 }$ of these models was far below that of our main model. In addition, we calculated the unbiased beta weights for each coeficient and conducted a t-test<sup>7</sup> for each pairwise comparison [26]. ## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Discussions of the key findings

The objective of this study was to develop and test a theoretical framework that identifies the formation of switching behavior and the antecedents of inertia within mobile payment applications. Based on the PPM framework, we proposed that switching behavior is determined by a cognitive process involving push, pull, and mooring factors. Several general factors of IS switching behaviors have been found. Dissatisfaction on incumbent service was identified as a push factor of switching [17]. Critical mass of alternative was viewed as a pull factor [97] because of peer influence on user behaviors [79]. Trust and switching cost were more likely to be considered as mooring factors [36,44,86]. The efect of usage length on switching behaviors is controversial. One may argue that usage length is positively associated with users’ sunk costs of switching and should be viewed as a mooring factor. On the other hand, fatigue with incumbent service arises as consumer’s usage length increases. Therefore, length of use can also be regarded as a push factor. With the above factors being controlled, we incorporated contextual variables into the PPM model by considering the unique characteristics of mobile payment applications. The proposed model was tested using data collected from 3785 mobile payment users. Seven out of eight hypotheses were supported (see Table 5). The key findings are discussed as follows.

We found that privacy concerns push users to switch and the monetary rewards of alternatives pulls users to switch. This finding echoes previous privacy studies in that users may switch to alternatives once they are the victims of privacy invasion [82]. Inertia positively predicts switching behavior and negatively moderates the positive relationship between alternative monetary rewards and switching behavior. Moreover, the results show that the contribution of privacy concerns to shaping switching behavior outweighed that of inertia and the monetary rewards of alternatives. We also found that economic value and past investment positively predict swathing behavior and technological self-eficacy negatively predict switching behavior.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

The paper contributes to the literature in several ways.

First, this paper advances our understanding of the formation of switching behavior within the emerging context of mobile payment applications. Current studies on users’ attitudes toward mobile payment applications mainly focus on users’ initial adoption [38,40,85,99] and continuous usage [4,18,102]. The continuance and switch of any technologies, including mobile payment applications, are not opposite extremes on the same continuum but are driven by diferent factors [55]. Although users’ switching behaviors have been investigated within various contexts, such as email services [41], social networking [55], instant messaging [86], cloud storage services [97], online auctions [52], e-commerce [16], and e-health care services [45], the switching behavior of mobile payment service has been largely overlooked. Mobile payment applications have unique characteristics that may influence the formation of switching behavior. Therefore, investigating users’ switching behavior within the context of mobile payment may add value to the existing literature.

Second, we enriched the PPM framework by identifying specific contextual variables (i.e. inertia, monetary rewards, and privacy concern) of mobile payment applications, which influence users switching behaviors. Previous studies that utilized the PPM framework to understand IS switching behaviors mainly used general constructs (such as switching cost, alternative attractiveness, and satisfaction) extracted from human migration literature. To date, less attention has been paid to the variables that capture the unique characteristics of the research contexts.

Third, this study identifies mooring variables based on a personal–institutional–relational framework. Previous studies have primarily focused on personal-based mooring factors, such as trust [44], past experience [36], and habit [100]. The institutional and relational factors that may keep users staying with incumbent service have been largely overlooked. We proposed three mooring factors, technical expertise of users, perceived economic of incumbent service, and past investments, which broadly represent the institutional, personal, and relational forces that demotivate consumers from switching to a better alternative [80].

## 6.3. Managerial implications

Our findings have certain relevance for both incumbent service providers and newcomers. First, we found that privacy concerns were the most critical factor that pushes users to switch. This finding echoed previous studies in that feeling secure is one of the most influential factors regarding monetary transactions $[ 1 9 , 6 7 ]$ . Privacy concerns cast doubt upon mobile payment vendors’ ability and willingness to protect users’ personal information. Vendors of mobile payment applications are advised to deliver a message that they are able to safeguard users personal information. For example, vendors are advised to regularly upgrade their privacy protection technologies and, if possible, explain the mechanisms of their privacy protection technologies using easy-tounderstand languages. Once users hand over their personal informa tion, the information moves to a collective domain where both users and vendors become co-owners with joint responsibility for keeping the information safe [69,98]. Vendors should create the impression that they will keep watchful eyes on the protection of users’ personal information. For example, the privacy policy should be fair and placed prominently on the homepage of a mobile payment application. The responsibility of both users and vendors should be clearly defined within the privacy policy.

Second, monetary incentive is increasingly adopted by newcomers to attract potential users. How would incumbent service providers react to retain their users? On the other hand, users may abandon the newly adopted platform as the monetary incentive runs out. How would newcomers lock in the newly adopted users? This study was designed to answer these questions. The positive efect of monetary rewards on switching behavior suggests that monetary incentives for newcomers are advised to attract potential users. Incumbent vendors are also ad vised to cultivate the inertia of their users, as inertia on incumbent service attenuates the positive relationship between the monetary rewards of alternatives and switching behavior.

Our results show that perceived economic value, past investments, and technological self-eficacy served as the significant antecedents of inertia. Incumbent vendors are encouraged to grant users coupons, accumulate points, and discounts to increase the perceived economic value of the incumbent service providers. Similar to some of the pioneering mobile payment apps [59], mobile payment vendors are encouraged to incorporate additional services and products. For example, mobile payment apps may incorporate instant messaging, finance products, and ticketing services to increase users’ investment and attachment to their incumbent mobile payment apps. On the contrary, the newcomers should develop a strategy to help their target customers undercut their past investments. Users may go through a series of fussy tasks, such as linking mobile payment accounts with their bank accounts and setting passwords, preferences, and safety verifications, to get their mobile payment accounts working. To help users to transfer their account information and personal settings to alternative platforms is an efective way for newcomers to counter the lock-in efect of the incumbent vendors.

Third, for newcomers to compete efectively with the incumbent, there must be suficient advantages (i.e. monetary rewards) to ofset users’ inertia on the incumbent platform [52]. Considering the specific context of the mobile payment market, the monetary rewards of alternatives should outweigh the sunk cost of switching. For example, Andy Grove, former CEO of Intel, argued that the alternative advantages should be at least ten times greater than those ofered by the incumbent service to attract a substantial number of inertial users to switch (the renowned ${ } ^ { * } 1 0 \times { } ^ { * }$ rule).

Fourth, the negative relationship between technological self-eficacy and inertia contributes another idea for newcomers. Users with low technological self-eficacy may find it challenging to switch to an alternative service provider because they fear the prospect of a new technical environment. Newcomers need to ensure that their target users are given the necessary information and assistance to use their new mobile payment apps.

## 6.4. Limitations and future directions

There are several inevitable limitations in this paper that can be further improved. First, we did not capture the dynamic nature of users’ switching behaviors. Users may not completely stop using incumbent mobile payment applications and switch to alternatives. There is a gradual transition period in which users intend to try new services and simultaneously use the incumbent service [17]. Understanding the transition process from incumbent mobile payment apps to alternatives may add value to the literature. Second, the current study adopted the snowball sampling method to recruit survey respondents. We were unable to obtain the overall response rate and demographics on nonrespondents. While the proper measures were taken to detect the impact of non-response bias, future researchers are advised to adopt an experimental or random sampling method. Third, we granted survey respondents cash rewards, and bias that may emerge as a monetary reward of alternatives was regarded as a full factor in our model. Our model needs to be verified through a random sample. Lastly, the maturity and usage of mobile payment applications varied from country to country. Cross-regional studies may provide additional insights.

## 7. Conclusion

Drawing on the PPM framework, this study aimed to uncover the formation of users’ switching behavior in the context of mobile payment applications. We argue that switching behavior is determined by a cognitive process involving push, pull, and mooring factors. We propose that privacy concern serves as a push force and monetary rewards of alternatives serves as a pull force that drive users to switch. Perceived economic value, past investments, technology self-eficacy, and inertia are identified as mooring factors. Using the snowball sampling strategy, a large-scale empirical survey was conducted to test the hypotheses. The results show that both privacy concern and monetary rewards of alternatives are positively related to switching behavior. Inertia negatively predicts switching behavior and attenuates the positive relationship between alternative monetary rewards and switching behavior. Perceived economic value, past investment, and technological self eficacy were positively related to inertia. The results of our study in form both incumbent vendors and newcomers regarding how to maintain existing users as well as attract potential users.
