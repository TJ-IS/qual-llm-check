## A B S T R A C T

Professor Edgar Henry Sibley (the Founding Editor of Information & Management, in short, I&M) passed away peacefully in Virginia, USA on January 12, 2020. As an eminent scholar in Information Systems (IS) and the Founding Editor of I&M, Professor Sibley has left a remarkable legacy to both the journal and the IS discipline. If the journal (I&M) could talk, it would tell the story of the passion, efort, untold amount of time, and incredible rigor Professor Sibley put into this journal. As the second and the current Editor-in-Chief of the journal, I took the liberty to invite three IS scholars, one from each region of the Association for Information Systems (AIS) community to speak about Professor Sibley’s contributions to the IS discipline. They are Professor Prashant Palvia from the University of North Carolina Greensboro, USA Professor Jose Benitez from Rennes School of Business, France and the University of Granada, Spain and Professor T.P. Liang from National Sun-Yat-Sen University, Taiwan. All three scholars had diferent and positive interactions and experiences with Professor Sibley and I&M that they are eager to share with us. We hope the whole IS community will join us to acknowledge and pay our heart-felt tribute to Professor Sibley´s many contributions to our discipline.

Patrick Y.K. Chau, Editor-in Chief

## 1. Professor Edgar Sibley: the person

“Described by his colleagues as outspoken, impatient, honorable, and loyal... He was direct, honest, and generous to a fault. [Professor Sibley] was a man of the world who loved to explore new places, foods, and cultures… He set expectations, provided opportunities, and led by example. He was remarkably tolerant, ready to trust, quick to forgive, and never took himself too seriously.” [1].

Professor Sibley self-defined as an engineer, a consultant, and an IS scholar. Among his many qualities, he was an honorable, open, and generous man. He was an early pioneer in calling for inter-disciplinary work between the engineering and business domains, and between the behavioral and technical dimensions of the IS discipline. He built strong foundations for I&M, one of the best IS journals worldwide. His leadership was inclusive. During his tenure, he was open, constructive, and supportive to the authors irrespective of their afiliations, pedigree, or area of research. In his personal life, Professor Sibley was a true Renaissance man and to his family.

Professor Sibley was sincere and kind and especially enthusiastic in mentoring junior multidisciplinary faculty members and supportive of international IS research and IS education. Many of our observations are anecdotal and do not cover the full range of his contributions, but con versations with other people in the discipline confirm the essence of this remarkable man. As an example, one of the authors of this article, Ting-Peng Liang, mentioned his experience of publishing his first I&M article in 1987 when he was a first-year Assistant Professor. Professor Sibley not only provided him with constructive comments for improving the manuscript but also copy edited his non-native English writing. This treatment was a very special honor for a junior faculty member.

Prof. Sibley’s service as the Editor-in-Chief of I&M encouraged authors to submit their best papers to the journal, thereby creating its stellar reputation and securing its place among the leading journals in the IS discipline. His stylistic flair and technical rigor ensured the quality and substance of papers published in I&M. Patrick Chau, the current Editor-in-Chief of I&M, shared his experience of his early interaction with Professor Sibley. He recalls an instance in which Professor Sibley cut one third of his article before accepting it for publication in I&M, stating that the paper must be concise and precise without extraneous verbiage. Indeed, this was more the rule than the exception of Professor Sibley’s editing style. Jose Benitez recalls the first I&M paper he read when he was completing his Ph.D. in IS at the University of Granada, Granada, Spain. It taught him the importance of relevance, rigor, parsimony, and impact of IS research which was confirmed in his research and consulting work in subsequent years. This paper and other articles published in I&M evoked in him a special engagement and passion for I&M that continues until today.

Professor Sibley was an advocate of international education and more than willing to share his knowledge with graduate students. He spent summers with the United Nations and its Agencies in Slovakia, Hungary, Israel, the Philippines, and at National Science Foundation’s projects in Russia (Picture 2). According to W. Terry Hardgrave, a former colleague and lifelong friend, “For me, Ed along with Prof. Alfred G. Dale… were my most influential mentors. Both were Englishmen and, being trained in England, both were excellent writers and editors. Both Prof. Sibley and Prof. Dale were active internationally and participated in the trips to the Soviet Union as part of the detente initiatives with the USSR.”

Professor Sibley’s international work also included lectures and guest keynotes across North America, South America, Europe, and Asia. Many of us had the opportunity to meet with him in person during these talks. For example, Professor Sibley and his wife, Sheila, were invited to visit National Sun Yat-Sen University at Kaohsiung, Taiwan, in 2004 (see Picture 3–5). They visited almost every year afterwards until 2013.

Professor Jen-Her Wu of National Sun Yat-Sen University mentioned that Professor Sibley was over 75 at the time and spent over 20 h travelling from the USA to Taiwan. His enthusiasm and positive attitude showed Professor Sibley’s commitment to international cooperation under the umbrella of IS education. In 2007, he spent a sabbatical at Sun Yat-Sen University in Taiwan, where he taught a doctoral seminar. He shared his experiences of studying in England and his doctoral studies at MIT. The course was among the most memorable for these students.

Professor Wu, who worked with Professor Sibley during his visits, was highly impressed by Professor Sibley’s commitment to academic research and his willingness to share his professional knowledge, future vision, as well as his editorial experiences with doctoral students and faculty members. They worked together on several research projects. Professor Sibley always provided intellectual ideas and insightful comments in these projects, all altruistically. As was his custom, he was generous to his co-writers and frequently passed up on opportunities to be listed as a co-author. “Professor Sibley is a very respectable gentleman who always talks politely and is willing to help others,” said Professor Wu.

## 2. Contributions to the IS research and IS discipline

Professor Sibley made outstanding contributions to the IS discipline and enriched the lives of an untold number of IS scholars. Each IS scholar touched by his selfless mentorship will remember him for a very long time.

Professor Sibley was a cultivated man with outstanding intellectual skills. During the first 20 years of his career, he worked as an engineer, co-inventor of the low temperature Electro-Magnetic transducer, consultant, and leader. He received a B.S. in Engineering from the University of London, UK and was awarded an M.S. in Mechanical Engineering and a Doctor of Science in Mechanical Engineering from Massachusetts Institute of Technology, USA. Following a career as an engineer and consultant in England, Canada, and the United States, he served as a faculty member in the Department of Industrial Engineering at the University of Michigan at Ann Arbor, USA between 1966 and 1973. Between 1973 and 1984, he held appointments as a Professor and Chair in the Business and Management, and Information Systems Managements Schools at the University of Maryland at College Park, USA. W. Terry Hardgrave notes: “Professionally, Ed helped all of the Maryland faculty on every front.” From 1979–1980, he was a Visiting Professor at the Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania. In 1984, he moved to George Mason University, USA and held the titles of University Professor and Eminent IS Scholar with joint appointments in the School of Information Technology and Engineering (SITE) and the Institute for Public Policy, and served as the Chair of the Department of Information and Software Systems Engineering. During the 1980s and 1990s, Professor Sibley served as a consultant to Argonne National Laboratories, Institute for Defense Analysis, and the U.S. General Accounting Ofice. He retired in 2011 from George Mason University, although he maintained his part-time research position. He continued to be the Editor-in-Chief of I&M until December 2013. His retirement was in name only; Professor Sibley never really retired from academic life.

“When an old man dies, a library burns to the ground” (an old African proverb). This sentiment is particularly true for Professor Sibley. As a metaphor, his contributions to the IS discipline are like a permanent tattoo, dificult to remove and forget. Professor Sibley had outstanding conceptual, analytical, and writing skills that he leveraged to publish over 100 papers in the fields of Computer Science, Leadership, Engineering, and IS. He produced seminal work on relational and networked data storage, and multi-schema database architectures, work that profoundly impacted database management and practices in civil, military, and business organizations [1]. Specifically, as Don Deutsch, a doctoral student and former colleague, recalled: “Working together at NBS [National Bureau of Standards, [now the National Institute of Standards and Technology], we initiated activities that resulted in domestic US and international SQL standards. One might say that Ed was the father (or grandfather) of NBS's work on database management, and of SQL relational database management systems standards in particular. Ed certainly substantially influenced my life – not only through my doctoral work, but also my career choices. Every job I held since NBS has been related to RBMS [Relational Database Management Systems] technology. While I had several line software engineering management position along the way, early and late in my career my positions have been primarily related to standardization. Thank you Ed!”

Not only the Founding Editor of I&M, Professor Sibley was an Associate Editor for several other journals and professional organizations, including the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), where he was the Chair of the Computing Practices Panel of Communications of the ACM. He served on the Committee of the National Research Council, Washington, D.C. (USA), Chairman of the Conference/Committee on Data Systems Languages (CODASYL) from 1971 to 1975, Chairman of the Board of The International Federation for Information Processing (IFIP) from 1978 to 1981 (Holland), a reviewer for the National Science Foundation (Washington, D.C., USA), and a Senior member of Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE). Professor Sibley´s research interests covered database design, models, IS management, e-commerce, privacy, systems design, and the analysis and understanding of IS research. He provided expert witness services to several federal cases, including pro bono work in support of juvenile victims. He was listed as a noteworthy IS educator, engineer, and consultant by Marquis Who’s Who in the World.

One of Professor Sibley’s biggest contributions was his early recognition. in the 1980s. that Engineering Schools were deficient in their lack of teaching fundamental business acumen. He called for interdisciplinary work between Engineering and Business Schools and role modeled this at the University of Maryland, where he held joint appointments in the Business and Engineering Schools. Rainer Sommer, a former doctoral student and faculty member at George Mason University recalls: “During my tenure at SITE, Ed was the only one at that time in the Engineering School who was actively working to convince government sponsors that their change management and systems implementation issues were not just technical problems, but rather more complex process and people issues. Ed saw it first.” Similarly, Thomas Gulledge, another former colleague recounts: “Edgar was not a one-dimensional technical researcher. He focused on behavioral and technical issues as well.”

Illustrations selected randomly from Professor Sibley´s research highlight his work on data definition languages by describing both logical and physical aspects of data, a key aspect of database management systems (e.g., [2]). One of the critical subdomains of the IS discipline is understanding how IT afects organizations, an area in which Professor Sibley made important contributions. For example, in Le Moigne and Sibley’s [3] paper, they questioned the usage of “old fashioned” IT management models which did not consider new disruptive information technologies appearing in the market at that time. Over decades, he worked extensively on IT international standards. Specifically, he was engaged in the US and international standardization of databases, data modeling, and data dictionaries. As the Chairman of the Board of Editors since its inception in 1977 and Founding Editor of I&M,

Professor Sibley also examined the profile of IS research published in I& M. This was done by identifying the most prolific authors and universities, the most impactful IS research topics, and the most used research methods (e.g., [4]). As another example, he investigated the challenges in using textual data mining tools in a large dataset, as in Fortune 500 companies. He and his co-authors proposed diferent ways to refine these tools as well as expanded on the value for IS research of the self-reported NAICS codes reported by large companies [5]. His research, insights, and leadership benefitted from his consulting and managerial work in all areas of large-scale information and general systems design, in companies such as A.O. Smith Corporation, Alpha Omega Group, Dynatech Corporation (USA), and several major governmental agencies.

Professor Sibley was a noteworthy educator in the field of IS. He wa a professor, mentor, and academic father to many students. He contributed as a Chair, Advisor, or Examiner in about fifty Ph.D. disserta tion committees and was supportive and always ready to assist IS col leagues. As Murad Mehmet, a former Ph. D. student, wrote: “Dr. Sibley was a father, role-model, mentor, advisor (academia and life) for the most critical years in my life and since after my graduation under his scholarly tutoring. Since knowing Dr. Sibley in 2003, he paved the way for me to succeed in professional and academic life as a professor/mentor/father. I grew fond of his compassion, straightforwardness, honesty, and sharp in telligence. He was the flag that most of us tried to hold under, knowing that it is rare to find such quality human-being like himself in life. Our dear Dr. Sibley preserved the rare quality of scholars that vanished in a nowadays selfish world. Dr. Sibley was a guidebook that all humans meant to read and benefit from to learn good ethics and be productive for science and humanity. It has been one of my utmost honors in my life to know Dr. Sibley, work for/with him and be nurtured from his rare wisdom and compassionate teaching. He taught me how to be patient the best I can while achieving the end-goals and help others.” [1].

## 3. The legacy of information & management

## 3.1. The beginning of the journal

Founded in 1977 by Professor Sibley, Information & Management (I& M) is one of the earliest journals in the IS discipline. It was launched in the same year that MIS Quarterly came into existence. Professor Sibley served as I&M’s founder and steward for over 36 years. In 2014, he handed over the Editor-in-Chief role to Professor Patrick Chau.

In establishing I&M, Professor Sibley was a pioneer and a trailblazer, far ahead of his times. He set out to ensure the journal stood out from the existing array of academic journals, which tended to focus only on academic rigor, many times at the expense of practical relevance. While rigor is always important in academic IS research, Professor Sibley emphasized practical and organizational relevance as well. He wanted the journal to combine both behavioral and technical dimensions of IS, with openness to new ideas and innovations. He also placed a heavy emphasis on the international aspects of IS research and clearly stated that international coverage was a virtual necessity. In the opening editorial from the first issue in 1977, he articulated:

1) The material that will be presented in the journal will have general applicability. We will endeavor to show no bias concerning geographic boundaries, diferences in language, or social systems.

2) The rapid increase of international commerce and knowledge in terchange and the trans-border flow of data will accelerate the need for international standards in data handling and the devices used for this purpose; decisions that are taken at the international level afect practitioners in every country.

3) Organizations that operate in more than one country need to be kept informed of developments in the countries in which they operate, including information on governmental regulations that may afect those operations; e.g., we already see the emergence of governmental regulations to protect data as a personal or national resource.

4) At the large-scale and intra-national level, only an international forum can draw on suficient users of equal scale for the meaningful interchange of experience.

These were not mere words. Professor Sibley implemented these goals, selecting an Editorial Board across diverse backgrounds and disciplines throughout the world. He initially chose 24 Board members from eleven countries, eighteen of whom were from industry and six from academia. This selection is truly visionary when you realize that it was 1977, long before expansive globalization came into the business world. He saw it first.

## 3.2. The I&M journey

Over the years, I&M continued to prosper under the adept leadership of Professor Sibley. It regularly published six to eight issues every year. Each issue contained approximately four to eight articles per issue or fifty to sixty articles per year. This number of papers was a huge volume, especially when the Editor-in-Chief was a hands-on person and maintained the highest standard of quality. As a reflection of the increasing popularity and high quality of the journal, starting from humble beginnings, the impact factor of I&M rose to 2.60 in 2010, and now it is at a historically high level of 4.12 in 2018.

One of the authors of this article (Prashant Palvia) had the good fortune and honor of working with Professor Sibley on rigorously examining research published in I&M [4]. Along with doctoral students, they reviewed and profiled the research published in I&M for fourteen years from 1992 to 2005. Besides the high quality of the publications, one word captures the results of the review: inclusive. Research pub lished in I&M was inclusive in all respects: authors, topics, and methodologies. Thus, I&M was living up to its promise of inclusiveness, relevance and applicability, and unbiasedness. As an example, the following table shows the university afiliations of authors who published in I&M from 1992 to 2005. Even though much of IS research was conducted in the USA, it was refreshing to note that the top universities included three universities outside of the USA, accounting for 38% of the top publications (Table 1).

I&M had also been highly inclusive in the topics to be published in the journal. No topic was of-limits as long as it was in the realm of the IS discipline. As a testimony, Table 2 shows the topics and their frequency in articles published in I&M from 1998 to 2005, during Professor Sibley’s tenure.

Inclusivity was also demonstrated in the choice of research methods employed by the authors. In general, the journal and the Editor-in-Chief do not have control over the choice of research methodologies employed by the authors, but the point is that they have been inclusive of all methodologies and are not focused on any specific methodologies unlike some other IS journals. We believe this is a positive strategy that contributes to academic expression of diferent streams of thought. In particular, while the survey method was the dominant research method used by most authors, there was a good balance among the other methodologies included in I&M during the tenure of Prof. Sibley (see Table 3). It is heartening to note that the traditions and direction established by Professor Sibley for I&M have largely been preserved by his successor, the current Editor-in-Chief, Professor Patrick Chau. While im portant changes have been made by Professor Chau, the basic philosophy and underpinnings of the journal remain the same. A follow up study examined the contents of I&M from 2004 to 2014 [6]. It corroborated the inclusive nature of I&M in all aspects: authors, topics and methodologies. In terms of topics, the number of topics examined had increased even more; however, electronic commerce research had taken over IS adoption and usage research. All research methodologies con tinue to be accepted by the journal. As a sign of times, while the survey method and the positivist paradigm remained dominant, the qualitative methods and the interpretive paradigm were beginning to make inroads.

## 3.3. Editorial philosophy and some personal observations

From personal observations of the four authors of this article, it can be surmised that Professor Sibley’s editorial philosophy was rooted in the principles of inclusivity, openness, quality, fairness, and helpfulness. Some of the leading IS journals today might summarily reject papers without providing helpful comments or developmental assistance. Not Professor Sibley. In this respect, he was an iconic figure in the IS field and, at the same time, very humble. We would like to make the following observations.

1) Professor Sibley was helpful to all authors, irrespective of their affiliation or status. There might be a perception, true or not, that publications in some of the top IS journals are dominated by leading names or people with power, pedigree or status. Not so in I&M. As long as you do quality work, you have a chance to publish in I&M, even if you are at the bottom of the totem pole.

2) Professor Sibley listened. There are instances when an Associate Editor or Reviewers rejected a paper. In such a case, most Editors would reject a paper and would not entertain protests from the authors. But Professor Sibley actually listened to arguments made by the authors. We know of instances when desk-rejected papers, as well as papers first rejected by Associate Editors, were later published in I&M after suitable revisions.

3) Professor Sibley loved the journal and worked very hard, giving personal attention to each paper accepted in the journal. Once a paper was in revision, he would personally invest countless hours in editing and rewriting major portions of each paper. By the time a paper went for production, it would be a superior submission based on the quality of writing. Many of us are indebted to him for teaching us how to write a good paper clearly and concisely.

4) Professor Sibley’s apparent motto was “keep it simple,” similar to Occam’s razor. William of Ockham “(philosopher and theologian) used preference for simplicity to defend the idea of divine miracles” [7]. Professor Sibley followed a similar product policy for writing, guiding, and accepting research papers at I&M. He would eliminate much of the hyperbole, flowery language, and repetition (which may sometimes be a defining characteristic of some leading IS journals). He would substantially shorten endless verbiage spent on literature reviews and discussion sections of a paper. He knew full well that most readers would skip over these sections anyway. He would also repudiate excessive references in a paper and limit them to a reasonable number in the range of 40-50. Many top journal papers today have references well in excess of 100. But if we are honest to ourselves, we all know that authors do not read all of the papers, but they list them only to show thoroughness and, in some cases, to please respected figures in the IS field, among other reasons.

Although several of us met Professor Sibley only a couple of times in person and we therefore cannot speak authoritatively about his traits, we would confidently say that Professor Sibley’s editorial philosophy reflected his personal values. These personal values are selflessness, simplicity, empathy, and excellence. Of course, he was an intellectual giant and an icon in the IS domain, but his personal values are what defined him. We have the greatest admiration for him, and we salute him.

## 4. Conclusions

It is always sad to hear that an IS colleague has passed away, especially if his work has been outstanding and has made tremendous contributions to the IS discipline. However, let us celebrate Professor Sibley´s legacy, for the IS discipline. The IS field in general, and journal in particular, would not have been as respected, shining, and forward looking without Professor Sibley´s vision, work, and passion.

You will be missed, Professor Sibley. Peace. Quo Vadis?
