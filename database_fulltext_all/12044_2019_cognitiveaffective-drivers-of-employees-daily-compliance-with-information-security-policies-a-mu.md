# Cognitive‐affective drivers of employees' daily compliance with information security policies: A multilevel, longitudinal study

John D'Arcy<sup>1</sup> | Paul Benjamin Lowry<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Department of Accounting and MIS, University of Delaware, 356 Purnell Hall, Newark, DE 19716, USA

<sup>2</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, P7912, Academic Building 1, 83 Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Kowloon, Hong Kong

John D'Arcy, Department of Accounting and MIS, University of Delaware, 356 Purnell Hall Newark, DE 19716, USA. Email: jdarcy@udel.edu

## Abstract

We present a model of employee compliance with information security policy (ISP) that (1) explicates stable, cognitive beliefs regarding the consequences of compliance and noncompliance as well as state‐based affective constructs, namely, positive and negative mood states and episodic, security‐related work‐impediment events, and (2) provides an expanded conceptualisation of moral considerations and normative influences regarding employees' ISP compliance. Because affect is central to this theorisation, we ensure that the model captures and explains differences in day‐to‐day affective constructs to account for the often fleeting nature of affective states. We test our multilevel model using an experience‐sampling methodology design, in which employees completed daily surveys over a 2‐week period, followed by a hierarchal linear modelling statistical assessment. Our contribution to theory is a unique account of ISP compliance that integrates affective factors with constructs from rational choice theory and theory of planned behaviour and that diverges from prior conceptualisations of ISP compliance as a purely stable and reason‐based phenomenon. For practitioners, our results suggest that a combination of cognitive and affective influences may produce discrete episodes of ISP compliance that do not coincide with prior behavioural trends.

## KEYWORDS

affect, information security policies (ISPs), ISP compliance, rational choice theory (RCT), theory of planned behaviour (TPB), multilevel analysis

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Numerous studies have documented the staggering financial and reputational costs borne by organisations due to information security breaches caused by certain actions of employees and other users with legitimate access privileges (Cole, 2015; PwC, 2017). As organisations combat the variety of internal threats and ways in which interna breaches can occur (cf Willison & Warkentin, 2013), organisational personnel and researchers should consider employees who deliberately violate information security policies (ISPs). Information security policies specify the proper uses of organisational information and technology resources and include formalised sets of guidelines, proce dures, and technical controls to which employees must adhere (Bulgurcu, Cavusoglu, & Benbasat, 2010; Cram, Proudfoot, & D'Arcy, 2017: Lowry & Moody, 2015), Research indicates that even though most organisations have ISPs in place, many employees knowingly and routinely disregard and/or circumvent these ISPs (Dell, 2015; Goel & Chengalur‐Smith, 2010). Estimates vary, but it has been reported that over half of all information security breache stem from deliberate ISP violations committed by users (eg, Anderson, 2013)

Because security breaches can have serious consequences for multiple organisational stakeholders, information systems (IS) security scholars have become especially concerned with understanding and predicting employees' ISP compliance. The IS security literature has identified an array of individual and workplace factors associated with ISP compliance. Much of this work is rooted in the rational view of human behaviour, such that ISP compliance is predicated on a cognitive evaluation of costs and benefits associated with compliance, along with other relatively stable, security‐related attitudes and beliefs (eg, attitudes towards compliance, moral beliefs, perceived threats, self‐efficacy, and social norms). Studies in this realm have drawn from several rationality‐based behavioural theories (eg, deterrence theory [DT], protection motivation theory [PMT], rational choice theory [RCT], and theory of planned behaviour [TPB]). However, the relationships described in TPB are among the most fre quently tested and expanded upon in research delineating the drivers of ISP compliance (Lebek, Uffen, Neumann Hohler, & Breitner, 2014; Sommestad, Hallberg, Lundholm, & Bengtsson, 2014). In fact, the 3 main constructs of TPB (attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control), along with a series of antecedent, compliancerelated belief constructs based on RCT, provide one of the most comprehensive models of ISP compliance in the literature (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). We similarly draw on both RCT and TPB to form the nomological core of our research model.

Although ISP compliance studies based on the rational view of human behaviour have contributed immensely to the IS security literature, they are not entirely comprehensive, because they often overlook affect, which is a key aspect of rational decision‐making. Affect refers to an individual's feeling state or how an individual feels at a given moment (Watson, 20o0). Affect can vary substantially within individuals from day to day, which can result in fluctu ations of employees' job‐related attitudes, beliefs, and behaviours over time (Judge & Kammeyer‐Mueller, 2012). Although affect may seem separate from and perhaps antithetical to the reason‐based cognitive processing that characterises rational decision‐making (per theories such as RCT and TPB), interdisciplinary research has suggested otherwise (Finucane, Alhakami, Slovic, & Johnson, 2000; Forgas, 2008; Lowry, Twyman, Pickard, Jenkins, & Bui, 2014; Pessoa, 2008). Such research has maintained that any behaviour is, by definition, both cognitive and affective because the cognitive processing of a stimulus cannot operate independently of affective factors. For example, Slovic, Finucane, Peters, and MacGregor (2004) argued that “people base their judgements of an activity not only on what they think about it but also how they feel about it” (p. 315; emphasis added). Current perspectives on rational decision‐making therefore recognise the joint influence of cognitive and affective modes of thought (Forgas, 2008; Slovic et al., 2004).

Drawing on this contemporary view of rational decision-making, we develop and empirically test a model of ISE compliance that captures both cognitive and affective elements. Specifically, our model integrates state‐based affective constructs—in the form of positive and negative mood states and episodic, security‐related work‐impediment events—along with stable, cognitive beliefs based on RCT and TPB to delineate drivers of daily ISP compliance, which we propose as at least partially an affect‐driven behaviour. We also extend prior TPB‐based models of ISP compliance that represent normative influences solely in terms of subjective norms (ie, perceptions of what significant others think one ought to do) by adding descriptive norms (ie, perceptions of what significant others do themselves). Theor of planned behaviour literature describes normative influences in terms of both descriptive and subjective norms (Ajzen, 2011; Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005), and empirical evidence in non‐IS contexts supports this broader normative influence concept (Rivis & Sheeran, 2003; Sheeran & Orbell, 1999). Nonetheless, descriptive norms have received lit tle attention in ISP compliance studies

Finally, our model provides an expanded conceptualisation of moral considerations that are relevant to ISP com pliance (ie, organisational deviance and organisational citizenship behaviours), which goes beyond the simplified con cept of moral beliefs that has been used in past studies. Insofar as ISP compliance decisions are moral dilemmas (Myyry, Siponen, Pahnila, Vartiainen, & Vance. 2009), and because TPB lends itself to the inclusion of individual moral ity constructs in such contexts (Ajzen, 1991; Beck & Ajzen, 1991), providing an expanded representation of moral considerations in our model is warranted and should improve our understanding of the ISP compliance decision‐makin process. Based on our research model, the primary research questions of this study are as follows:

(1) Do factors associated with affect work in conjunction with cognitive, compliance‐related beliefs in predicting daily attitudes towards ISP compliance?

(2) Do daily attitudes towards ISP compliance, along with moral considerations and normative influences regarding ISP compliance, predict daily ISP compliance behaviour?

Because of the transient nature of affective states (Weiss & Beal, 2005; Zhang, 2013), which are central to our theorising, we ensure that our model captures and explains day-to-day differences in affective constructs. That is unlike prior ISP compliance studies, we do not assume that emplovees' security-related attitudes and behaviours are completely stable over time; rather, we posit that such attitudes and behaviours fluctuate because of affective influences. By including in our model both state‐based affective constructs, which can vary daily, and stable, cognitive beliefs, we adopt a multilevel approach that encompasses both within‐ and between‐individual levels of analysis. We test our multilevel model using an experience‐sampling methodology (ESM) design in which employees complete daily surveys over a 2‐week period (ie, 10 working days), followed by a hierarchical linear modelling (HLM) statistica assessment. The results support a unique, affect‐inclusive adaptation of RCT and TPB to the context of IS security and provide fresh insights into the drivers of employees' ISP compliance decisions—including negative and positive dail work events, which have been overlooked by researchers

The paper proceeds as follows. After reviewing the relevant literature and highlighting the focus of our study, we lay out its theoretical foundation, which is based on RCT and TPB, and explain the role of affect in the rational deci sion‐making process that is innate to these theories. We then formally propose our research model and its associated hypotheses. The subsequent sections present the methodology, data analysis, and results. We conclude with a discussion of the study's findings, its contributions to research and practice and its limitations and with our suggestions for future research.

## 2 | RELEVANT LITERATURE

As the approach of IS security research has shifted from a purely technical to a sociotechnical perspective, scholars in this area have increasingly focused on the human elements of IS security—specifically, employee behaviour. Researc on the behavioural aspects of IS security reveals a host of factors that either hinder or facilitate employees' ISP com pliance (eg, Boss, Galletta, Lowry, Moody, & Polak, 2015; Lebek et al., 2014; Sommestad et al., 2014). Although non rational factors have occasionally been considered in this work (eg, habit, Vance, Siponen, & Pahnila, 2012; reactance Lowry & Moody, 2015; Lowry, Posey, Bennett, & Roberts, 2015), most studies have been conducted under the assumption of near‐pure rationality in decision‐making

Because our study is framed by RCT and TPB. both of which are rationality-based theories, we review the ISP compliance studies that are rooted in these theories. With regard to selecting RCT and TPB for our model rather than other rationality‐based behavioural theories that have been applied to ISP compliance (DT and PMT in particular), we argue the following: DT is a subset of RCT that pertains to the perceived‐cost portion of the rational decision process (Hechter & Kanazawa, 1997); thus, our model subsumes aspects of both theories. Regarding PMT, some IS securit scholars have argued that the theory is not accurately specified for ISP compliance and is better suited for persona usage contexts (eg, protecting a home computer), where the consequences of insecure behaviour affect the individual directly (Johnston, Warkentin, & Siponen, 2015; Sommestad, Karlzen, & Hallberg, 2015). Finally, because the combi nation of RCT and TPB underlies one of the most comprehensive models of ISP compliance in the literature (Bulgurcu et al., 2010), it provides an established theoretical foundation from which this research stream can be advanced

The central tenet of RCT is that individuals perform a mental cost‐benefit analysis when deciding whether to engage in a particular behaviour. Because of its narrow focus on costs and benefits, RCT has been merged with other rationality‐based theories of human behaviour to delineate a pathway from the expected costs and benefits of ISP compliance to the performance of this behaviour. In this vein, Bulgurcu et al. (2010) proposed and empirically tested a combined RCT‐TPB model of ISP compliance. This theoretical integration is fitting given that TPB constructs are regarded as drivers of rational behaviour (Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005) and because they have been shown to predict ISP compliance in several studies (Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Herath & Rao, 2009; Hu, Dinev, Hart, & Cooke, 2012; Ifinedo, 2012; Siponen, Mahmood, & Pahnila, 2014; Sommestad et al., 2014). Supporting TPB, Bulgurcu et al. (2010) found that ISP compliance‐related attitude, subjective norms, and self‐efficacy were each positively associated with compliance intention. Supporting RCT, they also found that several cognitive beliefs related to the costs and benefits of ISP compliance and noncompliance were significant predictors of attitude. These beliefs were, in turn, predicted by several outcome beliefs related to ISP compliance and noncompliance, which themselves were predicted by information security awareness. Other RCT-based ISP compliance studies have founc that perceived intrinsic and extrinsic benefits, as well as various formal and informal costs, influenced ISP compliance decisions (Chen, Ramamurthy, & Wen, 2012; Han, Kim, & Kim, 2017; Hu, Xu, Dinev, & Ling, 2011; Li, Zhang, & Sarathy, 2010; Vance & Siponen, 2012). Notably, there are some mixed findings regarding the influences of forma costs in these studies, which warrant further exploration of the cost portion of RCT as applied to ISP compliance. Appendix S1 provides a summary of RCT‐based ISP compliance studies.<sup>1</sup>

Regarding TPB, although its core relationships have been established for ISP compliance, the conceptualisation o its normative‐influence component in this context provides a basis for additional inquiry. With few exceptions (Herath & Rao, 2009), TPB‐based ISP compliance studies have considered normative influences solely in terms of subjective norms. Yet the TPB literature (Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005) offers an expanded view of normative influences that includes both subjective and descriptive norms. Descriptive norms would seem particularly germane to ISP compliance given the evidence that employees establish security routines based on the activities of their co‐workers (Hwang, Kim, Kim, & Kim, 2017). As has been the case with other behaviours (Rivis & Sheeran, 2003; Sheeran & Orbell, 1999), applications of TPB to ISP compliance might achieve greater explanatory power by including descriptive norms. Doing so would also enable a more nuanced understanding of social pressures that drive ISP compliance by delineating the relative influences of descriptive and subjective norms regarding this behaviour.

Another issue related to the application of TPB to ISP compliance is the role of moral considerations. Moral considerations are not formally specified in TPB, but one of the authors of TPB suggested that adding a personal mora norms construct (or a related individual morality construct) to the theory would improve its ability to predict morally relevant behaviours (Ajzen, 1991). Importantly, there is some empirical support for this position (Beck & Ajzen, 1991) Because employees may view ISP compliance decisions as a form of moral dilemma (Myyry et al., 2009), accounting for moral considerations when applying TPB to this context is warranted. Interestingly, prior TPB‐based ISP compliance models have not included an individual morality construct, although other ISP compliance studies (Li, Sarathy, Zhang, & Luo, 2014; Lowry, Posey, Roberts, & Bennett, 2014; Myyry et al., 2009; Sommestad et al., 2014), including some that are RCT-based (Hu et al 2011: Li et al 2010: Vance & Siponen 2012) have found that moral beliefs related to ISP compliance are strong predictors of this behaviour. These findings provide impetus to enhance our understanding of the moral considerations that are relevant to ISP compliance, beyond a simplified moral belief con cept. The organisational literature provides 2 such constructs—organisational deviance and organisational citizenshi behaviours—and we apply both in this study.

In summary, the literature reviewed in this section supports various evaluative beliefs regarding the costs and benefits of ISP compliance as drivers of the behaviour. The literature also supports the integration of RCT with TPB, along with moral considerations, to predict ISP compliance. Notably absent from these studies is the concept of affect. Indeed, the construct conceptualisations and research designs of the studies are limited to the purely cognitive drivers of ISP compliance; yet interdisciplinary research recognises the joint influence of cognitive and affective factors in rational decision‐making (Forgas, 2008; Slovic et al., 2004). The omission of affective influences from the extant RCT‐ and TPB‐based ISP compliance studies (and most behavioural IS security research in general) was a key impetus for our study and forms the basis of our multilevel theoretical model. There are also opportunities to expand the moral considerations and normative influences that relate to ISP compliance, and we address them in this study. Addressing these gaps is further motivated by the emerging evidence that moral and social influences are better predictors of ISP compliance than are factors rooted in purely extrinsic motivations, such as formal sanctions (Sommestad et al., 2014; Son, 2011). In the next section, we detail the theoretical foundations of our model, starting with TPB and then moving on to RCT and the role of affect in our theorising.

## 3 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

## 3.1 | Overview of TPB

Theory of planned behaviour posits that intention towards a given behaviour can be predicted by attitudes towards the behaviour, along with subjective norms and perceived behavioural control related to the behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). Intention is an indicator of an individual's readiness to perform a given behaviour, and there is vast empirica evidence of a strong correspondence between intention and behaviour, especially when there is a short time gap between the stated behavioural intent and actually performing the behaviour (Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen, 2011). The strong intention‐behaviour relationship has also been confirmed for ISP compliance (Lebek et al., 2014; Siponen et al., 2014)

Implicit in TPB is the presumption of an objective to be attained, which in our case is volitional ISP compliance behaviour. We adapt key TPB constructs for our model (see Table 1), including compliance attitude, compliance behav jour, compliance self-efficacy, and subiective norms. Based on our model's multilevel nature, we diverge from prior work by augmenting the purely cognitive view of compliance attitude with a broader conceptualisation that includes both cognitive and affective dimensions. Like compliance attitude, our conceptualisation of compliance behaviour captures both cognitive‐ and affect‐driven behaviours. Our conceptualisation of compliance self‐efficacy is replicated from the literature and serves as a proxy for the perceived behavioural control component of TPB (Ajzen, 1991; Bulgurcu et al. 2010). We also build on the TPB concept of normative influences. Our extension of norms encompasses both subjec tive and descriptive norms in the ISP compliance context.

## 3.2 | Overview of RCT

Rational choice theory posits that individuals determine how they will act by balancing the costs and benefits of a given action (Paternoster & Simpson, 1993). The theory has been particularly effective in explaining deliberate deci sions about deviations and compliance in organisational settings (Paternoster & Pogarsky, 2009). Rational choice the ory is relevant to the organisational compliance context because, just like criminal acts, violations of organisationa policies are subject to sanctions (eg, a reprimand from the organisation for breaking the rules) (Tyler & Blader, 2005)

The assumptions of RCT fit the ISP compliance context well. These assumptions are as follows: (1) People ar rational and self‐interested, (2) people act rationally when making decisions, and (3) people try to increase their pleasure or profit through cost‐benefit analyses (Cornish & Clarke, 1987; Paternoster & Simpson, 1996). Notably, this cal culation favours the maximisation of pleasure: hence, it represents more of a hedonic calculus than a merely extrinsic calculus (Gul, 2009). This key point, which lends itself to the inclusion of affect, has been overlooked in most ISP appli cations of RCT. That is, in rational decision‐making, the individual first recognises alternative courses of action and then contemplates the likely outcomes of each option. Because people have outcome preferences, each result is asso ciated with costs or benefits depending on how much satisfaction/pleasure or dissatisfaction/pain the outcome produces (McCarthy, 2002).

The rationality assumption of RCT includes the idea of bounded rationality, which means that individuals make less‐than‐fully‐rational decisions because of the difficulties of anticipating or calculating all potentially relevant information and alternatives (Elster. 1986). A key implication of this RCT assumption is that rationality is based on an individual's perceptions of costs and benefits and not on externally calculated costs and benefits (McCarthy. 2002) Also consistent with bounded rationality is that individuals are forced to make decisions in the midst of affective influ ences, which may lead to rational choices regarding the same behaviour that vary over time. We adopt this assump tion in our model by accounting for aspects of the individual's affective state that can change from one workday to the next. This perspective fits with the concept of affective rationality used in the decision‐making literatur (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2004).

## 3.3 | Integrating affect into rational decision‐making

The literature broadly delineates 2 ways in which affect influences the rational decision‐making process that is innate to RCT and TPB: indirectly and directly. Affect works indirectly by adjusting the individual's cognitive cost‐benefit appraisal. Here, affect occurs prior to and directs judgments of costs and benefits as they are conceptualised by RCT, such that, for example, someone in a good mood is more apt than someone in a negative mood to perceive amplified benefits for a particular act. This indirect pathway has been confirmed in decision‐making studies (eg Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2004) and in IS contexts involving online reviews (Yin, Bond, & Zhang, 2014), website trust formation (Lowry, Twyman, et al., 2014), and disclosures of personal information (Yu, Hu, & Cheng, 2015). Similarly, in the IS security context, Posey, Bennett, Roberts, and Lowry (2011) found significant relationships between employees' trait negative affectivity, perceived organisational trust (a form of cognitive processing), and computer abuse.

The literature has also supported the direct influence of affect in rational decision‐making (Forgas, 2008; Lowry, Twyman, et al., 2014; Pham, Cohen, Pracejus, & Hughes, 2001; Yu et al., 2015). Notably, this pathway has yet to be verified in the ISP compliance context, although prior IS security research has suggested that affect has a direct influ ence on compliance‐related behaviour (Baskerville, Park, & Kim, 2014). The direct approach recognises the indepen dence of affective and cognitive processes, which is articulated in terms of the 2 processes working in parallel to influence rational judgement and decision‐making (Finucane et al., 2000; Pham et al., 2001). This position supports neuroscientific evidence that shows how affect and cognition each directly contribute to the control of thought and behaviour (Forgas, 2008; Pessoa, 2008)

Although there has been empirical support for the simultaneous, direct influences of affective and cognitive pro cesses on judgement and decision‐making in both IS (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011; Lee, Keil, Smith, & Sarkar, 2017; Lowry, Twyman, et al., 2014) and non‐IS contexts (Pham et al., 2001), many theory‐driven studies of the direct influ ence of affect have omitted cognitive factors. This is also true for RCT‐based ISP compliance research, wherein no studies have investigated the direct influences of affective and cognitive processes, as our review in Appendix S1 shows.

In summary, although the literature has provided some empirical support for the indirect pathway for affect in decisions involving ISP compliance‐related behaviour (eg, employee computer abuse: Posey, Bennett, & Roberts 2011), there is no empirical evidence that the direct pathway for affect in the ISP compliance context can encompass both cognitive and affective factors. Accordingly, a key contribution of our model is to use a variant of the direct approach and to theorise parallel effects of affective influences and cognitive appraisals on ISP compliance attitude and behaviour. Our positioning of affect as working through compliance attitude—as opposed to influencing compliance behaviour directly—aligns with the TPB aspect of our model. Specifically, comprehensive accounts of TPB hav been advanced in which affect influences attitudinal evaluations of a behaviour (Ajzen, 2011), and we draw on this perspective and integrate it with RCT.

Because we draw onTPB and position compliance attitude as a focal construct in our model, integrating joint cog nitive‐affective perspectives into the model requires an explicit recognition of the multidimensional nature of attitude Research in social psychology and other disciplines has confirmed that attitude has both affective and cognitive dimensions (Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005; Eagly & Chaiken, 1993). When its affective dimension is considered, attitude can be conceptualised as a type of affective evaluation; when emphasis is placed on its cognitive dimension, attitude can be understood as a form of reason‐based, cognitive evaluation that is stable over time (Zhang, 2013).

Organisational research has framed the multidimensional view of attitude within job attitude, and studies have considered the state‐like, affective dimension of job attitude, its antecedents, and the behavioural consequences of affect (Ilies, Scott, & Judge, 2006; Judge, Scott, & Ilies, 2006; Judge, Woolf, & Hurst, 2009; Matta, Scott, Colquitt, Koopman, & Passantino, 2017; Rodell & Judge, 2009). Weiss and Cropanzano (1996) referred to these behavioura consequences as affect‐driven behaviour. Supporting the distinct, affective dimension of job attitude, studies have found that the measurement of this construct fluctuates from day to day and that these fluctuations can predict and be predicted by other substantive work variables, such as daily feelings related to the job, episodic workplace events, and task performance (Judge & Kammeyer‐Mueller, 2012). Given that ISP compliance attitudes are a subset of more general iob attitudes. they too can be expected to have an affective dimension and thus exhibit day-to-day variation, as would their behavioural consequences and any affect‐based antecedents. We investigate this issue through multilevel theorisation and an ESM research design, which conceptualises and measures certain affect-basec constructs at the within‐individual level (ie, measured on a daily basis) and other cognitive belief‐based constructs at the between‐individual level (ie, measured one time). The multilevel nature of our model leads to several cross‐leve hypotheses, which we describe in the following section. Because the between‐individual variables are measured once (ie, a “single shot,” between‐individual design), we assess their influence using average daily ratings across individuals for their dependent variable of interest. For the hypothesised within‐individual relationships (ie, single‐level hypotheses), the level of analysis is within, not across, individuals

## 4.1 | Affective states

Regardless of rational calculus, when individuals are in the moment of deciding on or performing an action, the affective states evoked can be quite acute and thus further influence the decision (Kaufman, 1998; Simpson, 2000). An umbrella term for feeling states, affect encompasses both moods and emotions (Lowry, Twyman, et al., 2014; Zhang, 2013). Moods are thought to be less intense than emotions and of longer duration (Lowry, Twyman, et al., 2014; Zhang, 2013). With respect to work life, employees may be in different moods from day to day that influence how they feel about the organisation and certain aspects of their jobs (Rothbard & Wilk, 2011). Certainly, moods can be influenced by workplace interactions and events that occur throughout the day, but moods are generally detached from specific causes or referents (Loiacono & Djamasbi, 2010). In this vein, research has conceptualised moods as exogenous factors that predict job attitudes and decision‐making in both IS and non‐IS contexts (Lee et al., 2017; Loiacono & Djamasbi, 2010).

We similarly include moods as affective states in our model based on the previously described direct theoretica pathway for affect in rational decision‐making (ie, affect working in parallel with cognitive cost‐benefit appraisals) and based on the comprehensive account of TPB in which affect influences attitudinal evaluations of a behaviour (Ajzen, 2011).<sup>3</sup> Following extant research (eg, IIies & Judge, 2002; Rothbard & Wilk, 2011), we conceptualise positive and negative mood states in terms of daily positive affectivity (PA) and negative affectivity (NA).<sup>4</sup>

We predict that daily PA and NA will influence daily compliance attitude (at the within‐individual level). Here, compliance attitude is an affective evaluation of ISP compliance because we focus on the affective dimension of com: pliance attitude. Supporting the influences of PA and NA on daily compliance attitude, when emplovees are in a pos: itive mood, they are more likely to have positive feelings towards the organisation, its goals, and their work (Ilies et al., 2006). Insofar as ISP compliance signifies the organisation's goal of protecting its information assets and is an element of task performance, PA should enable more positive attitudes towards ISP compliance. Conversely, when employees are in a negative mood, they tend to focus on the negative aspects of their jobs (IIies & Judge, 2002; Rothbard & Wilk, 2011). One such aspect is adherence to ISPs, which is often viewed as a nuisance and detriment to productivity (D'Arcy, Herath, & Shoss 2014: Posey, Roberts Lowry, & Hightower, 2014: Puhakainen & Siponen 2010) It follow that NA should evoke more negative feelings towards ISP compliance, resulting in a worsened attitudinal state regarding this behaviour. ## 4.2 | Appraisal of consequences

Rational choice theory holds that people first recognise alternative courses of action and then consider the likely out comes of each. Here, the alternative courses of action are ISP compliance and noncompliance. We use the costs‐ and benefits‐of‐compliance concepts and the costs/risks‐of‐noncompliance concept to represent the perceived consequences of these actions.

Costs of compliance are the overall negative outcomes expected by employees for complying with ISPs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). Prior research has described these costs in terms of the perceived time and effort required for compliance, positing as the lone antecedent work impediment, which is defined as “a detriment to an employee's daily job‐related tasks and activities resulting from compliance with the requirements of the ISP” (Bulgurcu et al., 2010, p. 532). We use the work‐impediment construct to directly represent the costs‐of‐compliance concept. Support for this adaptation stems not only from its theoretical concision but also from Bulgurcu et al. finding that the work‐impediment and costs‐of‐compliance constructs are highly correlated (r = .82). The current study also differs from previous work in that it conceptualises a work impediment as an event that varies from day to day (ie, at the within‐individual level), unlike a stable, cognitive belief. Although stable beliefs regard ing work‐impediment events certainly exist, research has suggested that employees episodically experience impediment events—technology related or otherwise—to their primary work tasks (Ayyagari, Grover, & Purvis, 2011; Gooty, Gavin, Ashkanasy, & Thomas, 2014; Rodell & Judge, 2009). Such security‐related events include experienc ing periodic downtime for required security updates to computers, needing to recall complex passwords and being forced to use 2‐factor authentication instead of a simple password for access to a particular system. Although research has indicated that employees experience such events with relatively high frequency (D'Arcy et al., 2014; Posey et al., 2014), they may not occur every day nor to the same degree every day, which is why we con ceptualise the work‐impediment construct in episodic terms.

Viewing work impediment in episodic terms is consistent with the notion of affective events in the organisational literature. According to affective events theory (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996), certain workplace events are affect laden (affective events) and thereby drive attitudinal states, which in turn produce affect‐driven, episodic work behaviours. Within‐individual‐level research has considered affective events in terms of workplace hassles or stressors and has reported significant negative relationships between daily instances of these events and employees' daily job‐related attitudes (Rodell & Judge, 2009). We likewise posit a negative association between daily work‐impediment events and daily attitudes towards compliance. Given that employees often regard ISP requirements as interfering with their daily job functions, work‐impediment events will likely trigger a negative attitudinal response. Herath and Rao (2009) stated that “if employees believe that complying with pol icies is a hindrance to their day‐to‐day job activity, they are less likely to have favorable views towards security policies” (p. 12). Thus,

H3 Daily work‐impediment events are negatively associated with daily attitude towards ISP compliance.

Turning to the stable, cognitive beliefs that work in conjunction with the state‐based affective factors described in H1 to H3, we focus on how employees evaluate the benefits of compliance and costs/risks of noncompliance. Ben efits of compliance are the overall positive outcomes expected by an employee for complying with the organisation's ISPs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). By contrast, costs/risks of noncompliance are the overall negative outcomes or risk expected by an employee for not complying with the organisation's ISPs (Bulgurcu et al., 2010). These constructs represent the cognition portion of the direct theoretical pathway in which affect and cognition work in parallel withi rational judgement and decision‐making

Both intrinsically and extrinsically oriented benefits are salient in the rational assessment process that considers the consequences of ISP compliance (Hu et al., 2011; Li et al., 2010). We therefore account for both types of benefits within our benefits‐of‐compliance construct. This representation is consistent with descriptions of perceived benefit in RCT literature (McCarthy, 2002) and in certain RCT‐based ISP compliance studies (Li et al., 2010; Vance & Siponen, 2012). Additionally, we emphasise that the benefits of compliance are beliefs that result from a cognitive‐evaluative process and can therefore be expected to remain relatively stable over time. Hence, we model the benefits of com pliance at the between‐individual level.<sup>5</sup>

Bulgurcu et al. (2010) articulated the logic for the relationship between the benefits of compliance and compliance attitude: “If an employee perceives that benefits are derived from compliance … a favorable attitude toward compliance is formed” (p. 530). This relationship is rooted inTPB's expectancy‐value approach to the formation of atti tudes, such that “attitudes develop reasonably from the beliefs people hold about the object of the attitude” (Ajzen, 1991, p. 191). In this manner, when people attribute positive features or consequences to a behaviour, they subsequently acquire a positive attitude towards the behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). We extend this reasoning to our mode and posit that the benefits of compliance will have a positive influence on daily compliance attitudes. Specifically, we posit a cross‐level relationship between the between‐individual benefits of compliance and the within‐individua compliance attitude. Hence,

H4 The benefits of ISP compliance are positively associated with average daily attitude towards ISP compliance.

Although ISP noncompliance is often a passive event, it is likely that employees do consider its consequences. The threat of sanctions is a primary means by which organisations convince employees that noncompliance is costly (Straub & Welke, 1998). The IS security literature provides certain constructs, known as security countermeasures, that centre on the sanction concept and/or have served as proxies for the threat of sanctions (D'Arcy, Hovav, & Galletta, 2009; Straub, 1990). We use one such construct—perceived computer monitoring—to represent the costs/ risks of noncompliance concept in our model. This is based on the rationale that potential offenders perceive risk of sanctions as directly proportional to efforts to monitor and uncover ISP compliance (Kankanhalli, Teo, Tan, & Wei, 2003; Straub, 1990). Following extant work, we conceptualise perceived computer monitoring as a stable, cognitive belief and, accordingly, represent it at the between‐individual level.

Examples of computer monitoring include tracking employees' network and internet use and performing secu rity audits (eg, Urbaczewski & Jessup, 2002). Viewed as an appraisal of the costs/risks of noncompliance, com puter monitoring encourages compliance by increasing the perceived likelihood of getting caught and punished Monitoring practices signal to employees which computing activities are viewed by management as most important and therefore worthy of punishment; thus, the use of monitoring practices is linked to increased ISP compli ance (Vance, Lowry, & Eggett, 2013; Vance, Lowry, & Eggett, 2015). Also, awareness of computer monitoring ha been shown to directly increase accountability for security actions and to increase ISP compliance (Vance et al. 2013; Vance et al., 2015).

Insofar as perceived computer monitoring makes noncompliance less desirable, employees should have a more positive attitude towards compliance. Theory of planned behaviour's expectancy‐value approach to the formation of attitudes once again supports this position, because individuals learn to favour behaviours believed to have positiv consequences and to disfavour those with undesirable consequences (Ajzen, 1991). Accordingly, we predict that if employees perceive greater costs/risks for ISP noncompliance, as per increased perceptions of computer monitoring this will have a positive influence on their daily attitudes towards compliance. Hence,

H5 Perceived computer monitoring is positively associated with average daily attitude towards ISP compliance.

## 4.3 | Compliance attitude

Theory of planned behaviour posits that attitude towards a behaviour is a strong predictor of an individual's intentio to engage in that behaviour, whereby intention is a behavioural action tendency (Ajzen, 1991; Eagly & Chaiken, 1993). The reviewed ISP compliance literature provides evidence that attitude influences compliance behaviour. Our study differs from the extant work in that we model compliance attitude at the within‐individual level, consistent with its affective dimension and our focus on daily compliance attitudes. Hence, we predict that daily compliance attitude will be related to daily compliance behaviour. Although this within‐individual relationship has not been explored in IS security research, organisational studies have found that, on a daily basis, more positive job attitude is related to desir able workplace behaviour (Ilies et al., 2006; Judge et al., 2006). Thus

H6 Daily attitude towards ISP compliance is positively associated with daily ISP compliance behaviour.

## 4.4 | Moral considerations

Prior research has shown that moral beliefs are strong predictors of ISP compliance (Li et al., 2010; Vance & Siponen, 2012). In our context, moral beliefs refer to the degree to which the individual perceives violation of the organisation's ISP as morally unacceptable (Hu et al., 2011). Consistent with the empirical evidence, and because TPB lends itself to the inclusion of an individual morality construct for behaviours that are morally relevant, we predict that moral beliefs will have a direct influence on compliance behaviour.<sup>6</sup> As with the constructs that represent the benefits‐of‐compliance and the costs/risks‐of‐noncompliance concepts, we assume that moral beliefs are relatively stable and the result of cognitive deliberation. For example, employees' moral beliefs regarding a behaviour that violates their organisation's ISP will likely not vary in the short term. Hence, we consider moral beliefs at the between‐individua level in our model.

We also move beyond the simplified concept of moral beliefs in the criminology literature (Paternoster & Simpson, 1996) and broaden the moral considerations that are relevant to ISP compliance to include employees daily organisational citizenship and deviance behaviours. We couch these behaviours as moral considerations because they provide insight into the employee's overall moral compass. Moreover, organisational research has cast these specific behaviours in moral terms (ie, citizenship behaviours are righteous acts, whereas deviance is outside the norms of acceptability) (Ilies, Peng, Savani, & Dimotakis, 2013; Judge et al., 2006) and maintained that they predict aspects of job performance (Podsakoff, Whiting, Podsakoff, & Blume, 2009; Rapp, Bachrach, & Rapp 2013).

Organisational citizenship behaviours (OCBs) are largely discretional and improve the effective functioning of th organisation through acts such as helping and supporting others (Organ, Podsakoff, & MacKenzie, 2006). As OCBs reflect increased employee concern for the organisation's social and psychological functioning, employees wh engage in such behaviours can be viewed as having a stronger sense of personal morals. An apt example of OCB i our context is employee engagement in extra‐role behaviours, which are helpful security behaviours outside their normal job specifications that are not required by an ISP and not encouraged through punishments or reward (Hsu, Shih, Hung, & Lowry, 2015).

Conversely, an employee's engagement in deviant work behaviour can indicate a weaker sense of persona morals. Organisational deviance (ie, deviance) is “voluntary behavior that violates significant organizational norms and in doing so, threatens the well‐being of the organization or its members, or both” (Bennett & Robinson, 2000 p. 349). Because organisational norms consist of basic moral standards rooted in traditional community standards, vio lations of these norms such as spreading rumours, intentionally working slowly, or acting rudely towards a co‐worker, can be seen as immoral or unethical.

Organisational research has provided strong evidence that OCB and deviance behaviours vary from day to da (Ilies et al., 2006; Judge et al., 2006; Rodell & Judge, 2009). Thus, consistent with the other behavioural variables in our model, we model OCB and deviance at the within‐individual level. Again, prior research has suggested that

OCB and deviance are related to aspects of job performance, which includes the execution of role expectations (Podsakoff et al., 2009; Rapp et al., 2013). Hence, we anticipate that these behaviours will be predictive of ISP compliance, which is part and parcel of most employees' daily job functions (D'Arcy et al., 2014). Specifically, because OCB is representative of an employee's concern for the effective functioning of the organisation (Organ et al., 2006), we expect daily OCB to be positively associated with daily compliance. Conversely, because deviance is indicative of a lower regard for the rules and the moral standards of the organisation, we expect daily workplace deviance to be neg atively associated with daily compliance. Thus,

H7 Moral beliefs are positively associated with average daily ISP compliance behaviour.

H8 Daily OCB is positively associated with daily ISP compliance behaviour.

H9 Daily deviance behaviour is negatively associated with daily ISP compliance behaviour.

## 4.5 | Normative influences

Theory of planned behaviour literature has articulated normative influences in terms of subjective and descriptive norms (Ajzen, 2011; Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005), and psychological research has indicated that both types of norm are powerful determinants of human behaviour (Rivis & Sheeran, 2003; Sheeran & Orbell, 1999). In our context, sub jective norms represent the degree to which employees believe other key personnel at their organisation want them to comply with the ISP, whereas descriptive norms are the degree to which employees believe other key personnel at their organisation are actually performing the behaviour prescribed by the ISP (Herath & Rao, 2009).

Descriptive norms have received limited attention in ISP compliance research (Herath & Rao, 2009), which has prompted some IS security scholars to call for their further inclusion in this context (Bauer & Bernroider, 2017; Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Accounting for both subjective and descriptive norms appears particularly germane to ISP compliance, because this behaviour is often viewed by employees as onerous (D'Arcy et al., 2014; Lowry & Moody, 2015; Posey et al., 2014; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). It follows that both the social pressure exerted by co‐workers towards ISP compliance and whether co‐workers themselves engage in this largely unappealing behaviour are salient drivers of compliance. Indeed, there is evidence that employees establish security routines based on the activities of their co‐workers (Hwang et al., 2017). We thus adopt subjective and descriptive norms for our model.

Following Bulgurcu et al. (2010), we conceptualise subjective norms as stable, cognitive beliefs and therefor model them at the between‐individual level. For descriptive norms, we take a different approach and use daily coworker ISP compliance behaviour. Notably, these are not stable beliefs in the cognitive-evaluative sense but rather varying, daily perceptions of co‐worker behaviour; hence, we model them at the within‐individual level. Casting co workers' behaviours as descriptive norms is consistent with the construct's description as “the behaviour of significant others that motivates the person by showing him or her what is the typical or normal thing to do” (Sheeran & Orbell, 1999, p. 2112). In short, we posit that in addition to the influence of stable beliefs on what ought to be done (subjec tive norms), the perceived daily compliance of co‐workers (descriptive norms) will influence employees' daily compli ance behaviour. Therefore,

H10 Subjective norms are positively associated with average daily ISP compliance behaviour.

H11 Perceived daily co‐worker ISP compliance is positively associated with daily ISP compliance behaviour.

## 4.6 | Self‐efficacy

Finally, for nomological completeness with TPB and following several ISP compliance studies that have drawn on TPB (eg, Bulgurcu et al., 2010; Herath & Rao, 2009; Ifinedo, 2012; Siponen et al., 2014), we predict that employees' beliefs regarding their self‐efficacy to comply (at the between‐individual level) will influence their daily compliance behaviour. Hence,

H12 Self‐efficacy is positively associated with average daily ISP compliance behaviour.

## 5 | METHODOLOGY

Because our model incorporates both state‐based affective constructs (at the within‐individual level) and stable, cognitive beliefs (at the between‐individual level), we needed a research design that accounts for the multilevel nature of our theorising. To this end, organisational researchers have used ESM designs in which job attitudes and related con structs are measured once a day for multiple weeks with the objective of measuring fluctuations in these constructs over time (Fisher & To. 2012).7 Such ESM designs account for the state-like nature of affective job attitudes and their correlates by allowing the researcher to statistically separate the within‐individual from the between‐individual vari ance in these constructs.

Participating in ESM studies requires considerable time and commitment, which makes it difficult to find organi sations that are willing to allow their employees to partake in such a study (Fisher & To, 2012). To deal with this issue, some organisational researchers have taken to online recruiting strategies (eg, advertisements on Craigslist and on Facebook's targeted advertisement system) to obtain ESM study participants (Judge et al., 2009; Judge, Simon, Hurst, & Kelley, 2014; Rodell & Judge, 2009; Simon, Hurst, Kelley, & Judge, 2015). Similarly, we recruited participants using postings on Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk) online research tool. Our study targeted employed, computer‐using professionals from the United States. The participants were compensated up to \$32 for their full participation. Appendix S2 describes MTurk, our recruiting procedures, and the screening techniques we used to ensure an appropriate sample for our organisational context, which were guided by recent recommendations for using MTurk in organisational research (Lowry, D'Arcy, Hammer, & Moody, 2016).

After the participants confirmed their acceptance of the study parameters via a consent form, they were directed to a one‐time online survey that contained the measures for the between‐individual (level 2) variables. Next, we used an interval‐contingent ESM design in which participants completed online surveys over a 2‐week period (ie, 1 consecutive workdays), A period of 2 weeks is recommended for organisational ESM studies because it is considered a representative snapshot of an employee's work life (Trougakos, Hideg, Cheng, & Beal, 2014).

Each daily survey contained measures for the within‐individual (level 1) variables, which were randomly presented for each survey to mitigate any pattern of responses or ordering effects. Following the convention of daily‐level ESM studies (eg, Judge et al., 2006; Kammeyer‐Mueller, Judge, & Scott, 2009; Matta et al., 2017), all items on the daily sur veys referred to strictly daily responses (eg, Today at work, … I complied with the requirements of the ISP), and each daily survey explicitly instructed the participants to consider that day only, and not prior days, when responding to the survey items. As described later, we also statistically controlled for prior‐day responses for our level 1 dependent var iables. Each day, we posted a separate MTurk task that contained a link to that day's survey, and participants were instructed to complete the survey upon completion of their workday (eg. All surveys were matched based on MTurk ID, which is included in any MTurk task submission.

We opened our study to 125 participants and initially received full participation. Twenty‐five participants were eliminated based on the exclusion criteria described in Appendix S2 (ie, no knowledge of an ISP or obvious nonconscientious responding). Another 23 participants were excluded because they completed only the one-time sur: vey and/or fewer than 5 daily surveys. Because each participant could complete up to 10 surveys (for a total of 770 daily surveys), this corresponded to a daily survey response rate of 82%. There were no significant differences on any study variables between participants included in the final sample and those excluded due to missing daily surveys Appendix S3 presents the demographic profile of the sample. ## 6 | ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

## 6.1 | Use of HLM

Because we used an ESM design in which days were nested within employees, we performed HLM (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002), which allows researchers to simultaneously analyse variables at multiple levels of analysis in a series of regression equations that account for data nonindependence.<sup>8</sup> For our study, the lower level (level 1) comprised the repeated, daily measures of positive affect, negative affect, work impediment, compliance attitude, OCB, organisational deviance, compliance behaviour, and co‐worker compliance behaviour; and the upper level (level 2) comprised the one‐time measures of benefits of compliance, computer monitoring, moral beliefs, subjective norm and self‐efficacy to comply.

Following Hofmann, Griffin, and Gavin (2000), we centred the level 1 predictor variables at each individual's mean (ie, group mean) and the level 2 predictors at the average mean (ie, grand mean across individuals). Group mean centring removes any between‐individual variance among the variables, meaning that the relationships among th level 1 variables are not confounded by individual differences, such as response tendencies or personality traits. This form of centring alleviates some concerns about common methods variance (CMV).

We first investigated whether multilevel analysis was appropriate by partitioning each level 1 variable into its within- and between-individual components. We did this by running null models in Hl M (regressions with no leve 1 or level 2 predictors) for each of the level 1 variables. As shown in Table 2, a sizable proportion of the tota variance was within‐individual, and the between‐individual differences were significant (P < .01). These results confirm the need for, multilevel analysis because there was, both within- and between-individual variance to be explained in our dependent variables.<sup>9</sup> More specifically, the significant relationships that we found within the within‐individual variance (Table 3) indicate that this variance component was meaningful and systematic rathe than random measurement error.

## 7 | DISCUSSION

Our study was driven by 2 primary research questions, which are addressed in the following sections.

## 7.1 | (RQ1) Do factors associated with affect work in conjunction with cognitive, compliance‐related beliefs in predicting daily attitudes towards ISP compliance?

Based on the results of the study, our answer to RQ1 is yes. The results of our multilevel analysis suggest that a combination of state‐based affective factors and stable, cognitive beliefs work in parallel to predict daily compliance atti tude, which in turn predicts daily compliance behaviour. We found that positive affect and negative affect were associated with daily compliance attitude in positive and negative directions, respectively. We also found that cogni tive beliefs regarding the benefits of compliance and the costs/risks of noncompliance predicted daily compliance atti tude. Together, these findings are consistent with the contemporary view of rational decision‐making and in particula the direct theoretical pathway in which affect and cognition work in parallel towards rational decisions.

As in previous RCT‐based studies of ISP compliance, benefits of compliance had a particularly strong influence in our model. This finding suggests that RCT's cost‐benefit appraisal process is a hedonic calculus that leans towards the maximisation of pleasure, a finding that has emerged in privacy‐related contexts (eg, Hann, Hui, Lee, & Png, 2007), and thereby lends credence to the application of RCT to the ISP context. Additionally, because we conceptualised a work impediment as an affective event, its significant association with daily compliance attitude further supports the role o affect in daily ISP compliance decisions. From an RCT‐TPB perspective, the results indicate that work impediment—a cost of compliance—weakens employee attitude towards compliance. However, as a departure from prior research, our results support a unique, episodic conceptualisation of work‐impediment events and indicate their role as cata lysts of affect‐driven fluctuations in compliance attitude and behaviour. Our findings on this matter also complement emerging research on the potentially deleterious effects of security requirements on ISP compliance (eg, D'Arcy et al., 2014; Lowry & Moody, 2015). Furthermore, the results shed light on the cost portion of RCT as applied to ISP compliance. As noted, prior RCT‐based ISP compliance studies have produced some mixed findings regarding the influence of formal costs (ie, sanctions). Based on our results, it could be that affect‐laden events such as securityrelated work impediments, rather than formal sanctions, are the costs employees incorporate most prominently into their rational decisions regarding ISP compliance, at least on a daily basis

## 7.2 | (RQ2) Do daily attitudes towards ISP compliance, along with moral considerations and normative influences regarding ISP compliance, predict daily ISP compliance behaviour?

Based on our results, our answer to RQ2 is yes. Consistent with the TPB portion of our model, we found that dail compliance attitudes, moral beliefs, organisational deviance, and descriptive norms were each significantly associated with daily compliance behaviour. Notably, neither OCB nor subjective norms was significant in our model (we addres this shortly). Our multilevel model and analysis provided for a test of TPB in a new context of daily attitudes and behaviour. Accordingly, we found that sizeable portions of the total variances of both compliance attitude and behaviour were within‐individual, which indicates the presence of distinct affective and cognitive dimensions in eac construct.

Regarding moral considerations, the results for moral beliefs and organisational deviance support the position that TPB can be expanded with individual morality constructs for behaviours that are morally relevant, and specifically for ISP compliance. The findings also broaden our understanding of the moral considerations relevant to ISP complianc beyond the oft‐studied moral beliefs. We provide evidence that employees' overall engagement in morally question able behaviour (ie, organisational deviance) on a daily basis at work extends to the highly contextualised ISP context.

Turning to normative influences, our results suggest that descriptive norms regarding ISP compliance are salient drivers of the behaviour. In view of the finding for subjective norms, which was not significant, it is possible that employees rely on the actual compliance behaviours of co‐workers for their daily compliance decisions, whereas their general perceptions of whether co‐workers and other important referents want them to comply are not influential (ie, subiective norms at the between-individual level). The evidence that emplovees disregard the opinions of co-workers when it comes to actual daily ISP compliance starkly contrasts with the results of other between‐individual studies which found that subjective norms were strong predictors of employees' ISP compliance (eg, Sommestad et al., 2014). Our findings underscore the importance of our within‐individual approach, because daily ISP compliance appears to be subject to different normative influences than overall assessments of this behaviour, as captured in between‐individual studies. In particular, the affect‐laden nature of daily ISP compliance seems to make this behaviour more prone to transient normative influences (ie, daily co‐worker compliance) than to stable beliefs regarding subjec tive norms.

## 7.3 | Contributions to research, theory, and practice

Beyond the contributions that pertain directly to our research questions, this study makes important contributions to IS security research and theory. First, although affect is an essential component in many forms of judgement and deci: sion‐making, little theoretical progress has been made regarding the role of affect in judgement and decision‐making in the context of ISP compliance. In particular, the transient nature of affect has not received explicit attention in the literature on ISP compliance. Prior IS security studies that have considered affective phenomena have focused on affective traits, which are dispositions or tendencies that are stable over time. This consideration has either been explicit, as in the conceptualisation of negative affectivity as a dispositional trait that predicts computer abuse (Lowry et al., 2015; Posey, Bennett, & Roberts, 2011), or implicit, as in studies based on cross‐sectional, between‐individua research designs that capture affective construct(s) at a single point in time (Boss et al., 2015; Posey, Roberts, & Lowry. 2015). Our study contributes to the development of theory about and methods for identifving the role of state‐based affect in employees' ISP compliance decisions. We advance a multilevel RCT‐TPB–based model that accounts for both within‐ and between‐individual variance in certain constructs. Our finding that both state‐based affective factors and stable, cognitive beliefs influence employees' ISP compliance decisions is noteworthy, because prior research has assumed that employees are more or less prone to ISP compliance based on stable security‐related beliefs, dispositions, and workplace factors. Our results suggest that cognitive factors do not fully explain ISP compliance, and we provide a theoretical explanation for how and why employees' daily ISP compliance attitudes and behav iours can differ from their general tendencies

Additionally, this is one of the first studies to incorporate moods into the ISP compliance literature. Beyond demonstrating the influence of moods alongside cost‐benefit appraisals in the rational decision‐making process, our results highlight the stand-alone influence of moods on ISP compliance. Given that moods are general affective states that are not tied to particular behaviours (Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988), our findings open new avenues for the study of potential determinants of security‐related behaviour—such as general organisational factors—that have traditionally been considered outside the realm of IS security (Willison & Warkentin, 2013)

Methodologically, our ESM design and subsequent HLM analysis are unique to the ISP context and provide one of the few longitudinal investigations in the IS security domain. Scholars have called for within‐individual studies that consider day‐to‐day fluctuations in organisational phenomena to complement between‐individual assessments, even arguing that within‐individual studies are necessary for a full understanding of workplace behaviour (Ilies et al., 2006; Weiss & Beal, 2005). We bring this complementary, within‐individual perspective to the domain of ISP compliance. This is a powerful case in which a novel method generates theoretical insights not available with between‐individua designs. We are confident that this method will lead to further insights in IS security research (or broader IS research)

In short, an. ESM design combined with a multilevel analysis is ideal for, situations, in which the phenomena involved are not stagnant, but tend to fluctuate. These methods might be applied also to workplace disruptions due to information technology (IT) failure, the unfolding of massive security breaches, or even changes in workplace morale after major IT outsourcing. We also believe that ESM could be particularly useful for research involving human‐computer interaction and system design, which would inform researchers about how a design is meeting users needs over time. Articles by Fisher and To (2012) and Reis and Gable (2000) shed further light on the power and possibilities of ESM in organisational contexts as well as on how to design highly reliable and valid ESM studies.

This study has important practical implications for organisations. Perhaps the most obvious is that compliance attitude and behaviour fluctuate on a day‐to‐day basis. Hence, although employees display characteristic levels of ISP compliance over time (as found in between‐individual measures that presumably reflect aggregations or trends), there also exist discrete episodes of ISP compliance that may not coincide with prior behavioural trends. Given that a single instance of ISP noncompliance can have dire conseguences for organisations, it is incumbent upon them to predict these day‐to‐day fluctuations, especially to the extent that organisations introduce negative shock events that are affect laden (eg, lavoffs, restructuring and mergers, decreases in health care benefits. bad public news, and exec: utive management scandals). These events could be inadvertent security threats that require further security triage Our results indicate that variations in positive and negative mood states and security‐related work‐impediment events are somewhat influential in this regard. Organisations would therefore be well advised to focus on mood management strategies, such as fostering a positive work environment and offering a pleasant physical work environment, as a means of enhancing IS security efforts. Moreover, our results suggest that organisational efforts to promote ISP com pliance should move beyond the IS department and include other functional areas, such as human resources. Based on the evidence that work‐impediment events produce a negative attitudinal response, joint IS–human resources efforts should strive to ensure that ISP requirements do not stifle employees' performance or create negative attitudes. To mitigate work‐impediment events, organisations can consider tailoring ISPs to contextual aspects of the position organisation, or industry.

Our results suggest that organisations must make employees cognisant of the benefits of ISP compliance. Extrin sic benefits can be enhanced by making compliance a formal, rewardable aspect of an employee's job; intrinsic benefits may not be as amenable to intervention. but emphasising the moral relevance of ISP compliance may raise its intrinsic value. For instance, security education, training, and awareness programs should include a module on th moral responsibilities pertinent to situations in which an employee is faced with noncompliance opportunities. In addi tion to clearly articulating that computing activities are monitored by the organisation, security education, training, and awareness programs and the ISPs that underlie them should stress the negative effects of ISP violations on fellow employees, the organisation, and society as a whole, thereby accentuating the costs of noncompliance. Finally, organisations should provide employees with the knowledge and technical skills necessary for compliance with ISPs, thu enriching their self-efficacy in this domain

Organisations also need to be aware that employees' deviant workplace behaviours have ramifications for ISP compliance. Again, this speaks to the value of the IS working in tandem with other functional areas to address ISP non compliance. Additionally, our finding that employees look to their co‐workers' ISP compliance behaviours as socia cues suggests that organisations must make a concerted effort to publicly recognise compliant behaviour throughout the organisation, Such efforts can include formal acknowledgements of top security performers, either individually o within work units.

## 7.4 | Limitations and directions for future research

This study has certain limitations that suggest future research opportunities. The first is the inability of our model to affirm full causality because the level 1 variables were collected each day at the same time. Although it would have been possible to have respondents complete multiple surveys per day (at different times and with different constructs being measured) and thereby achieve temporal separation for some or all of the level 1 variables, we decided agains this option for pragmatic reasons. Such a design is extremely demanding for participants and is thus not recommended for ESM studies that exceed 1 week (Fisher & To, 2012). Instead, we used a common ESM approach of using end‐of workday measures (eg, Ilies et al., 2006; Judge et al., 2006; Kammeyer‐Mueller et al., 2009). The consistency of ou findings with RCT and TPB lends credence to the sequencing of our variables, as does our supplemental lagged analysis, which partially infers causality. Nonetheless, future studies with alternative designs are needed to verify th full causal chain of our model

A related limitation is the ever-present threat of CMV when dealing with behavioural surveys. Statistical tests revealed that CMV was not a serious concern at level 1 (see Appendix S5), and our ESM design also weakened th potential for CMV. Common methods variance is less of an issue when constructs are measured over time (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). Additionally, as described, our group mean centring of the level 1 variables rules out many of the usual CMV explanations, such as personality confounds, mood differences, and response tendencies In fact, mood states were valid sources of variability that explained other constructs in our study. For the between individual analyses, the predictor and criterion variables were reported on different surveys at different times, thereb reducing the potential for CMV. These points aside, our research would be strengthened by gathering measures of ou independent and dependent variables from different sources.

It is also important to point out that although the amount of variance explained for compliance attitude and behaviour was somewhat modest, these were daily measures that fluctuated over the course of the study. Hence, these values should not be interpreted in the same manner as $R ^ { 2 }$ values for stable constructs (ie, average values in between‐individual studies). Extrapolating our findings over a longer period in which employees experience additiona instances of the affective events and states captured by our model would make the results increasingly meaningful. This is akin to Abelson paradox (Abelson, 1985), in which he describes situations (eg, in baseball, the percentage of variance in any single batting performance explained by batting skill is about one-third of 1%) for which a smal amount of explained variance becomes meaningful over time. In the context of IS security, forensic investigations of several large‐scale data breaches have traced their roots to an amalgamation of minor and seemingly innocuous (at the time) insecure employee behaviours that were left unchecked and continued to escalate (Willison & Warkentin 2013). Similarly, we regard the explained variance in daily compliance attitude and behaviour as instrumental for a longer‐term understanding of employees' ISP compliance decisions

Finally, certain findings run counter to our hypothesising. This first is subiective norms. Bevond the explana tion that subjective norms are not as salient as descriptive norms in the affect‐laden context of daily ISP compliance, the nonsignificance of subjective norms could be driven by our research design. Our design measured subjective norms and compliance behaviour on different occasions using different instruments, which may have enabled a more accurate assessment of this relationship compared to the results of prior between‐individual stud ies. A potential explanation of the nonsignificance of OCB is that OCBs deplete cognitive resources and put employees in a weakened state that is not conducive to ISP compliance. In view of the result for deviance, which was significant, it could be that deviance behaviours are cathartic and do not deplete cognitive resources to the same degree as OCBs, thereby leaving employees with the stamina to actively disregard ISP requirements. Likewise, it takes more effort to comply with ISPs than to ignore them. Further research is needed to verify the accu racy of these conjectures and whether our findings regarding the moral considerations and normative influences are unique to this study.

## 7.5 | Conclusion

Even with ongoing efforts to combat employees' noncompliant computing behaviours, the problem of deliberate ISP violations persists. One reason for this predicament is that ISP compliance is subject to affective influences that vary from day to day. We explored the concept of affective rationality in the ISP context by proposing and testing a mul tilevel RCT‐TPB–based model that includes cognitive and affective antecedents to daily compliance attitude and behaviour, along with moral considerations and normative influences. Complementing prior work that has found employees to be more or less prone to ISP compliance based on stable beliefs and other invariant factors, we found that ISP compliance was predicted by a combination of stable beliefs with state‐based affective factors and daily workplace events and behaviours. A key implication of this study is that employees' attitudes towards ISP compliance and ISP compliance behaviour are not stagnant, We hope this study paves the way for continued exploration of affective phenomena in the IS security domain and spurs more robust theoretical and methodological approaches that account for the various manifestations of affect.
