# Bullying on the net: adverse behavior on e-mail and its impact

Yehuda Baruch<sup>\*</sup>

University of East Anglia, School of Management, Norwich NR4 7TJ, UK

Accepted 2 February 2004

Available online 22 July 2004

## Abstract

A study was conducted into the effect of bullying at work, focusing on the use of e-mail, in a large Multi-National Corporation. A considerable level of bullying was identified in both e-mail and other communication media, leading to negative outcomes, regardless of the media utilized. Bullying was associated with anxiety and intent to leave the organization, and negatively associated with job satisfaction and performance. These results manifest the negative psychological impact of bullying and expand current understanding of the drawbacks associated with the misuse and abuse of IT in an organizational context.

Keywords: Bullying; E-mail; Adverse behavior; Information systems; Computer-mediated communication

Most of the literature in organizational psychology and management has focused on its positive side. In particular, conceptual frameworks have been developed, emphasizing positive elements in terms of both attitudes (satisfaction, commitment, and involvement) and behavior (performance and citizenship). Studies of negative behavior (such as misconduct and aggression) have been the exception. However, in the late 1990s a growing literature on adverse behavior in the organizational context emerged. Typically, one of the characteristics of the early stages was a lack of a clear definition to explain the phenomenon. ‘Deviant behavior’ [82]; ‘Misbehavior in organizations’ [94,84]; ‘Antisocial behavior’ [32]; ‘Dysfunctional behavior’ [36]; ‘Mistreatment in organizations and ‘Incivility’ [3] were some of the many taxonomies used to reflect negative, antisocial, and sometimes destructive behavior of members of organizations towards colleagues, subordinates, and even managers. One such construct is bullying, i.e., verbal aggressive behavior [1].

It occurs in communication and some media are more vulnerable than others. The introduction of new communication technologies to the workplace was not expected to create a new route for bullying; indeed, most studies focused on their positive impact: research on negative aspects is sparse [51]. Nevertheless, misuse and even abuse of IT systems for bullying is a real life phenomenon, as suggested in a UK study [73]. However, no study has attempted to determine whether e-mail communication reduced, increased, or just altered the nature of bullying. Glendinning [33] suggested that workplace bullying was an issue that had a great effect on organizational dynamics, especially in the current turbulent business environment [31], where organizations are electronically networked, both internally and externally. Indeed, two recent papers published in Information & Management [57,75] did not identify it as a key issue in the literature.

## 1. Theoretical model development

The theoretical base for understanding adverse behavior and its outcomes within organizational context can be traced back to Gouldner’s norms of reciprocity [34].

Bullying is verbal aggressive behavior which inflicts pain, an ‘‘aggressive behavior arising from the deliberate intent to cause physical or psychological distress to others’’ [79]. The alleged bully may not always be aware of its consequences. It can be deliberate or unconscious [15], and if unintentional, it may be merely uncivil (e.g., escalation in e-mail misuse due to ignorance [87]). Bullying is distinct from other forms of aggressive behavior [52], although its definition may be idiosynchratic [61]. Some definitions are too general: ‘‘the systematic abuse of power’’ [88] or ‘‘intention to inflict pain’’ [15]. The intention is a factor in both aggressive and non-aggressive communication [39]. Others have applied more legally oriented definitions such as ‘‘a repeated aggression, verbal, psychological or physical, conducted by an individual or group against others’’ [20]. UNISON’ definition [93], cited by [80], is ‘‘Offensive, intimidating, malicious, insulting or humiliating behavior, abuse of power or authority which attempts to undermine an individual or group of employees and which may cause them to suffer stress.’’

Some have argued that bullying occurs only when systematically applied [58,22], while others [81] referred to sporadic cases. Different explanations about bullying cause a significant difference in reported its level [85]. It is even a matter of geography and linguistics: the term is used mostly in the UK and Scandinavia [24], but both bullying and harassment may be used in the US, while it is also termed ‘Mobbing’ in Europe.

Work bullying ‘verbal’ rather than ‘physical’, ‘active’ rather than ‘passive’ [14], and both ‘direct and ‘indirect’ [69]. It is less visible but more prevalent than physical violence in organizational contexts [35]. Bullying through malicious or defamatory messages to others is of particular relevance here.

1.1. Information technology, communication, and organizations

Information technology (IT) has brought changes matched only by the industrial revolution, particularly for on management and organizational life. Much of the literature has focused on its performance enhancing benefits [19,72].

## 1.1.1. E-mail

Cost-effective and user-friendly, e-mail has become one of the primary means of communication for white collar employees [48]. Two conflicting factors characterize organizational use of e-mail [62]: positive— social closeness (between managers and subordinates) and negative—de-personalization.

Richness of a medium is an important factor in determining its effectiveness. Among the various communication media, e-mail may be placed in the middle range of richness, complexity, and formality. It is easily accessed, quick in response, and allows unsynchronized two-way conversation. These features can cause communication problems [44]. While being less formal, it is still official, recordable, and all interactions can be retrieved, printed, forwarded, and reused. Records of e-mail communication might be used against the employer in legal disputes [97]. This should deter the sensible sender from using this medium for bullying and other malicious practices. Nevertheless, reporting adverse behavior such as harassment does not always end with better work outcome [10].

E-mail communication contains neither the tone of the spoken word nor eye contact. Sproull and Kiesler [90] pointed out the risks of misunderstanding and distortion of messages meaning. This lack of social context cues in e-mails, makes this medium vulnerable to misunderstanding and misinterpretation. Little legislation has been developed in relation to its handling by employers [49]. In addition, immediacy or rapid response might prompt a shoot-from-the-hip response. Messages considered by the sender as innocent humor can trigger an escalating spiral exchange of e-mail bullying [5]. This follows Anderson and Pearson’s framework of mistreatment and its spiral acceleration.

Several studies have explored e-mail as a unique and effective communication medium [60].

Communication creates and develops relationships between people, and these apply to a variety of contextual levels—the individual, team [64], organization, societal levels [9].

## 1.1.2. Existence of bullying in e-mail

Rational, legal minded thinking would conclude that e-mail communication should be free from bullying because it is documented and can serve as evidence in case of doubt [6]. However, the lack of ‘netiquette’ (net etiquette) may cause people of different views and backgrounds to misinterpret other’s communication (see [2]). Sparrow and Daniels [89] analyzed the expected impact of a knowledge intensive work environment and argue that intensive IT environments may have adverse effects on the well-being of employees. This leads to:

Hypothesis 1. Bullying will exist on e-mail systems in a similar way and extent that exists in other communication media.

## 1.2. Bullying at work

The issue of workplace bullying has resulted in involvement of legal procedures (e.g., a UK psychiatric injury case, ruled to be caused directly by bullying [4,21]). Flame e-mail can cause an increase in stress related illness and harassment [74,96]. Bullying and harassment are not necessarily similar, but bullying might lead to, or be associated with, harassment, which has severe negative impact on people’s attitudes and behavior [28].

Bullying is an obstacle to effectiveness. Indirect aggression causes a high level of anxiety and stress, although organizational justice partially mediated the effect of abusive supervision on anxiety [92]. Stress and anxiety lead to low performance and high turnover [16], and general counterproductive work behavior [77]. Workplace violence has been characterized in three dimensions: (1) expressions of hostility, (2) obstructionism, and (3) overt aggression [70]. Bullying is a clear manifestation of workplace incivility [76]. A study of the UK labor market [41], found that 24.7% had been bullied within the last 5 years. Thus:

Hypothesis 2. E-mail bullying will lead to negative work-related outcomes in a similar way to the impact of bullying via other media of communication.

## 1.2.1. The impact of bullying on job attitudes, emotions, and behaviors

The model of turnover [55,56] examined the impact of negative emotions on organizational perception and satisfaction, resulting in intent to leave. Other negative outcomes are absenteeism [71], turnover [65], negatively associated with job satisfaction [50,68].

Hochschild’s pioneering studies [40] introduced the issue of psychological emotions at the workplace as a subject for study. Fineman [26] argued that emotions at work depended on the employee’s need to suppress private feelings as organizational and professional norms. However, feelings and expressions, which are irrelevant for the formal employee role, can penetrate to places that have little to do with emotions [13] such as e-mail. Office politics provide an example of emotions in the work environment. Clarke [17] suggested a framework to distinguish between motives and level of competence in organizational politics. E-mail abuse may result from negative motives. The office is losing its reputation of being hazard free [66], with stress related issues held responsible for the shift.

Emotions at work were neglected by organizational psychologists [7], but interest in the topic is increasing [27]. Others [12] specifically refer to work place aggression as negatively influencing performance.

Many studies and theoretical frameworks have discussed the anticipated effects of stress and anxiety [11]. Stress leads to anxiety [29], which can be a response to an actual situation or a perceived threat. It therefore ‘‘may involve complex chains of behavior and vicious cycle phenomena’’ [38]. Excessive levels of anxiety as a result of bullying will induce poor job attitudes and performance.

Hypothesis 3(a). Bullying on the e-mail will be negatively associated with job satisfaction and posi tively associated with anxiety and intent to leave.

Hypothesis 3(b). Such association will mediate further negative outcomes in terms of performance, and absenteeism.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Sample

A questionnaire was sent as an attachment to an email message to users at the UK subsidiary of a large USA-based Multi-National Corporation (in the Fortune 500 list). The target population consisted of most of the company’s office-based workforce, including managers, professional, and support staff. Altogether the list included some 1600 employees. The questionnaire was accompanied by an explanation of the research aim, a request for a response, and an affirmation of confidentiality. One day before the e-mail was sent from the UK university, an e-mail message from the HR director of the company asked employees to respond, and assured them that the company will not read any responses. Respondents could send a ‘reply’ e-mail directly to the researcher at the university, but were allowed to respond through regular mail or fax (see guidelines for researching sensitive topics [54]). A follow up email was sent out 2 weeks later.

## 2.2. Instruments

The respondents were asked to report a set of single item measures for the following research variables: job satisfaction, performance, anxiety, absenteeism, and intent to leave, plus explanation of the meaning of bullying (see Appendix A). Respondents were asked if they had experienced bullying at work, and if so, whether it was conveyed via the e-mail system. Six hundred and forty-nine participants replied: a response rate of 44%, a level considered sufficient [8,83]. Those who had experienced bullying via e-mail were asked to respond to a wider set of questions, including several multiple-item measures already validated in the literature [37,46]. Sixty people did so, along with 19 bullied by other means, responding to the full questionnaire (these 79 employees were termed the sub-sample).

## 2.3. Measures

Several questions utilized a single-item measure to evaluate the main research variables for the entire sample. The use of single-item measures in the behavioral sciences is generally discouraged, but this should not be perceived as a ‘fatal error’ [95]. Moreover, our unique setting enabled further validation of some single item measures by comparing them to the comprehensive questions in the sub-sample.

In addition, single item measures were successfully used to evaluate non-standard variables such as ‘perceived probability that effort leads to performance’ [42]. Of course, the use of single item measures is not new. Those for attitudes such as antecedents of organizational commitment [67] and job satisfaction were extensively applied in the past and published in leading journals [30]. A meta-analysis investigated 17 studies, and found a considerably high correlation between the single measures and other measures [95]. The construct validity of single-item measures could be higher than that of multiple-item measures [86], and while multi-item measures are preferred for measuring a complex construct, single measures are acceptable for homogeneous constructs [59].

## 3.2. Association among the research variables

## 3.2.1. Validating the single measure with multiple item measures

The questionnaire for people who were bullied included a wider set of multiple-item, previously validated measures. The correlation of 0.60 between the single item measure for job satisfaction and a four items measure for the same variable (Cronbach alpha ¼ 0.75; N ¼ 75), and the correlation of 0.73 between the single item measure for ‘intent to leave and a four items measure for the same variable (Cronbach alpha ¼ 0.89; N ¼ 75) strongly supported the validity of the single item application. Subsequently, the single-item measures were utilized. Tables 3 (correlations) and 4 (regression model) depict the results of the impact of bullying.

## 3.2.2. Bullying impact on job attitudes and behaviors

To study the possible impact, a structural equation model (SEM) analysis was also conducted. Path analysis (LISREL procedure) was employed. It provided a chi-square value and three additional indices that assessed the fit of path models, the goodnessof-fit index (GFI), the adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI), and the comparative fit index (CFI) [43,47].

The regression analysis suggests that bullying is a significant factor (just after job satisfaction) as an antecedent of intention to leave. However, no significant impact was identified for performance. There may be an indirect path to $\mathbf { i t } ,$ as is supported by both the correlation data and the path analysis. The SEM (as shown in Fig. 2) produced a very high fit with the suggested model. All in all, these results strongly support Hypothesis 3, that bullying on the e-mail is negatively associated with desired individual and organizational outcomes.

## 4. Discussion and conclusions

The results expand our understanding of the bullying phenomenon in the area of IT and computermediated communication. They show the existence of a significant, negative relationship between adverse behavior at work and job and organizational attitudes. Bullying at work, while not new, was found in the novel media of e-mail, and resulted in similar negative effects to those from conventional bullying with the exception that the gender issue was not found to be a significant factor, as Lee [53] argue.

These findings send an alarm signal to managers in organizations. The implications of the study are of importance, since the use of e-mail systems is rising and for some flexible work arrangements, e-mail is essential [18,78,91].

It has been argued that computer-mediated communication might weaken social influence, and that ‘social anonymity’ is a strong indication of the depersonalizing caused by technology [45]. This may be that due to lack of ability to transmit latent elements of communication (such as body language), e-mail communication may be wrongly interpreted.

To the many possible antecedents of bullying, this study has added e-mail as a new media that provides a different type of bullying. It allows an additional route of abusive behavior. It is a less personalized system, and its excessive use might add to a feeling of social isolation. Thus, e-mail might become an enabler of vicious behavior.

## 4.1. Managerial implications

The negative outcomes of bullying are significant for both individuals and their employers. It was shown to have significant impact on an employee’s intention to leave. It was also highly correlated with negative job attitudes and behaviors. Managers should realize that e-mail allows a wider population to exert abusive behavior. Faragher [25] found that there were few differences between workers, supervisors, middle, or senior managers relating to their experience of self-reported bullying, in line with the present study. Lack of awareness of the phenomenon raises costs due to employee reduction in effectiveness and possible litigation. Stating and distributing organizational policy is essential. Bullying causes stress, which is a significant health hazard. Results from several empirical studies suggest that many victims of bullying even suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder [23].

## 4.2. Limitations

The contribution of this study should be considered in light of its limitations. One is that it was conducted in one company only. Another is the use of single-item measure. Two additional limitations result from the use of surveys: reliance on partial response rate and the issue of common method variance. However, when studying sensitive issues such as bullying within organizational context, any attempt to use additional sources would hamper the validity and feasibility of the study.

Yehuda Baruch is a Professor of Management at UEA Norwich UK and formerly a Visiting Associate Professor at the University of Texas at Arlington, USA and a visiting Research Fellow at London Business School. He holds a B.Sc. in Electronic Engineering (Ben Gurion, Israel), M.Sc. and D.Sc. in Management and Behavioral Sciences (The Technion, Israel). After being a project manager in

high technology industry he encounter career in the academia. His research interests are Careers, Strategic and Global HRM, and technology impact on management. He published more than 60 papers in these fields in a number of journals, including Human Relations, Human Resource Management, Organizational Dynamics, Journal of Vocational Behavior, and Organization Studies and a book titled Managing Career: Theory and Practice. Professor Baruch is the Editor of Career Development International and the Chair for the Careers Division of the Academy of Management 2004.
