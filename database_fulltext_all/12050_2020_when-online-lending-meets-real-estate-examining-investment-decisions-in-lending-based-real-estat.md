Abstract. In lending-based real estate crowdfunding, borrowers are required to pledge their housing properties as collateral to secure the loans. This nascent practice differs from ordinary peer-to-peer lending in that lenders, to make sound investment decisions, need to process additional information other than basic loan attributes. We examine how lender behavior of investing in real-estate-secured loans is shaped by information that is particularly relevant in such an emerging market. We collect and analyze the data from a large lending-based real estate crowdfunding platform, where each loan is secured by either a mortgage (a mortgage-secured or MS loan) or a borrower's own house (a house-secured or HS loan). Our analysis reveals that lender decisions of how fast to invest and how much to invest are influenced by both on-platform and off-platform information. For on-platform information, we find that lenders as a whole prefer HS loans to MS loans, as reflected in quicker and larger lending transactions. Experienced lenders tend to invest more aggressively, in both time and amount, but exhibit a weaker preference for HS loans as compared with their inexperienced counterparts. As to off-platform information, our results show that a rise in housing prices is associated with quicker investment decisions, and this association is found to be even stronger on HS loans. Further, when stock market volatility is large, lenders tend to slow down their investment behavior; however, we find such a tendency weaker on MS loans. This research contributes to the literature by establishing relationships between crowdfunding activities, housing prices and stock market performance. Our findings also provide implications for managers and platform designers who desire to stimulate and leverage the fundraising momentum.

## 1. Introduction

The rise of financial technologies has transformed how fundraisers and funders interact with each other (Burtch et al. 2013). Enabled by various online platforms, fundraisers nowadays have easy access to reach a large funder base to seek money for different purposes. A recent application, termed real estate crowdfunding (RECF), has been developed to facilitate fundraising for projects and loans that involve real estate, including residential, commercial, or industrial properties (Schweizer and Zhou 2017). According to industry reports, RECF amounted to a \$3.5 billion industry in 2016 (Massolution 2015), and the market size was projected to reach \$250 billion by 2020 (Delia 2018). This nascent practice currently takes two different forms: (equity-based)

syndicated investments in real estate development projects and (lending-based) real estate secured P2P loans (P2PMarketData 2019). In both forms, the value of the involved real estate has an important implication for investors. In equity-based RECF, the real estate value determines one's financial returns, whereas in lending-based RECF, the real estate value alleviates one's investment risk.

Meanwhile, the increased number of defaulted loans over time has gradually overshadowed the promise we have once seen from online P2P lending. LendingClub, the largest P2P lending platform in the world, reported that its borrower default rate has increased by 86% from 2010 to 2017 (Lee et al. 2017). The issue has escalated into a national P2P lending crisis in China. To attract funding under extreme competition, most platforms guaranteed that they would repay the loans even in an event of borrower default. $^{1}$ Due to the lack of a unified credit rating system (Feng 2018), however, borrowers without good repayment ability were still able to get funded in the P2P lending market, resulting in a high borrower default rate (Jao 2019). In this situation, small- to medium-sized platforms without a large reserve fund were vulnerable and thus faced insolvency to make repayments to their lenders. Industry experts anticipated that $70\%$ of the platforms that were operating at the end of 2018 would cease their operation by 2019 after the government's massive crackdown (Liu 2019).

Besides the lack of a well-functioned credit system and appropriate regulations in place, China's recent turmoil could also be attributable to the unsecured nature of the P2P lending practice. Platforms commonly offer unsecured loans, meaning that platforms (in the case of China) and lenders (in the case of the United States) would suffer financial losses when borrowers fail to repay. As borrower default rate may continue to rise (Shoffman 2018), lending-based real estate crowdfunding, which involves the use of real estate to secure P2P loans, has emerged as a new phenomenon and a promising alternative with lower risk. Leading websites offering such a form of secured lending include RealtyMogul and Fundthatflip in the United States and LandlordInvest in the United Kingdom. These platforms require borrowers to pledge their housing properties as collateral; in an event of default, platforms will claim and sell the collateral to recoup lender losses. Although borrowers who possess real estate are likely to acquire credits from traditional financing channels (e.g., banks), lending-based RECF is expected to hold a multitude of advantages, such as ubiquitous access, less restrictive eligibility, quicker turn-around time for approval, and oftentimes more competitive interest rates, as borrowers have experienced from ordinary P2P lending (Pritchard 2019).

From a lender's perspective, lending-based RECF differs from ordinary P2P lending in two main aspects. First, in addition to basic loan attributes (e.g., amount requested and interest rate), lenders, to make sound investment decisions, also need to evaluate the housing property that is used as collateral. This additional information, such as the ownership status of the collateralized properties (i.e., with or without a mortgage), plays an important role in determining loan quality, which, in turn, may influence lenders' decision making. For instance, lenders are likely to associate a higher level of risk with a loan secured by a mortgage, and thus exhibit a preference for a loan secured by a borrower's own house (without a mortgage). Further, most lending-based RECF platforms offer little information about borrowers' creditworthiness (e.g., credit ratings), making the role of collateral information more pronounced in this market. $^{2}$

Second, lenders who participate in lending-based RECF may pay special attention to overall economic conditions, such as the real estate market and the stock market. Because real estate is pledged as collateral, changes in overall housing prices affect the value of borrowers' housing properties, which, in turn, may influence loan risk perceived by potential lenders. Further, the performance of the housing market closely impacts borrowers' accessibility to traditional financing products (e.g., bank loans) and hence affects their use of crowdfunding as an alternative financing source (Kim and Hann 2019). Such dynamics may also influence investment behavior indirectly through the supply side of the crowdfunding market as the number of available loan listings changes. On the other hand, the conditions of the stock market may also influence the demand side of the crowdfunding market. Compared with lending-based RECF in which returns are relatively stable (i.e., with fixed interest rates), the stock market is highly volatile and thus offers arbitrage opportunities for investors. Because crowdfunding serves as an investment alternative (Hornuf and Neuenkirch 2017), the overall condition of the stock market (as a traditional investing channel) is expected to have an impact on lenders' investment behavior through the substitution effect between the two options.

Motivated by those notable features, this research aims to investigate how lenders' investment behavior is shaped by both the on-platform and off-platform information in such an emerging market. For the on-platform information, we are particularly interested in the impact of collateral characteristics, with a focus on the ownership status of the collateralized properties (i.e., secured by a mortgage or a borrower's house). For the off-platform information, we consider a few macro-level indices that can characterize economic conditions, including housing prices, stock market returns, and stock market volatility. We examine individual lenders' decisions of how fast to invest and how much to invest at the transactional level. Understanding lenders' decision making in both temporal and amount dimensions have useful implications for platform managers and designers who desire to stimulate and leverage the momentum of funding (Li and Duan 2016) through maneuvering the power of the lender herd (Ho et al. 2019).

Our research context is a leading lending-based RECF platform in China. When a borrower creates a new loan, she is required to indicate the type of property to be collateralized, that is, whether the loan will be secured by her own house (hence, a house-secured or an HS loan) or a house with mortgage (hence, a mortgage-secured or an MS loan). Our empirical analysis proceeds as follows. First, we employ the propensity score matching approach to control for the imbalance in the distribution of observable covariates between HS and MS loans. Next, we use the matched sample to conduct our time-to-invest and amount-to-invest analyses, accounting for unobserved heterogeneity among lenders. Our analyses reveal that lender decisions of how fast to invest and how much to invest are influenced by both the on-platform and off-platform information. For the on-platform information, our results show that lenders as a whole prefer HS loans (to MS loans), as reflected in quicker and larger lending transactions. Experienced lenders tend to invest more aggressively, in both time and amount, but exhibit a weaker preference for HS loans as compared with their inexperienced counterparts. As to off-platform information, our results show that a rise in housing prices is associated with quicker lending decisions, and this association is found to be even stronger on HS loans. Further, when stock market volatility is large, lenders tend to slow down their investments; however, such a tendency is attenuated on MS loans.

Our work makes several contributions to the extant research. We extend crowdfunding literature by incorporating the role of various off-platform information into the decision-making mechanism that underlies lenders' investment behavior. There are few prior studies that consider the impact of housing prices and primarily use it as a proxy for credit availability from traditional financing sources. Butler et al. (2016) examine whether access to credit from bank affects requested interest rates on Prosper. Kim and Hann (2019) investigate how access to bank loans that are based on housing collateral influences entrepreneurs' crowdfunding use on Kickstarter. Unlike these two papers that look into fundraisers' behavior, our work focuses on the role of housing prices from a lender's perspective. Further, complementing to the two prior studies that use aggregated regional-level data (e.g., a metropolitan statistical area), our analyses are conducted at the more granular, loan level. These two distinctions allow us to uncover new insights into individuals' decision making in crowdfunding activities.

In this research, we also consider the impact of stock market performance, another important yet underexplored macro-level factor that can characterize the overall economic conditions. Much of the prior work neglects the potential effect of stock market conditions on crowdfunding activities. One exception we are aware of is Hornuf and Neuenkirch (2017), who find that fluctuations in stock prices are positively associated with crowd funders' willingness to pay on an equity-based crowdfunding platform. We further show that stock market volatility and returns both serve as important influencers of investment behavior in lending-based RECF markets.

Extending to a majority of extant research examining the decision of how much to invest, we empirically investigate lenders' decision of how fast to invest. Given the importance of the timing of funding transactions (Burtch et al. 2011), we attempt to contribute to this research stream by examining investment behavior from a temporal perspective. Zhang and Liu (2012) examine borrower default by relating the time that passes before default (if any) to various loan covariates, and conclude that well-funded loans are less likely to default. Different from this paper, which analyzes time-to-default outcomes at the loan level, we focus on time-to-invest decisions at the transaction level. The associated findings help advance our knowledge in lender behavior specifically in a temporal dimension.

This research is, to the best of our knowledge, one of the first few studies that examine lending-based real estate crowdfunding. Motivated by the contextual uniqueness discussed earlier, we examine how crowdfunding activities are affected by both on-platform and off-platform (macro-level) information. We find evidence that lenders do incorporate collateral characteristics into their decision making: they prefer HS loans to MS loans, as demonstrated in quicker and larger lending transactions made to the former. We also identify several moderating effects on lenders' preference in the collateral (loan) type. In sum, we make novel contributions by establishing relationships between crowdfunding activities, housing prices, and stock market performance.

## 2. Research Background

## 2.1. Lending-Based Real Estate Crowdfunding

Our empirical study exploits lending-based real estate crowdfunding in which debt investments are made to loans that are secured by housing properties (i.e., as collateral) until repaid. In an event of borrower default, the platform will enforce security by selling the collateral to recoup lender losses including principals and interests.

Such a practice is attractive to both sides of the markets. On the borrower side, lending-based RECF, as a specific form crowdfunding, is expected to hold several advantages, such as ubiquitous access, less restrictive eligibility, quicker turn-around time for approval, and oftentimes more attractive rates, as we have seen from ordinary P2P lending (Pritchard 2019). These advantages can be attributed to factors like stronger online presence, more agile and flexible application processes, lower operational costs, and perhaps stronger supply of funding due to an easier access to a large lender base. Therefore, RECF offers an alternative financing option with lower fees, at a faster speed, and with higher flexibility than traditional financing sources (ZeusLending.com 2016) even though individuals who possess real estate are more likely to get loans from traditional financing channels (relative to those who do not possess real estate). Besides, as traditional banks continue to tighten their lending standards by setting higher interest rates and tougher collateral requirements (Ward 2019, under "How could this impact you?"), home owners may find lending-based RECF even more attractive. $^{3}$ In a case where borrowers simply need more funds in addition to credits they have acquired from traditional banks, lending-based RECF serves to fulfill such a need.

On the lender side, lending-based RECF provides a secured and financially stable option for investors. First, it takes a form of secure lending because creditors can claim the collateral to coupe their losses in an event of borrower default. Loan investments in ordinary P2P lending in contrast are unsecured and depend solely on the borrowers' creditworthiness without collateral. Second, returns from lending-based RECF markets are determined by the interest rate offered and the amount invested, both of which are fixed and do not fluctuate over time. On the contrary, the profitability is relatively more volatile for other traditional investment vehicles like stocks.

## 2.2. Research Framework

In this section, we provide a research framework in which we discuss lender behavior of investing in house-secured loans and several influencers of our interest for later empirical testing.

2.2.1. Investment Decisions in Lending-Based Real Estate Crowdfunding. In our context, the amount to invest is perhaps the most straightforward decision each individual lender needs to make. Indeed, a majority of prior work on crowdfunding has focused on the decision of how much to invest, at the transaction level (e.g., Burtch et al. 2016, 2018), the loan level (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012), and the platform level (e.g., Jiang et al. 2018). Extending this research stream, we explicitly consider the decision of how fast to invest in this research.

When deciding how fast to invest a loan, individual lenders may need to strike a balance between the profitability and the associated risk. Given that most loans in our context are open for funding for only a short period of time, making a prompt lending decision allows a lender to secure a spot and invest in an interested loan before it turns inactive (after it is fully funded); nevertheless, doing so also puts a lender at higher risk in that she may not have enough time to carefully assess the loan quality. By taking a more deliberated evaluation process, in contrast, a lender is more likely to identify a loan with high quality, but at the expense of losing the opportunity of investing in an interested loan at all. As a result, investigating time-to-invest decisions not only advances our knowledge in lender decision making from the temporal perspective, but also provides useful implications for borrowers and platform designers who desire to leverage the fundraising momentum (Li and Duan 2016).

## 2.2.2. Key Influencers of Lenders' Investment Decisions.

Based on prior research and the unique features of lending-based RECF, we propose a link between individuals' investment behavior and a series of on-platform and off-platform information. We refer to on-platform information as factors that are disclosed and assessable on the crowdfunding site, namely collateral characteristics and lenders' experience. Off-platform information is used to refer to factors that are not directly observable on the site and are more related to economic conditions at a macro level, such as housing prices and stock market performance.

Collateral characteristics. Existing literature suggests that loan attributes, such as borrowers' demographic characteristics, geographic location, and loan purposes, are important determinants for fundraising outcomes (Herzenstein et al. 2011, Duarte et al. 2012, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). In addition to those borrower- and loan-level characteristics, lending-based RECF platforms also disclose information about housing properties that are pledged as collateral (e.g., the estimated value, year of purchase, gross area, etc.) Such collateral information is expected to play a crucial role in influencing lenders' decision because it helps lenders better evaluate the level of risk associated with a given loan. Among all collateral characteristics, the ownership status of the housing properties (i.e., with or without a mortgage) is perhaps one of the most straightforward attributes that can signal loan quality. Consider the two loan types in our context: house-secured (HS) loans and mortgage-secured (MS) loans. Because a borrower of an MS loan still needs to pay off the mortgage and thus is likely to have a weaker balance sheet (relative to a borrower of an HS loan), lenders may perceive an MS loan to be riskier than an HS loan. Further, if a borrower defaults, it could be more inconvenient for the platform (and hence lenders) to deal with an MS loan due to more complex regulations and legal process to claim and sell a house with mortgage. Based on that discussion, we incorporate collateral characteristic, with a focus on the type of collateral (HS loans versus MS loans), into lenders' decision making.

Lender experience. An individual's experience with a specific task helps develop expertise which, in turn, would affect their subsequent actions (Staats et al. 2017). This could also be applied to those who participate in lending-based RECF activities for two reasons. First, lenders need to process additional collateral information when assessing loan quality. Second, they also need to take into account the economic conditions, which may affect the value of collateralized properties as well as potential profitability from other investing vehicles (as discussed next). As a result, we expect lenders who have more (less) previous lending experience to make a funding transaction more (less) quickly and allocate a larger (smaller) amount.

Housing price. Housing prices may affect lenders' funding decisions in both direct and indirect manners. As the overall housing prices increase, we should expect the value of housing properties that are pledged as collateral to rise as well. In this situation, lenders may perceive a real estate secured loan as less risky because the platform would be able to collect more income by selling the collateralized properties in an event of default. On the other hand, prior research shows that housing prices impact individuals' accessibility to bank loans, affecting borrowers' use of crowdfunding as an alternative financing source (Ramcharan and Crowe 2013, Kim and Hann 2019). This substitution relationship suggests that when housing prices increase, borrowers would have better accessibility to bank loans such that online lenders would consequently have a decreased number of loans to choose from. In this scenario, lenders are expected to behave more aggressively with respect to both time-to-invest and amount-to-invest decisions.

Stock market performance. Crowdfunding offers online lenders with a new investing option other than traditional vehicles like stocks. Hornuf and Neuenkirch (2017) empirically show that that stock market volatility affects lenders' willingness to pay in equity-based crowdfunding, implying that lenders consider stocks and crowdfunding substitutes. In line with this substitution relationship, we anticipate that when stock market volatility and return are high, there would be more opportunities for arbitrage, rendering crowdfunding a less desirable investing option (as reflected in slower arrivals and smaller amounts of lending transactions).

## 3. Data and Setting 3.1. The Platform

Launched in 2014, the platform under our study primarily focuses on loans that are secured by real estate. As of March 2018, the platform had achieved a trading volume of 87 billion Chinese yuan (equivalent to \$13.36 billion U.S. dollars). In what follows, we describe the lending procedure of loans on this platform and highlight platform features that are related to lenders' investment behavior.

A borrower starts a loan application by providing her profile information, such as gender, age, occupation, and bank account. The borrower can then submit an application by specifying the requested amount, interest rate, loan duration, method of repayment, and the details of the housing property to be used as collateral. Upon the submission of a new application, the platform verifies the entered information, investigates the housing property, and posts the approved loans on the home page (see Figure A1 in the online appendix). A potential lender can view a particular loan for detailed information, such as the amount requested, interest rate, loan duration, repayment method, the percentage of the requested amount that has been funded, and collateral information (see Figure A2 in the online appendix). For an interested loan, a lender can submit a lending transaction either manually, or prespecify a set of criteria to authorize the platform to submit a transaction on behalf of her automatically when loan attributes meet those criteria (e.g., a desired range of interest rates and durations). Once the cumulative investment amount reaches the requested target, the loan would stop receiving funds and become inactive. Then, the legal documents of the borrower's housing property will be submitted to the institution that is in charge of real estate management for registration. Funds will be transferred from the lenders to the borrower, who is then required to repay the loan on a monthly basis. In an event of borrower default, the property will be sold to recoup lenders' losses, including the principal, interests, liquidated damages, and other expenses.

## 3.2. Data Description

Our data contain all 2,332 real estate-secured loans that are posted and successfully funded from June 1, 2014 to December 31, 2015. $^{4}$ For each loan, we observe loan characteristics, such as the amount requested, interest rate, loan duration, repayment method, purpose of a loan, and posting date. We also observe borrower characteristics, such as age, gender, education, marital status, monthly income, length of employment, and whether a coborrower is listed. The collateral information includes the collateral type (i.e., an HS or an MS loan), year of purchase, gross area of the property, and estimated value. $^{5}$ Finally, for each lending transaction, we record the lender ID, investment time, investment amount, and type of transaction (manual versus automatic). Table 1 provides the description of our key variables.

The outcome variables of our interest are lenders' investment time and investment amount, which are constructed to measure lenders' time-to-invest and amount-to-invest decisions, respectively. We use the time that has elapsed since the arrival of the first lending transaction made to the same loan as an approximation for the investment time variable, because only the posting date of a loan can be observed from our data. $^{6}$

## 3.3. Matched Sample

Before conducting the empirical analysis, we construct matched samples. As previously shown, MS loans and HS loans differ in a systematic manner, which could introduce estimation biases in our analysis. Researchers typically rely on regression adjustment to account for the differences between the two groups. They find that this strategy performs poorly when there is an insufficient overlap of the distribution of the covariates between the two groups (Stuart 2010). Our study mitigates the effects of confounding by combining the matching method with regression adjustment (Rubin 1973, Rubin and Thomas 2000, Abadie and Imbens 2006). We first employ a matching method based on propensity scores to reduce the covariate biases between MS loans and HS loans. Then, we conduct regression analyses using the matched sample to adjust for small residual biases.

For each MS loan, we select a similar HS loan that is statistically comparable with respect to the distribution of observed covariates. The propensity score for loan j, denoted by $e_{j}$ , is defined as the probability that the loan is secured by a mort-gaged house, given the observed covariates

$$
e _ {j} = \operatorname * {P r} \bigl (\text { Collateral   type } _ {j} = 1 | X _ {j} \bigr),\tag{1}
$$

where $X_{j}$ includes a set of covariates, including Loan amount, Loan rate, Loan duration, Loan repayment, Loan purpose, Age, Gender, Education, Marital status, Monthly income, Employment, Coborrower, and Repeat borrower. We estimate the propensity score using a logit model. The distance between any pair of loans is the absolute difference in the estimated propensity score between them. We then employ the kernel matching algorithm to match each MS loan with a weighted average of all HS loans, with the weight of each HS loan as inversely proportional to its distance from the target MS loan. Samples that are in the region of common support (i.e., areas where there is distribution overlap on propensity score) are used for robust inferences. The reduction in sample size may not be an issue because only seven MS loans with extreme values of propensity score are discarded. The remaining sample and the resulting weights will then be used in the subsequent regression analysis.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

## 4.1. Time-to-Invest Analysis

Our first objective is to examine the effect of various covariates on lenders' decision of how fast to invest.

We use a continuous-time survival model to investigate how quickly lending transactions occur during the course of the fundraising period. Applying survival modeling to our context, the outcome variable represents the time discrepancies between the first transaction of a loan and the following transactions of the same loan. Consider that loan j is posted on the platform for funding at time $t_{0}$ . Transaction i arrives at time $t_{i}$ , where $i = 1, 2, 3 \ldots I$ , such that the arrival process of transactions ends when the loan is fully funded and closed at $t_{I}$ . Under survival modeling, we can model the arrival time of a transaction as a random variable whose distribution is governed by the following hazard function:

$$
h \big (t _ {i j} \big) = \lim _ {\Delta t \to 0} \frac {P \big (t _ {i j} \leq T <   t _ {i j} + \Delta t | T \geq t _ {i j} \big)}{\Delta t}.\tag{2}
$$

The denominator $\Delta t$ denotes the duration of a time interval, whereas the numerator represents the conditional probability that a transaction occurs during time interval $[t_{ij}, t_{ij} + \Delta t)$ but has not occurred before $t_{ij}$ . Accordingly, Equation (2) captures the instantaneous probability that transaction i of loan j arrives at time $t_{ij}$ .

Assuming the random variable follows a Weibull distribution, $^{9}$ we can specify the following proportional hazards model:

$$
h \big (t _ {i j l} | Z _ {i j} \big) = h _ {0} \big (t _ {i j l} \big) \mathrm{exp} \big (Z _ {i j} \beta + \gamma_ {l} \big).\tag{3}
$$

Here, $h_{0}(t_{ijl})$ is the baseline hazard function, which takes the form $h_{0}(t)=\alpha pt^{p-1}$ , with p and $\alpha$ representing shape and scale parameter, respectively. $\exp(Z_{ij}\beta)$ is the relative hazard function, which links a proportionate increase or reduction in arrival time to covariates $Z_{ij}$ through a vector $\beta$ . p, $\alpha$ , and $\beta$ are the parameters to be estimated. $Z_{ij}$ includes a series of variables, including loan characteristics (Loan amount, Loan rate, Loan duration, Loan repayment, and Loan purpose), borrower characteristics (Age, Gender, Education, Marital status, Monthly income, Employment, Coborrower, and Repeat borrower), and collateral characteristics (Collateral type, Years of purchase, Gross area, and Estimated value). We also include the percentage of the requested amount that has been funded prior to the arrival of a given transaction (Percent funded) and its quadratic term (Sq Percent funded). Adding these two time-variant covariates allows us to account for the possibility that lenders' behavior may systematically change during the course of the fundraising period (Ariely and Simonson 2003, Burtch et al. 2018, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2018) in a nonlinear fashion. Finally, we insert a random intercept $\gamma_{l}$ into the relative hazard function to account for unobserved heterogeneity at the lender level (e.g., risk tolerance, financial expertise, etc.) that may affect time-to-invest decisions. Given the specification in Equation (3), a positive estimate of $\beta$ will inform us that the corresponding covariate is associated with quicker lending transactions.

A notable feature of our context is that lenders are able to make lending transactions either manually or automatically. The two types of transactions “compete” in a sense that a given investment can only belong to one of the two transaction types (i.e., either manual or automatic) at any given time. To account for the competing risks, we enhance Equation (2) by specifying a cause-specific hazard function as

$$
h _ {k} (t _ {i j l}) = \lim _ {\Delta t \to 0} \frac {P (t _ {i j l} \leq T _ {k} <   t _ {i j l} + \Delta t , K = k | T _ {k} \geq t _ {i j l})}{\Delta t},\tag{4}
$$

where k denotes the type of a given transaction (k = 0 for automatic and k = 1 for manual transactions). The cause-specific hazard function for competing risks captures the probability of the occurrence of a transaction from a given transaction type (type of interest) in the presence of the other transaction type (competing type). Accordingly, the model based on the cause-specific hazards is given by

$$
h _ {k} (t _ {i j l} | Z _ {i j}) = h _ {0 k} (t _ {i j l}) \mathrm{exp} (Z _ {i j} \beta_ {k} + \gamma_ {l}),\tag{5}
$$

where $h_{0k}(t_{ijl})$ and $\beta_{k}$ are cause-specific baseline hazard and coefficients. Following prior literature (Joseph et al. 2015, Ramasubbu and Kemerer 2015), we fit separate regression models for each transaction type. $^{10}$

## 4.2. Amount-to-Invest Analysis

Next, we turn our attention to lenders' decision of how much to invest. Specifically, we consider the following multilevel linear regression:

$$
y _ {i j l} = Z _ {i j} \delta + \eta_ {l} + \alpha_ {k} + \varepsilon_ {i j l}.\tag{6}
$$

The dependent variable, $y_{ijl}$ , is the logarithm of the amount of transaction i of loan j made by lender l. $Z_{ij}$ includes the same set of covariates as is included in the time-to-invest analysis, and $\eta_{l}$ represents the random effect at the lender level. As previously noted, a lending transaction could be either a manual or an automatic investment. To account for the effect of the transaction type on the amount decision, we introduce a random intercept $\alpha_{k}$ to denote the baseline effect specific to transaction type k. $^{11}$

The error term is $\varepsilon_{ijl}$ and we cluster the standard errors at the lender level. $\delta$ is a vector of coefficients to be estimated. A positive element of $\delta$ will inform us that the corresponding covariate is associated with a higher investment amount.

## 4.3. Role of On-Platform and Off-Platform Information

Finally, we proceed to examine how the lenders' decision is affected by the various information discussed in our research framework. Specifically, we enhance Equations (5) and (6) by including additional covariates of our main interest, including lender experience (EXP), housing price changes ( $\Delta HPI$ ), stock market return (SMR), and stock market volatility (SMV). We also allow these covariates to interact with the collateral type to investigate their moderating effects. Accordingly, we have the enhanced specification for the time-to-invest analysis

$$
\begin{array}{l} h _ {k} (t _ {i j l}) = h _ {0 k} (t _ {i j l}) \mathrm{exp} (\pi_ {1 k} E X P _ {i j l} + \pi_ {2 k} \Delta H P I + \pi_ {3 k} S M R \\ \qquad + \pi_ {4 k} S M V + \rho_ {1 k} E X P _ {i j l} \times C o l l a t e r a l t y p e _ {j} \\ \qquad + \rho_ {2 k} \Delta H P I \times C o l l a t e r a l t y p e _ {j} + \rho_ {3 k} S M R \\ \qquad \times C o l l a t e r a l t y p e _ {j} + \rho_ {4 k} S M V \times C o l l a t e r a l t y p e _ {j} \\ \qquad + Z _ {i j} \beta_ {k} + \gamma_ {l}). \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

Similarly, we have the enhanced specification for the amount-to-invest analysis

$$
\begin{array}{r l} y _ {i j l} & = \sigma_ {1} E X P _ {i j l} + \sigma_ {2} \Delta H P I + \sigma_ {3} S M R + \sigma_ {4} S M V + \tau_ {1} E X P _ {i j l} \\ & \times \text { Collateral   type } _ {j} + \tau_ {2} \Delta H P I \times \text { Collateral   type } _ {j} \\ & + \tau_ {3} S M R \times \text { Collateral   type } _ {j} + \tau_ {4} S M V \\ & \times \text { Collateral   type } _ {j} + Z _ {i j} \delta + \eta_ {l} + \alpha_ {k} + \varepsilon_ {i j l}. \end{array} \tag {8}
$$

## 5. Empirical Results 5.1. Main Results

We report the hazard ratio (i.e., the exponentiated form of $\beta$ ) estimates from the time-to-invest analysis in Columns (1)–(2) of Table 4. The estimates show that lenders' decision of how fast to invest is largely affected by characteristics at various levels. For example, the estimated hazard ratios of Collateral type (1 for MS loans) are statistically significant and less than 1, suggesting that lenders are less aggressive in investing in MS loans (relative to HS loans), as exhibited in slower occurrences of their lending transactions. Loans that request a smaller amount and provide a higher interest rate tend to receive investments in a shorter time. Borrowers who are highly educated are more likely to receive funding more quickly. The estimated hazard ratio is less than 1 for Percent funded and greater than 1 for Sq Percent funded. These two parameters indicate a nonlinear relationship: lenders engage in crowdfunding activities with a faster pace at the beginning and the end of the fundraising lifecycle. Column (3) of Table 4 presents the estimation results from the amount-to-invest analysis. The estimated coefficient of Collateral type is negative, indicating that lenders tend to invest a smaller amount to MS loans compared with HS loans. In addition, lenders tend to contribute more money to loans with a larger amount and a higher interest rate.

Next, we turn to the effect of various on-platform and off-platform information discussed in our research framework. We first look at lender experience. The estimates indicate that experienced lenders tend to invest more quickly (hazard ratios of EXP are greater than 1) and fund a larger amount of money (coefficients of EXP are positive) than do their inexperienced counterparts. The estimated hazard ratios of Collateral type × EXP from automatic transactions reveals an interesting finding: lenders' aversion toward MS loans is attenuated by their experience, suggesting that experienced lenders have a higher tendency to diversify their investment portfolio. However, lender experience has no significant moderating effect on amount-to-invest decisions.

The estimated hazard ratios of $\Delta HPI$ are greater than 1 from the time-to-invest analysis, indicating that lenders make quicker investments in response to a rise in housing prices. One plausible explanation is that because real estate is used to secure repayments, lenders would perceive loans as less risky when housing prices go up. Another possible reason is that when housing prices increase, borrowers would have better accessibility to credits from traditional financing sources (e.g., bank loans that use housing collateral), diminishing their use of crowdfunding (Kim and Hann 2019). Such a decline in the supply of fund-seeking projects would pose a scarcity effect, driving lenders to respond and invest in a more aggressive manner. Further, the estimated hazard ratios of Collateral type × ΔHPI informs us that lenders' preference for HS loans (or aversion to MS loans) is accentuated when housing prices increase. On the other hand, the results from the amount-to-invest analysis suggest that lenders tend to invest larger amounts and exhibit a weaker preference for HS loans when facing an increase in housing prices.

As to the role of stock market performance, the estimated hazard ratios of SMV are less than 1 from the time-to-invest analysis, suggesting that high stock market volatility is associated with a slower lending decision. Because investors consider stocks and crowdfunding substitutes (Hornuf and Neuenkirch 2017), greater fluctuations in stock prices typically present more arbitrage opportunities, rendering crowdfunding a less attractive option for investment. As a result, lenders would tend to be less aggressive in their time-to-invest decision because, in this situation, there would be fewer lenders competing for funding opportunity on the platform. Meanwhile, the estimated hazard ratios of Collateral type × SMV are greater than 1, indicating that the level of stock market volatility weakens lenders' hesitation to MS loans.

5.2.3. Alternative Models for Survival Analysis. The multiple contributions that a lender pledges may be correlated. To address the issue, we also perform a shared frailty model by introducing the random effects, $\kappa_{l}$ , to account for the variation in baseline hazard function across individual lenders (Vaupel et al. 1979, Hougaard 1986). That is, $h_{0}(t) = \alpha pt^{p-1}\kappa_{l}$ . The parameter $\kappa_{l}$ is assumed to follow a gamma distribution with mean 1 and variance $\theta$ . Next, we apply the Cox proportional hazards model to estimate the cause-specific hazard model. This semiparametric model imposes no parametric form on the baseline hazard function, allowing for greater flexibility in the shape of the hazard function (Cox 1972). We control for the same set of covariates $Z_{ij}$ as in the main analysis. Lastly, we employ an accelerated failure time (AFT) model, $^{12}$ which is a commonly used alternative to the proportional hazards model when specifying the cause-specific hazard model. Unlike the proportional hazards specification that models the effects of covariates on the probability that a transaction arrives at any given time, the AFT model captures the direct effect of covariates on lenders' time-to-invest decisions. The log-linear form of AFT model assumes that the logarithm of $T_{ijl}$ is a linear function of covariates ( $Z_{ij}$ ). That is, we have $\log(T_{ijl}) = Z_{ij}\varphi + \gamma_l + \sigma v_{ijl}$ , where $\varphi$ denotes the parameters of interest, $\gamma_l$ denotes the lender-specific effect, $\sigma$ is a scale parameter, and $v_{ijl}$ is the random error term that follows the Weibull distribution. ## 6. Conclusion

This research examines lenders' investment behavior to gain insights into lenders' decision making in lending-based real estate crowdfunding. We specifically focus on how such a behavior is shaped by both on-platform and off-platform information. Our results show that lenders tend to invest slowly and a small amount of money to loans secured by a mortgaged house. Interestingly, we find that lenders with more investment experience tend to invest quickly and a large amount of money. They exhibit a small aversion to mortgaged-secured loans than do less experienced lenders when deciding how fast to invest. When overall housing price rises, lenders are more likely to invest quickly. This acceleration in the time-to-invest decisions would decline for MS loans. Facing great stock market fluctuations, lenders tend to invest slowly. This reduction in time-to-invest is moderated for MS loans.

Our findings have important implications for the management of lending-based RECF platforms. For example, platform managers should take into account economic conditions at the macro level when deploying their marketing strategy. Our findings suggest that lenders tend to make a quicker investment decision as well as invest a larger amount in response to a rise in real estate value. To stimulate and to better leverage herding momentum among lenders, managers may consider running a more aggressive advertising campaign to entice the crowd to invest when the housing price index increases. Similar tactics should be applied when stock market volatility declines.

Moreover, lender heterogeneity in responding to different collateral types should be incorporated into the design of an automatic transaction or a recommender system. We show that although lenders as a whole prefer HS loans to MS ones, experienced lenders tend to be more risk-seeking as reflected by a weaker preference for HS loans. Accordingly, platform designers can implement a more personalized system that promotes HS loans to inexperienced lenders who are risk-averse but recommends MS loans to experienced ones who seek a more diversified portfolio.

Our research also provides useful insights for fund seekers and policy makers. For example, we find that using a housing property with high estimated value as collateral does not really help in attracting faster and larger lending transactions. Nevertheless, borrowers who are female or who own housing properties (without mortgage) are more favored in the lending-based RECF market. On the other hand, our results show that crowdfunding activities are associated with stock market volatility, lending further support to the argument that investors consider stocks and crowdfunding substitutes (Hornuf and Neuenkirch 2017). As a result, policy makers must carefully regulate the lending-based RECF market just like other financial instruments. More importantly, they should take appropriate actions in case of potential economic downturns.

We conclude by acknowledging some limitations of our work, which point clear directions for future studies. For instance, from our data we only observe successful loans that are fully funded. This data limitation occurs because the platform conceals loan listings that failed to reach the requested amount at the end of their lending period. Although we cannot completely rule out the potential issue arising from such a limitation, we are able to obtain consistent results when evaluating lending transactions made to loans with varying fundraising performance. Future work can use our framework to check whether lenders demonstrate similar behavior by including the failed loans. Moreover, information on loan performance (i.e., paid off or default) is unfortunately not available to us. It would be interesting to extend our work to explore the role of expected performance (i.e., perceived profitability versus perceived risk) in influencing lender behavior when such data become accessible. Finally, the current research focuses on lending-based RECF as the context and does not consider equity-based RECF markets. Because the value of real estate involved determines financial returns in equity-based RECF, it would be fruitful to look into how such a factor influences investor behavior in another novel emerging market.
