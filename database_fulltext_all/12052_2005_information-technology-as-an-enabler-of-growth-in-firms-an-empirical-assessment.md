# Information Technology as an Enabler of Growth in Firms: An Empirical Assessment

SABYASACHI MITRA

SABYASACHI MITRA is an Associate Professor of Information Technology Management at the College of Management at Georgia Tech. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Iowa in 1991 and his B.Tech. in Mechanical Engineering from the Indian Institute of Technology in 1985. His current research interests include the economic effects of IT, IT outsourcing, disaster-recovery and business-continuity practices, electronic commerce, ERP systems, and communication network design. His research has appeared in several journals such as IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, INFORMS Journal on Computing, and Journal of Management Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: This research provides empirical evidence of the role of information technology (IT) as an enabler of growth in firms. We postulate that as a firm grows larger, a superior IT infrastructure increases the productivity of other inputs by controlling the complexity-related costs that the firm incurs as it increases in size. In the empirical analysis to support this view, we find that firms with high-growth expectations increase their IT spending as their free cash flow increases, whereas low-growth firms maintain a constant level of IT spending, irrespective of their free cash flow. Further, we analyze the effect of IT investments in one period on growth-related metrics in subsequent periods. We find that a superior IT infrastructure significantly reduces the cost of operations for high-growth firms in subsequent periods. In summary, the theory and empirical evidence presented in this paper point to an indirect but important contribution of the IT infrastructure to a firm’s growth.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business value of IT, firm growth, free cash flow, Tobin’s q.

IN THE PAST FEW DECADES, U.S. BUSINESSES have made enormous investments in information technology (IT). Although this spending pattern continues unabated in the new century, researchers in information systems (IS) and economics have struggled to document the organizational gains from IT. Results from empirical studies that have investigated the effect of IT on firm performance have been decidedly mixed. Earlier studies that have used metrics related to the productivity of white-collar and information workers have failed to uncover significant positive contribution of IT on these measures [12, 41]. Later studies, which are often based on data that became available in the early 1990s, have documented the effect of IT on a diverse set of measures, such as operating costs [3, 15, 32], return on assets [5, 47], Tobin’s q [4], firm size [7], productivity [6, 18], and equity value [9]. Some researchers have also argued that although IT can lead to better customer service, product quality, and higher prices, it can also lead to higher costs and lower productivity, measured using traditional approaches [28, 45]. Recent research has focused on the complementary nature of IT [51], investments in enterprise systems [19], and managerial perceptions of IT [44]. A more complete discussion of this literature can be found in Chan [8]. Suffice it to say here that although a consensus is emerging that IT investments have beneficially affected several measures of firm performance, the evidence is by no means conclusive.

Senior executives have traditionally viewed IT as a support function that automates and increases the efficiency of business processes. This view considers IT as a backoffice function that is a “necessary cost” of doing business, but without serious strategic implications. However, in recent years, senior managers have been increasingly looking at IT as a strategic resource and as a key enabler of growth [13, 16, 40]. An evaluation of the trade literature points to a growing recognition of IT as a frontoffice function that affects customer service, customer satisfaction, and the creation of new products and services [16]. Several enterprise software vendors such as SAP have developed new functionality that focuses on the front office. Thus, the prevalent view in the trade literature postulates a direct effect of the IT infrastructure on revenue growth.

Although the trade literature has increasingly viewed IT as an enabler of growth, a careful investigation of that relationship in the academic literature is lacking. This paper attempts to fill that void by providing empirical evidence on the role of IT as an enabler of growth. We postulate that the primary effect of a superior IT infrastructure on firm growth is an indirect one. Specifically, a superior IT infrastructure enables a firm to grow more efficiently by controlling the complexity-related costs that increase as the firm grows larger. This view of IT does not preclude a direct effect of IT on revenue growth but it emphasizes an indirect (albeit important) role of the IT infrastructure in enabling efficient and profitable growth. We call this the IT-enabled efficient growth hypothesis.

In order to support this hypothesis, the empirical evidence provided in this paper bears on two related questions: (1) Are there differences in IT spending between high-growth and low-growth firm? If IT is a key enabler of growth, firms that have higher expectations of growth would likely spend more on IT to build their IT infrastructure over time. We empirically answer this question by evaluating a well-known database of IT budgets of large corporations in the years 1988–92. We use Tobin’s q (explained later) as a proxy for growth expectations. In this analysis, it is important to control for two variables that will likely have an impact on IT budgets. First, since large companies typically spend more on IT, we control for the effect of firm size on the IT budget. Second, it is well known in the finance and economics literature that a firm’s free cash flow (cash in excess of what is required to cover operating costs, debt service, and dividends) affects its investment choices. Consequently, we control for the free cash available to the firm in our analysis.

(2) What growth-related benefits do higher IT investments in one period achieve for the firm in subsequent periods? As explained earlier, IT can directly affect revenue growth, or it can affect profitability by reducing average cost per unit of output as the firm grows larger. The IT-enabled efficient growth hypothesis postulated in this paper emphasizes the second of the two effects discussed above. Thus, for high-growth firms, we empirically investigate the effect of higher IT investments (1988–92) on the firm’s cost of operations in the subsequent period (1993–97). Figure 1 graphically summarizes the time period of the analysis and the major findings.

For the first part of the analysis, the results are as follows. At average levels of free cash flow, we find no difference in IT spending between high-growth and low-growth firms, after controlling for other factors that can affect their IT spending levels. However, as their free cash flow deviates from the average level, important distinctions arise. We find that firms with high-growth expectations increase (decrease) their IT spending significantly as their free cash flow increases (decreases), whereas firms with low-growth expectations maintain a constant level of IT expenditures, irrespective of their free cash flow. This is consistent with the argument that (1) high-growth firms have many competing positive net present value (NPV) opportunities available and must ration spending when free cash flow is limited, and (2) managers in highgrowth firms view IT as a key enabler of growth and attempt to build up the IT infrastructure in anticipation of future growth when more free cash is available.

For the second part of the analysis, the results are as follows. Modeling the firm’s inputs through a Cobb–Douglas production function, we find a strong relationship between higher IT expenditures in the previous period (1988–92) and lower overall cost of operations in the subsequent period (1993–97). Further, this relationship is only valid for high-growth firms, and no such relationship exists for the low-growth firms in the sample.

## Theoretical Development and Hypotheses

## IT-Enabled Efficient Growth Hypothesis

THE CONVENTIONAL VIEW OF IT AS AN ENABLER of growth sees the IT infrastructure as having a direct effect on revenue growth. A cursory evaluation of the trade literature finds ample support for this view [16]. The arguments for a direct role of the IT infrastructure in revenue growth have been that a superior IT infrastructure (1) improves customer service and thus aids in the acquisition and retention of customers, (2) provides better customer intelligence and better targeting of customers, and (3) enables the creation of new IT-based ancillary products and services that directly generate additional revenue.

In this paper, we investigate a more subtle, indirect, and supporting role of the IT infrastructure in enabling firm growth. Our arguments focus on the cost side of the profit equation. A superior IT infrastructure allows a company to efficiently grow in revenue without an equivalent or greater increase in cost of operations [13, 40]. For example, Amazon.com (the online retailer) made significant investments in its distribution and IT infrastructures in anticipation of future growth [40]. Large size leads to higher economies of scale, but, without a superior IT infrastructure, revenue growth can be accompanied by high control and coordination costs, as the firm’s operations become more complicated. The IT-enabled efficient growth hypothesis is now stated more formally as a proposition.

## Free Cash Flow Effects on IT Spending Levels

A large empirical literature supports the hypothesis that a firm’s free cash flow is an important determinant of investment. Free cash flow, which we define more precisely later, is the cash available after expenses, taxes, interest payments and dividends. It is the cash flow that the company retains to invest in its operations, retire debt, or repurchase its equity. There are two related but separate views in the literature on the relationship between free cash flow and investments.

Jensen’s free cash flow hypothesis [20] and its related empirical evidence [26, 29, 31, 35, 43] focus on overinvestments caused by agency problems. Jensen argues that managers in firms with large free cash flows have an incentive to waste organizational resources on negative NPV projects rather than pay out the excess cash to shareholders through dividends or share repurchase schemes. Further, the effect of free cash flow on organizational inefficiencies is most pronounced in firms with lowgrowth opportunities. Such firms have fewer positive NPV investment opportunities than firms with high-growth prospects and they are more likely to waste their excess cash on non–value-added projects. The free cash flow hypothesis has generated tremendous interest in the finance literature and has been empirically verified in a variety of contexts [26, 29, 31, 35, 43].

Whereas the free cash flow hypothesis and its supporting evidence focuses on overinvestments caused by agency problems, an alternative stream of research focuses on underinvestment in the presence of constrained internal funds [11, 14, 17, 33, 50]. This view of the relationship between free cash flow and investments argues that lowliquidity firms often forego profitable projects because of the high cost of external funds. This has been examined in detail in the context of capital spending [11] and R&D investments [17]. Also, Lamont [24] demonstrates that capital expenses by nonoil subsidiaries of oil companies dropped significantly after the oil price crash, even though the crash did not have any effect on their investment opportunities. Taken as a whole, the articles in this literature provide significant evidence that a firm’s investment decisions are often constrained by its free cash flow.

In the context of IT expenses, there is ample anecdotal evidence in the trade literature that IT spending is adversely affected by decreased free cash. When profits are tight, IT budgets are often the first to be reduced [42]. Further, the trade literature also points to a link between economic downturns and reduced IT spending [10, 38]. If we view IT spending as having two components, a nondiscretionary portion that is required to sustain operations and a discretionary portion to enhance the IT infrastructure, it is reasonable to expect that firms will reduce discretionary spending when internal cash flow is limited. For example, new enterprise software implementations have been more severely affected by the economic downturn than hardware elements, such as networking gear, servers, and personal computers, which have to be replaced every few years to sustain operations [48]. In summary, the trade literature points to a link between IT spending and free cash flow levels in a firm.

How do growth expectations affect the IT spending level of the firm? If the IT infrastructure is a key enabler of growth, as postulated in the IT-enabled efficient growth hypothesis, firms with high-growth expectations will spend more on their IT infrastructure as their free cash flow increases as compared to firms with low-growth expectations. The logic here is simple. If you expect to increase significantly in size, you need a superior IT infrastructure to achieve that growth efficiently. However, when free cash flow is low, high-growth firms have many positive NPV projects that compete for the limited funds available. Consequently, high-growth firms also decrease their IT spending as their free cash flow decreases. On the other hand, lowgrowth firms have less need to invest in their IT infrastructure as their free cash flow increases. They also have fewer positive NPV opportunities available and there is less competition for the limited resources when free cash flow is tight. Thus, for low-growth firms, IT budgets are less affected by their free cash flow. The above arguments form the basis of the following hypothesis that explores the relationship between free cash flow and IT spending.

Hypothesis 1: For high-growth firms, higher free cash flow is associated with higher IT spending. No such relationship exists between free cash flow and IT spending for low-growth firms.

It is important to point out that Hypothesis 1 is not based on the agency cost arguments behind Jensen’s free cash flow hypothesis [20]. If higher IT spending can be attributed to agency costs associated with high free cash flow, then low-growth firms (where such agency costs are most pronounced) would be expected to increase their IT spending as their free cash flow increases. Clearly, that is not what Hypothesis 1 postulates. Rather, the reasoning behind Hypothesis 1 is as follows. High-growth firms invest their free cash to grow their operations (because they have many positive NPV projects available) and, consequently, have to make corresponding investments in their IT infrastructure to support that growth. Low-growth firms (since they do not have many growth opportunities available) typically use this free cash to retire debt, repurchase stock, or increase managerial compensation. None of these investments require a superior supporting IT infrastructure. Thus, IT spending by low-growth firms is not significantly affected by their free cash flow.

## IT Infrastructure and Cost of Operations

The IT-enabled efficient growth hypothesis postulates that the key to profitable growth lies in the firm’s ability to control its complexity-related costs effectively while achieving the benefits of size through better economies of scale. This argument is well recognized in the industry and the trade literature. For example, Amazon’s investment in its IT infrastructure was made at a time when the company was still not profitable and was under pressure from Wall Street to erase its red ink [40]. However, if the firm’s operations become more complicated without an adequate and scalable IT infrastructure, future revenue growth would be accompanied by high unit cost increases as well. Amazon has always been under pressure to justify its cost structure vis-à-vis other retailers. Clearly, the firm views its IT infrastructure as a key part of its strategy to build a cost-effective distribution system to support growth. Likewise, careful investments in logistic systems and IT over a decade have enabled United Parcel Service (UPS) to achieve high revenue growth with a declining cost per package delivery [13]. Based on the above arguments, we derive the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2: High-growth firms with a superior IT infrastructure will achieve a lower average cost of operations per unit of output when compared to firms with an inferior IT infrastructure, while the effect of the IT infrastructure on cost of operations will be less for low-growth firms.

It is interesting to note that Hypothesis 2 does not postulate a direct effect of the IT infrastructure on revenue growth. Revenue growth is a result of several factors, such as the market the firm operates in, new products and services that it introduces, and the quality of its management team. Rather, Hypothesis 2 argues that high-growth firms that invest in their IT infrastructure are able to achieve that revenue growth efficiently by controlling their cost of operations better than firms with an inferior IT infrastructure.

## Free Cash Flow, Growth Opportunities, and IT Spending

## Measuring Free Cash Flow

JENSEN DEFINES FREE CASH FLOW AS THE “cash flow in excess of that required to fund all projects that have positive net present values when discounted at the relevant cost of capital” [20, p. 323]. Identification of such projects from reported accounting data is a formidable (if not impossible) task. In this research, we use a proxy measure of free cash flow that was developed by Lehn and Poulson [29] and has been widely used in the literature [1, 27, 31, 35, 43]. This measure calculates free cash flow (FC) as the posttax cash flow that is not distributed to security holders as dividends or interest payments. It represents the cash flow that is available to the firm to reinvest in its operations. More precisely,

$$
F C = O I B D - T A X - I N T - P D I V - C D I V,
$$

where OIBD is the operating income before depreciation (sales less cost of goods sold [COGS] less selling, general, and administrative expenses [SGA] before deducting depreciation, depletion, and amortization); TAX is the total income taxes minus change in deferred taxes from the previous year to the current year; INT is the gross interest expense on short- and long-term debt; PDIV is the total amount of preferred dividend on cumulative and noncumulative preferred stock; and CDIV is the total amount of dividend on common stock.

Based on FC, we define the following modified measure of free cash (FCF) that better suits our purpose here. FCF represents the free cash flow available to companies before their IT expenditures are made. We explain the reason for this modification after describing the basic model where the FCF variable is used. Since IT expenditures are included in either COGS or SGA, they have been deducted to calculate FC. Thus,

$$
F C F = F C + I T B,
$$

where ITB is the total IT yearly budget of the company.

## Measuring Growth Expectations and Investment Opportunities

A widely used measure of the growth and investment opportunities available to the firm is its Tobin’s q, which is defined as the ratio of the market value of the firm to the replacement cost of its assets [30, 46]. Tobin [46] argues that if, at the margin, q exceeded unity, the firm would have an incentive to invest, since the market valuation of such investments would exceed its cost. As opportunities for such investments are exploited and few remain, the marginal value of q will tend toward unity.

The use of Tobin’s q to proxy growth and investment opportunities has several advantages. Since q is the market estimate of the present value of future cash flows divided by the replacement cost of tangible assets, no risk adjustment or industry normalizations are required to compare the q-values of different firms [25]. Further, a cutoff value of unity can be used to identify firms that have positive NPV growth opportunities under current management and economic conditions [25, 30]. Tobin’s q has been used extensively in past literature to measure the growth and investment opportunities [25, 27, 29, 31, 35, 39].

The measure of q that we use in this research is based on the procedure described in Agrawal and Knoeber [1], Perfect and Wiles [36], and Perfect et al. [37]. An alternative procedure is described in Lindenberg and Poulsen [30] that is more complex and requires data not available for many firms in our sample. However, Perfect and Wiles [36] report a correlation of 0.95 for the two alternative measures of $q \mathrm { : }$

$$
q = (E Q + D T + P F + C V) / A T,
$$

where $E Q$ is the market value of common shares outstanding; DT is the book value of long-term and short-term debt; PF is the book value of preferred stock; CV is the book value of convertible debt and convertible preferred stock; and AT is the book value of total assets.

## The Basic Model

To evaluate Hypothesis 1, the model presented here allows us to explore through ordinary least squares (OLS) regression (1) the relationship between a firm’s free cash flow and its level of IT spending, and (2) the difference in IT spending behavior between high-growth and low-growth firms, after controlling for other variables such as firm size and the median level of IT spending in the firm’s industry. The model is as follows:

## Model A

$$
\begin{array}{c} I T B / S = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (M I T B / S) + \beta_ {2} \times (F C F / S) + \beta_ {3} \times (Q D U M) \\ + \beta_ {4} \times (F C F / S) \times Q D U M + \beta_ {5} \times (S A L E S), \end{array}
$$

where ITB/S is the total IT budget of the firm as a percentage of its total revenue (sales); MITB/S is the median ITB/S for firms within the same two-digit SIC code; FCF/S is the free cash flow before IT spending (FCF) as a percentage of total revenue; QDUM is a dummy variable that is set to 1 if q >1 and 0 otherwise; and SALES is the total annual revenue of the firm.

In Model A, the IT budget and free cash flow variables are normalized by sales (revenue) to account for the size of the firm. Based on the model, the IT spending level of a firm is affected by (1) the median level of spending in the firm’s industry, and (2) the free cash flow available to the firm (before IT spending). Further, we include firm size (represented by sales) as a control variable for two reasons. First, it is possible that larger firms spend more on IT (as a percentage of sales) than smaller firms because they have greater control and coordination needs [32]. Second, it is also possible that economies of scale in IT allow larger firms to spend less (as a percentage of sales) than their smaller counterparts. Using sales as a control variable allows us to explore these possibilities.

Notice also that the QDUM variable is zero for low-growth firms $( q \leq 1 )$ ) and unity for high-growth firms. We therefore estimate the following two OLS regressions through the interaction variable $( Q D U M \times F C F / S )$ included in the model.

$$
\text { Low   Growth: } I T B / S = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times (M I T B / S) + \beta_ {2} \times (F C F / S) + \beta_ {5} \times (S A L E S)
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {High Growth:} I T B / S = \left(\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {3}\right) + \beta_ {1} \times (M I T B / S) + \left(\beta_ {2} + \beta_ {4}\right) \\ \times (F C F / S) + \beta_ {5} \times (S A L E S). \end{array}
$$

Thus, the $\beta _ { 3 }$ and $\beta _ { 4 }$ coefficients represent the differences in the constant term and the slope (for the FCF/S variable) between high-growth and low-growth firms.

As mentioned earlier, the free cash flow variable (FCF) is calculated as the free cash available to the firm before its IT spending. Otherwise, a spurious relationship would exist, since increasing the IT budget would decrease the firm’s free cash flow level. The $F C F$ variable now represents the free cash available to firms to reinvest in their operations including IT.

## The Data Set

The IT budget data was obtained from the editors of Computerworld, a leading trade publication. The data set spans a period of five years from 1988–92 and was collected by the International Data Group through surveys, with a response rate of 68 percent. The data has two major limitations. First, the IT budget data were self-reported values, which could lead to errors in reporting and self-selection biases. However, the average annual IT budget values were consistent with other surveys that have been conducted and aggregate computer investment data by the Bureau of Economic Analysis [18]. Second, the IT budget figure reported in the survey is the yearly budget of the central IS department. With the rise of end-user computing, this figure may underestimate the actual IT investments made by the firm. However, if the total IT investment is proportional to the central IS budget for firms in the sample, the analysis here will not be seriously affected. The data set also has several advantages. This data set has been used in earlier research and most researchers agree that the data are largely reliable [18]. Further, since the time period for the IT budget data is between 1988 and 1992, it allows us to examine the effect of IT investments in subsequent periods. Reliable IT budget data is difficult to obtain, and this is one of the few sources available.

We used the Compustat database to obtain the accounting data required to calculate the remaining variables in the model. After excluding banks, financial institutions, and utilities, there were 457 companies from the Computerworld database that we could match in the Compustat database.<sup>1</sup> Of these, an additional nine firms were excluded because their self-reported revenues did not match the corresponding Compustat values, within an acceptable margin of error. Of the remaining 448, two classes of firms were excluded. First, in order to reliably calculate the industry median ITB/S values (MITB/S), we required at least five firms within a two-digit SIC code, and SIC codes that did not meet this criterion were excluded. Second, we required at least two years (out of the five years in the research window 1988–92) of data to calculate the average values of the metrics (including ITB/S). This generated a sample of 262 firms with complete data for all the variables in the model.

In calculating the metrics in the model, we used a two- to five-year average value for each metric, depending on the number of years of data available. We used the average values for several reasons. First, sporadic IT data did not facilitate a year-byyear analysis. Second, for some variables such as IT budget, it is important to use the average of a few years to capture investment cycles that firms may follow. Perfect et al. [37] also discusses the importance of using an average Tobin’s q value in the analysis. Finally, and most importantly, the model is valid for long-term values of the variables and not their short-term values. Firms may not change their IT spending levels significantly from year to year, as their free cash flow or their investment opportunities change. However, in the long run, their IT budgets will be affected by the amount of cash they habitually retain and reinvest in their operations.

Table 1 shows some key statistics for the variables in our model. The mean IT spending was 1.6 percent of sales, with the standard deviation being almost the same. This indicates significant variation in the level of IT spending in the sample. Interestingly, the median q ratio in the sample was unity, indicating that the sample was equally split between high-growth and low-growth firms. ## Results and Interpretation

The results of the OLS regression using Model A and the data set described above are shown in Table 3. The OLS regression is performed after the following adjustments to the model variables. In the sample, the median value of FCF/S for low-growth firms is 6.7 percent (mean of 6.4 percent and standard deviation 5.4 percent), whereas the corresponding value for high-growth firms is 7.9 percent (mean of 8.8 percent and standard deviation 4.98 percent). To account for this difference between the two groups, the FCF/S value for each firm was adjusted by subtracting the median value for the corresponding group (high or low growth) from the FCF/S value of the firm. Adjusting financial variables by subtracting group-specific medians is a well-recognized practice in the literature when significant differences exist between groups [22, 41].

Further, the OLS regression is performed after applying a scale transformation on the MITB/S and SALES predictor variables by subtracting the full sample mean value of these variables from each of the data points [2, 34]. Mean centering has no effect on the regression coefficients but makes the constant term easier to interpret. In our case, the constant term obtained in an OLS regression without centering the MITB/S and SALES variables is essentially meaningless because it represents the predicted ITB/S value when these variables are zero, a situation impossible in practice. With mean centering, however, the constant term represents the predicted ITB/S value when all continuous predictor variables are at their sample mean values. Mean centering also reduces the multicollinearity problems when interaction terms are present [2, 34]. The $\beta$ values indicate how the ITB/S changes as the continuous predictor variables deviate from their sample means.

## IT Infrastructure Expenses

The IT budget data available to us represent two types of IT expenses made by the firm. The first, which we term IT operating expenses, includes the costs associated with running the IT operations (such as data center personnel costs, help desk personnel costs, common carrier expenses, yearly software licenses and fees, etc.). These expenses are fully “consumed” by the firm in the year they are incurred and do not represent investments. They mainly represent the nondiscretionary part of the IT budget. The second part of the budget, which we term IT infrastructure expenses, represents investments in additional servers, mainframes, telecommunication networks, databases, and enterprise systems that provide a superior IT infrastructure for the future and are not entirely “consumed” within the year. This represents the discretionary part of the IT budget. It is reasonable to expect that the effect of free cash flow is likely to be on the discretionary part of the IT budget. However, the Computerworld data do not report IT infrastructure investments separately. To verify that the relationship with free cash flow is valid for IT infrastructure investments, we develop the following proxy. For each two-digit SIC code, we calculate the minimum ITB/S value for all firms within that SIC code from our database of IT budgets for 1988–92. We assume that this minimum ITB/S value represents the IT investments required to sustain operations (nondiscretionary) and we designate IT expenses incurred by the firm in excess of this minimum percentage value (based on the two-digit SIC code) as IT infrastructure investments (discretionary). Thus, we adjust the IT budget value for each firm by subtracting this minimum value, based on the firm’s two-digit SIC code. The results of the regression analysis with the adjusted ITB/S value as the dependent variable are shown in Table 4. The results are similar to those reported in Table 3, with the fitted value of the coefficient of the interaction term $( Q D U M ^ { * } F C F / S )$ slightly higher in Table 4.

## The Cost Model

TO EVALUATE HYPOTHESIS 2, WE MODEL THE EFFECT of the IT infrastructure on the cost of operations of the firm. We use a Cobb–Douglas formulation similar to Brynjolfsson and Hitt [6]. We assume (without loss of generality) that the firm produces its output using two inputs: (1) the IT infrastructure (IT) built up over the past few years, represented by the average yearly IT infrastructure investment for the 1988– 92 period; and (2) the total annual operating cost (TOC), represented by the annual value for cost of goods sold (COGS) + selling general and administrative expense (SGA) for each year in the 1993–97 period. The output (REV) is measured as the annual revenue for each year in the 1993–97 period. Formulating the production function in this manner using two inputs allows us to examine the effect of the IT infrastructure (IT) built over the past few years on the firm’s total operating costs (TOC) per year for the subsequent period. The Cobb–Douglas specification for the production function is as follows:

$$
R E V _ {t} = e ^ {\beta 0} (I T) ^ {\beta 1} \left(T O C _ {t}\right) ^ {\beta 2}, t = 1 9 9 3 \text {   to   } 1 9 9 7,
$$

where $\beta 0 , \beta 1$ , and $\beta 2$ are positive constants.

Since the Cobb–Douglas formulation applies to the IT infrastructure investments rather than the whole IT budget, we adjust the ITB/S values as explained in the previous section. We also use a five-year average (1988–92) for the adjusted IT investment data for several reasons. First, we believe that the relationships studied here are valid for long-term values of the metrics. In other words, as firms make short-term changes in their IT investments, it is not likely to immediately affect their output, unlike the case of direct inputs such as labor or production machinery. However, IT investment changes, sustained over a period of time, are likely to have an effect. Second, using a five-year average allows us to take into account investment cycles that a firm may follow. Admittedly, investments made outside the five-year period are not captured in the analysis, which may bias the results if the investment cycle is more than five years for several firms in the sample. Third, the IT budget data is sparse and is typically not available for all the years for a firm, because the same firm did not respond to the Computerworld survey every year. In our sample, the percentage of firms with data for any given year varied from 43 percent in 1988 to 93 percent in 1992. Fourth, the IT budgets of firms changed nominally from year to year in the sample. The pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients for the IT budgets for the years 1989–90, 1990–91, and 1991–92 were 0.992, 0.991, and 0.967, respectively. Thus, using the average value did not cause us to lose valuable year-by-year IT investment information. Fifth, the IT budget data are self-reported by firms and may contain errors, since the data are not part of their audited financial statements. By taking a five-year average, we reduce the effect of reporting errors in any specific year.

Notice also that there is a lag between the time periods when the IT investments are made and when the benefits are measured. There is no theory that predicts an appropriate time lag for measuring benefits from IT investments. However, there is growing recognition in the trade literature that IT investments require complex business process changes, and that sustained investments must be made for several years before significant productivity gains can be observed. For example, a recent BusinessWeek article states that “it took UPS 15 years to build a computer system that rivaled the FedEx Cosmos system” [13, p. 66]. According to a recent McKinsey study, “boosting productivity, cutting costs and generating positive business payback on IT investments require a comprehensive action plan sustained over several years by multiple business units and departments beyond IT” [23, p. 36]. We evaluate the model for each year separately between 1993 and 1997, representing a lag of three to seven years from the midpoint of the IT investment period.

A positive value of $\mathbf { \beta } _ { \beta 1 }$ would indicate a positive contribution to output by the IT infrastructure. However, more important for our purpose is the technical rate of substitution (TRS) of the inputs [49], which is defined as

$$
T R S = - \frac {\partial (T O C)}{\partial (I T)} = \left(\frac {\beta 1}{\beta 2}\right) \left(\frac {T O C}{I T}\right).
$$

TRS represents the decrease in total operating cost (TOC) for a unit increase in IT spending (IT) for the same level of output. If TRS > 1, then \$1 invested in IT yields a cost saving that is greater than \$1. However, since there is a lag between the IT spending and when the benefits are measured, we would require a higher break-even TRS value to account for the cost of capital.

## Data Analysis

To evaluate Hypothesis 2 and estimate the parameters of the Cobb–Douglas function, we evaluate through OLS regression the following linear functional form of the Cobb– Douglas formulation [21].

Model B

$$
\log \left(R E V _ {t}\right) = \beta 0 + \beta 1 \cdot \log (I T) + \beta 2 \cdot \log \left(T O C _ {t}\right) \text {   for   } t = 1 9 9 3 - 1 9 9 7.

Of the 262 firms used for the analysis in the second section, exactly 218 had data available for the years 1993–97 in Compustat. The remaining 44 firms had either gone out of business, been acquired or merged into a new entity, ceased operations, or dropped from Compustat coverage for other reasons. Table 5 shows the results of OLS regression to estimate the parameters of the Cobb–Douglas formulation. In the runs reported in Table 5, the OLS regression was performed separately for highgrowth and low-growth firms,<sup>4</sup> identified using a cutoff QDUM value of 1. We used a DFFITS cutoff of 1.0 to identify outliers, and no data points were eliminated using this criterion.

As shown in Table 5, the coefficient for IT $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ is not significant for low-growth firms, indicating that the contribution of the IT infrastructure to output was not significant for such firms. However, the coefficient for IT $( \beta _ { 1 } )$ is significant and positive for high-growth firms, indicating that the IT infrastructure contributes positively to output for high-growth firms. For low-growth firms, the TRS values were lower than the break-even values, indicating that the savings did not cover the cost of capital. In summary, the results indicate that a superior IT infrastructure lowered the total cost of operations for high-growth companies, and no such association was observed for low-growth firms. We conclude from this analysis that the data support Hypothesis 2.

Several limitations of this analysis must be highlighted. The IT budget data does not report IT infrastructure investments separately, and the method we use to identify such investments is only approximate. Further, since we do not have IT budget data for the 1993–97 time periods, it is impossible to say whether lower costs of operations merely reflect IT investments already made or true savings in labor costs. The IT budget data may also be influenced by the level of IT outsourcing in the firm, and we are unable to control for these differences. The IT budget data may also exclude end-user computing investments made by the firm, which can often be substantial. In summary, most of the limitations of this analysis result from a lack of detailed information on IT expenses of firms during the time period of the study.

## Summary and Conclusions

THIS RESEARCH EMPIRICALLY EVALUATES the role of the IT infrastructure in supporting firm growth. In the first part of the analysis, we investigate the relationship between a firm’s growth opportunities, free cash flow, and level of IT spending. In the second part of the analysis, we examine the effect of a superior IT infrastructure on operating costs for high-growth firms. Our findings can be summarized as follows.

Two important insights emerge from the first part of the analysis. At the average level of free cash flow, there is no difference in IT spending levels between highgrowth and low-growth firms (approximately 1.6 percent of sales for both groups). However, as their free cash flow increases above this average level, important distinctions arise. High-growth firms increase their IT spending as their free cash flow increases, presumably to build up their IT infrastructure to support future growth. On the other hand, the IT spending level in low-growth firms is primarily determined by the median level of IT spending within their industry and is relatively insensitive to the free cash available. Since free cash flow represents the cash flow that the firm chooses (or is able) to reinvest in its operations, low-growth firms primarily invest the extra cash in areas other than IT. On the other hand, as free cash flow decreases, highgrowth firms have many more positive NPV opportunities that compete for the limited resources, and IT spending decreases as a result. For low-growth firms, there is less competition for resources, and IT spending remains constant even as free cash flow decreases. It is important to point out that our results do not imply that IT spending is constant for low-growth firms. Many other factors not captured by the model lead to the variability in IT spending levels. Our results simply indicate that free cash flow is not a major determinant of IT spending for low-growth firms.

Is higher IT spending a result of the agency costs associated with free cash flow? Based on Jensen’s free cash flow hypothesis [20] and its supporting empirical evidence, our results from the first part of the analysis indicate otherwise. Jensen argues that agency problems related to free cash flow are most severe in low-growth firms that do not have many positive NPV investment opportunities. However, we find that such firms do not increase their IT spending levels as their cash flow increases. On the other hand, high-growth firms that have the least severe agency problems according to Jensen’s hypothesis increase their IT spending when their free cash flow increases above the mean level.

The second part of the analysis in this research evaluates the effect of a superior IT infrastructure on operating costs in subsequent periods. We find that for high-growth firms, a superior IT infrastructure built through higher investments in the previous period leads to a lower average cost per unit of output in subsequent periods. Interestingly, no such relationship exists for low-growth firms. We conclude from this analysis that firms with high-growth expectations invest heavily in IT to effectively manage their cost of operations while achieving revenue growth. Without an effective IT infrastructure, any revenue growth would be accompanied by a significant increase in their cost of operations, as the complexity of the enterprise increases. This conclusion is supported through anecdotal evidence in the trade literature, which points to firms such as Amazon that build up their IT infrastructure in anticipation of future growth. Once again, it is important to point out that this is a possible explanation of the empirical results, and other explanations are also possible.

In summary, this research points to an indirect rather than a direct role of IT in enabling growth. Even if IT does not affect revenue growth directly, it provides the supporting infrastructure that permits growth without a corresponding increase in the complexity-related costs of the enterprise. Although a supporting role, this contribution is critical and strategic for the firm, because a lower-cost structure provides competitive advantage and pricing flexibility.
