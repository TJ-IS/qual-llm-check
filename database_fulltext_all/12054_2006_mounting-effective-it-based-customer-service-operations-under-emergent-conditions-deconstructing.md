# Mounting efective IT based customer service operations under emergent conditions: Deconstructing myth as a basis of understanding

Stephen Corea 米

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Department of Operations Research and Information Systems, Coventry CV4 7AL, United Kingdom

## Abstract

Recent research studies have revealed how social structures or contexts can lead to unanticipated and dynamic outcomes in IT based activities. However, they have not suficiently addressed the ques tion of how organizations can act efectively under such emergent conditions. This research sought to address this gap, by adopting a semiotic framework of analysis, and undertaking an in-depth, interpretive case study of the customer service operations at the call center of a large retail firm. The analysis of these operations takes into account the call center’s ‘myth’ – the underlying image on which its customer service aim or vision is based. This myth is used to highlight key tensions and incompatibilities arising in the center’s operations. These incongruities are used to identify improvements in the efectiveness of the center’s service practices and its use of IT. This research illustrates the potential value of using rhetorical formulations of performance aims as a means to engender useful understanding on the properties of IT use or organizational design in emergent contexts. - 2006 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IT use; Myth; Contradictions; Emergence; Efectiveness; Rhetorical; Semiotic; Customer service

## 1. Introduction

Information technology systems may be considered to a certain extent as pre-formu lated designs for organizational action, as they are used by firms to deliberately structure or prescribe the way work is done, or the way interactions are carried out with customers, suppliers and other parties. On the other hand however, numerous studies have shown that the use of IT systems in organizations is also significantly characterized by emergent forms and consequences – dynamic patterns of organizing or unanticipated outcomes of action which cannot be explained by a priori plans or intensions (Lee, 1999; Orlikowski, 1996).

There is thus a key requirement for theoretical approaches that can capture the complex, emergent nature of organizations (Lin & Cornford, 2000; Truex, 1991). Understanding emergent outcomes in IT based work activity is particularly important at present, given the frenetic pace of change and turbulent environments that many organizations now operate in (Orlikowski, 1996). The use of IT has amplified the unpredictability of emergent efects in organizational practices (Galliers & Baets, 1998; Lee, 1999).

Using such perspectives as structuration theory (Orlikowski, 2000), actor-network theory (Walsham, 1997) or situated action theory (Suchman, 1987), recent IS studies have revealed the emergent nature of IT based activities in terms of being a process, shaped by social and cognitive structures of human agency and the context in which they occur. Central to this stream of work is the diferent meanings or implications that social actors ascribe to the properties and use of IT (Markus & Robey, 1988; Truex, Baskerville, & Klein, 1999). These diferences are seen to engender the complex, unexpected outcomes characterizing emergent contexts of IT use.

However, this prevailing focus within the IS ‘emergence paradigm’ (Klein, 1996) may be seen to have left one area relatively unaddressed. Shulman (1999) asserts that while such studies have produced key insights on the structuring of organizational action, they ofer little guidance on understanding how efective organizational performance is enabled or inhibited by the use of IT. This suggested the need for a diferent approach to extend current IS understanding in this area. It appeared necessary to study emergent IT based action from a standpoint of organizational efectiveness, rather than process or social structure. This requires taking primary account of the functional role an organization is envisioned to fulfill, as expressed by its principle aim or vision.

The empirical context of call center-based service operations seemed a fruitful arena in which to apply this fresh stance in the investigation of emergent IT based action. Call centers are a modern IT based organizational form, that is at the forefront of a current trend of technological intensification of service activity (Taylor & Bain, 2000). They have had limited attention in past IS research, despite wide, dramatic rates of adoption (Boddy, 2000). A handful of IS studies have made quantitative assessments of the impact of IT on their work environment. Staf are seen to experience greater performance in service tasks, higher mental stress, diminished quality of staf relationships, and lower job satisfaction (Irving, Higgins, & Safayeni, 1986; Kraut, Dumais, & Koch, 1989; Knights & McCabe, 1998; Subramanyam & Krishnan, 2001; Turner, 1984). Few IS studies of call centers have been qualitative, however. Orlikowski (1996), studying the use of a Lotus Notes system at an ‘IT help-desk’ call center, found that work practices altered significantly in emergent ways, in terms of quality of knowledge, patterns of collaboration, and coordination and control features. Boddy (2000) also found that the implementation process of a new call center emerged, in unanticipated ways, from interactions between users, IT tools and structural properties.

Thus, while call centers have been seen to exhibit emergent features in their operations, there has been little qualitative research on the nature of their efectiveness as service worksites. Moreover, work conditions at these ubiquitous sites are often rife with tension and contradictions (Richardson & Howcroft, 2006), which are inadequately studied aspects of

IT use in IS research on emergence (Truex, 1991). The study reported here thus undertook to investigate the nature of efective IT based organizational action within the emergent conditions of call center operations.

In making an exploratory efort to study emergent IT based action from the standpoint of organizational efectiveness, this research was geared towards a particular focus. A central need was seen for addressing the rhetorical way by which primary functions or performance aims are often envisioned and expressed in companies. While many researchers tend to disregard the stylized, symbolic language that is used to communicate corporate goals or visions, others have urged the importance of this dimension (Gouldner, 1976). This research thus sought to take into account the rhetorical formulation of aims/visions as a means for understanding the efectiveness of IT based action, in an emergent organizational context.

## 2. Theoretical perspective

Organizational activity and IT use is typically geared towards fulfilling certain principal aims or visions. These fundamental performance goals, principles or visions are often formulated in a rhetorical manner as stylized statements or slogans expressed with a dramaturgical emphasis. Such statements appear to pose a key dificulty for research studies of IT use because they have generally been seen in the organizational studies field as expressing espoused rather than actual values, or as reflecting obvious interests (Argyris & Schon, 1978; Schein, 1992). Such rhetorical statements of aims thus tend to be ignored or debunked by researchers as being empty of any real significance. This may have precluded adequate treatment of their role in previous IS studies.

In contrast, theorists from the fields of semiotics and communication studies have long maintained that the rhetorical definitions of intensions and activities by organizations need to be taken into account seriously when studying them (Burke, 1962; Gouldner, 1976). Such symbolic language is considered crucial to the mobilizing of organizational action, being seen to shape the way that work is structured and achievement construed. Taking its cue from that perspective, this section introduces conceptual and analytical resources to develop the sense-making utility of the rhetorical dimension when studying the efective ness of IT based work practices.

## 2.1. Myth and inconsistency in organizational action

Barthes (1973) proposed a key concept in the study of rhetorical practices that is useful for understanding rhetorical definitions of organizational function, performance aims or visions. This was the concept of ‘myth’, which refers to a concrete social image or representation, that is commonly held among members of a social group, and which serves important functions. Before explaining those functions, it is important at the onset to recognize that Barthes’ concept of myth as defined above is strictly diferent from the various meanings of this term held in popular use: where it refers (disparagingly or negatively) to a false belief or misconception, or alternatively to a fictional story or legend. Instead, Barthes conceived of myth in a non-pejorative way, as having a key function agreed to by theorists of rhetorical studies (Burke, 1962; Gouldner, 1976). The role of a myth is to galvanize or inspire action among members of a social group, as follows.

A myth functions to present an uncomplicated, attractive image of how things are or how they should be, so as to motivate action among its recipients (Barthes, 1973). This attractive state of existence that is depicted owes its desirability to the highlighting of certain appealing features of attitude or behavior, at the expense of other incompatible or untoward aspects. Barthes (1973, p. 116) notes that: ‘‘Myth acts economically; it abolishes the complexity of human acts, it gives them the simplicity of essences, it does away with all dialectics, with any going back beyond what is immediately visible, it organizes a world without contradictions’’. Such simplification is geared at fostering unified, collective action by members of a social group, because the broadly agreeable image that is invoked provides a common point of identification to which those with diferent or rival interests can assent and rally to (Gouldner, 1976). Barthes (1973) also observed that a myth can serve as a perceptual frame for connoting or implying what needs to be done to achieve the desired state. It is thus similar to an extended metaphor, helping group members make sense of experience from a common point of perception (Chandler, 2001).

Barthes’ concept of myth and the functions it serves may be seen to be consistent with the characteristics of the rhetorical slogans or statements by which many companies formulate their fundamental vision, role or aim. Such formulaic statements distill a desired function or outcome of a set of activities into a dramaturgical phrase or image, to imply or order the behavior of those they address (Burke, 1962). For example, in response to industry deregulation and competition in the 1990s, the telecommunications firm Nortel undertook a major redesign of the structure of its corporate and IS units, based on the vision of ‘a World of Networks’ (Volkof & Newson, 1999). This myth served those functions noted earlier. It depicted (or promoted an understanding of) a current reality in which telecommunication networks are ubiquitous and indispensable elements. This conception of reality legitimized Nortel with a crucial role, lending impetus to a need for its staf to act urgently to establish it as a dominant player in that environment. This myth was also a metaphorical blueprint for action: Nortel reorganized its business units and governance structures in a matrix form, to match the requirements of that new ‘networked order’ in its environment.

The analytical value of the concept of myth is seen here to correspond to the complexities or contradictions of socially situated activity which it masks (i.e. so that shared action or belief may be galvanized). Giddens (1984) noted that the notion of contradictions is indispensable in the study of organizational or social action: ‘‘[It] expresses the main ‘fault lines’ in the structural constitution of social systems’’ (p. 193). IS studies have evoked strong recognition that IT based practices are often strained or threatened by incompatibilities or tensions in the social context (Truex, 1991). A significant need therefore exists for concepts or analytical tools to treat the nature of IT use accordingly, from an oppositional standpoint (Robey & Boudreau, 1999).

The concept of myth is amenable to addressing that need, immediately drawing attention to those contradictions which are accentuated in particular forms of IT use, or exposed when a fundamental vision is subjected to the tests of reality. For example, in the case of Nortel above, it is interesting to note that its restructuring efort to achieve the organizational vision of ‘a world of networks’ faced major dificulties, since the new structure introduced too much fragmentation and lack of central control in the firm’s operations (Volkof & Newson, 1999). According to the sense-making perspective which this paper is proposing, that outcome might be framed from the viewpoint of a ‘contradiction’ hidden or implicit in the company’s guiding myth – that a network structure invokes key challenges of coordination or control. In other words, myths may be seen to contain the seeds of contradiction in the uncomplicated images they present.

This may be seen to apply even in the case of myths that play on a sense of contradic tion or paradox to mobilize action. For example, the Danish firm Oticon re-engineered its business processes and use of IT in unconventional ways, spurred on by its CEO’s vision to ‘Think the Unthinkable’ (Bjørn-Andersen & Turner, 1998). A central ‘unthinkable change made by Oticon was to allow its workers to hold not one but many jobs i.e. work for diferent departments at the same time. However, the firm’s management was surprised to find in the course of this change that many workers preferred to stick to only one job. In retrospect, given ordinary human needs for certainty or stability, this unanticipated outcome might seem a very ‘thinkable’ one. It might be suggested, therefore, that in focusing on the ‘unthinkable’, the Oticon management failed in that situation to adequately recog nize the possibility of the ‘thinkable’ occluding the outcome.

It is therefore proposed that an analysis of myths (encapsulated in rhetorical formulations of performance aims) may be used to illuminate the emergent nature of IT based work activities from the standpoint of organizational efectiveness, by permitting an enframing or disclosure of critical tensions or inconsistencies that are implicated in such activ ity. This paper’s proposition enlists the use of an analytical schema, described next.

## 2.2. Signification systems

The semiotician Greimas (1987) sought to systematically elaborate key relations of value (or significance) characterizing the interaction of various elements of organizational or cultural practices. He viewed both oppositional and associative relations as essential features of a ‘system of signification’ by which the value/significance of a set of activities is ordered: ‘‘By definition, a system’s rules of injunctions describe compatibilities and incompatibilities (a system without incompatibilities would not be an ordered system)’’ (p. 52). He created an analytical schema to express a signification system. This figurative device, seen in Fig. 1, is also referred to as the ‘semiotic square’ in the social studies field

This schema is used during analysis to structure or to frame an understanding of social phenomena in terms of logical contrasts of value, significance or meaning. The canonica formulation of the schema (Jameson, 1987, p. XIV) is explained next.

This schema enables a more comprehensive analysis of binary oppositions, by permitting an enlargement of their implications. The upper corners of the square, S1 and S2, may represent the opposing terms of a particular concept, S. For example (see Fig. 1), the concept S could represent the concept of ‘absence of colors’ (Jameson, 1987). The two opposing terms that make up this concept would be ‘black’ (S1) and ‘white’ (S2). The bottom corners of the square, -S2 and -S1 (or ‘Not S2’ and ‘Not S1’ respectively) are generated by forming negatives, in a traverse direction, of the two opposing terms on the upper axis (S1 and S2). Thus, -S2 would represent ‘non-white’, while its opposing position, -S1, designates ‘non-black’. Both these bottom slots represent opposing positions of the concept -S, which might then perhaps suitably represent the concept ‘presence of color’. It can be seen at once that the opposing lower terms, -S2 and -S1, identify implicative positions not accounted for by the binary oppositions on the upper axis. For example, ‘non-white’ encompasses much more than ‘black’ (i.e. a range of colors). Moreover, diferent logical relations of value or significance (oppositional and associative) are represented by the various axes of the square: (1) contrary relations i.e. S1 vs. S2; (2) contradictory relations i.e. S1 vs. -S1; and (3) implicative relations (or relations of association/complementarity) i.e. S1 vs. -S2. Both contrary relations and contradictory relations designate opposing positions of value (i.e. incongruity or inconsistency), but contrarieties denote stronger or more forceful oppositions.

Jameson (1987) elaborates the use of this analytical schema, asserting its heuristic value for supporting interpretive research. It supplies a means to map interrelated themes or diagnostic interpretations that are encountered in the course of a study, thus helping to clarify or to generate understanding. It can be used in IS research to investigate the structuring of signification or value comprehensively. For example,<sup>1</sup> the concept of ‘telecommunicating’ can initially be represented by the polar opposite meanings of ‘work in ofice’ (S1) and ‘work at home’ (S2). This schema permits that immediate significance to be expanded to encompass the following meanings: ‘not work at home’ (-S2) and ‘not work at ofice’ (-S1). It is instantly perceived from this operation that the spatial dislocation implied by the notion of telecommunicating implies much more than solely the idea of working from home (as opposed to the ofice): it also implies other venues or spaces in which work may be carried out remotely via networked computer systems.

In line with this paper’s aim therefore, this schema is used here to provoke an insightful appreciation of IT based organizational activities in an emergent context. This entails using the schema to frame the significance of such activity in terms of an associated myth. The illustration here of this approach involved a case study of call center based customer service operations.

## 3. Methodology

This research sought to illustrate the utility of the concept of myth for investigating the emergent nature of IT based activity from the standpoint of organizational efectiveness. It was characterized by the assumptions of the interpretive approach, and the use of the interpretive case study method for data collection and empirical engagement.

## 3.1. Research approach

The strategy underlying this inquiry was informed by the assumptions of the interpretive approach (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1993), in which the nature of social phenomena is considered to be a shared, inter-subjective construction of the participants involved in the study. The focus of an IS researcher working in the interpretive tradition is the ‘accounting’ process: he/she seeks to elicit and register the accounts by which social actors render their world sensible. This enables the researcher to get inside the viewpoints of organizational members regarding the phenomena at stake. The aim of doing this however, is to go beyond: to uncover the ‘structures’ which explain such accounts or which reveal their deeper significance (Klein & Myers, 1999; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). If the first-hand descriptive accounts of actors are seen as primary accounts, then the role of the researcher is to employ appropriate ideas and concepts to articulate a secondary, meta-account i.e. one which succeeds in reconciling and illuminating those pri mary accounts, but from the higher-level perspective of an integrative, theoretical framework.

Spender (1989) notes that the researcher’s intuition also assists, along with the conscious application of concepts or analytical schemas, in this task of deriving new structures of meaning from primary accounts, and transforming their ‘episodic’ coherence (i.e. their separate, disjointed nature) into a synthesized, structural (i.e. theoretical) coherence. The knowledge thus derived is mediated and inter-subjective, formed from the accounts of study participants and the theoretical and experiential grounding of the researcher. The results of interpretive research thus constitute a way of seeing (Walsham, 1993) or an alternative rationality (Spender, 1989), whose validity rests on its degree of plausibility and insightfulness (Walsham, 1995; Galliers, 1991). This approach can produce useful insights on complex issues, which might then be used to inform other studies of similar phenomena in diferent contexts (Walsham, 1993).

## 3.2. Data collection

This research was carried out using the variant of the interpretive case study method described by Walsham (1995). in which the formulation and elaboration of a fresh theoretical perspective is undertaken through the dual use of case data for concept generation and empirical substantiation i.e. to adapt existing or new concepts and assess their appli cability. The case study contributed in developing the relevance of those concepts presented earlier, by supplying a rich context of empirical observations to specify their theoretical utility. An in-depth investigation of IT based call center operations was chosen for this engagement. This empirical environment was seen as ideal for two reasons. Call centers are centrally implicated in the rhetorically expressed commitments of many contemporary organizations to provide efective customer service to their clients (Taylor & Bain, 2000). In addition, call centre environments are typically characterized by conflicting tensions in work conduct and IT use (Richardson & Howcroft, 2006; Korczynski, 2001). Permission was thus obtained from a major UK-based organization to carry out an indepth study of the activities of its call center.

The case data were collected onsite during multiple visits to the call centre made between February and May 2001. The data consisted partly of 22 semi-structured interviews held with staf at the center, who fell into three broad groups: (i) managerial staf, such as the corporation’s director of customer service, the call center manager, and the manager of the center’s business interface team; (ii) service staf, such as customer service representatives (CSRs) and leaders of the CSR teams; (iii) support staf, such as the teamleader of the CSR trainers and the center’s IT support analyst. That data was supplemented by non-participant observation of work activities of CSRs (including periods of listening-in to calls), informal conversations with staf, and review of company documents (e.g., customer service logs, service manual). Inquiries were focused on the center’s performance aims, the factors shaping the development of its activities, its interactions with its parent firm and customers, and key challenges faced in work roles, information practices and use of IT. The interview questions were structured as ‘what are’, ‘why’ or ‘how’ type open-ended questions and probes, drawn from a guideline prepared in advance for each interview. ## 3.3. Data analysis

The collected case data were analyzed using the hermeneutic method, following Ricoeur (1991). The hermeneutic method of data analysis has seen useful application in past IS research (Butler, 1998; Lee, 1994; Boland, 1991). It involves ‘reading’ data on organizational phenomena (i.e. in such forms as interview accounts, company documents, descriptive notes of artifacts or researcher’s experiences) as text-analogues; an approach first proposed by Ricoeur (1971), and introduced to the IS field by Boland (1991).

The aim is to make sense of these organizational text-analogues as a coherent whole or system (Ricoeur, 1991): an ordered appropriation that comprehensively integrates the parts of the phenomena under study (e.g. IT use, actions, events) through interlinking relations. An iterative process of engagement, termed the hermeneutic circle, is required for this. The study’s data were subjected to several cycles of close examination, in which potential themes or layers of meaning were identified, and then, along with their implicit assumptions, made the object of subsequent questioning and revision in a progressive spiral of analysis and reflection, until a relatively stable and insightful construction of knowledge was reached. This data analysis process involved three phases (in a recurring cycle) identified by Ricoeur (1991): pre-figuration, configuration, and re-figuration. These phases are detailed next.

‘Pre-figuration’ (Ricoeur, 1991) involved identifying preliminary patterns or structures of understanding regarding the mass of IT related actions, means, circumstances and outcomes in the data. This was done by coding interview and other notes into meaningful categories as they were gathered (e.g. service objectives, service challenges, IT capabilities, IT constraints), and re-reading them to surface major themes (e.g. emergence, control of per formance) and issues (e.g. tensions, contradictions). The understanding gained was useful but fragmented and episodic.

‘Configuration’ is described by Ricoeur (1991) as a critical phase of hermeneutic analysis that seeks a mediated fit between the empirically-rooted understanding of heterogeneous factors obtained in the prefiguration phase, and the theoretical understanding gained in the re-figuration phase. Resolving this central challenge of interpretation requires a ‘configurational arrangement’ or synthesis, by which the fragmented, episodic comprehension of data from the preceding phase can be synthesized into a coherent, integrated matrix of significance (Ricoeur, 1991). The need to achieve this comprehensive inter-relational form of understanding in relation to this study’s data provoked a search for suitable theoretical resources on which to underpin it. The semiotic square schema (Greimas, 1987) was eventually found to be a useful conceptual tool to carry out this ‘work of synthesis’ (Ricoeur, 1991), providing a means to represent systems of signification that ordered the case data in illuminative ways (i.e. umbrella oppositions that formed a common denominator for the issues and themes identified in the text-analogues).

In this phase, the semiotic square permitted an understanding of call center efectiveness and IT use to be analytically framed in an inclusive, systematic and incisive way. This schema also helped to encatalyze or engender fresh understanding of the data (e.g. properties of IT use discussed shortly in Section 5.2), a capability noted by Jameson (1987). The achievement of this synthesized or fresh understanding of data through the use of the semi otic square, however, did not result from a one-time act, but developed from a maturing process of appreciation of the data that evolved with key changes of understanding in the other two phases of the interpretive cycle. Significant engagement with the data, involving repeated scrutiny of the data and the themes and issues identified in association, was thus found to be necessary before comprehensive patterns of significance (and ways of repre senting them on the schema) coalesced, and insights crystallized.

The ‘re-figuration’ phase (Ricoeur, 1991) of hermeneutic analysis involved activities of theoretical learning that reframed or cast new light on the data, thus augmenting or challenging earlier perceptions or interpretations, and invoking new patterns of understanding. The activities that constituted this theoretical grounding included study of IS research lit erature and periods of reflection. For example, the recognition of and focus on the emergent nature of IT based action in this paper, which emerged from the preliminary understanding of data (in the pre-figuration phase), was augmented in this phase by reading other IS studies on emergence. On a related note, the primary concept of myth (Barthes, 1973) was found in this phase to be initially pertinent for understanding the center’s rhetorically expressed service aim, and the organization of its work environment. In subsequent cycles, as increased focus (fuelled by growing awareness of issues raised by the data) came to be placed on analyzing service efectiveness in these operations, the concept of myth grew correspondingly in prominence, as it was seen to lend itself to highlighting critical inconsistencies or tensions in the center’s activities. This emphasis, in turn, consequently helped in the ‘configuration’ phase to crystallize an integrated understanding of customer service efectiveness around the call center’s myth, involving various elements or factors positioned in relations of contrariety, contradiction or implication to that myth (as illustrated shortly).

Thus this iterative process of data analysis permitted patterned regularities in the data (i.e. themes, issues/dilemmas) to be inductively identified, theoretically referenced and illuminated in an ordered, inter-related way. The understanding and insights derived are reported next.

## 4. Illustration: case study

This section illustrates the application of those concepts and schema introduced earlier. It begins by describing the customer service operations at HealthCare Corporation (a pseudonym), and the myth characterizing the call center’s primary service aim/vision. The challenges faced by staf in mounting these service activities are also described. Subsequently (Subsection 4.5), the form of analysis proposed earlier is applied. The efectiveness of these service operations, and the capabilities and constraints of IT support by which they are afected, is conceptualized and illuminated by analytically framing their emergent outcomes in terms of the center’s myth.

## 4.1. Company background

Healthcare Corporation (HealthCo) is a major British retail firm that manufactures and distributes health and beauty products and services. It engages in a portfolio of businesses, but its main concern is a large pharmacy business: it runs a chain of over 1400 pharmacy stores and outlets in the UK. The wide range of products and services marketed in its stores fall into broad categories: beauty (cosmetics, toiletries etc), medicines, healthcare dispensing, gift merchandise, photographic, baby and food products. Each category is produced and administered by a separate business product unit in the company. HealthCo runs a successful customer loyalty card scheme, which has 13 million cardholders. The ‘Wellbeing Card’ allows customers to accumulate points from purchases at HealthCo stores, which can be traded subsequently for other products.

HealthCo has traditionally operated along decentralized lines. Its business product units, chain of pharmacy stores and other smaller business concerns, run relatively independently. The centralized functions at its headquarters include a corporate administration, a marketing and MIS department. A centralized customer services function has grown in size relatively recently. Prior to the mid-1990s, customer service responsibilities had mainly been devolved to the individual pharmacy stores, which were expected to deal locally with inquiries or complaints by customers. Serious complaints, or those to be handled at a higher level, were passed to the specific ‘business product unit’ responsible. In the mid-1990s however, concern grew among the company’s senior management regarding such factors as key inconsistencies across the business product units in the type and quality of responses to customers, lack of capability in promoting customer loyalty, and falling customer satisfaction ratings in surveys. This led to a perceived need for centralizing customer service operations through a call center, which was imple mented in 1997.

## 4.2. Call centre organization

By 2001, the HealthCo call center had 65 full-time staf, including 45 customer service representatives (CSRs) who handle calls or letters/e-mail, and a 16-member ‘business interface team’ that carries out coordination tasks and work linking the center with other HealthCo units. The center deals with around 12,000 calls, 800 e-mails and 1,000 letters per week (approx. 70% enquiries, 25% complaints, 5% comments). Specific service performance targets are observed on a day-to-day basis by CSR staf. These concrete targets, known as ‘Key Performance Indicators’ (KPIs), included call-handling time-based targets and other commitments, such as: answer 90% of calls within 20 s; respond to all letter con tacts with at least an acknowledgement within 48 h; produce monthly reports to the business units by the 10th working day of each month via the intranet etc.

## 4.2.1. Work roles

The CSRs, divided into teams, undertake a range of permanent call-handling duties, that involve answering inquiries or complaints about products/services under diferent categories of calls e.g. General; Wellbeing; Optician. The CSRs also perform ‘occasional call-handling duties often linked to marketing campaigns. They keep to prescribed time periods and other targets in dealing with customers i.e. average length of call, percentage of calls answered in 20 seconds. Problematic calls not closed within target times are passed to a few specialized CSRs, known as information seekers, to resolve. These CSRs have had several years of job experience working in other departments within HealthCo. Their knowledge of work mores and information resources in those units is a crucial asset in their problem-solving role.

While the CSR teams deal with customers, the principle role of the call center’s ‘business interface team’ is to interact with other units within HealthCo, carrying out information gathering and coordination tasks required for supporting the CSRs in their role. It also produces numerous regular or ad-hoc reports on customer issues for HealthCo management staf. The team also works with relevant corporate units to ensure that follow-up actions in response to complaints are duly carried out (e.g. process changes, compensatory or legal action). ## 4.2.3. Work environment

The call centre worksite consists of a large, comfortably furnished open-plan ofice, with CSR workgroups, managerial and support staf seated in clustered desk formations at diferent areas within. No partitions exist throughout the ofice, permitting easy visual and verbal contact across its length. Staf frequently interact across their desks and seating zones. Comfortable rest and refreshment facilities set up in a corner are amply used by them. This pleasant work setting was playfully described by the manager as ‘‘an open range rather than a battery type farm’’. Staf seemed generally com mitted in their roles. The positive work environment and culture promoted at the center may have contributed to its low annual rates of staf attrition (2%) and absenteeism (2%) – well below industry averages of 15–20% in both areas (Income Data Services, 2000).

Several CSRs described the nature of their service activity as ‘‘reactive’’. The call center’s operations on a daily basis are marked by volatility, as it is dificult to anticipate what issues (i.e. inquiries, complaints) customers may ring up about. Correspondingly, recruitment of new CSRs involves finding people who can not only cope but thrive in this environment. Desired attributes include having an outgoing, friendly and emphatic personality, able to project a caring response. Candidates are asked for a contactable phone number when submitting applications. Recruitment staf always contact them initially by phone to arrange interviews and ask basic questions. The recruiters place particular emphasis in these contacts on assessing how candidates react or sound in a somewhat stressful situation (i.e. having the recruiter’s call sprung on them) since this will be an incessant part of the job role. A similar parallel involves test calls to unsuspecting CSRs by project staf from other HealthCo units, during pilot trials of new services hosted for those units.

## 4.3. Myth in service performance

From the onset of its operations, the call center’s management formulated a fundamental vision for mobilizing a work environment of high-quality service provision. This was formulated in terms of a mandate of ‘‘surprising the customer’’: CSRs were to provide service that delighted customers and surpassed their expectations. The first page of the center’s staf service manual (Table 3) displays this injunction, that was described by the center’s manager as its ‘‘fundamental performance aim’’ (he meant ‘aim’ in a broad, essential sense, that encompassed, but should not be confused with, the specific service targets or KPIs mentioned earlier). The company’s director of customer services referred to that mandate as the center’s ‘‘service vision’’. She noted that:

Our [call] center’s role goes beyond simply providing information or resolving problems. We want to build lasting relationships with our customers, which can only be done by meeting and exceeding their expectations. It is the vision which drives our center.

This fundamental aim or vision was linked to the ‘spirit’ said to have been instilled by the firm’s founder, who had laid down a strong ethos of friendly, professional service to customers. Surveys of the public had consistently shown that these qualities of service were a major reason they shopped at HealthCo stores. The call center’s management was thus seeking to duplicate, in every call or letter dealt with at the center, that quality of pleasurable interaction experienced by customers in stores. The idea of delivering a customer service that ‘surprised and delighted’ was conceived as a means to embed and mirror this central aim.

The call center’s management has sought to evoke this theme of delightful surprise in concrete motivational practices, that center on a popular staf reward and recognition scheme known as ‘Magical Moments’. Besides monthly and quarterly awards to teams and individuals in this scheme, admirable one-of acts of customer service by CSRs or other staf are rewarded by a ‘lucky dip’ from a basket of sealed prizes set in a corner of the ofice (e.g. wine, gift vouchers, cinema vouchers). The manager noted that they actively encourage and reward ‘‘innovative ways of surprising the customer’’. In relation to this, a ‘good ideas’ scheme is run, in which staf are rewarded for suggestions even if not implemented (i.e. cinema voucher for non-adopted ideas; bigger rewards if adopted). No diferentiation is made between internal and external customers in these schemes, since not all staf deal directly with the public (e.g. the business interface team).

In line with its vision, the center’s management has sought to establish a work culture and procedures that would allow staf to ‘‘take ownership’’ of calls and exercise initiative in pleasing customers. The mandate to ‘surprise the customer’ has taken concrete form in certain practices of IT use. CSRs have the discretion to gladden customers when they call, or to recompense them for inconveniences or problems that they have experienced or complain about. If the customer has a Wellbeing Card (as is typical), then the common practice is for CSRs to award extra points on the card (the points convert to exchange value for HealthCo products). The customer’s account is retrieved from the database and points added to the card’s total. The CSRs are under guidelines to grant any number of points up to a total of 250, but they may exceed this if they feel that the circumstances call for it (they need to justify this to their supervisors however, who are generally perceived as supportive of such actions). Alternatively, CSRs can arrange for the customers to be sent vouchers, beauty products, flowers or other gifts by using a function in CustomerQ. Besides such available means to ‘delight’ customers, the center’s manager also stressed the importance of CSRs being equipped beforehand with relevant information for responding to customer calls. To meet or exceed customer expectations, he stated that it was necessary to be proactive – ‘‘ready with the answer before the phone rings’’.

Within the demanding service task environment of the call center, this image is a compelling representation of how staf could see themselves acting meaningfully in relation to customers contacting the center i.e. in the empowered (or ‘heroic’) role of actively pleasing those customers. ## 4.4. Challenges and dificulties in service performance

The development of service activities performed at the call centre since its inauguration has been marked to a significant extent by expediency. The scope of duties undertaken by its staf owe their establishment both to the emergence of corporate initiatives that use the center as a point of contact, as well as the increasing range and variability of demands posed by customers. Key challenges that have been faced in these service operations are described next.

## 4.4.1. Coordination with parent firm

The creation and implementation of the call center had largely been an internal project by the customer service department, without significant participation by other units. Consequently, the business interface team faced key dificulties from the start in their role as intermediaries between the center and the parent firm. They were handicapped by a lack of formally instituted channels of communication and support from the business product units. Such arrangements are necessary to facilitate timely, accurate flows of information for the CSRs to handle inquiries. Such arrangements are also needed for setting in motion suitable responses to incidents e.g. a risk assessment meeting to evaluate the dangers posed by a product that has caused an injury, and to activate follow-up actions. The center’s staf thus had to exercise their own initiative to forge crucial working relationships with staf in those corporate units, and convince them of the benefit of working in partnership, as noted by members of the business interface team:

We had to make sales pitches to key players in the business units. We told them the type of information we could provide, how we could prioritize it in terms of issues they could work on to get real benefits.

Some of them [staf in business product units] saw customers only as a source of niggling complaint. We had to show them how the feedback could be a source of value.

Customer feedback and query-based reports derived from data in the CustomerQ system, and distributed through e-mail or the intranet, have been key forms of collateral in this process of ‘‘persuasion’’. This feedback to corporate units has been used to efect tangible improvements in such areas as the marketing and packaging of products, enhancement of product and service features, and the withdrawal of faulty products. The center’s staf have thus gained (and continue to seek) credibility and leverage, by using the outputs of IT systems to secure the vital working relationships and flows of information needed to support the center’s activities.

## 4.4.2. IT and information assets

A key challenge in handling customer inquiries or complaints is having adequate access to information. The dificulties faced at the center are mainly due to the range and variability of issues by callers, and to required information not being available or appearing as discrepancies on the center’s IT systems. The product information on the firm’s legacy mainframe database, as well as information in the CustomerQ database, have both presented key problems of search and retrieval. A common fault is inadequate specification of details. The warehouse inventory system is a repository of data on a large range of items formerly and currently marketed by HealthCo. The data stored in this system is well suited for purposes of inventory control, processing, and internal management reporting, but does not include suficient descriptive detail for CSRs to efectively answer the kinds of queries often raised by customers. The product data typically lacks details on such simple features as the shape, color, or dimensions of product items. It does not include information regarding the uses of the product, or other qualities that customers may want to know in order to make assessments of suitability. This lack of details limits the quality of response CSRs provide. The problem of inadequate specification in the CustomerQ system has to do with the index categories by which the data in this system is organized. Some of the indexes have proved too general in their categorization, making it dificult to locate specific information for responding to queries. Eforts at reprogramming a wider range of categories by the center’s IT technician had produced some improvement, but to a limited extent. Another feature that the CSRs cited as highly lacking in the center’s IT-based resources is digitized pictorial images of the company’s products. Customers often make inquiries which might be answered more easily if CSRs simply had pictures by which to visualize products being discussed. These problems in the level of IT functionality and information assets are seen to have prevented the required level of ‘‘visibility’’ of company-wide operations and customer-relevant information needed to respond efectively to customer inquiries. Reflect ing on these shortcomings, the center’s manager noted:

They are like blind spots. They block the full view of product and customer we need to get better one-call resolution rates.

In addition, there are other IT-related problems, not involving the lack of information, that hamper efective work performance by CSRs. This includes frequent occurrences of slow network and application response times, and the use of too many software applications by CSRs. The lack of integration between various software packages at the center was cited by its manager as being a significant barrier to more eficient performance. CSRs are prevented from working faster in the course of handling calls by the need to frequently switch from one application to another, and the need to adjust to diferent procedures of use in each application. Staf from the company’s centralized MIS department had been called in to undertake improvements, but they have been slow to take action to address the shortcomings, being bogged down by other projects and IT problems in the company that had taken precedence over these dificulties. The center’s manager saw this as an instance of the lower priority given by some corporate staf to supporting the call center’s operations, because of the perception of the center as being ‘‘merely a complaint-handling function’’. He felt that part of the solution to improve the center’s IT systems lay in the need to raise the center’s status in corporate resource-allocation decisions.

## 4.4.3. Expansion in scope of duties

The growth in scope of the call center’s activities has been driven by the expectations of HealthCo’s senior management that the center demonstrate its value to other corporate units in HealthCo. The rise in workload has been fueled by temporary call-handling duties for marketing campaigns devised in collaboration with those units. These added duties are not always received favorably by CSR supervisors or staf. They are seen to pose two kinds of problems. First, the support given has on several occasions badly stretched the center’s resources. This problem lies with the dificulty of predicting the level of response to those campaigns. An example cited by a supervisor as typical was a marketing scheme that promoted discounted bookings of conference facilities. They had been told by the marketing department to expect 250 calls per week for the 6 weeks of the promotion. However in the first week alone, well over 1000 calls came in, resulting in a period of serious degradation in performance. These cases of overloading are a special worry for supervisors, who need to ensure that the call-handling performance targets are maintained.

The second type of problem involves task instructions, pertaining to those schemes, that are given to CSRs to observe during call interactions. These instructions are issued by marketing department staf responsible for the schemes. Such instructions have often been seen as running counter to the desire of supervisors and CSRs to rein in the direction and pace of interactions. Dissimilar outlooks line the seam of discord. To the marketing department staf, for example, an instruction for the CSRs to ask customers a ‘simple question at the end of each conversation, and to record the response in their system, seemed easy enough to perform. In practice, however, this task unduly prolonged the call interactions. Customers typically did not react in the manner anticipated by the marketing staf. The simple question incurred other questions, clarifications, or comments. This outcome, repeated in hundreds of calls, afected the ability of CSRs to keep to their timebased performance targets. Disgruntled CSR supervisory staf see such instructions as betraying poor understanding by marketing staf of the impact of these additional tasks on their eforts to meet performance targets. Marketing staf are seen as not appreciating the dificulty of steering customer responses, as one CSR supervisor observed: ‘‘They have no idea of the knock-on efect of each extra task they have us do’’. From the standpoint of the marketing department however, the innumerable customers contacting the call center were seen to present a valuable market research opportunity. Significant strategic value could be derived from probing customers about their views or tastes, as noted by a member of that department’s management:

Our success depends on understanding not just what our customers want but why. When they buy shower gel for example, are they looking for soap, or lubrication? By knowing the diference we can make a diference to their lives.

While the center’s management staf have been seeking ways to expand the portfolio of services, they have also been significantly concerned with controlling or reversing a consistent trend of rising numbers of calls from customers. The rise in contacts also included growing email trafic, seen as a concern by the manager because handling emails required ‘‘too much back and forth’’ i.e. email communications can be time-consuming. Substantial increases in staf efort or other resource outlays are required to cope with the service demands posed by this proliferation. These increased commitments are at odds with management’s eforts to minimize its short and long-term operational costs, and so a key aim has been to stabilize customer contact volumes.

Ironically, the high quality of the service provided by the center’s staf is seen by its manager to have contributed to mounting work commitments. It is perceived to have induced an inordinate preference among customers to contact the center with inquiries or complaints which they could or should, according to the manager, have directed to their local stores, where they can be resolved more eficiently (for an example of this, see the notes on a customer complaint in Table 1 earlier: in that incident, the customer might have resolved her issues directly with staf at the store, rather than contacting the center to act as an intermediary). The center’s manager has been grappling with discriminating what ought to constitute the ‘‘stock focus’’ of CSRs. He felt this was important to determine, because if clear boundaries were not set, then the CSRs might ‘‘stick their head above the parapet’’ too often – suggest too much willingness to assist customers with their inquiries or demands – thus drawing greater expectations and requests that would then cause further unnecessary displacement of workload i.e. the center ends up dealing with inquiries that might have been resolved directly at the stores.

Other factors may also have contributed to the increasing contact volumes. HealthCo is a household name in the United Kingdom especially in regard to health, beauty, and children’s products. Members of the public aware of the center as a point of contact are thought to phone with queries on any products within those broad categories, simply because HealthCo is the first familiar name that occurs to them as a potential source of answers. Phoning to obtain advice or information, especially for older people, is much easier than physically visiting a store to do so The company’s reputation is thus seen as subjecting the center to rising casual inquiries as public awareness of its existence as a service grows. On a separate note, the awarding of free Wellbeing points seems to have bred an unexpected outcome. It is perceived by the CSR supervisors that a growing familiarity with this aspect of the center’s practices has encouraged some customers to contact it on the expectation of free points when expressing dissatisfaction or grievances. CSRs are aware of certain callers who make repeated complaints and exclude these from such action.

The center’s management has consequently devoted significant attention to finding ways of moderating the rapid growth of contacts. Mass customer awareness of the call center derives mainly from such acts of publicity as the advertisement of the center’s telephone number and its status as a point of inquiry on the back of every purchase receipt obtained at HealthCo stores. Recently, the call center’s management took the unusual step of significantly reducing the size and prominence of that contact information on receipts. This was a discreet attempt to diminish public awareness and temper the rising calls. Another recent measure to trim call volumes is the efort by the center’s business interface team to place an increasing amount of commonly sought information and FAQ sheets on the firm’s internet website.

## 4.4.4. Service procedures

Many of the operational challenges faced by the center are seen by its management staf to stem from its relatively recent origin as an operational unit in HealthCo – from being ‘‘bolted on late’’ onto preexisting processes and structures, as the manager described it. This late arrival was seen to have produced, to a certain extent, a mismatch between the center’s requirements and the firm’s legacy procedures and infrastructure. HealthCo’s traditional orientation towards logistics management and decentralized operations were seen as responsible for processes and priorities that impeded the call center’s eforts to improve its service performance.

One example of this concerns a problem with the use of the mainframe-based warehouse inventory system by CSRs. The product records in this system are indexed by a Product-ID field. When customers call up to inquire about particular products, the CSRs find and retrieve the corresponding record of the product from this system. This retrieval process however, often takes too much time. Customers do not know the Product-IDs of the items they are looking for, since such information is not made available in the Health-Co’s marketing catalogue or advertisements provided to them. All that callers typically know is the popular name of the product, which may often difer from its technical name as stored in the system. Thus, it takes some efort to pull up the correct product records. The CSRs get customers to name the item by its general or catalogue name, or describe it by its main features. Searching with this information usually pulls up several potential candidates. The required product can then be identified and retrieved; but if still not recognizable the CSR have to continue asking customers for more detail while checking against search results for a match. Some CSRs have adopted a workaround to this, meticulously writing down the Product-IDs from the warehouse system beside associated products in their own copy of the marketing catalogue. When the need arises, those CSRs first perform manual searches to locate the product in their printed catalogue (that also has pictures that can be matched against customer descriptions) and then use the Product-ID to get its details from the system. In both computerized or manual searches, the correct product is almost always eventually retrieved.

However, the time wasted on performing searches might be averted if Product-IDs were published in HealthCo’s marketing brochure or advertisements for customers to quote when they phoned up. This obvious solution had been raised with relevant decision-makers in the marketing department. However, the marketing staf insisted that it would be inappropriate to print those Product-IDs in catalogues, on the grounds that these codes were not user-friendly and would not make sense to customers. The call center’s manager also cited another reason: that the thousands of products managed by the business product units changed often (e.g. withdrawn or substituted by newer products) and thus it was too problematic to keep the marketing department updated so that Product-IDs in catalogues are current. Thus, the CSRs continue to cope with this constraint. Supervisory staf viewed this as reflecting a lack of priority by other units towards changing their procedures to support the center’s service eforts.

On the whole however, despite the hitherto described challenges in service performance, the call center’s management has been encouraged by perceptions of a rising sense of recognition among HealthCo corporate units regarding the value of the center’s eforts. Customer feedback passed to business product units has efected tangible improvements. The center’s management has also perceived intangible benefits from the center’s eforts, such as the gradual re-orientation of HealthCo’s operations towards greater customer focus and service eficacy.

In addition, ITrelated shortcomings, such as inadequacies in information resources on legacy systems (e.g. warehouse inventory system), shortcomings of functionality (i.e. limitations of search mechanisms, lack of systems integration, slow response times), and support from other corporate units (e.g. non-provision of Product-ID to customers, low priority accorded by MIS department to solving center’s IT problems) are contributing to a significant shortfall in service preparations. The inadequate access to information resources or information channels in the parent firm also reflects the dificulty of operating the call center’s centralized inquiry or complaint-handling activities in a corporate environment mainly geared in its systems or procedures to decentralized activity and decision making. The success of deliberate eforts by staf at the call center to heighten customer satisfaction has led to some unwelcome consequences: customers being seen to take advantage of those beneficial arrangements, thus ‘anticipating’ the center’s designs. These actions, seen to constitute mildly aberrant or opportunistic behavior, include the displacement of casual inquiries from stores to the call center, and contacting the center rather than stores to make complaints because of the potential to gain Wellbeing points in recompense. Such outcomes appear dificult to control or abrogate, however, given that satisfying customers and establishing strong bonds with them is a fundamental priority of work organization at the center. The various elements that constitute this dimension thus represent emergent situations in which the deliberate intensions and preparations of the center’s staf are to a certain extent being ‘undone’ in unexpected ways. While the functional capacities of IT systems have thus enabled the center’s staf to carry out useful service activities, these systems have not equipped the staf to prevent certain actions on the part of the customer that are seen as not desirable. ## 4.5.2. Service efectiveness in an emergent context

A synoptic appreciation of the emergent nature of HealthCo’s call center operations has been formed above, sensitized in inter-relational terms through a signification system based on the center’s myth. This understanding can be used in two ways to help clarify the response that the center’s management staf may adopt: by raising awareness, and supporting sense-making. A clear benefit of mapping the characteristics of the center’s use of IT against the axial positions of the signification system is a comprehensive identification of the way IT is enabling or inhibiting customer service performance. Suitable interventions to improve those information systems can thus be made, based on this heightened awareness of existing shortcomings.

Secondly, this understanding of the center’s operations, framed in counter-factual terms, might be used by management to make sense of how to respond in a prudent way. One example of such an approach is illustrated next, with reference to the schema in Fig. 3, in which various dimensions of the center’s ability to respond to customer service needs have been super-imposed over the signification system used in the preceding analysis.

A critical issue that the call center’s management must address is the significant lack of visibility regarding HealthCo’s operations and its products, which has hampered the eficacy of CSR responses to customer inquiries/complaints. This stems from the limitations of information resources on IT systems and the shortcomings in their functionality e.g. search mechanisms and systems integration. This current state, tending towards CSRs being ‘surprised by the customer’, may be taken to suggest the obvious need for the center’s management to focus on proactive measures to increase the range of data resources and retrieval capability (i.e. ‘not to be surprised by the customer’), so as to enable CSRs to be equipped with tools and assets to actively resolve calls and exceed caller expectations (i.e. ‘to surprise the customer’). Such a transformation, that may be seen constituting an ‘obvious’ response, is mapped by the bold arrow shown in Fig. 3. However, as highlighted by the preceding analysis, this is not a balanced or sustainable solution, as the achievement of such a state of high-quality service provision can promote (as it appears to have done before) a rise in volume of ‘unnecessary’ calls (casual inquiries, repeat complaints) i.e. accentuate the contradictory state of ‘being anticipated by the customer’. Doing so may thus incur increased costs in commitment of resources and efort.

This may be taken to suggest the need for the center to provide information that increases the mass availability of product/customer-relevant information to the public, but without creating a corresponding need to commit added resources (i.e. to promote the visibility of information to customers while yet remaining ‘invisible’ in response). This may be addressed by increasing the range and depth of relevant information and appropriate functionality on web-pages at the firm’s internet site e.g. good search engine, detailed product catalogue information (with pictures), FAQ sheets, forms for structured reporting of complaints. In other words, a second transformation is needed, made concurrently and with as much efort as the first. This ‘balancing’ transformation is mapped by the broken arrow in Fig. 3. The intention is to move away from the state of being ‘surprised by the customer’, by proactively assembling the necessary range of information assets (i.e. ‘not to be surprised by the customer’) and then placing them online for mass public access. This will help mitigate growing call volumes, and help forestall the rise of unnecessary calls (i.e. avoid ‘being anticipated by the customer’), but in a passive way i.e. not requiring CSR eforts.

Thus, given the emergent context in which it is embedded, two tangential transformations are needed to prepare the centre with IT-based capabilities to deliver efective service operations. This illustrates how analyzing emergent IT based action from a standpoint of myth can be used to aid sense-making eforts at generating insight or new perspective on improving performance. Such sense-making should be undertaken with suficient circumspection. For example, it may be noted that care needs to be exercised in the above suggested move towards ‘passive service’ (i.e. using web-based functionality to stem rising call volumes), since such a move might displease (not exceed the expectations of) some customers. Jameson (1987) asserts that a contradiction can never be resolved with finality. He observes that while social actors may alter a situation through action so that (in a Hegelian sense) a contradiction is rendered irrelevant, its place however, is likely to be substituted by a fresh contradiction. This perspective can alert organizations to what new incompatibilities they might unlock, in acting to suppress an old one.

## 5. Discussion

Further explanation and discussion follows next, regarding the analytical use of myth to illuminate the emergent nature of IT based organizational efectiveness, as illustrated above. The understanding gained on the properties of IT use and call center operations, in a customer service environment, is also reviewed subsequently in turn.

## 5.1. Enframing action through myth

The rhetorically stated aim of ‘surprising the customer’ constituted the call center’s myth: a stirring image of the role of staf as empowered, active agents. This image contrasted sharply with the reactive character of the center’s operations, and the complications of dealing with the requirements of a large, unpredictable range of callers. The case analysis illustrated how such emergent outcomes can be framed and illuminated. A myth may be deconstructed – analytically unfolded to account for multiple zones of oppositional and associative consequence – in order to highlight the contextually-rooted complexities of IT based work performance, and infer suitable enhancements. The utility of this deconstructive form of analysis is further clarified next.

## 5.1.1. Organizational efectiveness in emergent conditions

A sizeable body of IS research has sought to account for the complex, recalcitrant nature of IT based organizational activities. These ‘IS emergence studies’ arose from the 1980s onwards in response to a previously dominant stream of IS research which had emphasized deterministic, ‘rational actor’ models of IT use (Markus & Robey, 1988). Such models reduced the nature of organizational functioning or the use of IT to simplistic cause-and-efect chains with predictable consequences. They assumed that human meaning or significance is straightforward and given, and that all actors embody correct, regular ways of behaving or perceiving and alike subscribe to one common goal of maximizing economic eficiency.

Contrary to such assumptions however, IS emergence studies have instead disclosed the complex, unpredictable, indefinite nature of much of organizational activity. They have revealed that meanings associated with practices of IT use are often problematic, ambiguous or contested, and that such practices are typically shaped by the ‘irrational’ actions of actors holding dissimilar aims or interests (Markus, 1983). Such studies (e.g. Ciborra, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000; Walsham, 1997) have uncovered in rich detail various processes by which social, institutional or cognitive structures (e.g. legitimacy, power) dynamically shape the actions of organizational actors around the use of IT, and lead to emergent outcomes. It might be asserted however, that the ‘success’ of these recent IS emergence studies has created a particular theoretical dificulty. This dificulty is in figuring out how to qualitatively assess the eficacy of IT based organizational action without denying its emergent nature, as clarified next.

In order to analyze an organization’s efectiveness, it is necessary to take primary account of its functional purpose: the principle aim or role that it is designed or envisioned to fulfill. IS emergence studies, however, had firmly established that organizations are made up of diferent groups of stakeholders, who hold to dissimilar interests and goals (Truex et al., 1999; Robey & Boudreau, 1999). Moreover, emergence in IT based action was imputed to those dissimilar perceptions or interests among stakeholders, that shape their behavior and use of IT in ‘irrational’ or unanticipated ways (Markus & Robey, 1988). Given those diferences of multiple aims/interests among actors (causing emergent outcomes), how is it possible then to identify a common organizational aim as the basis of analysis? Correspondingly, how can significance or value of performance be construed in a non-simplistic way, to account for multiple viewpoints? This theoretical dificulty does not seem to have been adequately resolved by past IS emergence studies. This could be why Shulman (1999) has complained that such studies ofer little guidance on understanding efective performance although they have produced rich insights on the structuring of IT based action.

The form of analysis demonstrated in this paper may be seen as one possible solution to that challenge of theoretically accounting for the disparate nature of organizations i.e. to avoid having making an arbitrary choice among rival stakeholder aims as the basis of analysis. A myth is a common frame of perception by which social actors authenticate a sense of purpose (Barthes, 1973). To motivate action, organizational myths depict a generally desirable, uncomplicated state or aim which diferent stakeholder constituencies with competing interests can broadly identify with, and adopt as a basis for collective sense-making (Gouldner, 1976). The deconstruction of a myth thus presents a relatively inclusive analytical vantage point, or posture, by which to review the eficacy of performance, and the contribution of IT. In the earlier case account, for example, both market ing and CSR supervisory staf would agree with the aim of ‘surprising the customer’, but the supervisors’ unhappiness over additional duties issued by marketing is explained by their concurrent desire to prevent unpredictable customer responses from disrupting the ability to meet computer-based performance targets (i.e. not to be surprised by the customer).

This approach thus equips IS emergence studies with a performance-oriented perspective. The case analysis surfaced key incompatibilities straining the center’s eforts, but strictly from a standpoint of their relevance to its essential role or aim (i.e. myth). The significance of IT based activities can thus be considered in relation to functional aims or visions, over and above (but not excluding) its relation to social actors. Moreover, this approach permitted the significance of the center’s work practices and use of IT to be apprehended not in a simplistic way, but as a complex of multiple dimensions e.g. the practice of issuing Wellbeing points is shaped simultaneously by opposing polarities of value: delighting customers vs. promoting unnecessary calls.

By incorporating a theory of signification (i.e. Greimas, 1987), this approach captures the contested, intricate quality of performance as the nature of accomplishment in emergent contexts. The significance of IT based activities is revealed as being constituted by a matrix of diferential valuations. In other words, significance or value is not seen as objec tively given (or inherent) in an activity or artifact on its own. Nor is it seen as purely a subjective construction or attribution of actors. Rather, the significance of performance is constituted negatively, by its entrenchment in a network of diferences. The use of IT tools is simultaneously entangled within opposing and associative frames of pertinence (Robey & Boudreau, 1999).

By thus capturing the disposition of IT based performance in terms of polar diferences, or oppositional logic (Robey & Boudreau, 1999), this analytical approach may help clarify how organizations can act efectively in emergent settings. The case analysis, for example, suggested the requirement for the call center’s management to pursue two tangential approaches to enhance the quality of IT based service activities. Elasticity of action appears critical: since acting along one trajectory or axis of efort incurs unforeseen disruptive efects to the contrary, it is necessary at the same time to act in a converse direction to contain or mitigate such efects. Organizations thus need to skillfully negotiate contradictions (Truex et al., 1999). This method of analysis may serve as a tool of awareness, sensitizing organizations to the need for a multi-lateral (or a multi-polar) course of action, to counteract the intractability of emergent contexts.

It is to be noted however, that insights gained from this approach are organic to a context (i.e. organization-specific), and provisional (given the mutability of emergent settings). They are not generic solutions that apply indiscriminately to all organizations. This type of analysis aims at ‘sense-making’ (Weick, 1995): the understanding it forms is retrospective, enactive of sensible actions based on focused cues, and marked by plausibility rather than accuracy. Its tactical utility is limited to the unique context it frames, in which it can nevertheless engender useful learning.

## 5.1.2. Rhetorical formulations as sense-making

Contemporary researchers have asserted the importance of not neglecting the rhetorical dimension of technology-based organizational action (Newell, Swan, & Galliers, 2000; Gouldner, 1976). The approach embodied here does not dismiss as irrelevant the rhetorical formulations by which the value of performance may be understood and communicated in organizations. Instead, it treats the rhetorical basis of performance as an integral part of evaluating IT-related outcomes, privileging it as a means of gaining perspective. This is done by surfacing the inadequacy of the images (i.e. myths) established by rhetorical statements of performance aims or visions: in other words, by opening up to analytical regard multiple dimensions of organizational functioning that are de-emphasized or ‘edited out’ (i.e. displaced from consideration) by such images.

In this way, this form of analysis is loosely based on the technique of discourse or textual analysis called deconstruction (Fairclough, 2003), that opens up a text to multiple interpretations, especially those suppressed or marginalized by the text’s rhetorical devices. This technique has been used in IS and management studies, to uncover fresh understanding or insights from textual artifacts that are strikingly diferent from the dominant interpretations authors may have tried to impose (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994; Kilduf, 1993). This research broadly adapts that strategy of a deconstructive reading of rhetorical formulations to analyze accounts of organizational action and IT use, which are treated as text-analogues (Boland, 1991). The value of this is as follows.

A myth facilitates sense-making because of its connotative richness as a representation (Barthes, 1973): the image of surprising the customer could inspire (or ‘translate’ into) concrete practices, such as the IT-enabled issuing of card points/gifts, or the thematic design of the staf recognition scheme. Correspondingly, the method of deconstructing a myth seen here can extend such sense-making utility, by uncovering associative or oppositional connotations to enlarge the richness of the frame of perception (e.g. to surprise the customer, steps must taken to avoid being surprised). Somewhat paradoxically, added significance pertaining to organizational action or IT use can be released, and the utility of a myth enhanced, by demolishing it (i.e. deconstructing it). Thus, this study has extended the utility of a deconstructive analysis for understanding IT based action in relation to rhetorical formulations of organizational aims or visions.

## 5.2. Understanding properties of IT use

In the case analysis (see Tables 4 & 5 earlier), this deconstructive approach had surfaced multiple dimensions of capability and constraint characterizing the support of IT systems at the call center. These dimensions are incorporated in the signification system seen in Fig. 4. This approach appears to have significant utility in helping clarify the characteristics of IT use.

As seen in this figure, this approach permits an understanding of the properties of IT use to go beyond simplistic binary dichotomies when holding that IT enables and constrains action. Instead, it encourages concurrent consideration of multiple dimensions of enabling or preventive capacity of IT systems. In particular, it shows up an interesting zone of susceptibility under the preventive dimension of IT i.e. IT does not enable to avoid being anticipated by the customer. The case study had produced mild examples of such susceptibility: while enabling the call center with the capacity to eficiently handle (via the call-distribution algorithms of the ACD system) a mass range of customer calls, the existing functionality of IT could not prevent those customers being seen to contact the center for ‘unnecessary’ reasons e.g. displacement of causal inquiries from stores to the center. Other examples (outside this case), that are more pernicious, may also be cited to illustrate this: for instance the growth of credit card fraud or identity theft in soci eties can be linked to the IT based capacity for electronic transactions.

Thus a deconstructive approach discloses the inverse capacity of IT: the same capacity for IT tools or features to be manipulated in the interest of planned actions or desired behaviors incurs a reverse capacity to be manipulated in return, through unanticipated actions or undesired behaviors. The inverse capacity of IT may also be seen to encompass not only compromising but also inhibitive efects. For example, while widely promoted as the key enabler of organizational change initiatives, IT systems (e.g. legacy software) have also been found to be a key inhibitor of such eforts in certain contexts (Broadbent, Weill & St Clair, 1999).

This approach, for discerning the properties of IT use from an emergent standpoint, may be seen as useful in light of recent calls to theorize the IT artifact (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). The value of this approach is that it discloses intermediate qualities and compound implications in the nature of IT use, or the characterization of IT based work activity. This can also be further demonstrated in terms of the properties of flexibility and rigidity, which feature prominently in IS theory. IT tools have been seen to exhibit flexibility in terms of physical feature-configuration or ‘interpretive flexibility’ (Orlikowski, 2000) i.e. latitude of meaning for diferent users. On the other hand, IT tools have also been seen to incorporate or to induce rigidity, by imposing highly structured, inflexible procedures into organizational workplaces (Salzman, 1998). The way in which an understanding of both these qualities of IT use can be expanded beyond a simple binary categorization (i.e. IT is flexible vs. IT is rigid) is illustrated next, through reference to the earlier case study and Fig. 4.

The issuing of points in the Wellbeing database, the arranging of compensation (vouchers etc) or coordinated remedial action via the CRM system, and the use of informative feedback to gain leverage with corporate units, are instances of the way the center’s IT systems have proved amenable to management aims to promote loyalty among customers. This may be said to reflect a ‘positive’ type of flexibility, both in an interpretive and material sense, in those features of IT use. However, the flip side of the coin is this has invoked certain unwanted consequences (from the management’s viewpoint) like the rise of casual inquiries or repetitive complaints. This may be taken to imply ‘negative flexibility’, in which the existing functional capacity of IT to support mass dealings with customers is not suficiently qualified to check undesired behaviors (e.g. lack of ability to pre-identify caller issues and address ‘unnecessary’ calls).

On a related note, the present shortcomings in the data resources or functionality of the center’s IT systems (e.g. limitations of search mechanisms, lack of systems integration and slow response times) constitute a significant amount of ‘negative rigidity’ that has impeded the ability of CSRs to respond quickly or adequately. In contraposition, there has been a degree of ‘positive rigidity’ brought about through the material and symbolic conscription of IT tools. The business interface team’s ability to prepare the CSRs with required information to handle customer calls has resulted from the use of IT capacity to establish a permanency of communication exchanges with other corporate units i.e. the use of the intranet or data exchange tools like email/Notes has provided stable material channels for hosting collaboration with such units. The regular feedback reports created from the CRM database and passed to those units has cemented strong symbolic bonds of obligation that sustain the timely provision of information.

Interestingly, this inter-relational analysis may be taken to suggest that (or infer how) the positive flexibility which characterizes the use of IT capacities is pre-supposed by the positive rigidity amounting from the same: and contrawise, the negative rigidity which characterizes the use of IT capacities is pre-supposed by negative flexibility. For example, in order to improve the center’s ability to exploit the use of IT assets to ‘surprise the customer’ (e.g. have eficient access to required information needed by callers), it is necessary to harness the use of IT to facilitate or pre-determine regular collaboration and information exchanges with corporate units (i.e. positive rigidity). Conversely, the dificulties faced by the staf in coping with the wide range of customer requirements and issues (i.e. negative rigidity), might be ascribed to the undiferentiated capacity (i.e. negative flexibility) of the center’s computer telephony system to admit calls from a diverse, geographically distributed customer environment. This form of inter-relational understanding is consonant with a ‘systems-thinking approach’ (Klein, 1996), that is vital for understanding how to improve IT based organizational efectiveness from an integrative standpoint.

Thus, the above illustration of the properties of IT use in customer service operations has uncovered a richer set of qualities than the simple binary alternatives of rigidity and flexibility. The use of this approach demonstrated here may be seen to equip IS researchers and practitioners with a way to investigate and represent the complex, multi-dimensional nature of IT properties.

## 5.3. IT enabled service operations

This research has illuminated certain challenges in customer service operations. This is a vital arena for contemporary organizations, as pressures to deliver high-quality service functions have become pervasive. The satisfaction of customer needs is now a key organizing principle of work practices and IT based innovation (Scarbrough, 1998): studies have established its positive efect on organizational performance (Gronroos, 2000; Narver & Slater, 1990). Many firms, like that in the above case study, have thus sought to centralize and improve their customer support activities through a call center. Key lessons that can apply to them may be drawn as follows.

The case account suggests that the process of integration between a call centre and parent organization, especially in large companies operating in a decentralized manner, can form a key area of dificulties. The HealthCo call centre did not appear to have been implemented as part of a wider, well-coordinated scheme of re-organization in the delivery of customer support activity. Instead it seemed to be operating to a certain extent as a supplementary or ‘tack-on’ function in the company. This led to dificulties in the center’s service eforts, in such areas as the existence of channels of collaboration and information exchange with corporate units, and the availability of data assets or functionality in legacy IT systems for handling customer queries. Such problems highlight the need for relevan aspects of a firm’s processes or IT systems to be re-formulated to sustain a call center. There is a danger such technologically sophisticated organizational forms as these centers are treated as self-suficient or standalone units, that can be incorporated without a need to make significant adaptations in the rest of the firm (Boddy, 2000).

The increasing complexity of service work and customer environments often places too heavy a demand on the information management capabilities of organizations (Seltsikas, 1999; Davenport, 1993), as illustrated by those shortcomings of the center’s information systems in the case account. This research thus highlights the need for management to comprehensively ensure suficient capabilities in the IT systems of a call center. These centers play a vital function at the frontline of a company’s interactions with its clients (Frenkel, Korczynski, Shire, & Tam, 1999). They thus need to be equipped with an adequate knowledge-base of customer and product-relevant information. In this regard, the analytical approach shown here (or other frameworks promoting an inclusive, multi-dimensional review) can be utilized to assess the complexity of service work environments and help identify the requirements they invoke for information handling.

However, given the emergent, unpredictable nature of customer service environments, it is crucial that management responses are marked by vigilance, and by an informed under standing of the tensions and inconsistencies that exist or arise as a result of IT based action. The approach illustrated here can facilitate such understanding and insight, which might even lead to emergent responses more efective in outcome than those originally intended.

## 6. Conclusion

Rather than treating as trivial or insignificant the rhetorical basis by which organizational aims may be formulated, this paper had developed its value for illuminating the emergent nature of IT based activities, through the concept of myth. A heightened appreciation may be gained of the way in which organizational performance and IT use is dynamically embedded in a network of dependencies and inconsistencies, as exemplified by this study of customer service operations. Eforts to improve work activity and IT can be informed by the awareness or insights generated.

This study’s contribution lies in extending current understanding of IS emergence. While previous studies had focused on the way social structures breed emergent outcomes, this research addressed the relatively uncharted area of the functional efectiveness of IT based organizational action in emergent conditions. Its case study highlights key challenges in achieving high-quality customer service operations. This study also improves the fledging qualitative research treatment of call centers in the IS field. Last but not least it precipitates new ground by applying ideas from semiotic theory to the study of IT use and organizational design. Many organizations develop distinctive forms of rhetorical ‘languaging’ that frame the significance of technology use and set up the conditions for performance (Von Krogh & Roos, 1995). This study has illustrated how the use of a semiotic framework, to analyze emergent IT based activity against a rhetorical profile, can provoke fresh understanding of organizational efectiveness.
