# THE COMPLEX EFFECTS OF CROSS-DOMAIN KNOWLEDGE ON IS DEVELOPMENT: A SIMULATION-BASED THEORY DEVELOPMENT<sup>1</sup>

Jungpil Hahn School of Computing, National University of Singapore, SINGAPORE 117417 {jungpil@nus.edu.sg}

Gwanhoo Lee Kogod School of Business, American University, Washington, DC 20016 U.S.A. {glee@american.edu}

Information systems development (ISD) requires both business domain and technology domain knowledge. How cross-domain knowledge affects ISD outcomes is one of the most fundamental and persistent problems in the IS field. We argue that the effect of cross-domain knowledge depends on its distribution across business and IT units and ISD complexity in terms of level and pattern of design element interdependencies. We systematically investigate the complex effects of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance by using simulations based on the NK fitness landscapes model. We find that cross-domain knowledge not only increases ISD performance but also reduces its variability. These effects are greater when ISD complexity is greater. Crossdomain knowledge is found to increase the robustness of ISD performance across different patterns of design element interdependencies. Interestingly, ISD performance is higher when cross-domain knowledge is unevenly distributed across business and IS units and when its distribution pattern matches the pattern of the design element interdependencies. These findings suggest that the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance is not fixed but depends on its structural pattern, the level and pattern of design element interdependencies, and their interactions. This study illuminates how and why challenges in ISD might be structural and how, why, and when cross-domain knowledge might help to overcome these challenges. We develop a theoretical framework and propositions based on our findings.

Keywords: Cross-domain knowledge, information systems development (ISD), design element interdependence, complexity, NK fitness landscapes model, simulation, theory development

## Introduction

Information systems development (ISD) requires both business domain knowledge and technology domain knowledge (Faraj and Sproull 2000; Kang et al. 2017; Khatri et al. 2006). How these two distinct types of knowledge are utilized and integrated plays a critical role in ISD (Lyytinen and Robey 1999; Pee et al. 2010; Tiwana 2009). Prior research has long recognized the importance of the business and IS units having the other domain’s knowledge for facilitating knowledge utilization and integration (Lee and Han 2008; Nelson 1991; Walz et al. 1993). In this paper, we refer to cross-domain knowledge as business users’ possession of technology domain knowledge as well as IS personnel’s possession of business domain knowledge (Reich and Benbasat 2000). Having cross-domain knowledge is considered valuable for organizational work because organizational members need a certain level of common ground in their respective knowledge bases to effectively communicate, coordinate, and integrate collective action (Cramton 2001; Espinosa et al. 2007a; Mitchell 2006). Therefore, organizations are expected to strive to develop cross-domain knowledge. However, cross-domain knowledge in the business and IS units is often low (Bassellier et al. 2003; Curtis et al. 1988; Nambisan et al. 1999).

Cross-domain knowledge comes at a cost because its development requires significant individual and organizational investment of resources and time in formal training, communities of practice, knowledge management tools, job rotation programs, etc. (Chau et al. 2003; Tiwana 2012). For example, although job rotation programs can promote the creation of cross-functional knowledge, they may result in added cost, decreased productivity, and even the loss of human capital (Bapna et al. 2013). Given the costs associated with developing cross-domain knowledge and its critical role in ISD, it is important for organizations to understand when and how it affects ISD performance so that they can allocate resources more effectively (Bapna et al. 2010; Nissen et al. 2008; Postrel 2002; Ramasubbu et al. 2012).

Many prior studies have reported a positive impact of general cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance (Adelson and Soloway 1985; Curtis et al. 1988; Kotlarsky and Oshri 2005; Lee 2001; Pee et al. 2010; Walz et al. 1993). However, our understanding of its effect on ISD performance is underdeveloped with regard to two important aspects. First, the effect of cross-domain knowledge can be much more complex because its distribution may vary across the units (Nambisan et al. 1999; Reich and Benbasat 2000; Tiwana 2004b, 2009). Prior research (e.g., Khatri et al. 2006; Shaft and Vessey 1995; Tiwana 2004a) has focused on a generalized notion of cross-domain knowledge (i.e., assuming a symmetric distribution of domain knowledge between business and IS units) or a completely asymmetric distribution (i.e., by only focusing on the business domain knowledge of IS personnel). However, in the real world, both business users and IS personnel may possess technology domain knowledge and business domain knowledge, respectively, and the extent of such crossdomain knowledge across business and IS units may vary. Therefore, how the distribution of cross-domain knowledge could vary and affect ISD performance has been neglected (Gruenfeld et al. 1996).

Second, although researchers have begun to investigate the factors that moderate the effect of cross-domain knowledge and revealed that the effect is more complex and nuanced than prior conceptions (Curtis et al. 1988; Khatri et al. 2006; Lee 2001; Tiwana 2004b, 2009), our understanding of the effect still remains fragmented and underdeveloped in part because few studies have taken a systematic approach to investigating how ISD complexity influences the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance. Complexity is a critical contingent factor for ISD; not only does it influence ISD performance (Lee et al. 2013; Xia and Lee 2005) but it also determines the effectiveness of ISD approaches (Shenhar 2001). ISD complexity increases when there are many design elements and interdependencies among them (Campbell 1988; Simon 1962; Wood 1986; Xia and Lee 2005). These interdependencies may exist across business and technical design elements, exhibiting various structural patterns.

Considering these limitations of the extant literature on crossdomain knowledge, there is a compelling need for theoretical development to further our understanding of the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance. In this study, we seek to provide theoretical insights into these important gaps in the literature by addressing the following research questions:

(1) How do the level and distribution of cross-domain knowledge affect ISD performance?

(2) How does cross-domain knowledge interact with the level and structural pattern of ISD complexity in affecting ISD performance?

Answering these questions allows us to shed light on some of the inconsistent findings in prior research. For example, Reich and Benbasat (2000) found mixed correlations between the level of cross-domain knowledge and the performance of IT implementation. Such inconsistency could be resolved by considering the distribution of cross-domain knowledge and the level and structural pattern of ISD complexity. This study also contributes to developing theory that reflects modern ISD environments. The high complexity of information technology and business operations has increased variance in cross-domain knowledge across individuals, teams, and firms. Further, the explosion of business software applications ranging from mobile apps to cloud-based enterprise systems resulted in varying levels and structures of ISD complexity. The extant theory of cross-domain knowledge does not incorporate these factors and thus needs to be updated.

This study aims to generate deeper theoretical insights on the role of cross-domain knowledge in ISD, which is one of the most fundamental and persistent problems in the IS field. We hope that this paper stimulates and guides future theoretical and empirical research on the topic. Toward this goal, we adopt a simulation-based computational modeling approach. The simulation methodology has recently been used by IS researchers to tackle challenging problems driven by the increased complexity in sociotechnical systems (e.g., Brunswicker et al. 2019; Haki et al. 2020; Malgonde et al.

2020; Nan 2011; Nan and Tanriverdi 2017; Oh et al. 2016; Pentland et al. 2020). We follow the simulation-based theory development process prescribed by Davis et al. (2007). Having stated the research questions, we draw from existing theoretical and empirical research to develop a theory-based computational model of ISD. After validating the computational model with prior research findings and domain experts, we conduct a number of simulations experiments with the model to explore the effects of the level and structural pattern of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance under different levels and structural patterns of ISD complexity manifested in terms of interdependencies of design elements. We also measure performance variability, which is an indicator of the reliability and predictability of ISD performance. Repeated experiments using a computational model make it possible to measure such performance variability, which is difficult, if not impossible, in field studies.

## Theoretical Foundation

In this section, we set the theoretical foundation for conceptualizing ISD as an iterative and incremental design problem solving process and for highlighting the critical role that cross-domain knowledge plays in it. We define two important dimensions of cross-domain knowledge (i.e., level and structural pattern) and review prior research on the effects of crossdomain knowledge on ISD performance. Finally, we discuss how ISD complexity in terms of the level and structural pattern of design element interdependence will interact with cross-domain knowledge in affecting ISD performance.

## ISD as an Iterative and Incremental Design Problem Solving Process

ISD is fundamentally a design problem solving process in which the project team develops an IS design that confers value to the organization (Aladwani 2002; Cerveny et al. 1990; Garrity 2001; Kozar 1989; Li and Kettinger 2006; Pitts and Browne 2004; Vitalari and Dickson 1983). When an information system is being developed, the project team identifies a business problem, which is defined as a gap between existing and desired states of business processes; the team then generates and evaluates alternative system designs to reduce the gap; finally, the team selects the system design that best solves the business problem, thus satisfying organizational needs (Cerveny et al. 1990; Vitalari and Dickson 1983). This design problem solving process is iterative and incremental in that the optimal system design is seldom found in a single iteration due to the complexity, ambiguity and uncertainty of the problem (Boland 1978; Curtis et al. 1988; Simon

1973) but needs to be discovered through iterative cycles of refinement. A design choice in each iteration becomes the input for identifying a revised gap in the next iteration and a satisfactory design is eventually found through multiple iterations (Larman and Basili 2003).

Some ISD methodologies such as the waterfall model and the SDLC V-Model may use a linear process of system development (Avison and Taylor 1997; Iivari et al. 2000). However, the majority of contemporary ISD projects use an iterative and incremental process adopted by various agile approaches, the Spiral Model, RAD, DSDM, etc. (Hewlett Packard 2017). Therefore, contemporary ISD can be generally characterized as an iterative and incremental process for solving a design problem in which business design decisions and technical design decisions are made iteratively and incrementally until a satisficing design is reached. Business users typically provide inputs for system requirements and IS professionals build a working product on which the users provide feedback (Cockburn 2007; Larman 2004; Lee and Xia 2010).

## Cross-Domain Knowledge

Knowledge plays a critical role in the design problem solving process. Knowledge provides the means by which business problems are identified and the expected costs and benefits of design alternatives are evaluated. ISD requires both business domain knowledge and technology domain knowledge (Batista Dias et al. 2003; Iivari et al. 2004; Kang et al. 2017; Khatri et al. 2006; Rus and Lindvall 2002; Vitalari 1985). As a result, ISD typically involves both a business unit consisting of users and business analysts who have application domain knowledge and an IS unit consisting of system analysts and developers who possess technical expertise (Tiwana 2009).

Although the actual decision making process in the real world could be more nuanced and complex, the business unit is ultimately responsible for determining user requirements and making business design decisions such as defining business processes and business rules whereas the IS unit is ultimately responsible for making technical design decisions (Whitten and Bentley 2005). Prior case studies of ERP implementation support this view (Balint 2011; Livermore and Rippa 2010). Such decision partitioning arises because the two units possess different sets of knowledge and competencies (Grant 1996; Postrel 2002; von Hippel 1990) and the organizational structure confers legitimate decision authority and problem ownership. However, in order for an ISD project to be successful, business and technical knowledge need to be combined and integrated (Faraj and Sproull 2000). Cross-domain knowledge helps ISD teams overcome the difficulties arising from task partitioning.

Cross-domain knowledge has two important dimensions: level and structural pattern. Level here refers to the amount of cross-domain knowledge possessed by the business and IS units and structural pattern here refers to the distribution of cross-domain knowledge across the units. Prior research has found that cross-domain knowledge positively affects ISD outcomes, in terms of overall project performance (Pee et al. 2010; Walz et al. 1993), outsourcing performance (Lee 2001; Tiwana 2004b), partnership quality (Lee 2001), team collaboration (Kotlarsky and Oshri 2005), team learning (Walz et al. 1993), project decision control and management (Espinosa et al. 2007b; Tiwana 2009), design specification quality (Curtis et al. 1988), schema understanding (Khatri et al. 2006), computer program comprehension (Shaft and Vessey 1995, 1998), and conceptual IS model comprehension (Burton-Jones and Weber 1999). In addition to its direct impact on ISD task performance, cross-domain knowledge has been found to influence other organizational factors that are frequently associated with ISD success. For example, it strengthens business-IT alignment (Reich and Benbasat 2000), improves IS organizational performance (Nelson and Cooprider 1996), intensifies organizational IT use (Boynton et al. 1994), IT strategic agility (Tiwana and Kim 2015), and increases managers’ propensity to innovate using IT (Nambisan et al. 1999), intentions to champion IT (Bassellier et al. 2003) and propensity to take on a leadership role in IT work (Rockart et al. 1996).<sup>2</sup>

The effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance becomes more complex because cross-domain knowledge is not necessarily evenly distributed across the business and IS units comprising the ISD team (Tiwana 2004b). That is, patterns matter, not just levels. In some organizations, IS personnel may possess more business knowledge than business users possess technical knowledge, but the opposite might be true in other organizations. With the rise of the end-user development paradigm, many end users are acquiring a nontrivial amount of technical knowledge and skills (Lieberman et al. 2006; Saha and Mukherjee 2003). On the other hand, many IS personnel have gained substantial amounts of knowledge of functional domains through experiences in the development projects of enterprise systems such as ERP and CRM. Tiwana (2009) found that the level of IT unit’s business application domain knowledge was significantly higher than the level of the business unit’s technical knowledge in internal ISD projects. Similarly, Bassellier et al. (2003) found that business functional managers possess a low level of technical knowledge. By contrast, Curtis et al. (1988) found that application domain knowledge is thinly spread among IS developers. Nambisan et al. (1999) observed substantial variation across organizations in business functional managers knowledge and ability to explore technology. Reich and Benbasat (2000) did a field study with 10 business units of three large insurance companies and found that the amount of cross-domain knowledge possessed by business managers and IT managers varies greatly even within the same business unit. Despite all these findings in prior work, our review of the literature reveals that these studies have only scratched the surface of the issue because no prior research has looked at how the structural pattern (i.e., distribution) of cross-domain knowledge affects ISD performance. Instead, prior research (e.g., Khatri et al. 2006, Shaft and Vessey 1995, Tiwana 2004a) has implicitly assumed either a symmetric distribution of domain knowledge between business and IS units (i.e., same amount of cross-domain knowledge) or a completely asymmetric distribution (i.e., the IS unit has application domain knowledge, but the business unit lacks any technology domain knowledge, or vice versa).

## ISD Complexity and Design Element Interdependence

The increased complexity in sociotechnical systems warrants new theorizing and rethinking of many fundamental IS problems including digitized processes, digital platforms, and IS architecture (Benbya et al. 2020; Haki et al. 2020; Malgonde et al. 2020; Pentland et al. 2020). ISD problems are no exceptions. Difficulties in ISD also arise in part due to the complexity of the design task itself (Boland 1978; Curtis et al. 1988; Goel and Pirolli 1992). The design task typically involves business design elements (e.g., business processes and business rules) and technical design elements (e.g., configurations of software, hardware, and network). These design elements are configured to collectively meet the IS requirements. The design elements are often interdependent in that the value implication of the setting of a certain design element depends on how other design elements are set. Much like cross-domain knowledge, design element interdependence has two important dimensions: level and structural pattern. Level refers to the extent of all interdependencies that exist between any two design elements and structural pattern refers to the distribution of the interdependencies across business and technology domains. The extent of design element interdependence increases the complexity of ISD (Xia and Lee 2005). Dealing with complexity is a fundamental problem in ISD as higher levels of ISD complexity has been found to be associated with lower ISD performance in terms of time, cost, system functionality, and user satisfaction (Xia and Lee 2004).

Prior research suggests three canonical structural patterns of design element interdependencies. First, there are ISD projects in which technical design decisions are more dependent on business design decisions than the other way around (i.e., business-driven design interdependencies). It is important to note that interdependency does not refer to temporal sequence of design decisions. Rather, it refers to the situation where the value contribution of a design decision to the system performance is influenced by the other design decision. Take ERP implementation as an example. Some companies implement a single integrated ERP system whereas others implement a best-of-breed system that encompasses a variety of specialized systems optimized for certain business functions and processes. The implementation of an integrated ERP system exemplifies business-driven design interdependencies. An integrated ERP system is optimized to support a set of predefined business processes. Although it can also support other business processes outside the predefined set, the implementation of such processes typically requires system modification and customization. As a result, those business processes can be supported only at a sub-optimal level due to the difficulties in integration, maintenance and upgrade (Fryling 2015; Light 2001). That said, the value contribution of a given technical design choice significantly depends on the choice of business processes (i.e., whether the business process was selected from the ERP system’s predefined set or was newly created). On the other hand, the value contribution of a given business process choice to the overall system performance does not depend on technical design choices as much because different technical choices within the same integrated ERP system (e.g., different technical configuration options such as choice between push versus pull data flow model or choice between full virtualization versus paravirtualization) are comparably capable of supporting the business process. Gosain et al. (2005) provide two real-world cases that support this pattern. TelEquipCo that implemented an integrated ERP system realized that it was the choice of business processes rather than technical choices that largely determined the system performance. ConsCo that also implemented an integrated ERP system found that the customi zation to support business processes that were not included in the ERP system’s predefined set would cause concerns related to maintainability and system upgrades. These companies realized that the ERP implementation performance is driven by business process design choices, not technical ones.

Second, there are ISD projects in which business design decisions are more dependent on technical design decisions than the other way around (i.e., technology-driven design interdependencies). In the context of ERP implementation, a best-of-breed system demonstrates technology-driven design interdependencies. When implementing a best-of-breed system, organizations select a set of specialized systems that are best suited for certain business functions such as accounting, HR, or sales (Angst et al. 2017). In this case, the performance of a chosen business process is significantly affected by the choice of a technical solution, because the best-of-breed system, by definition, is optimized to support its target business processes better than any other systems. On the other hand, the performance of a best-of-breed system does not depend on the business process choice as much, because the system is superior to other systems in supporting various business processes within the specialized functional area. For example, the value contribution of a virtualization technique of the bestof-breed system would not change much if the choice of demand forecasting time horizon is short- or long-term as the technique is designed to function regardless of different time horizons. Taken together, the best-of-breed ERP implementation demonstrates a case for “technology-driven” design dependencies where technical design choices affect the value contribution of a business design choice more so than the other way around.

Finally, there are ISD projects where business design decisions and technical design decisions are equally dependent on one another (i.e., balanced design interdependencies). For example, some organizations take a hybrid ERP implementation approach to combine the best of the two-worlds by using an integrated ERP system as a backbone and adding a few best-of-breed solutions to the backbone. In this case, the dependencies between business design decisions and technical design decisions tend to be balanced. We describe our operationalization of the three distinct structural patterns of design element interdependencies later. Prior research on design modularity in software development has studied the structure of design element interdependence (MacCormack 2001; MacCormack et al. 2006; Parnas 1972). However, it has focused on interdependencies of only technical design elements, and therefore cross-domain knowledge was irrelevant. No prior research has extended the notion of interdependencies to embrace not only technical design elements but also business design elements.

## Interactions between Cross-Domain Knowledge and Design Element Interdependence

Researchers have begun to recognize that the effect of crossdomain knowledge on ISD performance might be moderated by factors such as novelty of project and process (Tiwana 2004b), the degree of decentralization of decision control rights and decision management rights (Tiwana 2009), task type (Khatri et al. 2006), and organizational capability (Lee 2001). Prior research also suggests that ISD complexity, manifested by extent of design element interdependence, might interact with cross-domain knowledge in affecting ISD performance. Curtis et al. (1988) argued that application domain knowledge of IS developers is especially important for more complex systems development. Tiwana (2004a) found that higher levels of integration of application domain knowledge and technical knowledge played a more important role for complex software development projects. Gosain et al. (2005) found that cross-domain knowledge played a critical role in the implementation of highly complex information systems such as ERP systems. The Obamacare portal website (healthcare.gov) is a case in point. The portal was required to integrate dozens of databases and incorporate different policies and laws of the 50 states. The complex interdependencies of user requirements and technological platforms made the project a daunting task (Lee and Brumer 2017). This problem was exacerbated by the contractors’ lack of knowledge in the healthcare domain and the healthcare administrators’ lack of knowledge in the technology domain (Chao 2018). As a result, the portal was suboptimally designed when it was released to the public. Despite these studies, there is little knowledge about how the level and structural pattern of cross-domain knowledge interact with the level and structural pattern of design element interdependence in ISD.

Due to such complex nature of the ISD development phenomenon intricately involving many different constructs (i.e., cross-domain knowledge and design element interdependence) that take shape in many different ways (i.e., level and structural pattern), we adopt the simulation methodology (Davis et al. 2007) that allows us to systematically investigate and explore the phenomenon.

## Development of an NK Fitness Landscapes Model of ISD

Having established that the ISD process can be conceptualized as design problem solving where relevant knowledge is used to identify and evaluate design alternatives but where relevant knowledge may be distributed across different units involved in the ISD process, the next step is to develop an appropriate computational representation of the key theoretical relationships (Davis et al. 2007). Among the various simulation approaches (e.g., systems dynamics, genetic algorithms, cellular automata, etc.), the NK fitness landscapes model (Kauffman 1993) that models organizational adaptation as design problem solving is most appropriate for our current study. We extend the NK fitness landscapes model to the ISD context.<sup>3</sup>

First, the ISD problem space is composed of design elements which are partitioned into two separate knowledge domains: business versus technology domains. Second, the ISD team is comprised of two distinct decision making units with specialized knowledge: business unit versus IS unit. The ISD process is modeled as iterative and incremental elaboration of IS design in which each decision making unit chooses a configuration for the design elements within its knowledge domain that leads to increased value of the system. Third, ISD complexity is characterized by the amount and pattern of interdependencies among business and technical design elements. We further refine design element interdependencies by considering interdependencies both within and across business and technology domains. Finally and most importantly to our research objective, we allow for the possibility of crossdomain knowledge such that each unit may have knowledge of the other domain which determines the accuracy with which alternative design choices are assessed. We discuss these model extensions in detail below.

## The ISD Problem Space

We model an ISD project as consisting of N IS design elements comprising of business and technical design elements (i.e., requirements), where each design element $d _ { i }$ can take one of two values, 0 or 1.<sup>4</sup> A design element could represent an application domain related design decision (e.g., business processes, business rules, etc.) or a technology related design decision (e.g., configurations of hardware, software, and network). For example, the various business and technology requirements in an ISD project for a manufacturing company to develop an information system to support its order ful fillment processes could include the choice of demand forecasting time horizon (e.g., short-term versus long-term) $( d _ { I } ) _ { \ l }$ , the level of integration between shipping and delivery $( d _ { 2 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , choice of order processing method (e.g., real-time versus batch) $( d _ { 3 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , choice of organizational unit responsible for choice of order handling and fulfillment (e.g., regional offices versus headquarters) $( d _ { 4 } ) _ { : }$ , choice of data privacy and security policy (e.g., opt-in versus opt-out) $( d _ { 5 } ) _ { ; }$ , choice of software source $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , open source versus proprietary) $( d _ { 6 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , choice of communication and data flow model (e.g., push versus pull) $( d _ { 7 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , choice of cloud model (e.g., public versus private) $( d _ { 8 } )$ choice of virtualization technique (e.g., full virtualization versus paravirtualization) $( d _ { g } )$ , choice of data analytics approach (e.g., deep learning versus semi-supervised learning) $( d _ { I 0 } ) .$ , etc.

Formally, an IS design can be represented as a vector of N design elements: $\mathbf { d } = < d _ { I } , d _ { 2 } , . . . , d _ { N } >$ , which can take on $2 ^ { N }$ possible configurations. Each IS design configuration is associated with a fitness value representing the business value of the IS. The design elements can be interdependent in the sense that the value contribution of one design decision may depend on the configuration of K other design elements. Such interdependencies can exist within the business or IS domains as well as between domains. The objective of ISD is to find a configuration of design elements with greatest fitness.

## Knowledge Domains and Decision Allocation

In ISD projects, the different types of design decisions are owned and made by different units due to specialized knowledge and institutionalized decision authority (Grant 1996; Nissen et al. 2008; Postrel 2002; von Hippel 1990). To incorporate such decision allocation, we model the ISD project team as composed of two units—a business unit and an IS unit—where the business unit is ultimately responsible for business design decisions and the IS unit is ultimately responsible for technical design decisions. Formally, the IS configuration vector d is partitioned into two subsets $< \mathbf { d } _ { \mathbf { b u s } } , ~ \mathbf { d } _ { \mathbf { I S } } >$ where $\mathbf { d _ { \mathrm { { b u s } } } } = < d _ { 1 } , d _ { 2 } , . . . , d _ { m } >$ represents business design decisions and $\mathbf { d _ { I S } } = < d _ { m + 1 } , d _ { m + 2 } , . . . , d _ { N } >$ represents technical design decisions $( 0 \leq \mathbf { m } \leq N )$ . For ease of exposition, although only one parameter is required, we will henceforth use $m _ { b u s }$ and $m _ { I S }$ $( m _ { b u s } + m _ { I S } = N )$ to represent the number of design elements under the purview of the business and IS units, respectively. In our example above, design decisions $d _ { 1 }$ through $d _ { 5 }$ are related to the application domain, whereas decisions $d _ { 6 }$ through $d _ { 1 0 }$ are related to the technology domain. In other words, there are five business $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \mathbf { d _ { b u s } } = < d _ { 1 } , . . . , d _ { 5 } \mathrm { > ; } m _ { b u s } =$ 5) and five technology requirements (i.e., $\mathbf { d } _ { \mathrm { { I S } } } = < d _ { 6 } , . . . , d _ { 1 0 } > ;$ $m _ { I S } = 5 )$ , which are within the purview of the business unit and the IS unit, respectively.

## Design Element Interdependence: Level and Structural Pattern

Design element interdependence can exist within or across knowledge domains. For example, interdependence exists within domain $( K _ { w i t h i n } )$ if the value contribution of a design choice of a domain depends on other design choices of the same domain (e.g., the value contribution of the decision with regard to a business requirement on whether to process orders in real-time $( d _ { 3 } )$ may depend on how the demand forecasting horizon has been set $( d _ { 1 } )$ , a related business requirement; or the value contribution of the decision with regard to a technology requirement on whether to store data in the cloud $( d _ { 8 } )$

may depend on the setup of the communication data flow $( d _ { 7 } )$ another technology requirement). Conversely, interdependence exists across domains $( K _ { b e t w e e n } )$ if the value contribution of a design choice of a domain depends on other design choices of the other domain (e.g., the value contribution of the decision with regard to a technology requirement on how to store data in the cloud $( d _ { 8 } )$ 1 may depend on whether or not orders are set to be handled and fulfilled by each regional office or consolidated at headquarters $( d _ { 4 } )$ , a business requirement). The patterns of interdependencies among design elements can be defined and represented in an $N { \times } N$ influence matrix (INF), where $I N F _ { i j } = 1$ if design element j (column) influences the value contribution of design element i (row), or 0 otherwise. The influence matrix allows us to model the various structural patterns of the within- and between-domain interdependencies.<sup>5</sup>

We model three different archetypical structural patterns of design element interdependencies with the influence matrix. An ISD project exhibiting business-driven design interdependencies can be modeled with an unevenly distributed influence matrix such that technical design decisions depend on business design decisions more than business design decisions depend on technical design decisions. Conversely, an ISD project exhibiting technology-driven design interdependencies can be modeled with an unevenly distributed influence matrix where business design decisions depend on technical design decisions more than technical design decisions depend on business design choices. Finally, an ISD project exhibiting balanced design interdependencies can be modeled with an evenly distributed influence matrix where business design decisions depend on technical design decisions to the same extent as technical design decisions depend on business design decisions.<sup>6</sup>

## Cross-Domain Knowledge: Level and Structural Pattern

The business and IS units involved in an ISD project have specialized knowledge in their respective domains. First, let us consider the case of fully specialized knowledge (or alternatively, with no cross-domain knowledge) where the business unit only has knowledge of the application domain and does not have any knowledge of the technology domain, and the IS unit only has knowledge of the technology domain and does not have any knowledge of the application domain. Later, we will relax this restriction to allow cross-domain knowledge across units. Although the ISD project involves setting the configurations for N design elements, since each unit only has knowledge of a subset $( m _ { b u s } \ \mathrm { o r } \ m _ { I S } )$ of the design elements, they can only accurately assess the performance implications of the choice of design elements for which they are knowledgeable. In other words, although each unit can assess the performance implications of its own design choices, its assessment of the full implications of their design choices will be imperfect because it does not understand the performance implications of the choices of the design elements of the other unit. We model such imperfect assessment due to knowledge specialization (and hence, lack of knowledge of the other unit’s domain) using Gavetti and Levinthal’s (2000) model of simplified cognitive representations.

The intuition underlying the model of simplified cognitive representations is that cognition is based on an actor’s representation of the problem space. Actors’ representations of the problem space, while generally consistent with the actual fitness landscape, are inevitably simplified abstractions of the actual landscape. Within the NK fitness landscapes framework, this can be achieved by reducing the dimensionality of the landscape. Formally, although the structure of the landscape is defined by N design elements, an actor can be conceptualized as having a boundedly rational representation consisting of N' dimensions (where $N ^ { \prime } { < } N )$ because the actor is mindful of only a subset of the design elements that govern the fitness structure of the problem domain. The fitness contribution of a design choice based on an actor’s simplified cognitive representation can be unbiasedly represented by the average of the fitness contributions of the known decision variables across all the possible configurations of the unknown ones.<sup>7</sup> In our ISD context with fully specialized units possessing no cross-domain knowledge, each unit is mindful of only $N ^ { \prime } { = } m _ { u n i t }$ design elements that impact the fitness of the N-element system design. As a result, each unit will have an imperfect assessment of the fitness of the design solely based on its own knowledge domain, which will be used to guide the design choices that they enact.

Cross-domain knowledge exists when a decision making unit possesses knowledge in the domain of the other unit. For instance, in our running example, the business unit may also have knowledge about what it means for the data/communications model to be push- or pull-based (d ). Such crossdomain knowledge goes beyond simple information about design choices made by the other unit. It allows one unit to assess the value implication of the other unit’s certain design choice so that the focal unit can make informed design decisions to maximize systems design value. We model cross-domain knowledge with parameter $c _ { u n i t } ,$ which represents the number of design elements in the other unit’s knowledge domain, for which the focal unit has knowledge. At the extremes, if $c _ { u n i t } = 0$ , then there is no cross-domain knowledge (i.e., the fully specialized case as explained above); if $c _ { b u s } = m _ { I S }$ or $c _ { I S } ~ = m _ { b u s } ,$ then the units have full knowledge of the other unit’s knowledge domain.<sup>8</sup> With cross-domain knowledge, the units’ cognitive representations of the problem space become more refined (i.e., more precise) as they are mindful of $m _ { u n i t } + c _ { u n i t }$ design elements. The sum of $c _ { b u s }$ and $c _ { I S }$ defines the total amount of cross-domain knowledge that exist in the ISD team. By varying the relative values of $c _ { b u s }$ and $c _ { I S } ,$ we can model different structural patterns of cross-domain knowledge such that one unit possesses more knowledge of the other unit’s domain than the other way around.

## ISD as an Incremental and Iterative Process

In our model, the ISD project team’s objective is to find the highest value point in the fitness landscape of IS designs. As discussed earlier, ISD can be generally characterized as an incremental and iterative process to solve a design problem. The organizational units identify incremental alternative designs whose fitness value is superior to their current level of fitness. While conducting search, the units will satisfice by adopting an alternative design so long as its fitness value is higher than that of its current state (March and Simon 1958). The units may not consider all alternative neighboring configurations to find the best one but will consider alternatives sequentially and choose to adopt the first one whose fitness value is greater than the status quo. In our model, the order in which the units consider alternative neighboring configurations was randomized.

In our model, once a unit has adopted a new design, we switch the focal search unit. In other words, if the business unit has found a new (higher performing) business configuration, then the IS unit searches for a new (higher performing) technology configuration. If the IS unit finds a new (higher performing) technology configuration, then the business unit takes over to search for a new (higher performing) business configuration, and so on. This iterative process repeats until the ISD team can no longer improve system performance.<sup>9</sup>

## Validation of the Model

An important step in the simulation-based theory development process is the validation of the computational model (Davis et al. 2007). However, the notion of validity for computational models is judged with a different set of criteria as for empirical models in field studies due to the high level of abstraction of computational models. Of particular relevance is the assessment of construct and content/face validity (Burton and Obel 1995). First, the assessment of construct validity must ensure that the computational model contains parameters, variables and relations which yield outcomes with correspondence to the real world. To this end, we validated our model by confirming that our basic simulation results are consistent with findings from prior research. The results show that (1) cross-domain knowledge generally improves ISD performance and (2) ISD complexity (i.e., design element interdependence) generally has an adverse effect on ISD performance, which are consistent with intuition and prior research findings.

Second, the assessment of content/face validity should answer the question of whether the model makes sense to domain experts. Although we discussed in the “Theoretical Foundation” section that the assumptions of our NK fitness landscape model are consistent with ISD processes documented in prior literature, a relevant group of domain experts must be persuaded that the model captures some important aspect of the real-world phenomenon, and as a result provokes an expectation to learn something about the phenomenon from the model. We conducted face-to-face and phone interviews with 16 senior managers from public and private sector organizations in a broad range of industries including financial services, media, entertainment, retail, higher education, IT services, software, telecommunication, manufacturing, and the U.S. federal government. Our semi-structured interviews focused on validating the following building blocks of our NK model: (1) ISD problem space is partitioned into two separate knowledge domains (business and technology), (2) ISD team is comprised of two distinct decision making units (business and IS), (3) the business unit is ultimately responsible for making business design decisions and the IS unit is ultimately responsible for making technical design decisions, (4) crossdomain knowledge exists and its distribution across business and IS units may vary, (5) ISD is an incremental and iterative goal-directed search process, and (6) interdependencies among design elements can exist within and across domains. In addition to the field interviews, we presented an earlier version of this paper at a local chapter meeting of the Society for Information Management (SIM) in Washington, DC, and received feedback from the participants on the model assumptions and specifications. Most of the participants were senior IS managers supervising large ISD projects.

The interviewees and the SIM meeting participants confirmed that our model assumptions are reasonable and reflect the essential characteristics of contemporary ISD process. For example, one manager from a financial services company mentioned, “albeit a bit simplified, these [assumptions] are very consistent with the way I think of software development and how my team approaches the task.” Table 1 summarizes our field interview results with quotes that support the validity of our model and assumptions. We conclude that our NK model is valid and the simulation results based on the model can provide useful insights for research as well as practice. However, it is worth noting that some participants pointed out that our NK model represents a high-level, simplified, and mechanistic abstraction of the real-world ISD process that is more complex, disorderly, organic, and social.

## Discussion

In this paper, we explore the relationship between crossdomain knowledge and ISD complexity in effecting the efficacy of ISD projects. Our NK fitness landscape model of ISD equips us with a theoretical and methodological framework to investigate the complex effects of the level and pattern of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance and its variability under varying levels and structural patterns of design element interdependence manifesting ISD complexity. Our study extends and refines the findings of prior research that the cross-domain knowledge shared between business and IS units positively affects ISD performance. The primary objective of this study was to derive new theoretical insights about the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD. In what follows, we discuss the main findings of this study and their implications for research and practice. We then discuss limitations and directions for future research.

## Findings and Implications for Research and Practice

Our results suggest that it is not only how well the various ISD participants know about the knowledge domain of the other units but also how this knowledge is distributed across the units that influences ISD performance. We also find that the level and structural pattern of cross-domain knowledge interact with the level and structural pattern of design element interdependence to affect ISD performance. We derived several propositions from the simulation results. We integrate the propositions by mapping them into a framework and a model shown in Table 3 and Figure 7, respectively. The details are discussed next.

The simulation results suggest that cross-domain knowledge does improve ISD performance (Proposition 1a). Furthermore, cross-domain knowledge is found to reduce the vari ability of ISD performance, thus making ISD performance more predictable and reliable (Proposition 1b). Reduced variability of expected performance can help organizations better manage risks and boost confidence in their investments in ISD projects. While greater cross-domain knowledge leads to higher ISD performance and lower performance variability, this effect is also shown to amplify as the level of interdependencies between design elements increases (Propositions 2a and 2b). These results have significant implications for prac tice. Considering the costs and effort required for developing cross-domain knowledge, organizations should mindfully allocate their resources to enure effective ISD. The results suggest that practitioners should make disproportionately greater investments in acquiring cross-domain knowledge when the ISD project is highly complex because of the increasing return on investment in terms of the level of and predictability in project performance.

How does cross-domain knowledge help to mitigate the challenges arising from IS design element interdependence? Cross-domain knowledge provides a structural mechanism whereby each unit’s search efforts are emergently coordinated toward a unified set of design choices that yield superior search performance. Although each unit independently searches its own design space, knowledge of the other unit’s domain helps to have a more accurate assessment of how its design choice changes will impact the overall fitness for the ISD project. As a result, when a unit searches its own design space, it is not only concerned about how its changes impact the fitness for its own design elements but also considers how these changes might impact the fitness with respect to the choices of the other unit and thereby jointly maximize the overall performance of the ISD project. In doing so, the ISD team as a whole can be guided toward better searching high performance regions within the overall landscape.

Our study provides additional insights into how structural properties of IS design element interdependence interact with cross-domain knowledge in affecting ISD performance. Although much of the literature using the NK fitness landscapes model has assumed a random distribution of interdependencies to operationalize complexity, Rivkin and Siggelkow (2007) alerted that it is not the overall degree of interdependencies but the patterns of interdependencies that determines the complexity of a system. Our study extends this line of work by investigating different patterns of design element interdependencies when there are multiple sub-units with specialized knowledge.

Our analyses show that even when the overall degree of design element interdependence is held constant, the distribution of interdependencies has a significant impact on the difficulty associated with searching for high fitness locations on the landscape. We find that it is more difficult to achieve greater performance when interdependencies are evenly distributed across within and between knowledge domains (e.g., W4B3 and W3B4 in Figure 4). The flip side is that ISD projects are more likely to attain higher performance levels when interdependencies exist mostly within knowledge domains (e.g., W7B0 and W6B1 in Figure 4). When interdependencies exist mostly within knowledge domains, then the influential design elements are more likely to be within each unit’s purview. Our results suggest that when multiple units are independently searching for higher performing designs, one unit’s search activities can result in unreliable assessments if the fitness of its design choices heavily depends on the design choices of the other unit. As a result, when interdependencies are distributed across the units’ specialized domains, each unit’s design choices will alter the nature of the search problem for the other unit, which makes it challenging to converge upon an optimal design configuration.

Cross-domain knowledge enables the ISD team to overcome the inherent difficulties arising from the complexity due to cross-domain interdependencies. With a better assessment of how the other unit’s design choices impact one’s own search, each unit need not be blindsided by the search activities of the other units. As a result, a higher level of cross-domain knowledge will limit the amount of non-promising exploration and consequently reduce the variance of ISD performance across different distributions of interdependencies, thus increasing robustness to different complexity structures (Proposition 5). When the level of cross-domain knowledge is low, we find that an even distribution of interdependencies greatly undermines ISD performance. However, when the level of crossdomain knowledge is high, the even distribution of interdependencies does not undermine ISD performance much. Consequently, the performance gap between a higher level of cross-domain knowledge and a lower level of cross-domain knowledge widens as interdependencies become evenly distributed across the units. Conversely, when interdependencies are concentrated in certain areas (e.g., modular systems, modular business processes), the marginal benefit of cross-domain knowledge decreases (Nissen et al. 2008). When all interdependencies are within each unit’s specialized domain, then while the sub-landscape for each unit may be more complex to search, each unit’s search activities will be not impacted by the design choices of the other unit. In such cases, each unit can independently search for fertile ground without worrying about the consequences of the other unit’s search activities. These insights contribute to the literature on software design modularity by extending design element modularity to two distinct knowledge domains and revealing its interaction with cross-domain knowledge.

These results suggest that practitioners should not only consider overall level of IS design element interdependence but also its structural pattern when deploying cross-domain knowledge. Specifically, cross-domain knowledge within the ISD team becomes more crucial when the project involves business and technical design elements that are interdependent, compared to when they are relatively self-contained within each respective domain. Further, our results suggest that practitioners can improve consistency and robustness in ISD performance against different patterns of design element interdependencies by developing a high level of cross-domain knowledge. Therefore, organizations benefit from developing a high level of cross-domain knowledge especially when they undertake the ISD projects such as ERP, CRM, or SCM systems that require tight integration between business processes and technology architectures or when they carry out multiple projects with different structural patterns of complexity.

One of the unexpected and surprising results relates to the distribution of cross-domain knowledge between units. Contrary to conventional wisdom, we find that higher performance is attained when cross-domain knowledge is asymmetric, skewed, and unevenly distributed across the two units. In other words, it seems more beneficial to have one unit know a lot about the other unit’s domain rather than have both units equally but moderately knowledgeable of each other’s domains (Proposition 3a). One plausible explanation is that the unit with more cross-domain knowledge takes the role of scanning the overall landscape for a high-performance region and the other unit seeks the locally optimal position within that region, thus achieving high performance. Although the finding is counter-intuitive, prior research provides some anecdotal evidence that implicitly and partially supports it. In their field study of 10 business units of three large organizations, Reich and Benbasat (2000) report that the most successful IT implementation was found in the unit where IT managers possessed the highest level of business knowledge, but business managers possessed only a moderate level of IT knowledge. On the other hand, one of the most unsuccessful IT implementations was found in the unit, although its IT managers and business managers both possessed a moderate level of cross-domain knowledge.

The positive effect of unevenly distributed cross-domain knowledge is further amplified at greater levels of design element interdependence (Proposition 4a). Unevenly distributed cross-domain knowledge also reduces performance variability (Proposition 3b) and this effect is also stronger as design element interdependence increases (Proposition 4b). Interestingly, the effect was more pronounced when the distribution of the interdependencies mirrored the distribution of cross-domain knowledge (Proposition 6). For example, when business design mostly dictates technical design, skewed distributions of cross-domain knowledge in favor of business domain knowledge of the IS unit are associated with higher ISD performance compared to even distributions or skewed distributions of cross-domain knowledge in favor of technical knowledge of the business unit. To achieve the highest possible ISD performance, therefore, practitioners should deploy cross-domain knowledge such that its distribution matches the distribution of design element interdependencies. An understanding of the power of this matched asymmetry between cross-domain knowledge and design element interdependence helps organizations to better staff their ISD projects with right team members with the right knowledge sets. Although our findings show the optimal distributions of cross-domain knowledge under different conditions of design element interdependencies, it would be difficult for practitioners to achieve them in real-world projects due to factors including bounded rationality, time and resource constraints, and organizational silos. Nevertheless, this study helps them understand how complex the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD is and thus understand why it is so difficult to optimally integrate business and technology knowledge.

Our findings also challenge us to rethink whether the curriculum and pedagogy in teaching core IS courses such as systems analysis and design and/or IT project management that we take for granted are well suited to tackle these fundamental ISD problems. Our results suggest that one of the most influential factors in affecting ISD effectiveness is design element interdependence. However, extant IS analysis and design methodologies and modeling languages such as the Rational Unified Process and the Unified Modeling Language do not include any formalisms to explicitly model value-based design element interdependencies. Although extant methodologies do allow for the modeling of dependencies but these tend to focus on temporal and structural dependencies, but not on value dependencies, which was found to be of critical importance. Similarly, project human resources management, one of the 10 core project management areas in the Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK) does not explicitly consider cross-domain knowledge or design element interdependencies as important factors to consider when developing the project team.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without limitations. Our computational model adopts a somewhat simplified and functionalistic view by conceptualizing ISD as landscape search, and we acknowledge that ISD, as a whole, is a much more complex, dynamic, socio-technical process (Doherty and King 2005) and multiple conceptions of ISD exist in the literature (Hirschheim and Klein 1989; Iivari et al. 1998; Sambamurthy and Kirsch 2000). For example, although the business unit may own business design decisions and the IS unit may own technical design decisions as we modeled in this study, the actual process by which the two units negotiate design decisions could be more dynamic and complex (Ovaska and Stapleton 2010). Despite the advantages of the simulation method in its ability to incorporate complex dynamics without being constrained by analytical tractability and its ability to study constructs of interest that may be difficult if not impossible to manipulate or observe in field studies, simulation models are stylized theoretical models of reality that require rigorous validation through empirical testing.

Another limitation of this study is that our model is not completely specific to the ISD context. Our model could be applied to any two organizational units that follow an incremental, iterative problem-solving process such as new product development. However, we do not necessarily see this as a weakness for the following reasons. First, a more generic model allows us to make a theoretical contribution to other domains or disciplines. IS scholars have called for IS research that contributes to reference disciplines (Baskerville and Myers 2002). If the insights gained from our study are useful for other disciplines, we see it as an important contribution. Second, even if the model is generic, we can gain context specific insights through the experiment design addressing the problems that are particularly salient in the ISD context. For example, unlike other domains such as finance, accounting, or product development, the ISD domain tends to have a larger variance in the amount of cross-domain knowledge across business and IS units. If accounting and finance are collaborating, the general vocabulary, terminology, and the fundamental knowledge base are relatively similar so there is high cross-domain knowledge in both units. Similarly, in the design of product-service systems, the product unit and the service unit tend to possess a great amount of both product knowledge and service knowledge (Wiesner et al. 2016). Furthermore, the significance of design element interdependence is particularly salient in the ISD context as modern information systems integrate a large number of business elements and technological elements that are interdependent. Our experimental design was constructed to test a wide range of combinations of the amount and structural pattern of crossdomain knowledge under varying amounts and structures of IS design element interdependence. As a result, our simulation experiments produce insights that are specifically useful for the ISD context.

Despite the limitations, our NK landscapes model of ISD has allowed us to provide valuable theoretical insights that can stimulate further theoretical and empirical research. We call for future research in several directions. First, future studies can empirically test the propositions we derived from this study. However, we do not think that a single study can test all the propositions at once since researchers may not be able to freely manipulate all variables included in our simulation model in one study. Therefore, it is important for future empirical studies to report detailed results so that findings can be compared, accumulated, and integrated by meta-analytic studies. Second, future research can extend our study by investigating additional structural patterns of design element interdependence. For instance, it would be interesting to find out how different forms of design modularity interact with cross-domain knowledge. This line of research can contribute not only to the ISD literature but also to the digital platform literature as modularity is a key design principle for digital platforms (Yoo et al. 2010). Third, future research may investigate how cross-domain knowledge influences ISD projects time to reach maximal performance. As time is one of the most crucial constraints for ISD projects, it would be important to understand the effect of cross-domain knowledge on the speed of ISD project execution. Fourth, future studies can generate more actionable insights for practitioners than the present study offers by modeling a specific ISD methodology such as Scrum in greater detail (e.g., Zorin and Hahn 2020).

Finally, there are many opportunities to designing new formalisms that can represent value-based design interdependencies for next generation systems analysis and design and project management methodologies.

## Conclusions

Leveraging the power of the NK landscapes model and simulation methods, our study challenges us to rethink one of the most fundamental and persistent problems in the IS field, namely the structural problem of knowledge disparity among business and IS units, and the role that cross-domain knowledge has in affecting ISD performance. By taking a broader perspective compared to prior research, this study reveals that the effect of cross-domain knowledge on ISD performance is not fixed but rather depends on its structural pattern, the level and pattern of design element interdependencies, and their interactions. That is, the effect is more complex and nuanced than previously understood. We hope that the propositions and insights derived from this study will spark new interests and ideas in this research problem and provide pathways for future research endeavors.

## Appendix B

## The NK Fitness Landscape Model of ISD: An Illustrative Walkthrough

## Problem Space Setup

Following the basic framework of the NK fitness landscape model, an ISD project is conceptualized as consisting of N systems design choices (i.e., requirements), where each design choice $d _ { i }$ can take one of two values: 0 or 1. In other words, an IS configuration can be represented as an N element vector of design configurations: $\mathbf { d } = < d _ { I } , d _ { 2 } , . . . , d _ { N } >$ , which, as a result, can take on $2 ^ { N }$ possible configurations. Each IS design configuration is associated with a fitness value, which represents the performance (i.e., business value) of the IS design. A requirement could represent an application domain related design decision or a technology related design decision.

Continuing the example from the main body of the paper, the various business and technology requirements in an ISD project for a manufacturing company to develop an information system to support its order fulfillment processes could include the choice of demand forecasting time horizon (e.g., short-term versus long-term) (d ), the level of integration between shipping and delivery $( d _ { 2 } )$ , choice of order processing method (e.g., real-time versus batch) $( d _ { 3 } )$ , choice of organizational unit responsible for choice of order handling and fulfillment (e.g., regional offices versus headquarters) $( d _ { 4 } ) .$ , choice of data privacy and security policy (e.g., opt-in versus opt-out) $( d _ { \mathfrak { s } } )$ , choice of software source (e.g., open source versus proprietary) $( d _ { 6 } )$ , choice of communication and data flow model (e.g., push versus pull) $( d _ { 7 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , choice of cloud model (e.g., public versus private) (d ), choice of virtualization technique (e.g., full virtualization versus paravirtualization) $( d _ { g } )$ , choice of data analytics approach (e.g., deep learning versus semi-supervised learning) $( d _ { I 0 } ) ,$ etc.

## Organizational Structure and Allocation of Decisions

We model the organization as composed of two units: a business unit and an IS unit. Each unit is responsible for some portion of the requirements. In our example, requirements $d _ { I }$ through $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot d _ { s }$ are related to the application domain, whereas requirements $d _ { \theta }$ through $d _ { \imath \jmath }$ are related to the technology domain. In other words, there are five business $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \mathbf { d } _ { \mathrm { b u s } } = < d _ { I } , . . . , d _ { \bar { s } } > ; m _ { b u s } = 5 )$ and five technology requirements $( \mathrm { i . e . , \bf d _ { I S } }$ $= < d _ { 6 } , . . . , d _ { I 0 } > ; m _ { I S } = 5 )$ , which are within the purview of the business unit and the IS unit, respectively.

Such interdependencies can exist within the business or IS domains $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , the value contribution of the decision with regards to a business requirement on whether to process orders in real-time $( d _ { 3 } )$ may depend on the decision with regards to a related business requirement on how to set the demand forecasting time horizon $( d _ { I } ) { \vdots }$ or the value contribution of the decision with regards to a technology requirement on how to store data in the cloud $( d _ { \delta } )$ may depend on the decision with regards to another technology requirement relating the data communication flow $( d _ { 7 } ) )$ and/or between domains $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., the value contribution of the decision with regards to a technology requirement on how to store data in the cloud $( d _ { \delta } )$ may depend on the decision with regards to a related business requirement relating to whether or not orders should be handled and fulfilled by each regional office or be consolidated at headquarters $( d _ { 4 } ) \lambda$ ).

## Local Assessment of Fitness

Given the organizational structure of the ISD team partitioned into two separate decision-making units with different knowledge bases, each unit will have an imperfect local assessment of the fitness of their design choices. This section outlines how knowledge partitioning impacts the search process. We first consider the case of fully specialized knowledge where the business and IS units only possess knowledge of the business and technology domains, respectively. Then we will consider the case where cross-domain knowledge exists (i.e., when business and IS units also possess technology and business domain knowledge, respectively).

To make the discussion more concrete, we will continue to use the example of the order fulfillment systems development project. Let us suppose that the IS configuration under current consideration is specified by the array $\mathbf { d } = < 1 , 0 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 >$ , signifying for example that a long-term time horizon is chosen for demand forecasting $( d _ { I } = 1 )$ , tight integration between shipping and delivery is chosen $( d _ { 2 } = 0 )$ , the organization plans to implement real-time processing for orders $( d _ { 3 } = 0 )$ at headquarters $( d _ { 4 } = 1 )$ , an opt-out policy will be implemented for data privacy and security $( d _ { \mathfrak { s } } = 1 )$ , the system will be implemented by developing proprietary software $( d _ { 6 } = 1 )$ to implement a push-driven communications and data flow model $( d _ { 7 } = 0 )$ in a private cloud $( d _ { g } = 1 )$ using full virtualization $( d _ { g } = 0 )$ , and deep learning will be implemented for the data analytics approach for the forecasting prediction model $( d _ { I 0 } = 0 )$ . Also recall the interdependencies in domain knowledge as previously illustrated in Figure B1.

## Specialized Knowledge

In the case of fully specialized knowledge, the fitness contribution of the value of a design choice for a requirement, say the second requirement $( d _ { 2 } = 0 ) .$ , of the configuration array depends on the values of three other business design choices $( d _ { 3 } , d _ { 4 } ,$ , and $d _ { s } )$ and two other technology design choices $( d _ { \theta }$ and $d _ { 7 } )$ So even if the fitness contribution of $d _ { 2 }$ (given $d _ { 3 } , d _ { 4 } ,$ and $d _ { s } )$ depends on the current values of $d _ { 6 } = 1$ and $d _ { 7 } = 0 ,$ if, due to fully specialized knowledge (i.e., conversely, no cross-domain knowledge), since the business unit does not comprehend the implications of different software sourcing approaches $( d _ { 6 } )$ or of different communications and data flow models (d ), the business unit’s assessment of the value contribution of $d _ { 2 } \left( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , c _ { 2 } \right)$ will be based on a generalized assessment of its own unit’s simplified five-dimensional cognitive representation of the fitness landscape. In other words, although the value of having a tight integration between shipping and delivery may be greater if imple mented with push-based data communication model with an existing open source software solution, the business unit’s assessment of its choice of level of integration between shipping and delivery $( d _ { 2 } = 1 )$ cannot take this understanding into account (without cross-domain knowledge), and as a result, will be in a generalized form, which can be estimated as the average of the fitness contributions given the four possible combinations of $\cdot d _ { \tau }$ and $d _ { \imath \jmath }$ values $( \mathrm { i . e . , } < d _ { 6 } , d _ { 7 } > = \{ < 0 , 0 > , < 0 , 1 > , < 1 , 0 > , < 1 , 1 > \} )$ . So although the fitness contribution of the second design variable $d _ { 2 } = 0$ may be quite large $( { \mathrm { e . g . } } , c _ { 2 } ( d _ { 2 } \mid d _ { 3 } , d _ { 4 } , d _ { 5 } , d _ { 6 } { = } 1 , d _ { 7 } { = } 0 ) = 0 . 9 5 4 )$ it can also be quite poor for some configurations of the unknown design variables $( \mathrm { e . g . } , c _ { 2 } ( d _ { 2 } \mid d _ { 3 } , d _ { 4 } , d _ { 5 } , d _ { 6 } { = } 0 , d _ { 7 } { = } 1 ) = 0 . 1 2 7 )$ ). Nonetheless, the business unit who is assessing the value of $d _ { 2 } = 0$ , estimates that, on average, the fitness contribution of setting the first design choice $d _ { 2 }$ to 0 will be 0.674. We can easily infer from this numerical example that each unit’s assessment of the fitness contributions of each design choice will be less accurate when there are a greater number of requirements interdependencies across knowledge domains. ## Cross-Domain Knowledge

To illustrate the case where cross-domain knowledge exists, let us suppose again that the IS configuration under current consideration is specified by the array $\mathbf { d } = < 1 , 0 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 >$ . We model cross-domain knowledge with parameter $c _ { u n i t } ,$ which represents the number of design elements in the other unit’s knowledge domain, for which the focal unit has knowledge. Suppose that $c _ { b u s } = c _ { I S } = 1$ and that it was determined that the business unit also has knowledge about what it means for the data / communications model to be push- or pull-based (d ). In this case, since the fitness contribution of the value of the first element $( d _ { 2 } = 0 )$ of this array depends on the values of the second $( d _ { 3 } = 0 )$ fourth $( d _ { 4 } = 1 )$ , fifth $( d _ { 5 } = 1 )$ , seventh $( d _ { 6 } = 1 )$ and tenth $( d _ { 7 } = 0 )$ elements, and the business unit now understands the fitness contribution of $d _ { 2 }$ (given $d _ { s } , d _ { 4 } , d _ { 5 } ,$ and d ), the only unknown element will be $d _ { 6 } ,$ or how sourcing the software via open source software or developing proprietary software may impact the value contribution of its order entry approach. As a result, the business unit’s assessment of c will be the average of the fitness contributions given the two possibilities for $d _ { I 0 } ( \mathrm { i . e . , } d _ { 6 } = \{ 0 , 1 \} )$ ). ## ISD Process as Landscape Search

ISD is modeled as an iterative and incremental process whereby the two separate decision-making units (i.e., the business unit and the IS unit) take turns in determining the IS design choices for their respective knowledge domains (i.e., the application domain and the technology domain). Continuing with the example of the order fulfillment system development project, let’s suppose that an ISD project’s current configuration at t is specified by the array $\mathbf { d } ( t ) = < 1 , 0 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 >$ , with $N = 1 0 , m _ { b u s } = m _ { I S } = 5 , \mathrm { s o } { \bf d _ { b u s } } ( t ) = < 1 , 0 , 0 , 1$ , 1> and ${ \bf d } _ { \mathrm { I S } } ( t ) = < 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 >$ Also for simplicity of the walkthrough, further supposed that knowledge is fully specialized (i.e., there is no cross-domain knowledge; $c _ { b u s } =$ $c _ { I S } = 0 ) , { } ^ { 1 6 }$ but the ISD process for the case where cross-domain knowledge exists will be exactly the same albeit with a local assessment of fitness as described above.

During the business process design phase $( \mathrm { i . e . , a t } t + 1 )$ , the business unit will consider up to 5 neighboring locations in the fitness landscape— $\{ < 0 , 0 , 0 , 1 , 1 > , < 1 , { \cal I } , 0 , 1 , 1 > , < 1 , 0 , { \cal I } , 1 , 1 > , < 1 , 0 , 0 , 0 , 1 > , < 1 , 0 , 0 , 1 , 0 > \} - \mathrm { t o t } { \cal I } > 0 \}$ identify an alternative configuration of the systems design choices in the application domain that improves the fitness of the order fulfillment system. Here, the assessment of fitness will be based on the business unit’s imperfect and local assessment given its lack of knowledge in the technology domain (see above). For example, the business unit may first consider, among the five alternative neighboring configurations, the second alternative (i.e., <1, 1, 0, 1, 1>) and actually perceive it to have higher fitness value than the current configuration of the business design choices, but this alternative may not actually produce higher value given the current configuration of the technological design choices $( \mathrm { i . e . , } { \bf d _ { I S } } ( t ) = < 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 > )$ . Nonetheless, the business unit will adop this alternative and the new configuration (i.e., $\mathbf { d _ { \mathrm { { b u s } } } } ( t { + } 1 ) = { < } 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 1 > )$ and the current design configuration of the overall order fulfillmen system will be ${ \bf d } ( t + 1 ) = < { \bf d } _ { \mathrm { b u s } } ( t + 1 ) , { \bf d } _ { \mathrm { I s } } ( t ) > = < 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 >$

During the information systems design phase (i.e., at t+2), the IS unit will now consider 5 neighboring locations— $\cdot \{ < 0 , 0 , 1 , 0 , 0 > , < 1 , l , 1 ,$ $0 , 0 > , < 1 , 0 , 0 , 0 , 0 > , < 1 , 0 , 1 , { \cal I } , 0 > , < 1 , 0 , 1 , 0 , { \cal I } >$ to identify an alternative configuration of the systems design choices in the technology domain that improves the fitness of the order fulfillment system. Again, the assessment of fitness will be based on the IS unit’s imperfect and local assessment given its lack of knowledge in the application domain (see above). Suppose the IS unit first considers (randomly) the third alternative (i.e., <1, 0, 0, 0, 0>), but perceives it as a poor choice as its resulting fitness will not be greater than the status quo. The IS unit will then (randomly) choose another alternative, say the fourth one $( \mathrm { i . e . , < l , } 0 , 1 , 1 , 0 > )$ an verify that it seems like a fitness enhancing alternative. However, this may or may not actually be the optimal choice given the current configuration of the business design choices $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \mathbf { d _ { \mathrm { b u s } } } ( t { + } 1 ) =$ <1, 1, 0, 1, 1>). This alternative will be adopted by the IS unit (i.e., $, \mathbf { d } _ { \mathrm { I S } } ( t { + } 2 ) = < 1 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 0 > )$ and the current design configuration of the overall order fulfillment system will be ${ \bf d } ( t + 2 ) = < { \bf d } _ { \mathrm { b u s } } ( t + 1 ) , { \bf d } _ { \mathrm { I s } } ( t + 2 ) > = < 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 1 , 0 , 1 , 1 , 0 >$ . The above iterative process of requirements determination and systems design continues until both business and IS units are unable to find fitness improving alternative configurations.
