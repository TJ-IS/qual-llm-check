RESEARCH ARTICLE

# A case study of the legitimation process undertaken to gain support for an information system in a Chinese university

Donal Flynn<sup>1</sup> and Yongqin Du<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Manchester, U.K.; <sup>2</sup>Shell International Petroleum Company Ltd, Shell Centre, London, U.K.

Correspondence: Donal Flynn, Manchester Business School, University of Manchester, Booth Street East, Manchester M15 6PB. UK. Tel: þ 44 (0) 1613063333; E-mail: donal.flynn@mbs.ac.uk

## Abstract

This research examines the legitimation process that was undertaken to gain stakeholder support for the development of a smartcard system for student services at a local university in China. We employed a theoretical framework consisting of Suchman’s typology of legitimacy and the Legitimation Activity Model (LAM) for sense-making of the process. We obtained insights into how legitimation of the system was gained, maintained and repaired through the project team’s strategic actions. We conceptualized the rotation of legitimation gaining, maintaining and repairing activities in the project and make suggestions for improving the LAM. We emphasize that monitoring and evaluating legitimation is a dynamic, continuous process, involving the concept of legitimation status, and linking the planned actions of legitimationgaining and responsive actions for legitimation maintaining and repairing. We suggest that to achieve legitimation for information systems, managers should plan strategies to construct and present an appropriate image of the system, in which stakeholders perceive that it meets their needs, and, in addition, closely monitor the status of legitimation granted over a continuous period. This is because legitimation is a ‘fragile flower’ that may wilt if not attended to adequately.

## Introduction

Social and organizational issues are important when developing information systems (IS) in organizations. A key issue is gaining stakeholder acceptance, as introducing new IS to organizations often involves major social consequences such as organizational change (Markus, 2004). Proposing changes to individual work practice and relationships often generates mistrust among employees and doubts that the IS would benefit them or the organization. For example, a recent study shows that the major success factor for eHealth system adoption is securing acceptance from stakeholders whose job roles and workflow would be affected (European Commission, 2009). Avison & Young (2007) emphasize that gaining support from end users and buy-in of key stakeholders is important for successful IS implementation.

Research has shown that the approval and commitment of senior organizational members for IS is a critical success factor, and can facilitate acceptance by other organizational members, especially in Asian countries such as China (Zhang et al, 2002; Reimers, 2003). Even when use of a new IS is mandated as a result of the exercise of power and domination (Myers & Young, 1997; Jasperson et al, 2002), it is still the case that stakeholder acceptance presents one of the biggest challenges to achieving successful IS (Brown et al, 2002). Furthermore, contemporary IS are reaching individuals who may or may not choose to use the IS. The need for stakeholder recognition and approval is greater if the use of an IS is not mandated.

IS projects frequently take place in an atmosphere of near-crisis, and the Standish Group Inc (2003) study indicates that 43% of software projects were over budget with 54% missing deadlines. IS managers often find their relationship with user groups difficult and problematic (Brown 1998; Keable et al, 1998; Gefen & Riding, 2003), and achieving continued input from key organizational stakeholders is therefore necessary for IS projects to secure resources and support.

Central to these considerations is that if stakeholders hold the belief that an IS and the changes implied by the IS will be beneficial to them, they will accordingly offer their acceptance and support. When stakeholders endorse and support goals and activities, it is said that legitimation is conferred (Elsbach & Sutton, 1992). Stakeholders are more likely to grant legitimation for a new IS when the goals of the system and consequential organizational changes seem acceptable to them (Kohli & Kettinger, 2004). In recent years, legitimation is increasingly accepted as an important issue in IS studies (Brown, 1995, 1998; Flynn & Hussain, 2004; Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006; Pawlowski et al, 2006; Wang & Swanson, 2007; Hussain & Cornelius, 2009; Kaganer et al, 2010), and is emerging as an alternative to the traditional discourse concerning IS acceptance, suggesting that there may be wider reasons for such acceptance other than technical rationality.

In this article, we aim to address a single research question: how are IS project events, activities and strategies, carried out within a legitimation process, understood by organizational participants? We present a case study of the legitimation process that took place in an IS project at a Chinese university, generating in-depth understanding of the meanings and perspectives behind the activities of the project team and other stakeholders, employing an interpretivist, qualitative approach to ascertain stakeholder meanings and interpretations. We adopt an integrated framework for case analysis due to Flynn & Puarungroj (2006) that is the most detailed account of the legitimation process in the literature to date, analysing the legitimation process at the project level and legitimation activities at the task level.

The article is structured as follows: we firstly introduce key elements of legitimation theory, followed by a review of IS legitimation research. After outlining the research method, we present the case study and analysis. We then discuss the case findings and show that legitimation was gained through several major phases, and that legitimation maintaining and repairing activities were carried out when stakeholder support and acceptance fell to an unsatisfactory level. Sets of legitimation strategies have also been identified. We conclude the article by suggesting a set of propositions and conceptualizations concerning the dynamic nature of the legitimationseeking process, based on modifications to the Legitimation Activity Model (LAM) (Flynn & Hussain, 2004).

## The evolving process view of legitimation

## Conceptualizing organizational legitimation

Legitimation of someone or something implies a perception that it is appropriate (Suchman, 1995), a normative acceptance of its ‘rightness’ (Habermas, 1973; Brown, 1998), or recognition that it is reasonable and just (Della Fave, 1991). The terms legitimation and legitimacy tend to be used synonymously, and a definition of legitimacy is:

legitimacy is a generalised perception or assumption that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate within some socially constructed system of norms, values, beliefs and definitions. (Suchman, 1995, p. 574)

Organizational actors obtain legitimacy for themselves and their activities in order to acquire the participation, enthusiasm and commitment from others that is necessary for managing their activities effectively (Pfeffer, 1981; Oliver, 1991), to ensure sustainable support for organizational leadership (Chakravarthy, 1997), to acquire resources for survival and growth (Zimmerman & Zeitz, 2002), and to facilitate organizational changes (Chakravarthy & Gargiulo, 1998). Achieving legitimacy for organizational change is particularly important because, as Nadan (1997) argues, all organizational stakeholders possess and are able to exercise some sort of power and influence to resist the change, irrespective of their status. Chakravarthy & Gargiulo (1998) discuss how legitimacy for leadership was achieved through understanding and influencing employee norms, and satisfying their demands such as incentive and empowerment. Researchers (Elsbach, 1994; Scott, 2001) suggest that the theoretical background of legitimation is in institutional theory and resource dependency theory. Berger & Luckmann (1966) suggest that legitimation is a ‘secondorder’ process of ‘explaining and justifying’ that arises from a discourse creating shared knowledge and beliefs.

## Suchman typology of legitimacy

One of the significant developments in our understanding is Suchman’s (1995) work on legitimation, based on organizational research. Integrating prior research on indicators or proxies of legitimation (Hunt & Aldrich, 1996; Scott, 2001), Suchman proposes a set of some 30 legitimation strategies, which are activities aimed at seeking legitimation. He categorizes the strategies into three groups: gaining, maintaining and repairing. Gaining legitimation occurs when, for example, organizations are initiating a new activity or operation. Once legitimation has been gained, it must be maintained, and organizations need to forecast future changes and protect accomplishments, converting legitimation from episodic to continual forms. Legitimation repair is a reactive response to an unforeseen decline in support, such as resource interruption or a shift in stakeholder norms.

Suchman further suggests that legitimacy can be classified into three types, with each legitimation strategy being associated with one of the types. Pragmatic legitimacy refers to the legitimacy given by an organizational stakeholder for self-interested motives, for example where a stakeholder accepts financial rewards and in return grants support and approval. Moral legitimacy reflects a positive normative evaluation of the organization and its activities (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994), where stakeholders perceive that they should provide their support towards such activities as it is ‘the right thing to do’ (Suchman, 1995, p. 579). Cognitive legitimacy borrows mainly from institutional theory (Scott, 1987, 1995) in that when particular activities become familiar and widely accepted in society, reproducing such collective activities becomes common and taken-forgranted for success (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994). Cognitive legitimacy thus refers to the legitimacy given by an organizational stakeholder based on their belief that activities should be supported, as they appear to be taken-for-granted practices (Hannan & Freeman, 1986; Zimmerman & Zeitz, 2002).

## Forerunners of legitimation studies in IS research

The main forerunner of IS legitimation studies is the area of individual adoption of IS within the organization. Scheepers et al. (2006) suggest two dominant streams: technology acceptance and user satisfaction. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) is a wellestablished and widely applied model for predicting user acceptance, based on the Theory of Reasoned Action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1980) and its successor the Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen, 1985). TAM suggests two determinants of usage intention: perceived usefulness (the extent to which users perceive that the use of an IS will enhance their job performance), and perceived ease of use (the extent to which the use of an IS will require much effort). Descendants of TAM, such as TAM 2 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), Task-Technology Fit (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995) and the Unified Theory for the Acceptance and Use of Technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003) include additional determinants. Rogers’ (1983) theory of innovation diffusion has also been applied to organizational IS adoption. In these models, acceptance is conceptualized in terms of variables that measure perspectives formed by users in relation to accepting technology, and that are used to explain variance in individual behavioural intention. TAM-like models have been criticized on various issues (Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 1996; Jensen & Aanestad, 2007), including their inability to address the intention-behaviour gap (Verheijden et al, 2008), but perhaps their most significant limitation is that they do not account for the source and construction of the perspectives that they use to explain variance.

The user satisfaction stream emphasizes user evaluations of their overall experience with an IS (Chin & Lee, 2000), and Scheepers et al. (2006) argue that for mandated use (as in this case study), user satisfaction is a more appropriate reflection of user acceptance than system usage. This stream takes a process approach and focuses on the meanings that individuals attribute to their attitudes and behaviour concerning IS adoption. For example, Davidson (2006) discusses the ways in which technological frames of reference explain how organizational members make sense of technology, and how such sense-making is critical in influencing their actions related to technology and technologically enabled change. Kaarst-Brown & Robey (1999) take a metaphor approach, finding that organizational members conceptualize appropriate styles of IT management in terms of five archetypes. Boudreau & Robey (2005) found that improvised learning accounted for the ways in which organizational members interpreted and used an ERP system.

Investigating user participation behaviour, Barki & Hartwick (1994) found a lack of user involvement in the activities of IS staff, and Keable et al. (1998) pointed out that the user–IS relationship can suffer from a legitimacy gap, where certain actions and decisions of IS professionals are viewed as not possessing legitimacy by users. To deal with these different perspectives, researchers recommend that user contributions to and influence on IS projects can close the gap (Davis, 1982; Eason & Harker, 1988).

User resistance has received great attention in IS research, and Bhattacherjee & Hikmet (2007) discuss how user resistance is an opposing force precluding potential IS acceptance which can be caused by the user’s lack of knowledge of the IS (Kaplan, 1997), its benefits (Ash et al, 2000), reluctance to change (Jiang et al, 2000), or fear of loss of prestige and organizational status (Worthley, 2000). Orlikowski & Gash (1994) link resistance with frame incongruence between relevant social groups. Such attitudes may generate insecurity among organizational members and contribute to doubts that the introduction of the IS would benefit them or the organization.

The user–IS developer gap can also cause user resistance as Markus (1983) states ‘resistance is the result of the distance between the designers and users’. User resistance may accordingly be viewed as an expression of a user perspective that they do not intend to grant, or may withdraw, legitimation. Recognizing the need to understand the underlying reasons for such user behaviour, Hirschheim & Newman (1988) encourage IS developers to be more engaged in finding out the norms of users and to take necessary reconciliatory measures to avoid undesirable consequences.

## Legitimation research in IS development

Klein & Hirschheim (1989) argue that a legitimation gap occurs when aspects of the IS (or the IS development method) do not match social norms and other factors in the environment where the IS is to be introduced. Failure to legitimate could widen the gap and lead to IS failures. Banville (1991, p. 112) states ‘the development or implementation of information systems are usually intended to be durable social changes: they have to be legitimated’. Markus (2004) provides insight into the origins of this gap in her discussion of technochange misfits, which are: ‘misalignments between a technology or a technochange solution and important dimensions of the organizational setting in which it is used’. Task misfits do not fit the ways people work in particular settings, cultural misfits are at odds with organizational cultures, and incentive misfits are misaligned with organizational authority and reward systems.

Recent IS research employs a legitimation perspective for explaining social and organizational issues in Information Systems Development (ISD) and suggests that seeking legitimation is important in achieving stakeholder acceptance in IS organizational adoption, and that legitimation has a large influence on the success or failure of IS projects (Brown, 1998; Keable et al, 1998; Flynn & Hussain, 2004; Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006; Pawlowski et al, 2006; Wang & Swanson, 2007; Hussain & Cornelius, 2009; Kaganer et al, 2010).

Brown (1995) describes how the sponsors of a Hospital Information Support System achieved legitimation by managing stakeholder perceptions towards the system. He suggests that in order to gain user acceptance of an IS, legitimation can be achieved through calculated arguments, control over the flow of information, and symbolic acts. Kohli & Kettinger (2004) describe a case where management, facing strong resistance to proposed changes in physician behaviour, modified norms and values so that previously unpopular changes received legitimation.

Flynn & Hussain (2004) analyse how legitimation was achieved through the strategic actions of a project team in three hospital IS projects in the U.K., and develop a process model of legitimation-seeking activities, the LAM shown in Figure 1. The legitimation-seeking process reflects the interplay between the two key actors in IS projects: legitimation seekers (LS), typically the project authorities (e.g., the project team) who are motivated to seek support for an IS, and legitimation providers (LP), who are the IS ‘recipients’ (e.g., users, senior managers, business partners) whose legitimation is being sought by the legitimation seekers.

In the LAM, the legitimation process commences when LS begin to construct a legitimation target. This is a description of the IS characteristics and its predicted organizational effects, similar to the concept of organizing vision (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). LS then identify a range of LP, who should be individuals or groups who are most sceptical of the project (Brown, 1995) or who have influence over others who may be key to gaining legitimation (Kohli & Kettinger, 2004). LS try to understand LP’s norms (e.g., beliefs, cultures, routines and practices), and compare these norms with those underlying the legitimation target. This comparison reveals whether there is a gap between the two. In order to close such a gap, LS apply several legitimation strategies based on their judgment of appropriateness. This may imply changing some IS characteristics, or, alternatively, persuading some LP to change their norms. After applying the strategies, LS check their effects, to establish whether LP have granted legitimation to the IS. Legitimation usually needs to be monitored over a period of time and if there appears to be a failure of legitimation, LS may need to repeat the steps of the legitimation process. Flynn & Puarungroj (2006) employ an integrated theoretical framework consisting of the LAM and the Suchman typology to investigate the legitimationseeking process in a hospital IS project in Thailand. The Suchman typology was utilized to understand the nature of legitimation strategies at the task level.

Recent legitimation research thus takes a process view of IS adoption, regarding individuals as social actors who exist in a co-productive relationship with their social and technological context (Orlikowski et al, 1995). The emphasis is on the perspectives (Davidson, 2002) that such actors bring to the workplace that shape their adoption-related behaviour, viewed as the creation and enactment of such perspectives through everyday work practices (Bansler & Havn, 2004). We take this approach when investigating the legitimation process in our case study, where we investigate the applicability of the integrated framework (Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006), focusing on strategies for maintaining and repairing legitimacy, as these constitute a gap in the current literature.

## Research method

## Design, data collection and analysis

We investigated a project for smartcard system development in a medium-size regional university located in

China, employing a case study approach similar to other researchers in this area (Brown, 1995, 1998; Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006). Access to the organization was proposed by one of the researchers (YD) based on his personal contact with one of the university’s vice presidents. Before the access was confirmed, a meeting was arranged with the acting project manager (director of the network centre) in order to evaluate suitability for research. The university was chosen as most key informants were available for interview and the case was believed to fulfil the research objectives.

We used an interpretivist, qualitative approach with the aim of establishing understanding, seeking to explore with participants, through everyday language, their perceptions of issues and behaviour surrounding legitimation and support that were related to the smartcard system. Such an approach allows researchers to construct ‘thick descriptions’ and to develop theory (Walsham, 1995) concerning the legitimation process in IS projects. In general, the endpoint of IS research is to generate new theory, and/or to explore the applicability of a given theory to a different research context (Chiasson & Davidson, 2004) and we chose a combination of retroductive and abductive research strategies (Blaikie, 2000; Buchanan & Bryman, 2009, p. 438).

In our retroductive strategy (Blaikie, 2000, p. 87), we selected, before data collection began, an initial set of categories and topics that originate from existing theories and used it as ‘sensitizing theory’ (Walsham, 1995) to guide the research. Sensitizing theory may be confirmed (within the particular case study context) or modified; new theory may also be generated. In this research, existing theories concerning the typology of legitimacy (Suchman, 1995), and the legitimation process (LAM) in IS projects (Flynn & Hussain, 2004) were used as sensitizing theory to inform the research. Given that in data collection researchers are hardly ‘theory-free’ (Silverman, 1997), the retroductive strategy was adopted because the existing legitimation theories provided a set of general topics that appeared relevant to guiding the data collection tasks. In data analysis, evidence supporting the sensitizing theory was sought and analysed. The abductive strategy (Blaikie, 2000, p. 89), focusing on the meanings and interpretations, motives and intentions of social actors, was used as it encourages detection of ‘surprising facts or qualitative anomalies’, which cannot be explained by existing theories, that may generate better explanations and new theories (Patokorpi & Ahvenainen, 2009). In data analysis, attention was paid to actors’ concepts (in their own language) concerning legitimation-related issues, which were used as the basis for category and theory construction to further understand legitimation-seeking.

Data collection was carried out in two field visits between October 2007 and May 2008 by the author (YD) who was familiar with the language, context, and culture of China. The main data collection method was semistructured interviews with the project team, various user departments and students (see Table 1 for a list of interviewees). A total of 29 interviews were carried in a private environment, and the average duration of interviews was 30 min. Based on the collected data, the history of the project was re-constructed. We consider the retrospective data collection to be a limitation of the study. To deal with the issue of retrospective accounts being offered by interviewees, we attempted to arrange the sequence of interview questions based on the timeline of the project and specifically asked the informants for their experience in particular project events. During the data collection we also gathered relevant project documentation to understand project background and organizational context. Triangulation between different sources (documents, individuals) was employed to minimize biased accounts.

We used a broad grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) approach to the analysis of interviews (as well as researcher notes based on observations) in parallel with the use of categories from our sensitizing theory. We iterated between transcripts and categories (both pre-defined and emergent), modifying categories as necessary. Discussion meetings between the two researchers were held frequently to define and refine categories. Initially, a sequence of main project phases was produced based on a reconstruction of project events (Keil, 1995).

Working from the transcripts, we used the sensitizing theory to firstly identify project team members as legitimation seekers, and those stakeholder groups consulted by the project team as legitimation providers. Secondly, the set of legitimation strategies (Suchman, 1995) was used to identify legitimation-seeking activities undertaken by the project team using two methods: (a) carefully considering the nature of a given activity and its context, (b) deciding on the type of legitimacy the activity was aimed at achieving. Suchman describes the nature of each strategy and also its aim, in terms of achieving pragmatic, moral or cognitive legitimacy.

Thirdly, Suchman classifies activities as gaining, maintaining and repairing, but these depend on an undefined notion of the status of legitimation, for example whether it is granted or not granted. To make sense of this, and also to understand project progress in seeking legitimation, we found it necessary to characterize the different types of legitimation status. Accordingly, we looked for terms and phrases in the transcripts that indicated legitimation being sought, gained, weakened or withdrawn, such as ‘we addressed . complaints’, ‘it’s [the system] a good thing’, ‘showed willingness in supporting the project’, ‘I am concerned’, and built corresponding categories.

## Case study background

Modern Technology University (MTU) was founded by the government of Changsha, the capital city of Hunan Province, China. The university employs about 700 staff and has over 12,000 students in 16 academic departments. Most of MTU’s IT projects were led by the university’s network centre which was responsible for producing and implementing IT strategy and systems. One of the technologies that had attracted the interest of the director of the network centre was smartcard technology. A smartcard is a pocket-sized card with embedded integrated circuits for identification, authentication and data storage, enabling data transfer and business transactions with minimal human intervention. Since the 1990s, smartcard technology has been widely adopted by educational organizations for student identification, access to facilities and services and electronic payments.

In September 2004, students complained that payment cards issued by each of five canteens in the university could not be used interchangeably. To address this issue, the university’s logistics department, which oversaw the affairs of catering and retailing, proposed to acquire a smartcard system to connect all canteens. The director of the network centre believed this to be an opportunity to develop a multi-function student service system utilizing smartcard technology, where students would access a variety of university services using the all-purpose smartcard. It was proposed that some staff of the university’s administrative and service departments might also utilize the system; for example, the finance department would be able to collect student tuition fees through the system and a security subsystem would be used as a time and attendance mechanism for campus security guards. Additional applications could be added depending on requirements and wishes – an important advantage, beside expected cost savings and increased administration efficiency and service levels.

From this starting point, the smartcard system project went through a 30-month period (September 2004 – March 2007), consisting of several stages of technical development, rolling out the system in various user departments, implementing new functions, and operation and maintenance of the system. In the next section, we present the history of the smartcard system project and our analysis of legitimation activities carried out in the project.

By ‘Advertising the product’ the logistics department and students were informed that the team was addressing their needs.

In March 2005, the team completed payment subsystem implementation in canteens as well as in campus shops. The security subsystem (which had not actually been a requirement of the security department but was implemented as it was the simplest component of the smartcard system) involved smartcard sensors installed across the campus. Security guards were required to touch their smartcard on a sensor during their patrol. The security department director commented:

I think I was impressed and I thought the system very advanced and cutting-edge.

However, security guards, who were employees of an external security service company, had not been consulted about this surveillance of their performance, and although they did not publicly voice their concerns, negative emotions started developing among them. A security guard mentioned:

Honestly, the system was only good for the management, but for individual staff, it was not very good for us. It controlled us too strictly. I would certainly prefer the manual way to log our shifts and have these paper records checked by my supervisors. But who would listen to us?

However, since both subsystems were operating without reported problems, the team considered that the first project deliverable was completed successfully, and they did not perceive any legitimation gap at this stage.

the work went on extremely smoothly and we were very optimistic about the project (team leader)

At this time the team leader was promoted to the post of deputy director of ‘the office of the presidents’. Consequently, the team adopted the symbolic identity of ‘the office of the presidents’ and enjoyed a significant advantage in organizing project activities and collaborating with other departments, as the office of the presidents had a higher organizational status than other departments. Regarding his promotion, he said:

I think our department has done a better job than any other university in terms of the coordination of the smartcard project y because I am also the vice-director of the office of presidents.

Grant legitimation In September 2005, aiming at a smooth roll-out, the team involved library staff to discuss smartcard system implementation in the university library. These co-optees had access to the decisionmaking and supportively facilitated the work. The library’s chief IT technician said:

I personally thought the system y [was] a right direction to go!

Other departments proposed new requirements that were not included in the original project plan. The academic registry office suggested replacing the student examination card with the smartcard. The student management department proposed crediting student loans and grants to student smartcard accounts, instead of paying cash. The team ‘responded to their needs’, stating that they would fulfil these new requirements, and staff of the two departments showed their appreciation, indicating they had granted legitimation. The exam officer commented:

a really good thing for us was that this largely eliminated the possibility of fake ID credentials.

it has greatly reduced our workload. We are now working more efficiently than ever before. (Student management department director)

Most students liked these additional features of their smartcard, for example the ‘top-up’ function by which a smartcard user can add credits into her/his account by cash or bankcard. Student 1 said:

Things like top-up, check balance, receiving supporting funds from the university y a lot of functions y it’s very convenient.

Firstly, by accepting the smartcard as a form of examination ID credential, this made it essential for students to enter examinations and thus became an ‘irreplaceable function’. Secondly, over a quarter of students received financial support from the university. By crediting the funds directly into their smartcard account, this segment of students was encouraged to use the smartcard more. This move ‘formalized’ the marginal top-up activities and brought them under official control (Suchman, 1995, p. 589).

In November 2005, some students energetically joined the network centre as part-time workers for system maintenance and user support. The team treated the student workers as ‘friendly co-optees’ (Suchman, 1995, p. 600) and offered them the opportunity to contribute to the success of the system by engaging them to become part of the project.

By May 2008, the total number of active users reached 12,000. The system covered every part of the university campus, with over 200 point-of-sale (POS), 10 top-up machines, and three touch-screen self-service machines. The volume of payments the system processed in 2007 topped 15 million RMB (approximately d1.1 million). The vice president of IT was satisfied by the achievements of the project:

I think the system has provided students with significant conveniences. The standardisation and control of canteen operations are better. From the management point of view, they are now working to expand the system’s functions. It might not be perfect but I will say it had absolutely no negative effect in any way.

## Maintaining legitimation

It is suggested that legitimation seekers should continuously monitor the outcome of their legitimation-seeking activities and the status of granted legitimation (Scheid-Cook, 1992), as it is difficult to satisfy or recognize unanticipated demands of all stakeholders. We discuss how the team detected and responded to events that threatened to damage system acceptance, and Table 3 summarizes the legitimation-maintaining strategies carried out.

Some canteen staff kept a written record of all transactions and compared it with system printouts. Canteen manager 3 stated:

I am concerned about missing money and that’s not acceptable for us. I have to have a clear idea about where my money is and if the system showed me a figure that I couldn’t trust, then there was a problem.

Shops were given POS to accept smartcard payments but shop owners were reluctant to use them because they needed to wait for 7 days to receive money from the finance department. A shop owner said:

There are always some monies staying in the system.

Realizing that user doubts could develop further and seriously undermine legitimation, the team produced a formal organizational policy in an attempt to ‘standardize new models’ (Table 2), which compelled all canteens and shops to use the smartcard system and eliminate all cash transactions. However, this attempted coercion resulted in little effect as canteen and shop staff ignored it.

The director of the network centre expressed his worry:

We were thinking: why had they [developed] negative attitudes and unwillingness to the project and were so difficult to work with?

Soon after students were given smartcards in April 2005, some complained that there were too few places where they were accepted.

I think if we can extend the use of smartcard to a wider range, for example, to pay for the use of public PC labs, that’s going to be great. (Student 4)

It became clear at this time that the team’s previous belief that departmental head support would guarantee user acceptance was wrong. User resistance drew the team’s attention to emerging challenges to legitimation, triggering legitimation-maintaining action. The team ‘consulted doubters’ (Table 3), realizing that they must gain support from additional stakeholders such as students, canteen and shop staff.

This was followed by activities to strengthen legitimation. Firstly, more smartcard POS were installed across the university. This decision to expand system coverage was to ‘respond to needs’ (Table 2) of students for more convenient use of the system. Secondly, the team ‘allowed more time for users to realize benefits’ (Table 2) by letting canteen staff familiarize themselves with the system. A reliable system over a period of time can facilitate growth of stakeholder comprehensibility and taken-for-grantedness (Suchman, 1995). Thirdly, the team decided not to enforce the organizational policy concerning compulsory use as, due to technical constraints, they could not modify the system to meet shop owners’ needs for a shorter fund processing cycle.

Another situation arose in September 2006 where the implementation of the system at a new student residence hall was delayed due to a shortage of funding. While the team worked to secure funding, students who moved into the hall became upset about the absence of the system, expressing negative comments about the project and the team. The network centre director said:

We held meetings to discuss the matters. Well, we have addressed student complaints by following our normal working procedure, and we announced what we did y on the website and on the campus notice board.

The team ‘consulted opinion leaders’ and obtained insight into student demands (Table 3). They explained that the external sponsor’s delay in providing funds was the root of the problem and that the team should not be responsible for problems caused by uncontrollable factors. The team demonstrated that they made every effort to solve the problem as quickly as they could, thereby ‘stockpiling esteem’ (Table 3) as they were acting responsibly in the event. Students appreciated the efforts the project team had made as Student 5 stated that:

we did understand that the network centre had tried hard, and it was really a matter of time to solve this problem.

## Repairing legitimation

Project teams may realize they are encountering a legitimation crisis when previous support from legitimation providers suddenly disappears. They must react to repair and regain legitimation, perhaps on new grounds. Table 4 summarizes the legitimation-repairing strategies carried out by the project team.

Since the security subsystem was put into service in March 2005, its surveillance feature had gradually begun to alienate security guards. Unfortunately the team was not aware of the decline in legitimation until June 2005 when they suspected that equipment had been damaged by the guards.

to [the security guards], it’s painful to work under the system . They don’t have much freedom. So they damage the equipment to avoid using it. (smartcard service manager)

In an attempt to remedy the situation, the team contacted the guards. The external security company, by whom the guards were employed, held a fixed longterm contract with the university. The security subsystem was not included in the service terms and conditions and the team’s attempt to persuade them to use it failed. The security department director, who had supported the security subsystem earlier, refused to discipline the guards, a typical example of a self-reinforced legitimation crisis where long-standing allies may disassociate themselves from troubles to avoid guilt and criticism (Suchman, 1995, p. 597). This was a signal that the team was in a crisis and had to promptly repair the lost legitimation.

Because we had no formal power to force y their [security guards] actions, there was nothing we could do to save the system. (project manager)

The team believed that, for the reasons above, rescuing the security subsystem seemed unlikely to succeed. Moreover, they thought that escalating more effort might endanger their image, because other stakeholders might interpret this as demonstrating team incapability and slack control of the project.

the situation could get worse and worse (smartcard service manager)

In November 2005, the team therefore decided to abandon (‘disassociate’) the security subsystem completely and to avoid mentioning this fact to other stakeholders in future (Table 4). This was relatively easy to do as the security subsystem had come ‘for free’ with the system. It was not part of the original requirement and had not been promised to any department by the team. The security subsystem was one of the first implemented components and only the project team and the security department were involved. Knowledge of the failure, if it spread to other organizational members, might have damaged the reputation of the project and the team. Separating the failing component from the wider system might prevent further damage (Zimmerman & Zeitz, 2002), as a possible ‘domino effect’ was avoided. Other stakeholders, who had covertly withheld their dislike of the smartcard system, might be encouraged by the fact that the guards had successfully rejected the security subsystem, and start questioning the purpose and utility of other parts of the system, threatening overall legitimation. Despite the security subsystem being quietly abandoned, this did not appear to harm the progress of other parts of the project.

The second example concerned the fee-collection function, which would have enabled the finance department to directly debit the tuition fee from student smartcard accounts, significantly increasing finance department productivity. The finance department initially granted legitimation on a pragmatic basis but, one year later (April 2006), the system vendor admitted that because of software design constraints, the function was technically infeasible. When this news was revealed, the finance department staff were disappointed and their service quality dropped dramatically.

If we can’t have the fee-collection feature, the smartcard is just a canteen and library card. So what’s the point? (finance department chief accountant)

To repair damage and win back the finance department’s support, the network centre director ‘admitted misunderstanding’, in that the team had inappropriately proposed the function based on their limited knowledge of the vendor’s product. They did not evaluate its feasibility properly before including it in the approved project plan:

Only when we began to use the system did we find out the ‘fee-collection-through-smartcard-system’ thing was unachievable, it was more about symbolic meaning, rather than [a] practical solution.

To prevent the finance department from demanding other alternative solutions for fee-collection automation, the team argued that the smartcard system project and the automation of tuition-fee collection were totally separate issues and should never have been related. Furthermore, they ‘reconfigured’ the system by rewriting project documentation, redefining project scope and excluding the function from the initial project plan. By doing so, new grounds for granting a modest level of moral legitimation were formed, based on the team justifying that they had met the project objectives and that the smartcard system without the fee-collection function was still legitimate. The finance department staff also felt under pressure as their resistance had largely impacted the day-to-day operation of the system that was generally regarded as a ‘success’ by other organizational stakeholders (as described in the ‘Grant Legitimation’ section). As a result, they started facing reality and normal service quality resumed eventually.

## Discussion

## Summary and discussion of case study findings

In this case study, the project team spent much time in carrying out a wide range of legitimation activities. This is consistent with previous research in that seeking legitimation is important in developing IS that will be accepted by stakeholders (Brown, 1995; Keable et al, 1998; Hussain et al, 2004; Kohli & Kettinger, 2004; Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006).

To address our research question, we considered Suchman’s (1995) framework of three generic types of activities within the legitimation-seeking process: gaining, maintaining and repairing, and we used a qualitative approach to build up a detailed analysis of participant understanding of strategies, events and activities that took place within this process.

We refined Suchman’s (1995) basic description of gaining legitimation into four main phases based on LAM activities: (1) Construct target, (2) Identify LP and learn norms, (3) Compare target and close gap, and (4) Grant legitimation. We found examples of maintaining legitimation, where the team perceived challenges (Ferneley & Sobreperez, 2006) to system acceptance; for example the student criticism of delayed system installation. The team detected the challenge and took corresponding action. We also identified examples of repairing legitimation, where the team rescued the project from a crisis; for example when the finance department discovered that the system could not debit student fees. The team had to react promptly to prevent damage to legitimation from spreading to other stakeholders.

A number of legitimation-gaining strategies (Suchman, 1995) were also carried out by the project team, previously summarized in Table 2. Many of these strategies were consistent with their description in previous IS legitimation studies. In addition, some legitimation-gaining strategies rarely mentioned in the IS literature were identified in this case study, shown in Table 5. We also identified legitimation maintaining and repairing strategies (see Tables 3, 4) that have not appeared in prior IS legitimation research.

Using legitimation as a sensitizing device for understanding ISD projects highlights the view that legitimation is a fragile flower; a stakeholder may grant legitimation but this may be damaged or withdrawn completely. Some actions, such as the repairing action that rewrote system objectives to remove all traces of the tuition-fee-collection function, emphasize the coproductive nature of the relationship between users and technology discussed by social appropriation theorists (Mackay, 1995). This action is an example of nihilation, or negative legitimation (Berger & Luckmann, 1966), as it denies the reality of a phenomenon that does not fit the institutional order. Other actions modify social organizational constructions, such as organizational rules (e.g., making system use compulsory for canteens and shop owners) or modify project identity or status (e.g., promotion for the project director), that affect technology indirectly by altering its meaning. There are also actions that attempt to change user perceptions directly, often by pragmatic means. The characteristics of a technology, individual perceptions of that technology, and organizational context thus change as a project progresses.

The concept of legitimation presents a potentially valuable lens for research as it raises issues such as user involvement, participation, acceptance and resistance in ISD, which are typically investigated in an unrelated manner. For example, Bhattacherjee & Hikmet (2007) note ‘research on user resistance has been limited, fragmented and non-cumulative’. The multitude of problems and challenges may be lower-level symptoms of a higher-level failure in legitimation-seeking. Obtaining top management support (Dong et al, 2009) is a well-known concept in ISD that can be conceptualized as gaining legitimation from top management. An example occurs in the case study where the project team director was promoted to the ‘office of the presidents’, a symbolic display of such legitimation. Many interviewees gave evidence of their granting of legitimation by referring to the pragmatic benefits of the smartcard system. These can be understood in the light of the ‘perceived usefulness’ factor of the TAM (Davis, 1989) or the ‘user satisfaction’ factor of the DeLone and McLean model (DeLone & McLean, 2003).

A focus on legitimation is a process approach, which, grounded in day-to-day work practices, allows us insight into individual perceptions and activities that may constitute the factors of TAM-like models. The in-depth details of the legitimation process also provide well-needed examples of the rather abstract activity of constructing legitimation structures as part of the duality of structure described by Giddens (1984).

## Limitations of LAM

This case study has provided evidence that the integrated framework (Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006) is sufficiently general to be applied to organizations that ‘share similar empirical circumstances’ (Lee & Baskerville, 2003), allowing us to make sense of their legitimationseeking processes. However, we found that, within this integrated framework, the LAM (Flynn & Hussain, 2004) did not fully explain all aspects of the case study. We now describe these aspects and the LAM limitations.

Firstly, we found that legitimation could be gained at any phase of legitimation-gaining. This is in contrast to LAM, which suggests that legitimation is achieved only in its final Grant Legitimation activity. For example, legitimation was gained from departmental heads in the Construct Target phase, from the finance department in the Identify LP and Learn Norms phase, from the security director in the Compare Target and Close Gap phase, and from the library staff in the Grant Legitimation phase.

Secondly, LAM only considers legitimation-gaining, and a major finding of our research concerns the analysis of maintaining and repairing activities within the overall legitimation-seeking process. Legitimation-maintaining activities are preventive actions where the project team detect and respond to threats to system acceptance. For example, seeking support from students, canteen and shop staff who withheld their acceptance for the system (Consult Doubters from Table 3). Legitimation-repairing activities are reactive actions where the project team find that previous support from legitimation providers suddenly disappears. For example, abandoning the failing security subsystem (Disassociate from Table 4).

Thirdly, it is clear that the activities of gaining, maintaining and repairing legitimation may be carried out concurrently by legitimation seekers throughout the entire legitimation-seeking process. For example, the legitimation-gaining strategy Expanding system coverage (Table 2) was carried out concurrently with the legitimation-maintaining strategy Identifying stakeholders who held negative attitudes as new legitimation providers (Table 3). The project team simultaneously carried out the legitimation-gaining strategy Recruiting users to maintain the system and provide services (Table 2) and the legitimation-repairing strategy Abandoning the failing subsystem to prevent the whole project from being defamed (Table 4).

Fourthly, although LAM proposes that once legitimation has been gained, legitimation seekers should monitor legitimation to ensure continued stakeholder support and acceptance (Suchman, 1995), no details are given. Another missing aspect of LAM is the view that monitoring and evaluating legitimation is a continuous, concurrent process, linking the planned actions of legitimation-gaining and the responsive actions for legitimation maintaining and repairing. If legitimation is not satisfactory, then maintaining or repairing may be required. Significant incidents (e.g., a stakeholder querying the usefulness of the system, or actually withdrawing legitimation) may pause the legitimation-gaining activities at any time and trigger maintaining or repairing.

In our case study the project team did not have a conscious legitimation strategy (such as a plan of legitimation activities targeted at particular stakeholders), but instead engaged in the monitoring of stakeholder support to detect legitimation damage or withdrawal. Jensen & Aanestad (2007) note that stakeholders may hold both ‘hostile and hospitable’ attitudes simultaneously, as in Lewin’s (1935) theory of persistence and desistence, and that such attitudes may change over time. Attitudes vary widely and may be based on perceptions of, for example the technology, the project team or the organization.

For example, it was apparent that the project team were monitoring student complaints about delayed system installation, as the network centre director said: ‘We held meetings to discuss the matters y we have addressed student complaints’. The team then initiated the maintaining action Liaising with student activists to respond to demands (Table 3). Another example concerns the finance department, who, having realized the fee-collection function would not work, dramatically dropped their service quality. The centre director realized: ‘y they had to accept the fact but this resulted in their negative attitude toward smartcard system-related work and poor cooperation with us’. This withdrawal of legitimation triggered the repairing action Removing unachieved system functions from initial project objectives (Table 4). In both these examples, monitoring is apparent whereas evaluating is implicit, the result of which may be ascertained by the responsive type of action (maintaining or repairing) performed. Further examples are shown in Table 6.

Fifthly, by choosing between legitimation gaining, maintaining or repairing activities, it was evident that that the project team was evaluating the levels of legitimation that had been attained, by making judgments concerning stakeholder support on an almost continuous basis, and we have conceptualized legitima tion status (not mentioned in LAM) as actively sought, granted, weakened or damaged, or withdrawn, constituting an advance on previous studies. We considered that activities for gaining legitimation occurred mainly in the early phases of the project. Activities for maintaining legitimation occurred when already-granted legitimation was weakened or damaged, and we characterized this as occurring where: (1) legitimation seekers expressed the view that support from a legitimation provider had become weakened (e.g., the project leader’s reaction to encountering user resistance to the payment system); or (2) a legitimation provider expressed doubt or opposition concerning the system by, for example questioning aims, benefits or utility (e.g., student complaints about delayed system installation). Activities for repairing legitimation occurred when already-granted legitimation was withdrawn, and we characterized this as occurring where: (1) legitimation seekers expressed the view that support from a legitimation provider had disappeared, or they described how they encountered resistance to system use or even system damage (e.g., project team awareness of damaged security subsystem); or (2) a legitimation provider expressed the view that the system could not be used, or was not fit for purpose (e.g., finance department doubts concerning system usefulness if the fee-collection function was absent).

The evaluating of legitimation status may not be straightforward. While overtly positive responses, such as active participation and positive expressions about a system, can be easily perceived as signifying the granting of legitimation, detecting weak legitimation or failure is more challenging. This is because time may be needed before the effects of legitimation-seeking activities become visible, or because legitimation providers’ reluctance to grant legitimation may, for example for political reasons, rarely be displayed overtly (Brown, 1995).

A lesson from the case study is that the team did not make any attempt to monitor the security guards’ attitudes to the security subsystem. Their reasoning was that as the guards were not university employees they did not need to be consulted. As a good example of the ‘deaf effect (Keil & Robey, 2001), this fact, in addition to the unwillingness of the guards to express their concerns (‘mum’ effect), led to the collapse of the subsystem.

Another lesson is that legitimation seekers should not be complacent when legitimation is granted early on in a project. As Wagner & Piccoli (2007) note, users often have little incentive to be fully engaged in project activities before the system starts to have an impact on their work. This is not due to unwillingness to be involved, but because it requires significant cognitive effort to conceptualize the system and associated changes. For example, when the smartcard system was proposed, the finance department granted legitimation based on promises made by the project team, who in turn relied on promises from the system vendor. When the finance department eventually realized system limitations (the undelivered tuition-fee-collection function), a crisis occurred as they withdrew their legitimation, and the project team had to redefine project scope by rewriting system documentation. The need to repair legitimation by this rather drastic means might have been avoided if the project team had worked more closely with the system vendor early on in the project, and had based their promises to users on a fuller understanding of the functional capabilities of the system. They would have learnt of the system limitation much earlier and could have developed an alternative solution. They may have been fortunate in the tolerance and the modest level of legitimation given by the finance department.

## Conclusions

Seeking legitimation should be considered a key resource that needs to be strategically managed and controlled (Ashforth & Gibbs, 1990). We were fortunate in gaining good access to management level staff, as their perceptions were vital to our understanding of organizational legitimation.

From the case study, our inference is that a key aspect of a trouble-free legitimation process is that maintenance and repair of legitimation is required over a sustained period of time. For the smartcard system project, little time and effort had been put into the tasks of defining and communicating system features and identifying legitimation providers, planting hidden perils for granting of legitimation in later stages. Legitimation maintaining and repairing may be more likely to occur in a hurried project where proper preparation is lacking.

IS and project team managers need to become aware of the importance of identifying appropriate legitimation providers, choosing suitable strategies and evaluating the status throughout the legitimation process. We suggest a set of legitimation strategies (Suchman, 1995) that managers may refer to while seeking legitimation for systems. Practitioners should always select such strategies by considering the context where they are to be used.

During the life of an IS project, managers should be on the alert to detect and resolve issues that could undermine support, and thus should not treat legitimation that has been granted as a fait accompli. One way to continuously monitor and evaluate legitimation status is to talk to stakeholders through regular information briefing meetings or personal contacts. While stakeholder discourse and communication can provide some insights into the status of legitimation, another indicator can be the extent of resource flow in an organization. In the view of Stone & Brush (1996), the greater the degrees of certainty with which resources are supplied to a project (finance, personnel, technology, contributions from stakeholders), the more legitimation the project possesses. Interruptions in project resource flow therefore could suggest legitimation decline.

There are several limitations to this research. Firstly, the LAM model understands legitimation-seeking as a ‘centrist’ activity originating in one organizational space (legitimation seekers – typically an IT project team) and being ‘diffused’ throughout the organization from that central space. However, as user departments increasingly question the hegemony of IT departments and their associated project teams, IS are increasingly being introduced from a variety of ‘polycentric’ organizational spaces, and future legitimation research should seek organizations where such non-centrist legitimation-seeking is ongoing.

Secondly, there are methodological limitations. LAM has been applied to seven case studies until now, but in all those studies data collection was not concurrent with the actual project, as access was gained after the project had finished. A disadvantage related to retrospective accounts can be errors in interviewee recall of historical events (Glick et al, 1990). Another shortcoming of using retrospective accounts is that interviewees may neglect important project events, or add their own judgment or interpretation into the narratives, in an attempt at postrationalization. In addition, it is well-known that single case study research does not allow us to take advantage of the strengths of multiple-case research (Herriott & Firestone, 1983) with regard to developing new knowledge about legitimation-maintaining and legitimationrepairing activities in IS projects. Biases may take places in single case research where cross-case analysis is infeasible (Eisenhardt, 1989).

This case study has demonstrated the importance to project teams of obtaining legitimation for IS projects, supporting previous research in this area (Brown, 1998; Keable et al, 1998; Kohli & Kettinger, 2004; Flynn & Puarungroj, 2006). We have provided in-depth insights into the legitimation process in the smartcard system project, identified sets of strategies for gaining, maintaining and repairing legitimation carried out by the project team, and proposed improvements to LAM, which we perceive as a dynamic process model.
