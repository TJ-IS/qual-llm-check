## ABSTRACT:

As digital natives, junior employees are believed to be highly tech-savvy and directly use enterprise system in daily work. However, prior literature has reported a considerable number of indirect system use by junior employees. This study aims to understand this seemly paradoxical phenomenon. We theorize indirect system use as a technology interaction behavior with delegation. Results of a survey among junior physicians show that quantitative overload, positional legitimacy power, and social legitimacy power are significant antecedents of their indirect system use. Moreover, social legitimacy power has a stronger influence than positional legitimacy power.

KEYWORDS: digital natives, indirect system use, technology interaction, organizational delegation, overload, organizational power.

## 1. Introduction

The field of Information Systems (IS) increasingly studies the behavior of digital natives in the workplace [1,2]. Unlike digital immigrants, who are usually forced to work with information technology (IT) in their adult lives, digital natives grow up in a digital world, where the use of IT is pervasive in every aspect of their lives [3,4]. The digital environment surrounding them influences the way they think and work [5]. Prior literature suggests that digital natives tend to use IT more frequently and deeply for both personal and professional purposes compared with digital immigrants [2]. In line with this thought, junior employees in an organization should naturally highly accept and directly use the IS.

#

However, this is not always the case. Prior literature reveals that junior employees from different job types also engage in the indirect use of IS (i.e., a designated user interacts with a system through one or more intermediaries) [6–11]. For example, in law firms, lawyers at all levels of seniority often ask paralegals and assistants to help them use the document management system [11]. Similarly, in the healthcare setting, both senior and junior physicians<sup>1</sup> are found to ask intermediary users to enter or retrieve information in the system for them [7,10]. Interestingly, a large-scale survey conducted by Medscape shows that although the majority of physicians under 30 are comfortable with electronic health record systems, nearly half of them still feel that the system slows down their workflow [12].

Given the seemingly contradiction between junior employees’ IT efficacy and system usage behavior, it is interesting to explore factors affecting their indirect system use behavior. As the main users of an IS, junior employees’ system use behavior can have distinct implications for an organization, depending on the circumstances. Take the hospital setting for instance, indirect system use could relieve designated physicians from busy work schedule so that they can have more time to attend patients [8,13]. However, such behavior may also induce the delay in recording the information in the system or cause information transcription errors [14,15]. Depending on their objectives and support structure, organizations may want to foster or prohibit indirect system use. Regardless of their option, understanding how and why junior physicians engage in indirect system use can help managers offer suitable tactics to manage such behavior.

Although previous studies have observed indirect system use behavior in different contexts, most of them are qualitative in nature [e.g. ,6–9], only with the exception of Tong et al. [10], who theoretically differentiated direct and indirect system use and empirically examined the influence of social power on both behaviors. There has been limited understanding of the unique nature of indirect system use, and its antecedents. Extant literature on IS use primarily focuses on direct system use and considers users’ evaluation of system-related characteristics (e.g., perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use) as the most important factors in explaining individuals’ use behaviors [16–18]. Considering that indirect system use is conceptually different from and not a full substitute of direct system use [7,10], traditional systemcentric perception constructs may not be sufficient to trigger indirect system use.

To address these gaps, we employ the technology interaction [16,19] and organizational delegation perspectives [20–22] to theoretically study junior employees’ indirect system use in the healthcare context. We argue that indirect system use goes beyond conventional technology interaction because of two unique characteristics that exhibit delegation. We then propose two prominent types of antecedents of junior physicians’ indirect system use of health IT—work overload and legitimacy power—to demonstrate the unique characteristics of indirect system use.

Survey data were collected from a large hospital to test the proposed research model. Results showed that junior physicians’ quantitative overload, positional legitimacy power, and social legitimacy power enhance their indirect system use. However, their qualitative overload does not significantly motivate indirect system use. In addition, social legitimacy power has a stronger influence over positional legitimacy power on their indirect system use. This study contributes an essential perspective to the literature on IS use by examining indirect system use and its antecedents in depth. The focus on junior physicians also addresses the need to further study the impact of digital natives in the workplace [2,3].

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

## 2.1 Indirect system use

Indirect system use is a complex multifaceted behavior that one performs in a way not directly operating the system but using the system information and/or completing system tasks through intermediaries who actually interact with the system [10]. It naturally involves extensive interactions between the designated user and system via intermediaries, and such interpersonal interactions have been widely identified. In examining how managers use computer-based information (CBI), Kraemer et al. [8] described a type of user who utilizes printed CBI via intermediaries, called “CBI consumer (also referred to as “indirect user”)”. They argued that, the CBI consumer took advantage of technological benefits through “delegation mode”. Similarly, Bisaso et al. [6], Larsen [9], and Saunder and Chiasson [11] also noted the pervasive presence of asking colleagues to perform IT-related tasks on one’s behalf across different contexts. Consistently, they called such phenomenon “delegated use”. Research in the healthcare context studied indirect system use as well. Kane and Alavi [7] proposed that healthcare professionals’ indirect system use was an approach augmenting IS use through the use of interpersonal interactions. Synthesizing prior studies, Tong et al. [10] considered delegated use and indirect system use interchangeable. They also argued that the interaction between designated users and system was realized by delegation. In line with these theoretical understandings, we believe indirect system use naturally captures the two underlying characteristics: (1) Indirect system use is a special type of technology interaction behavior in which the designated user employs the system via intermediaries. (2) The designated user indirectly interacts with the system by delegating system-related tasks to intermediaries<sup>2</sup>. On the basis of the understanding, we delegation.

## 2.1.1 Technology interaction view of indirect system use

Technology interaction in the work setting refers to all types of user–system interactions undertaken to accomplish organizational tasks [19]. Indirect system use is basically the interaction between the designated user and the technology through the actual operation of intermediaries [e.g., 6,8,10,11,22]. In early studies, indirect system use was referred to as the behavior through which a designated user, such as a manager, obtained system outcomes through the secretary for decision making [e.g., 8,22]. Recent research revealed that the tasks performed in an indirect system use setting have a broader spectrum. Qualitative evidences show that the designated users would require subordinates or interns to enter, retrieve, and analyze information for their planning, monitoring, and management [6,11]. In other words, indirect system use reflects the extent of interaction between designated users and the system via intermediary users across multiple system-related tasks [10]. Therefore, we believe technology interaction is deemed acceptable by the designated users engaging in indirect system use, although they may not directly operate with the system.

Therefore, this type of system use is commonly deemed to employ a delegation mode [8–11].

## 2.1.2 Organizational delegation view of indirect system use

Delegation is a behavior in which a person with high authority (i.e., the principal) assigns tasks to a subordinate (i.e., the agent) to carry them out [20,26]. This behavior widely exists in contemporary organizations across various cultures, industries, and positions [20,27,28]. Although delegation may be associated with agency problems, it can improve the speed and quality of organizational activities and reduce the workload of the principal if it is used appropriately [21]. Organizational delegation is an essential element of organizational effectiveness and efficiency [22].

Given that indirect system use requires intermediaries to operate the system, it is inevitably associated with delegation [10]. As shown in Table 1, during indirect system use, the junior physician takes advantage of the system benefits by delegating the execution of IT work (e.g., enter/retrieve patient information, or make orders) to the interns or other available intermediaries. The junior physicians, as the designated users of the delegated system-related tasks, serve as the principal, and the intermediaries between the designated user and the system serve as the agents [26]. Through interpersonal delegation, the designated users can simultaneously accomplish system-related tasks and non-system-related professional work [7]. Thus, indirect system use is a specific type of technology interaction involving task delegation. However, even though the designated user, i.e., the junior physician, specifies the systemdelegated system-related tasks [10].

Literature on organizational delegation provides a theoretical perspective to study the indirect system use of junior physicians [21]. On the one hand, delegating system-related tasks to others requires few personal efforts compared with employing the system by oneself, which involves the designated user’s own engagement (i.e., learning and operating) with the system [29–31]. Then, the junior physicians can focus on the professional work, such as investigation, monitoring, and intervention, and simultaneously exhibit a high level of interaction with the system through the intermediaries [10]. On the other hand, when choosing to delegate tasks to others, the designated users need to employ the intermediaries available [21,22]. In other words, the ability of the designated users to influence others to perform the system-related tasks on their behalf is crucial.

Synthesizing the technology interaction view and organizational delegation view, we theorize indirect system use as a special type of technology interaction via delegation. The technology interaction manifested in indirect system use is different from the direct interaction examined in earlier literature on IS acceptance [e.g. ,16,32]. Therefore, in the current study, we aim to examine the antecedents of junior physicians’ indirect system use and gain further insight by incorporating the two unique characteristics of indirect system use associated with delegation: (1) Indirect system use costs junior physicians less personal efforts than direct system use. (2) To execute indirect system use, junior physicians must be able to find the intermediaries to comply with their orders.

Considering that junior physicians are likely to possess high IT literacy, these two characteristics are linked with the literature on organizational overload [e.g. ,33–35] to explain the demand to reduce personal effort. In addition, we adopt the organizational power perspective [e.g. ,36,37] to explain the ability of individuals to influence others. In the following section, we provide an overview of these two theoretical perspectives and explain their relationship with organizational delegation in the form of indirect system use.

## 2.2 Antecedents of indirect system use

## 2.2.1 Work overload and indirect system use

Work overload refers to an individual’s perception that he/she cannot execute certain tasks owing to the lack of critical resources (e.g., time or certain skill). It is one of the major organizational stresses that affect employees’ behaviors, including system-related activities [33,38]. The influences of work overload (e.g., insufficient time or IT capability to complete work) have been consistently reported and examined in IT-enabled work environments. For instance, Moore [39] and Armstrong et al. [40] discovered that work overload is one of the strongest sources of technology workers’ turnover. Ahuja and Thatcher [33] empirically verified the impeding effect of work overload on the exploration of employees in technology. Generally, these findings indicate that work overload adds cost and requires high effort for IT-enabled work, especially when employees are confronted with plenty and diverse tasks and duties in different aspects of everyday work.

To cope with undesirable issues associated with work overload, theories of organizational delegation underscore the role of delegation, that is, having others perform certain tasks on one’s behalf [26]. Research on organizational delegation suggests that delegating work to others can free oneself from heavy work, enable one to concentrate on the core work, and increase individuals’ work outcomes [20,22,29,30]. For example, Leana [29,30] and Yukl [21] observed that some employees opt to delegate tasks to subordinates when faced with heavy workload. Asking others to work on their behalf indeed reduces overload and improves the speed and quality of work. Thus, work overload may have strong predicting power on junior physicians’ delegation in the form of indirect system use.

In general, work overload is reflected in two dimensions—quantitative and qualitative [34]. According to Ahuja and Thatcher [33], quantitative overload refers to an employee’s perception that he/she cannot accomplish system-related work owing to limitations imposed by the work environment (such as time), while qualitative overload occurs when the perceived requirement of the assigned systemrelated work exceeds an employee’s capability or skill level. As delegating system work to others costs less personal efforts and reduce work stress, indirect system use seems a possible way to deal with quantitative and qualitative overload. However, given that digital natives are creators and energetic participants in the new digital culture and tend to use IT more frequently and deeply in their work, the influences of quantitative and qualitative overload on junior physicians’ indirect system use may different.

When perceived with a high extent of quantitative overload, junior physicians are more likely to delegate system-related tasks to others. Generally, physicians view their work as centered on care delivery, including investigation, monitoring, intervention, and re-intervention, especially for junior physicians who are still struggling for a promotion [41]. For example, in a case study conducted by Jensen and Aanestad [42], it was discovered that physicians usually regard data enter as administrative work and did not associate system utilization as core in delivering care. As a result, the quantitatively overloaded junior physicians certainly seek to reserve adequate time for patient diagnosis and management. Under such condition, delegating system work to others is an applicable way (sometimes even an only option) to relieve the tensional workload [21,31]. For example, if a junior physician hurry to an operation without enough time to perform the preoperative prescriptions in the system, he/she has no choice but to ask others to enter his/her oral orders. Therefore, when junior physicians experience quantitative overload, they tend to engage in indirect system use.

H1: Quantitative overload increases a junior physician’s frequency of engaging in indirect system use.

On the contrary, qualitative overload does not serve as a strong motivator of junior physicians’ indirect system use. Of course, indirect system use is a possible solution for individuals to deal with qualitative overload. However, as digital natives who are habituated to the technology in every aspect in their life, junior physicians may actively engage in self-exploration to solve the technological issue [1,2]. In addition, young people usually are innovative, fearless to danger and difficulty, and emulative. They may feel humiliating to receiving technological aids in things that they are believed to be good at. Thus, when faced with qualitative issue, the junior physicians may not be hesitated to explore the technology rather than seeking help from others.

H2: Qualitative overload has no impact on a junior physician’s frequency of engaging in indirect system use.

## 2.2.2 Organizational power and indirect system use

Power is the capacity to exert influence [37]. In an organization, some people clearly have more power than others [36]. Holding the power distance, individuals who feel powerful are likely to force powerless individuals to perform certain tasks on their behalf. In other words, with the capacity to influence others, individuals can easily find the intermediaries to delegate system-related work. Thus, organizational power could be important antecedents of junior physicians’ indirect system use.

The influence ability of power is always attributed to the position discrepancy by many prior studies [e.g. ,43,44]. For example, assuming that a subordinate accepts the right of his/her supervisor to hold the position, the supervisor can prescribe behavior for the subordinate by virtue of this occupancy; the subordinate, in turn, feels obligated to accept these orders [45]. Other than positional power, in which the intermediaries comply because of a sense of obligation, individuals can also influence others through

#

other legitimate sources, such as the social responsibility norm [46]. Individuals have valid reasons to prescribe behavior when facing difficulties (e.g., lack of time, efforts, or certain skill) in work. For example, through an experiment, Schopler and Matthews [47] observed that a requester could get significantly more responses when his/her requirement for help was recognized as needed. Therefore, both position discrepancy and social responsibility norm are potential legitimate sources accepted by the organizational members, by virtue of which the designated user can assert his power [45]. In the current study, these two kinds of organizational power are referred to as positional legitimacy power and social legitimacy power, following Tong et al. [10]. Positional legitimacy power is conceived as a supervisor’s right to prescribe behavior for others (e.g., subordinate or intern) to comply, while social legitimacy power is one’s ability to oblige another person to assist him/her based on social responsibility norm [10,46].

In the hospital setting, both positional legitimacy power and social legitimacy power widely exist. In general, positional legitimacy power is derived from organizational structure of the hospital. For example, the physicians-in-charge have higher positional legitimacy power than other physicians [48]. Although the junior physicians are usually obliged to work with a lower status under the direction of the seniors, they still have the positional legitimacy power raised from being the persons who are legally authorized to coordinate or deal with the training of interns or induced by a sense of oughtness of their subordinates (if any) [49]. Social legitimacy power is raised with the intermediate’s sense to obey the junior physicians experiencing difficulties in performing healthcare activities [49]. The difficulties could originate from their computer proficiency, inertia to learn the system, or heavy workload [10]. Either positional or social legitimacy power enable junior physician easier to find and influence intermediaries to help them with system-related work. However, considering the characteristics of digital natives, the impacts on junior physicians’ indirect system use may vary across positional and social legitimacy power.

It is believed that positional legitimacy power cannot increase junior physicians’ indirect system use. Since junior physicians are habituated to the IT-enabled work environment, merely owning the power to influence others may be not able to trigger their proactively engagement in indirect system use. In addition, although junior physicians’ positional legitimacy power enables them to find the intermediaries who can use the system for them, it is less likely for them to actively access the positional power to influence others. For junior employees, who may just enter the workplace, it is annoying if they try to may have no impact on their indirect system use.

H3: Positional legitimacy power has no impact on a junior physician’s frequency of engaging in direct system use.

On the contrary, social legitimacy power increases junior physicians’ indirect system use. One the one hand, junior physicians’ social legitimacy power implies that they have insuperable difficulties in performing system work and really need help [46]. Indirect system use is an effective way to deal with such problem. On the other hand, social legitimacy power involves the value or standard to ensure the junior physicians influence others to perform system-related work for them. In other words, it is acknowledged norm for the intermediaries to help the junior physicians who have social legitimacy power [48]. Therefore, when junior physicians have a high degree of social legitimacy power, their indirect system use will increase.

H4: Social legitimacy power increases a junior physician’s frequency of engaging in direct system use.

## 3. Research methodology

To test the proposed theoretical model and hypotheses, a field study spanning approximately one year was conducted at a top-tier hospital in Mainland China. A survey with supplementary qualitative and observational data was employed to establish a comprehensive understanding of junior physicians’ indirect system use and its antecedents. The survey respondents were junior physicians (i.e., attending and resident physicians) using health IT on a daily basis as part of their work in the inpatient department. The inpatient department was chosen as the study setting for two reasons. First, a majority of junior physicians are resident physicians, who only work at the inpatient department. Second, at the outpatient department, physicians provide patient care service separately, thus, lack available intermediaries to help them measurements.

## 3.1 Research site

The focal hospital is a large-scale general hospital with over 500 physicians, approximately 50% of whom are attending and resident physicians. Over 50,000 inpatients were admitted to the hospital during the year we conducted this study. Health IT, such as electronic medical records (EMR) and computerized physician order entry (CPOE)<sup>3</sup>, has been installed by the hospital to facilitate physicians’ daily transaction in the inpatient department. The EMR and CPOE are equipped with features and functions that enable physicians to enter and/or retrieve patient records (e.g., diagnosis, clinical progress, discharge summary), view laboratory/imaging results, prescribe and/retrieve orders, and check billing histories, specifically to provide patient care service. We used a multiple-step method to collect data. ## 3.3 Measurements

To ensure reliability and validity, measures for each variable were either adopted from previously validated questions or self-developed based on contextual and literature study.

The dependent variable, i.e., indirect system use, was self-developed as a formative construct that incorporates major health IT-enabled tasks physicians need to perform in the focal hospital. We identified eight tasks for indirect system use based on the observations on care delivery and interviews with IT staff and physicians. The eight tasks are entering diagnosis and discharge summary, entering clinical progress, retrieving laboratory/imaging result, retrieving patient information (e.g., medical history, diagnosis), entering orders for prescriptions, entering orders for tests, retrieving billing history, and retrieving drugusing records. Participants indicated their extent of indirect system use through the following interpretation: 1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = occasionally, 4 = sometimes, 5 = frequently, 6 = usually, and 7 =always.

All the independent variables were measured as reflective constructs. Items of PU and PEOU were adopted from [17]. The overload variables were measured using the items adapted from [33]. To capture the legitimacy power of junior physicians, we used the items from [46] and [10]. In addition, age, gender, education, medical complex, number of teammates, routine use [52], facilitating conditions [32], and organizational commitment [53] were included to control for the effects of individual differences. All the reflective items were measured using a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

In designing these measures, we consulted several senior researchers to identify and rectify potential problems in the framing and phrasing of questions. Minor modifications were made on certain items based on their suggestions. Considering that the original version was developed in English, while all the respondents are Chinese, the entire questionnaire was translated into Chinese and then back-translated for translation accuracy by eight bilingual master students and a senior doctoral student. An IS professor then double verified and refined the questionnaire. We also conducted a pilot test and modified the questionnaire accordingly before the official survey. Therefore, the measurements have good content validity.

## 5. Discussion

#

By drawing on organizational delegation and work overload literature, as well as organizational power theory, the current study attempts to identify the factors that induce a junior physician’s indirect system use. Data analysis results show that the quantitative overload of junior physicians is a major reason for their indirect system use. The legitimacy power that influences subordinates and interns to comply is likewise found to be a facilitating source for junior physicians’ indirect system use, and social legitimacy power has a stronger impact than positional legitimacy power. The post hoc analysis allows us to further understand the influence of quantitative overload on indirect system use in terms of task type. Our results suggest that inputting-oriented tasks are more susceptible to quantitative overload compared with retrieving-oriented tasks.

Consistent with H1, a junior physician with a high level of quantitative overload tends to engage in indirect system use. This finding accords with prior organizational delegation literature, which contends that individuals employ delegation to deal with overload [21,22]. In general, junior physicians tend to regard their core work as delivering patient care (e.g., attending patients). Thus, they are less likely to sacrifice their time with patients to complete system-related work when faced quantitative overload. Under such condition, delegating system-related task to others could help them deal with heavy workload. When perceived with quantitative overload, junior physicians tend to exhibit a high extent of indirect system use.

Also as we expected in H2, the impact of qualitative overload on indirect system use is insignificant. Most junior physicians are digital natives. They grew up in an IT-enabled environment, and used to experiment with the innovation by oneself [2,60]. Thus, when junior physicians encounter technology issues in interacting with the system, they are more likely to figure it out by themselves rather than seek help from others. In other words, junior physicians are less likely to engage in indirect system use when facing qualitative overload.

Contrary to H3 and consistent with H4, both positional and social legitimacy power enhances the possibility of a junior physician’s indirect system use. These findings is consistent with prior organization

#

power literature [46,49]. Specifically, when a junior physician possesses a high level of legitimacy power over other people, he/she can easily prescribe behaviors for others. These findings are also consistent with prior observation that legitimacy power can facilitate physicians’ engagement in indirect system use [10]. Although we did not find the insignificant impact of positional legitimacy power on indirect system use legitimacy power in inducing indirect system use by junior physicians. This demonstrates that junior physicians are less likely to access positional legitimacy power to perform indirect system use compared with social legitimacy power.

User perceptions (i.e., PU and PEOU) have long been recognized as important predictors of users’ technology interaction [16]. However, the present study finds that neither PU nor PEOU significantly influences indirect system use. This finding is consistent with our standpoint that interactions within indirect system use are more complex than direct system use [8]. Interestingly, the current study also finds the significant effects of facilitating condition and number of teammates on indirect system use. This confirms our argument that facilitating resources (e.g., available interns) for organizational delegation are essential for indirect system use. This study depicts the antecedents of junior physicians’ indirect system use. Specifically, as digital natives, junior physicians’ quantitative overload increase indirect system use, while their qualitative overload does not. In addition, although both social and positional legitimacy power increase indirect system use, social legitimacy power has a stronger impact than positional legitimacy power. The differences in the effects between quantitative/qualitative overload and between positional/social legitimacy power are attributed to the characteristics of digital natives.

## 5.1 Theoretical implications

This study contributes to extant literature on IS in several ways. First, this study contributes to the literature on IS use by being one of the early studies unveiling the unique nature of indirect system use and its antecedents. Despite the prevalence of indirect system use across different contexts, most extant literature on indirect system use [e.g. ,6,8,9,11] is qualitative study and typically does not probe deeper into the nature of indirect system use, as well as the antecedents of such behavior. The current study theoretically identifies the unique characteristics of indirect system use by integrating the technology interaction view and organizational delegation view. Specifically, indirect system use is a combination of technology interaction and delegation. Based on the theorizing of such behavior, we further empirically examined the antecedents from overload and organization power perspectives. The current study enriches IS use literature by characterizing indirect system use behavior and understanding its antecedents.

Second, the current study goes beyond conventional relationships by highlighting the importance of external pressure from work environment and individuals’ power to influence others to comply in explaining system use behavior. Previous literature on IS use primarily focused on direct system use and deemed user perception toward the technology as the important determinant of technology interaction [e.g. ,16,32]. Indirect system use is a special type of technology interaction behavior in which the designated user employs a system via delegating system-related tasks to intermediaries. Although some studies such as Tong et al. [10] have examined the impact of social power on indirect system use, our understanding about why individuals engage in indirect system use is still scarce. This study integrates overload and organizational power literature to identify the antecedents of indirect system use. The findings imply that quantitative overload and legitimacy power significantly increase junior physicians’ indirect system use. Our findings provide valuable knowledge in explaining IS use behaviors in organization.

Third, this study extends the literature on health IT implementation by exploring a frequently observed but less studied phenomenon associated with health IT implementation. Given the fact that the impact of health IT implementation is based on the actual use of individuals, previous studies have called for additional research to examine healthcare professionals’ post-adoptive behaviors [51,62]. A few studies have depicted the prevalence and importance of indirect system use in the healthcare context [7,10,13]. However, extant literature on health IT implementation still focuses on direct system use [63– 65]. This study is one of the earliest to demonstrate the indirect use of health IT and its characteristics. By

#

drawing on three established theoretical perspectives (i.e. organizational delegation, overload, and legitimacy power), this study theoretically and empirically examines the important factors influencing junior physicians’ indirect system use. The results show that junior physicians tend to ask other to help them with system-related tasks when they perceive a high extent of quantitative overload, positional legitimacy power, or social legitimacy power.

Finally, this study responds to the calls for more research on digital natives [2]. Digital natives are believed to be comfortable with frequent and in-depth engagement in IT in their work [2,5]. However, the present study reveals that digital natives like junior physicians, still have a high possibility of engaging in indirect interaction with the system because of their work environment or the legitimacy power they possess. Although the conclusion is derived in healthcare context, it also can be generalized to other contexts having similar work nature, such as law firm. Thus, this study contributes to a nuanced understanding of the nature of digital natives as well as recommends future studies on the technology interaction of digital natives to consider the different characteristics of the user and the environment they are situated in.

## 5.2 Practical implications

This study also has important practical implications. First, this study implies that indirect system use widely exists among digital natives like junior physicians. Our findings indicated that junior physicians often employ indirect system use as an approach to deal with quantitative overload. Besides, physicians with positional and social legitimacy power over others can also easily delegate system-related work to others. Considering the prevalence and important role of indirect system use, a strict rule to mandate the physicians to directly use a system may not be practical and can even be harmful in some situations. By contrast, hospital managers should recognize the behavior and take such behavior into consideration when developing health IT. The design of the health IT should include functions that will enable junior physicians’ indirect system use, for instance, also to include the delegation feature.

#

Second, given that junior employees’ system use behavior has distinct implications for an organization in different circumstances, our findings also provide hospital managers specific tactics to manage junior physicians’ indirect system use behavior. If hospital managers consider indirect system use as a deviance and want to reduce such behavior, they may exert sufficient efforts to alleviate junior physicians’ quantitative overload, for instance, to employ more physicians to share the workload. If hospital managers recognize indirect system use as a useful approach for relieving physicians’ work pressure and want to promote indirect system use, they may enact rules to force organizational members including interns recognize the positional structure, thus help junior physicians establish a strong positional legitimacy power. Given the stronger impact of social legitimacy power than positional legitimacy power, to promote a good atmosphere of mutual help in hospital will be a good choice for hospital managers, if the assistants or interns are not available

## 5.3 Limitations and directions for future research

Despite the numerous contributions, the current study still suffers from some limitations. First, we examined the indirect system use of junior physicians in the healthcare context. Thus, our findings were inevitably influenced by the unique characteristics of the context. For example, physicians are more likely to experience overload than those who work in other industries. Given the prevalence of indirect system use across different cultures, industries, and positions, future research is encouraged to examine indirect system use across different contexts (e.g., manufacturing, service, or public sectors).

Second, this study employed literature on organization delegation, and overload, and legitimacy power as the theoretical lens to examine the antecedents of indirect system use. Other factors influencing technology interaction and/or delegation, for example, organizational culture [66], leader–member exchange [67], or the characteristics of designated users, may also affect indirect system use. Future research could further test these factors based on other theoretical lens.

Third, we examined the factors that influence indirect system use from the designated users perspective. Indirect system use represents the designated user’s usage executed through the

#

intermediaries. Thus, indirect system use naturally includes three important elements, i.e., designated user, system, and intermediaries [10]. However, at present, we only surveyed the designated users. We believe that the reverse significant relationship of H6 was caused by the fact that most intermediaries for junior physicians are interns, who are not official staffs of the focal hospital. Thus, the influence of positional legitimacy power on them is not so strong. However, the present study does not verify the arguments. Future research could test this conjecture and explore the antecedents of indirect system use from multiple perspectives, including the designated user–intermediary dyad.

Four, in this study, qualitative overload does not show a significant influence on junior physicians’ indirect system use. We believe it is related to the characteristics of digital natives. In addition, there exists a potential dependency between qualitative and quantitative overload. Thus, future research may further consider the influences of quantitative and qualitative overload and their relationship in other contexts.
