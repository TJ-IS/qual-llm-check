# Why end-users move to the cloud: a migration-theoretic analysis

Anol Bhattacherjee<sup>1,2</sup> and Sang Cheol Park<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Information Systems and Decision Sciences, University of South Florida, Tampa, U.S.A; <sup>2</sup>Global Service Management, Sogang University, Seoul, Korea; <sup>3</sup>Department of Tourism/Distribution Management, School of Business, Hyupsung University, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea

## Abstract

This study presents and empirically validates a model of end-user migration from client-hosted computing to cloud computing. Synthesizing key findings from IT adoption and post-adoption research, switching research, and cloud computing studies, it builds an integrative framework of cloud migration using migration theory as a theoretical lens, and postulates interdependencies among these predictors. A longitudinal survey of Google Apps adoption among student subjects in South Korea validates our proposed model. This study contributes to our nascent body of knowledge on IT migration by drawing attention to this emerging phenomenon, demonstrating how migration research is different from IT adoption research, identifying salient factors that enable or hinder cloud migration, elaborating interdependencies between these different predictors, and bringing in migration theory as a referent theory to the information systems literature. ## Introduction

Over the last decade, cloud computing has gained prominence and popularity as an innovative means of provisioning, utilizing, and managing information technology (IT) resources. Cloud computing is a new paradigm of computing where users employ third-party, Internet-hosted software applications, data storage, and computing services for their computing needs rather than applications on their local computers. Users acquire and release computing resources and services as needed, and are billed for their actual usage of accessed services. Cloud computing represents a synergistic intersection of Internet technologies and personal or business computing, and is driving changes in how computing solutions are designed, delivered, sourced, and managed. This network-centric model of computing, where all data, applications, and services are hosted on the network is a significant departure from the traditional client-centric model of personal computing, where data and software resources are hosted on a local computer, or the client-server model of organizational computing, where resources are hosted on organizational servers.

Cloud computing has many benefits for both organizations and individual end-users. First, it provides users with universal (anytime/ anywhere) access to cloud-hosted resources and processing power (Rayport & Heyward, 2009). Second, it generates significant cost efficiencies for users from economies of scale as well as from the inherent cost advantages of Internet-based computing. Third, the cloud based architecture shields users from the risk and responsibility of owning and operating their own IT infrastructure or software. Fourth, it protects users from demand elasticity or fluctuations in computing needs, and allows for scalability. Despite these benefits, many users are reluctant to migrate to the cloud because of concerns about the availability and reliability of service, data security, privacy, service incompatibility, errors in hosted software, dependence on the service provider, and legal liability for data on the cloud (ENISA, 2009; Armbrust et al, 2010).

In light of the above benefits and concerns, the goal of this paper is to understand why IT end- users may or may not want to move from the traditional client-centric model of computing to cloud computing. Using a positivistic tradition of inquiry, we extend migration theory (Lee, 1966) from the human geography literature to postulate a theoretical model of cloud computing migration, and empirically test research hypotheses derived from this model using a longitudinal survey of student subjects’ migration to Google Docs.

The rest of the article proceeds as follows. The next section reviews the prior literature on cloud computing and switching. The third section describes migration theory and postulates hypotheses for empirical testing. The fourth section describes our research methods, including instrumentation and data collection techniques. The fifth section presents our data analysis and results. The final section discusses the study’s limitations and implications for future research and practice.

## Prior research

## Cloud computing research

Cloud computing, research is still in its early days. Much of the current literature focuses on the benefits and risks of cloud computing, organizational case studies of cloud adoption, cloud computing architectures, or research agendas for cloud computing (e.g., ENISA, 2009; Morton & Alford, 2009; Armbrust et al, 2010; Marston et al, 2011).

Empirical studies on cloud adoption have relied heavily on IT adoption theories such as the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and the technology acceptance model (TAM) or their extensions. For example, Taylor & Hunsinger (2011) observed that TPB constructs – attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioral control – influenced endusers’ intention of cloud adoption, in addition to their affect toward the cloud. Examining the drivers and inhibitors of Software as a Service (SaaS) adoption, Benlian et al (2009) found that social influence, prior attitude, adoption uncertainty, and strategic value influenced SaaS adoption. Wu (2011) observed that TAM extended with constructs such as marketing effort, security, and trust, successfully predicted cloud adoption. Extending TAM to study acceptance of a cloud computing platform among community college students, Behrend et al (2011) found that contextual factors such as students’ ability to travel to campus influenced the usefulness perceptions, while ease of use was determined by their first-hand experiences with the cloud and instructor support.

The above research views cloud adoption as an independent decision, while in reality, cloud adoption often reflect a process of migration from client-hosted systems to cloud-hosted systems for their data, application, and storage needs. Hence, switching research, described in the next section, may be pertinent to understanding cloud adoption. Note that in the above review, we excluded research that are unrelated to cloud adoption, such as the economics of cloud computing or the design of cloud contracts.

## Switching research

Viewing cloud adoption as an instance of user switching from client-hosted to cloud-hosted systems, we also examine switching research in information systems (IS) and marketing literatures. Table 1 lists switching predictors examined in these studies, with the valence of each effect (positive or negative) on switching intention or behavior shown in parentheses.

Three key patterns emerge from Table 1. First, users switching intention and/or behavior are related to perceptions of the incumbent product/service (such as dissatisfaction with its use, its perceived quality, and prior usage) and expectations of substitute product/service (such as alternative attractiveness). Second, switching cost appears to be a persistent barrier to switching across many studies (Burnham et al, 2003; Bansal et al, 2005). Third, individual difference variables such as variety seeking propensity or risk-taking propensity may also influence switching.

However, what is still lacking from the above research is an overarching theory that can tie together these switching predictors within a comprehensive framework. Such a theory can help researchers make sense of the empirical findings, reconcile conflicting observations, and guide their choice of constructs and hypotheses in future switching research. In the next section, we present migration theory from the human geography literature as such an organizing framework and a theoretical ‘lens’ for building our theoretical model of IT switching.

## Theory and hypotheses

## Migration theory

Migration involves the physical movement of people (‘migrants’) from one geographic location to another for at least a certain period of time (Clark, 1986). Migration may be over short distances or over long distances, temporary or permanent, and voluntary or forced. However, to be considered ‘migration,’ the move must have some permanence and have clearly defined originating and destination locations (Lee, 1966).

The key tenets of migration theory are synthesized in Lee’s (1966) seminal paper ‘A Theory of Migration.’ Lee postulated that human migration is the result of negative factors at the originating location that encourage people to leave (‘push factors’), positive factors at the destination that attract people (‘pull factors’), and ‘intervening obstacles’ constraining such movement. Lee (1966) did not identify specific push or pull factors that drive migration. Instead. he stated. 'We can never specify the exact set of factors which impels or prohibits migration for a given person, we can, in general, only set forth a few which seem of special importancey Needless to say, the factors that hold and attract or repel people are precisely understood neither by the social scientist nor the persons directly affected’ (p. 50).

Though push and pull factors explain broad migration trends fairly well, they cannot explain why individuals faced with the same set of push and pull factors sometimes exhibit remarkably different migration behaviors. To account for such micro-level differences, Lee (1966) suggested that migration patterns are not shaped so much by the actual objective state of these push and pull factors, but by migrants’ perceptions of the same. He also suggested that for migration to occur, the overall effect of pull and push factors must overcome any intervening obstacles opposing such move, such as inertia, moving costs, and/or personal factors such as strong family ties at the originating location. Likewise, migration decisions may be influenced by individual differences such as migrants’ age (e.g., younger adults are more mobile) and personal life circumstances.

Despite its age, Lee’s (1966) work has withstood the test of time as the dominant theoretical perspective for migration research. In the next section, we examine how this theory can serve as a theoretic lens for explaining user migration from client-centric computing to cloud computing.

## Research hypotheses

End-user migration from client-hosted to cloud-based platforms is analogous to the process of human migration in that cloud users are usually not first-time users of IT, but are typically users migrating from client-hosted applications. This may be because they are dissatisfied with client-hosted systems (a ‘push factor’) or find the cloud platform superior in some ways (a ‘pull factor’). Furthermore, despite the presence of pull and push factors, users of client-hosted systems may not universally migrate to the cloud because of intervening obstacles (or ‘mooring factor’) such as switching costs and individual differences.

Migration theory does not specify which push, pull, or mooring factors are salient to cloud migration. However, migration may be seen as a combination of discontinuance of client-hosted systems (a post-adoptive behavior) and acceptance (an adoptive behavior) of cloud-hosted systems. Hence, the pull factors in migration theory are similar to the forces that motivate new IT acceptance, and the push factors are akin to the forces that drive IT discontinuance. Migration theory serves as a ‘theoretic bridge’ linking IT adoption and post-adoption research that have traditionally been studied separately in the literature. In the next few paragraphs, we identify specific factors from the IT adoption and post-adoption literatures, product/service switching research in marketing, and cloud computing studies that are related to cloud migration. Our overall research model is illustrated in Figure 1.

Migration theory views migration as a rational, goaldirected behavior that is based on a process of controlled and deliberate reasoning. This rational model is consistent with contemporary models of IT adoption and postadoption, such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis et al, 1989), the unified theory of acceptance and usage of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al, 2003), and the expectation-confirmation model (ECM) (Bhattacherjee, 2001). These models suggest that endusers’ IT usage behavior is framed by and consistent with their intentions to use IT. The generalized association between intention and behavior, extensively validated in IT adoption (Davis et al, 1989; Venkatesh et al, 2003) and psychology research (Ajzen, 1991), can be extended to the cloud migration context to postulate end-users’ intention to migrate to cloud-based services as the primary predictor of their actual migration behavior. Hence, we posit:

H1: Users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services is positively related to their cloud computing migration behavior.

Pull factors Pull factors are cloud-related factors that attract end-users to the cloud. Because migration theory does not elaborate what these factors are, we examine IT adoption research and cloud computing studies to identify pull factors salient to cloud adoption. Prior IT adoption research indicates that the strongest and most consistent predictor of new IT adoption is the performance expectancy fit adoption (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Also called perceived usefulness, this construct is defined as the degree to which users believe that adopting a new IT will improve their task performance, and has been validated to positively influence user intention to use IT across a wide range of IT adoption contexts (Venkatesh et al, 2003). Many end-users are attracted to the cloud because of its expected usefulness or benefits, such as improve their ability to create collaborative documents (e.g., wiki pages), share personal content or insights (e.g., photo-sharing or blogs), and coordinate joint activities (e.g., calendaring applications). File sharing is convenient using cloud-based tools such as Dropbox, which provides secure password-based access to authorized users and avoids the problem of sending large files through electronic mail attachments Furthermore. the cloud also relieves end-users from the burden of installing, maintaining, and upgrading their application systems. Although perceived usefulness construct in the IT adoption literature is often evaluated independently. in the case of cloud migration, the benefits of the cloud is evaluated against client-based systems that cloud adopters wish to migrate from. To capture the relative nature of this evaluation process against client-hosted systems as a baseline or reference frame, we call this construct relative usefulness. If end-users indeed view cloud computing as being relatively more advantageous compared with traditional client-based computing, they are more likely to migrate to the cloud. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H2a: Relative usefulness of cloud migration is positively related to users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

The cloud computing literature notes that the primary attraction of cloud is its anywhere/anytime access (Rayport & Heyward, 2009). Mobile professionals can quickly access cloud-hosted data and application resources from any location at any time using laptop, network, or tablet computers. Furthermore, cloud access is not restricted by expiration of software licenses, unavailability of given software, or downtime because of system maintenance, upgrade, or backup. Although e-mails may be inaccessible on a Microsoft Exchange server when the server is undergoing backup or maintenance, this is not the case with a cloud-based e-mail service like Gmail. Further, while accessing Exchange-based e-mails may require setting up a Microsoft Outlook client with appropriate access privileges, Gmail can be accessed from any web browser. Universal access, termed expected omnipresence in this study, is an attribute unique to cloud computing that has no parallel in the IT adoption literature. This is also a pull factor that attract send-users to cloud-based platforms, which is likely to enhance end-users’ intentions to migrate to the cloud. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H2b: Expected omnipresence of cloud migration is positively related to users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

Push factor Push factors are those related to the incumbent IT (client-hosted services) that push end-users to use cloud services. IT post-adoption research shows that the primary reason users discontinue incumbent IT products or services is their dissatisfaction with that IT (Parthasarathy & Bhattacherjee, 1998). The expectation-confirmation model (ECM) of IT continuance (Bhattacherjee, 2001) posits that satisfied users continue using an incumbent IT, while dissatisfied users consider switching to a replacement IT. Dissatisfaction is an affect representing users’ overall evaluative response to their prior first-hand experience with IT usage. If they had a poor experience with client-hosted computing, they are likely to be dissatisfied and migrate to cloud-hosted services. Conversely, users’ satisfied with client-hosted services will likely continue using the incumbent IT and not migrate to the cloud. Hence:

## H3: User dissatisfaction with client-hosted services is positively related to their intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

Mooring factors Mooring factors refer to intervening obstacles, such as migration cost, that constrain endusers’ migration behaviors despite the presence of positive pull and push effects. Migration cost has been conceptualized as switching cost in the marketing literature, defined as ‘one-time costs that customers associate with the process of switching from one provider to another’ (Burnham et al, 2003). Adapting Burnham et al’s (2003) taxonomy of switching cost to Internet access services, Ray et al (2012) propose six types of switching costs categorized into two groups: vendor-related costs (benefits-loss cost, service uncertainty cost, brand-relationship cost) and user-related costs (search and evaluation cost. transfer cost. and learning cost). Benefits-loss cost relates to the loss of benefits or rewards (e.g., loyalty points or discounted rates) caused by severing discontinuing the services of an incumbent vendor, brand relationship cost refers to psychological loss of severing ties with a brand that one has bonded with, and service uncertainty cost refers to the uncertainty associated with a new service. Search and evaluation cost is the time and effort required to find and compare providers before switching, transfer cost is the time, effort, and money required to transfer service from one provider to another, and learning cost is the time and effort needed to adapt to the new provider.

Amongst the above types of switching costs, two costs that are clearly relevant to cloud migration are transfer cost (hereinafter called ‘setup cost’ for conceptual clarity) and learning cost. Migrating to the cloud requires significant investment in setting up the cloud service to end-users’ needs, such as customizing cloud-based applications to serve unique personal needs, such as reformatting client-hosted data to fit the generic data formats of a cloud provider. Furthermore, learning how to use the cloud effectively may take substantial time and effort on part of end-users. Search and evaluation cost may also be relevant if users are required to search for and evaluate alternative cloud services prior to adoption. Benefits-loss and brand relationship costs are not relevant to cloud migration, given the impersonal nature of computing services. Although uncertainty with a new IT product and service may certainly discourage users from adopting new IT, it is viewed in the organizational literature as an independent construct, rather than as a switching cost. The above discussion leads us to hypothesize:

## H4a: Users’ switching costs are negatively related to their intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

A second mooring factor unique to the context of cloud migration is security concerns. The cloud computing literature suggests that this concern has discouraged cloud migration behaviors (Subashini & Kavitha, 2011). Internet-hosted cloud servers are notoriously susceptible to three types of security threats: (1) denial of service or hacker attacks (causing server unavailability), (2) data being altered maliciously or accidentally during transit from the cloud to the client (data integrity), and (3) viruses, malwares, or botnets that can attach themselves to downloaded content and infect client systems. Although most cloud computing services offer a wide range of security solutions, such as firewalls, anti-virus, back-up servers, and encryption, none of these mechanisms are foolproof or can assure immunity from all security threats. Furthermore, cloud providers generally do not disclose their risk-mitigation strategies for security reasons, and end-users may wonder whether such strategies are indeed adequate. Drawing upon Salisbury et al (2001), we define security concerns as users’ beliefs in cloud service providers’ ability to safeguard sensitive personal information from security breaches during transmission and storage. Prior studies have found security concerns to have a negative effect on IT adoption intention (e.g., Benson, 1983; White & Christy, 1987; Goodhue & Straub, 1991, Salisbury et al, 2001) and this effect should be no different in the cloud migration context. However, security concerns in the cloud context refer to cloud vendors’ ability to mitigate security breaches, rather than the breaches per se. Hence, this construct is not a cloud-related negative pull factor, but rather a situational mooring factor that keeps users anchored to extant client-based systems:

H4b: Users’ security concerns are negatively related to their intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

Moderating effects Migration theory suggests that intervening obstacles not only directly influence one’s propensity to migrate, but also moderate the effects of pull and push factors (Lee, 1966). These obstacles, or mooring factors, are presumed to attenuate the primary effects of the pull and push factors, thereby lowering one’s overall propensity to migrate.

In the context of cloud adoption, high expectations of relative usefulness (expected benefits) are likely to motivate end-users to migrate to the cloud. However, this positive effect can be tempered if users simultaneously experience high switching costs of such migration. Among end-users who view migration to the cloud as having high relative usefulness, those that experience high switching costs are less likely to migrate than those who experience low switching costs. Likewise, among users who view cloud migration to have high switching costs (and hence, less likely to migrate), those who view cloud migration as more useful than locally hosted systems are more likely to migrate to the cloud than those who perceive cloud migration as having less relative usefulness. Hence, switching costs and relative usefulness may be expected to have a negative moderation effect on intention to migrate to the cloud. Therefore, we postulate:

H5a: Switching costs negatively moderate the effect of relative usefulness on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

In a similar vein, users who view cloud computing to be omnipresent but also expect it to have high switching costs are less likely to migrate than those who expect cloud computing to be omnipresent but having less switching costs. In other words, high switching costs tend to compensate for the effect of high omnipresence on intention to migrate to the cloud, resulting in a lesser intent to migrate, while amplifying the effect of low omnipresence on migration intention. This expectation leads us to hypothesize:

H5b: Switching costs negatively moderate the effect of expected omnipresence on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

A similar pattern of moderating effect may be expected between switching costs and the push factor, dissatisfaction with client-hosted services. End-users dissatisfied with their local computing platforms are generally more likely to migrate to the cloud, but more so if they also experience low switching costs and less so if they experience high switching costs. High switching costs tend to dampen their intention to migrate even if they are strongly dissatisfied with their local service. In contrast, lower switching costs coupled with high dissatisfaction complement each other to result in a stronger effect on intention than each driver alone. Hence, we hypothesize:

H5c: Switching costs negatively moderate the effect of dissatisfaction with client-hosted services on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

Security concerns with cloud computing are also expected to have moderating effects with the pull and push factors, similar to that of switching costs, given that security concerns and switching costs are both mooring factors constraining cloud migration. Although end-users with high relative usefulness and expected omnipresence of the cloud, and dissatisfaction with client-based systems are more likely to migrate to the cloud, this effect will tend to be stronger if users have low security concerns about cloud migration than if they had high security concerns. Hence, high security concerns attenuate the effects of strong pull and push factors, and amplify the effects of weak pull and push factors, leading us to hypothesize:

H6a: Security concerns negatively moderate the effect of relative usefulness on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

H6b: Security concerns negatively moderate the effect of expected omnipresence on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

H6c: Security concerns negatively moderate the effect of dissatisfaction with client-hosted services on users’ intention to migrate to cloud computing services.

Control variables Among other predictors of IT adoption in the adoption literature, subjective norm, or the normative influence of key referent groups such as peers or coworkers on one’s behavioral choice, may also influence users’ cloud computing migration intention, if these referent groups have already migrated to the cloud or plan to do so in the near future. Likewise, perceived behavioral control (PBC), defined as one’s perception of the degree of control over a target behavior (Ajzen, 1991), may also be relevant to cloud migration. PBC includes two types of control (Ajzen, 2002): (1) internal control: one’s control over internal resources (e.g., personal ability) needed to perform the intended behavior and alternatively referred to as self-efficacy in the literature, and (2) external control: one’s control over external resources (e.g., financial resources, technical support) needed to perform that behavior. Though in the original TPB, Ajzen (1991) suggested that PBC influences both intention and behavior, subsequently Ajzen (2002) clarified that the internal control influences intention while the external control impacts behavior. Internal control has been operationalized as self-efficacy (internal control), defined as people’s judgment of their personal ability to perform a particular behavior, which is a well-established predictor of IT usage intention (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). However, external control is less relevant to cloud migration because technological and financial resources for maintaining the cloud are borne by cloud vendors and not by end-users. Hence, though not of theoretical interest, subjective norm and self-efficacy are included in our cloud migration model as control variables.

The cloud migration model proposed here is a preliminary and parsimonious model. It integrates predictors from different theoretical perspectives; nonetheless, there may be additional predictors of cloud migration, which are left open for future research.

## Research methods

## Research design

Our hypothesized model of cloud computing adoption was tested empirically using a longitudinal survey of Google Apps adoption among undergraduate student subjects at two Korean universities. Google Apps is a portfolio of office productivity (e.g., spreadsheet, presentation, and word processing software) and communication applications (e.g., Gmail – an e-mail application), online collaborative calendar (Google Calendar), data sharing/ collaboration software, and instant messaging software) that are hosted on remote Google servers and available to individual users free of cost using a single sign-in Google account. Google Apps allows users to store, access, and share documents on Google’s cloud-based servers as an alternative to storing the same on their personal computers. Students were reasonable subjects for our study because students are often early innovators of online technologies in their personal and academic lives, and many of them have already migrated to Google Apps.

At the start of the semester, subjects were introduced to Google Apps via a live demonstration. Their perceptions of push, pull, and mooring factors, intention, and control variables were measured after the introductory session. Subjects were encouraged to try and experiment with Google Apps and use it for their university-related work during the semester, but there was no mandate or incentive for migrating to Google Apps. During the semester, subjects completed four assignments as part of their course grade, which they could do using Google Apps or client-hosted applications (typically, Microsoft Windows). Their cloud migration behavior was measured at the end of the semester as the extent to which they used the different Google applications for completing these four assignments. Subjects with prior experience with Google Apps were dropped from our sample, since we wanted to focus on those who migrated to Google Apps during the course of our study.

## Construct measurement

With the exception of migration behavior, all constructs were measured using multiple-item, seven-point scales, using pre-validated measures wherever possible. The original English language instrument was translated into Korean for administration to subjects. However, to check for translation bias, a back-translation technique was employed, in which a different translator translated the Korean questionnaire back into English. The backtranslated items had a high degree of correspondence with the original English items, assuring the relative lack of translation bias. Scale items are listed in Appendix A.

Relative usefulness of using Google Apps was measured using Davis et al’s (1989) four-item perceived usefulness scale, after rewording these items slightly to capture subjects’ perceptions relative to their current desktop software. Omnipresence was measured using Buttell’s (2010) four-item scale. Dissatisfaction with incumbent IT was measured as a reverse-coded version of Bhattacherjee’s (2001) three-item semantic differential satisfaction scale. Security concerns were measuring using Pavlou et al’s (2007) four-item Likert-scale. Intention to migrate to the cloud was measured using Bhattacherjee’s (2001) three-item Likert-scaled measure. Subjective norm was measured using Taylor & Todd’s (1995) three-item measure, and self-efficacy was measuring using Agarwal & Karahanna’s (2000) eight-item measure.

Switching cost is a multi-dimensional construct comprising of two dimensions: learning and setup costs. These two dimensions are distinct and can vary in opposite directions, for example, some subjects may face high learning cost but low setup cost, or vice versa. To reflect this multi-dimensional nature and to represent the fact that its two constituent dimensions form (rather than reflect) the overall construct, switching cost was modeled as a second-order formative construct comprising of two first-order dimensions: learning and setup costs. The first-order dimensions were measured using three-item reflective scales adapted from Burnham et al (2003) and Jones et al (2002) respectively.

Finally, cloud migration behavior was measured at the end of the semester by aggregating subjects’ selfreported use of Google Apps in the four assignments they completed during the course of the semester. This was measured as a two-item formative construct consisting of Google productivity suite usage (aggregation of subjects’ use of the three Google Docs components: spreadsheet, presentation, and word processing) and Google communication suite usage (aggregation of G-mail, Google calendar, instant messaging, and collaboration applications).

## Data analysis and results

We employed the structural equation modeling (SEM) technique for data analysis, using AMOS 19.0 software. Covariance-based SEM approaches are particularly suited for statistical testing of complex models with direct, indirect, and moderating effects. In addition, unlike variance-based path analytic techniques, SEM provides multiple goodness-of-fit indices to assess the degree of convergence between a theoretical model and observed empirical data. Hair et al (1998) suggest a minimum sample size of 100 to achieve adequate power in maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) technique in SEM, which was met in our study (N ¼ 188).

## Descriptive analysis

From an initial sampling frame of 223 students, we screened out four responses with mostly missing values and 31 responses from subjects with prior Google Docs experience. This left us with 188 usable responses for an overall response rate of 84.3%. Table 2 shows the demographic profiles of these respondents and their cloud migration behaviors. Approximately 85% of our respondents were in the 21–30 age group and 66% were female. The highest migration was observed for Google document, G-mail, and data sharing/collaboration (N ¼ 153 for each application), followed in decreasing order by spreadsheet (N ¼ 128), presentation (N ¼ 123), Google Calendar (N ¼ 90), and instant messaging tools (N ¼ 57).

Although non-response bias was not a concern in our study given our high response rate, common method bias (CMB) was still a threat to the internal validity of our findings since most of our independent variables were measured at a single point in time using Likert scales. To test for this bias, following Liang et al (2007), we added a common method factor to our structural model, and allowed indicators of all constructs to be associated reflectively with this factor. As shown in Appendix B, all substantive factor loadings were significant and high (average 0.90; lowest 0.74) and method factor loadings were low and non-significant (average 0.014; highest 0.08). The average substantive explained variance for the hypothesized indicators was 0.812, compared with the average common method-based variance of 0.003, suggesting that CMB had no significant impact on our observed data.

## Measurement model

Convergent and discriminant validity were analyzed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) conducted using the SEM technique. All of our hypothesized constructs, except cloud migration behavior (which was a ratio scale measured at a later point in time), were modeled as reflective measures of their respective indicators. Convergent validity was assessed using three criteria recommended by Fornell & Larcker (1981) (1) all scale items should be significant and have loadings exceeding 0.70 on their respective scales, (2) the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50, and (3) composite reliability of each construct should exceed 0.70. Our data met all three conditions of convergent validity (see Table 3). In addition, Cronbach a value for each construct exceeded 0.70 – the minimum internal consistency norm for confirmatory analysis (Bearden et al, 1993).

For discriminant validity, we compared the square root of AVE for each construct with bivariate correlations between that and all other constructs (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). From the square roots of AVE indicated along the principal diagonal of the correlation matrix in Table 4, we find that the lowest value among all constructs (0.770) was higher than the highest bivariate correlation between any construct pairs. Hence, discriminant validity was also assured in our data sample.

Lastly, since formative constructs may be influenced by multi-collinearity, we examined the multi-collinearity between learning cost and setup cost – the two dimen sions of our switching cost construct. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 3.3 generally indicate the absence of multi-collinearity (Diamantopoulos, 2006). In our study, these figures were 1.144 and 1.143 for learning cost and setup cost respectively. ## Hypotheses testing

Path significance and magnitude of each of our hypothesized effects, and the explanatory power of our overall model were analyzed using SEM in two stages. The first stage examined the main effects specified in Hypotheses H1 through H4, and the second stage added the hypothesized moderating effects Hypotheses H5–H6 to the main effects model. This two-stage approach is appropriate for testing moderating effects of formative constructs such as switching costs, because the traditional product indicator approach of testing for moderating effects is not feasible for formative constructs. In this two-stage approach, latent variable scores from the first stage were used as weights to create a formative index for first-order dimensions of the switching cost construct. Interaction terms were created as element-wise products of the moderator index and latent scores of exogenous variables that it moderated. The results of the main and moderating effects models are presented in Tables 5 and 6, respectively.

The parameter estimates, fit indices and observed residuals showed acceptable goodness of fit between our proposed main effects model and the empirical data: normalized chi-square (X<sup>2</sup>/df ¼ 2.168) was less than the maximum recommended value of 3.0, comparative fit index (CFI ¼ 0.922) and non-normed fit index (NNFI ¼ 0.905) were each less than the minimum threshold value of 0.90, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA ¼ 0.079) was less than the maximum threshold value of 0.80 (Bentler & Bonnett, 1980). The only aberration was Goodness of Fit Index (GFI ¼ 0.820), which was lower than the threshold minimum of 0.90. Except GFI, the remaining indices were indicative of a good model fit.

## Discussion and implications

## Limitations of the study

The findings of our study should be evaluated in light of its limitations. First, our findings, drawn from a sample of Korean students’ use of Google Apps, may not be readily generalized to Western IT users, non-student (professional) users, or other types of IT applications. Western users tend to be more individualistic and rely on personal judgment in their decision making, while Asian users are more collectivist and influenced by social considerations, and these cultural differences may shape their IT migration behaviors. The professional workforce in Asian countries, and especially those working with IT, is significantly younger than that in Western countries, and hence, while our findings should generalize to the professional workforce in Asian countries such as China, India, and Philippines, where culture and workforce demographics are similar to that of Korean students, they may not readily generalize to Western countries. Furthermore, our study examined migration to Google Apps, a cloud-based SaaS service targeted at individual users. Cloud services targeted at organizational users, such as database, infrastructure, and storage management, may differ significantly in scope and complexity from Google Apps, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to organizational applications.

Second, other push, pull, and mooring factors not examined in this study may be relevant to cloud migration. Given that the purpose of this study was to postulate and test a preliminary model of cloud migration, we selected the most important predictors from prior research. Future research may explore additional predictors in order to expand the scope and explanatory power of our proposed model.

Third, end-users’ perception were captured in our study after subjects were shown a live demonstration of Google Apps during the introductory session. Although such a demo might have been adequate to capture subjects’ perceptions of pull factors such as relative usefulness and omnipresence of Google Apps, it may have been too short of a time for subjects to form an informed opinion on the security risk or switching cost of migrating to the cloud.

Lastly, statistical significance is concerned with whether a research result is due to chance or sampling variability while practical significance examines whether the result is useful in the real world (Kirk, 1996). Statistical significance is determined by significance level, while practical significance examines the magnitude of path coefficients. A hypothesized effect is considered substantive or practically significant if it is over 0.10 (size effect), but should ideally be above 0.20 (Asher, 1983; Hair et al, 1998), below which ‘everything correlates to some extent with everything else’ (Meehl, 1990, p. 204) because of ‘some complex unknown network of generic and environmental factors’ (Meehl, 1990, p. 209). The standardized effect sizes in our main and moderating effects model were in the 0.13–0.55 range, demonstrating both statistical and practical significance. However, observed effects less than the desired value of 0.20 may be indicative of limited practical significance.

## Research implications

This study makes four contributions to IS research. First, it presents one of the earliest theory-based models of IT migration that can inform and guide future research in this area. IT adoption research cannot be readily used to explain IT migration because migration occurs from an incumbent IT to a substitute IT, while adoption does not presume the presence of an incumbent IT. At a time when the majority of end-users, at least in developed countries, already use IT in their personal and professional lives, IT migration is more worthy of research investigation than IT adoption. Furthermore, users migrating to the cloud tend to be experienced users, which is another point of departure from adoption research.

Second, this study identifies several distinctive differences between IT migration and IT adoption. We found that relative usefulness, traditionally the dominant predictor of IT adoption, is less salient to explaining cloud migration, while expected omnipresence, security concerns, and switching costs that are largely irrelevant for IT adoption research are more influential. We suggest that future IT migration studies consider a broader basket of predictors than that available from current IT adoption research.

Third, our study introduces migration theory as a new and useful theory for IT usage research, and more generally, human geography as a new reference discipline for IS research. Our proposed model of IT migration integrates constructs from IT adoption (e.g., relative usefulness) and post-adoption (e.g., dissatisfaction with incumbent IT) research within a common framework, because IT migration is essentially a process of discontinuing one IT and adopting a different IT. In doing so, the proposed model provides a ‘theoretical bridge’ for integrating and extending the IT adoption and post-adoption streams of research that have historically proceeded along independent paths. Prior IS research has drawn from interesting reference disciplines such as biology, communications, and physics; our study continues that tradition by suggesting human geography as another reference discipline and a contemporary problem domain (IT migration) for applying this reference discipline.

Fourth, our study also draws attention to the negative factors or inhibitors of IT migration, such as switching costs and security concerns that have largely eluded contemporary IT adoption research. As Cenfetelli (2004) notes, inhibitors are not simply polar opposites of enablers because of their asymmetric effects; their presence discourages adoption but their absence does not necessarily encourage adoption. Furthermore, as our findings indicate, inhibitors such as switching costs not only negatively impact migration intentions, but also negatively bias the positive effect of enablers. Our study provides an illustrative example of how to study inhibitors and enablers simultaneously within a common migration theoretic model.
