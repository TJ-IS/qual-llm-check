# Building a successful relationship in business process outsourcing: an exploratory study

## Abstract

In the past decade, business process outsourcing (BPO) has emerged as an important economic and business issue. Successful offshoring and outsourcing of business processes to low-cost destinations has made BPO a strategic decision. Inconsistencies at the client and service provider levels globally are requiring clients to build competencies in outsourcing management. Clients are further pushing service providers to build capabilities such as business process management and information technology management competencies. A research survey is carried out with European clients to identify the critical competencies of the service provider and clients for building a successful relationship. The survey indicates that the clients’ focus is on BPO outcome delivery for building successful relationship and not so much on the service provider’s competencies.

## Introduction

Outsourcing implies the use of external agents to perform an organizational activity. Companies have always subcontracted tasks to outside agencies, but attention to outsourcing of information technology (IT) services like application development, maintenance of IT Infrastructure, and so on, called IT outsourcing (ITO), started in the 1990s, primarily as a way to control rising IT costs (Lee et al., 2003). Rapid improvements in information and communications technology (ICT) and globalization have contributed greatly to close integration of global labour and capital markets (UNCTAD, 2004). This has resulted in firms having greater access to human capital scattered around the globe. Outsourcing of IT services has continued to grow and the focus has shifted to international service providers. This shift in ITO to overseas locations began to be called ‘offshore outsourcing’ or simply ‘offshoring’. During recent years outsourcing has been extended to IT-enabled business processes (often called business process outsourcing (BPO)) to offshore locations, mainly to lowcost destination countries like India.

According to Gartner (2004), BPO is the delegation of one or more IT-intensive business processes to an external service provider who owns, administers, and manages the processes based on a defined and measurable set of performance metrics. Specifically, BPO involves contracting with one or more BPO service providers (vendors) for the execution of business process operations as per the client organization’s requirements (Kshetri, 2007; Youngdahl et al., 2007). The global market for BPO, which is only a segment of offshoring, was estimated to increase at a compounded annual growth rate of 9.5% and to reach U.S.\$ 173 billion in 2007 (Pfannenstein & Tsai, 2004). The small and mid-size businesses are adopting BPO in addition to the U.S. Fortune 500 and large European and Japanese multinationals (Carmel & Nicholson, 2005). A recent estimate shows even a higher BPO market amounting to U.S.\$ 975 billion in 2012 (ARM, 2008). As BPO has become more pervasive, the need to manage cross-border collaboration has become more important. Specifically, the need to manage outsourcing relationships on a long-term basis has become critical for both the client and the service provider (Lee et al., 2003). Unfortunately, there are indications that the road to an effective relationship is not without peril. A survey conducted in 2005 found that 25% of outsourcing customers reported a ‘relationship failure’ (Goles & Chin, 2005). Relationship failure is defined as the termination of the contract by the client, which had been in place for at least one year (Caldwell & McGee, 1997).

Several researchers have shown that poor vendor selection practices, misperception of vendor capabilities, and exaggerated client expectations have been sources of the failure in ITO deals (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a; Michell & Fitzgerald, 1997). Thus many clients have considered switching vendors. However, switching vendors during the course of contract execution involves substantial transition costs, including termination costs, switching costs, redeployment costs, relocation costs, and so on (Goo et al., 2007). After completion of the contract, the client company may have to search for another appropriate service provider or take the outsourced IT activities back in-house (Lacity & Willcocks, 1998). Many ITO activities are project-based. Thus such projects can be developed, tested, and then implemented without impacting the day-to-day operations of the client company. In contrast, most BPO activities, like a few ITO activities, are not project-based and they have to be handled in a real-time environment from day one. Thus such outsourced processes pose a greater challenge and risk for the clients if they have to switch service providers.

Outsourcing relationships, including partnership and trust in ITO domains, have been studied from very wide perspectives, such as interaction, contract, context, structure, governance, capabilities, and behaviour dimensions (Kern & Willcocks, 2000; Mani et al., 2006). Goo et al. (2007) have focussed on duration of the relationship with single vendor and the factors influencing the same in ITO, but no one has addressed the relationship issues in the BPO domain. In comparison to ITO, the study of BPO relationships is thus in a nascent stage.

In this research we study the BPO relationship in terms of extension of the contract for another period with the same vendor and enhancing the scope of the work during the outsourcing duration as measures of a successful relationship. In this study, we propose several important factors from key theoretical perspectives that may impact a successful relationship. We have also conducted a questionnaire survey of the clients and tested the proposed model. Thus as an expansion of BPO literature of the post-contract period, our study below provides new insights for building a ‘win-win relationship’, because repetitive outsourcing relationships are beneficial for both the service providers and the client companies. It takes the relationship to the next level, making the service provider a consultant and trusted advisor to the client (Webb & Laborde, 2005). Our study also provides a conceptual model that brings more focus on the important variables that impact such successful relationships.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section provides the theoretical foundation on which we have built a conceptual model. Then the following section elaborates on the variables of the model and the research hypotheses. The succeeding section discusses the research methodology including data collection from client companies in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, data analysis, and results. The last section provide conclusions and suggestions for further research.

## Theoretical foundations

The theoretical foundation for building a successful outsourcing relationship is based on two streams of literature. The client would look for a capable service provider who can deliver services at low cost. Hence the first stream is focused on capability sourcing and the second stream on economic perspective. A third stream of literature aims at clarifying BPO domain, as BPO is a relatively new concept compared to ITO.

## Business process outsourcing

A business process is defined as an integrated sequence of events that occur over time leading to an organizational outcome (Boudreau & Robey, 1999). Garvin (1997) puts it in different words: a business process is a set of coordinated activities that helps accomplish a particular business goal. The order of these activities (procedure) and the efficiency of those who perform the activities (process actors) determine the overall performance of the business process. An organization may outsource a single activity or a business process, as, for example cheque processing or accounts receivables or a collection of business processes encompassed in a business function, as, for example, finance and accounting. BPO is defined as the movement of business process (es) and /or activity(ies) from inside the organization to external service providers (Click & Duening, 2005). This research paper focuses on offshore outsourcing of IT-enabled business processes.

Published literature related to outsourcing of IT and business processes emphasizes outsourcing as a means to enhance an organization’s business performance. These benefits of business excellence are achieved at low cost, by favourably exploiting the cost disparity between in-house and external IT skills (DiRomualdo & Gurbaxani, 1998). Similar advantages of business excellence have been claimed by leveraging capabilities and investments of external firms. (Quinn. 1999).

In order to understand the management implications of BPO, it is desirable to develop a classification of business processes as viewed by both the client and the service provider. From the clients’ perspective, processes have been classified as peripheral, critical, and strategic ( Jenster et al., 2005); as core, critical but non-core, and non-core non-critical (Dole & Switser, 1998); and as critical, key, and support (Click & Duening, 2005). Similarly, from the service providers’ perspective, Niranjan et al. (2007) have classified the business processes according to their complexity and criticality. BPO can be categorized as transactional, transformational, and strategic in nature, depending upon the intended benefits, which are cost efficiency, improvement in quality, process transformation, and/ or business transformation. This categorization of BPO implies an increasing level of complexity in the outsourced process (Mani et al., 2006; Niranjan et al., 2007; Saxena & Bharadwaj, 2007).

In the initial stages of BPO, client organizations outsource only their peripheral processes (non-core, non-critical) primarily to reduce process costs and improve process performance. However, this has given the service providers an opportunity to develop capabilities in process domain expertise. Levina & Ross (2003) refer to the opportunity available to IT vendors (service providers) to develop sets of core competencies by virtue of focus on IT service delivery that generates significant benefits to both themselves and their clients. Alternatively, client organizations can uplift vendor capabilities through interaction and in that process also can elevate their own core knowledge capabilities (Willcocks et al., 2004). The scope of BPO has correspondingly evolved, bringing focus on the capabilities and skills of the service provider.

## Economic perspective of BPO

The economic perspective of BPO examines the efficiency, coordination, and governance of transactions between the client company and the service provider, based on the theory of transaction costs. According to this theory, the properties of a transaction determine what constitutes the efficient governance structuremarket, hierarchy, or alliance (Coase, 1937; Williamson, 1975; Williamson, 1979). In selecting a governance mode, organizations attempt to minimize transaction costs. A market governance mode is preferred when transaction costs are low, and an internal governance mode is preferred when transaction costs are high. According to this theory, the market is always considered the lowest cost producer of goods or services due to economies of scale and scope. Similarly, the production cost advantage of the market is overwhelmed by high transaction costs. Transaction costs are classified into two types: (i) pre-contract costs that include search costs and contracting (bargaining and drafting) costs; (ii) postcontract costs that encompass monitoring/enforcement costs, adaptation costs, bonding costs, and dissolution costs. If the client is able to derive transaction cost benefits from BPO. successful relationships should follow.

As BPO becomes transformational and strategic in nature, the intended BPO outcomes are now either process transformation or business transformation. Such outsourcing generally requires relational governance and hence relationship-specific investment and relational contract (Mani et al., 2006; Kedia & Lahiri, 2007).

## Capability sourcing perspective of BPO

The resource-based view (RBV) emphasizes that resources internal to a firm are the principal drivers of the firm’s profitability and strategic advantage (Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991). It rejects traditional economic assumptions that resources are homogeneous and perfectly mobile. Instead it argues that resources are heterogeneously distributed across firms and are imperfectly transferred between firms (Barney, 1991; Barney 1999). Barney (1991) categorized resources into three groups: physical resources such as plant, equipment, location, and assets; human resources such as the management team, knowledge, and skills; and organizational resources such as culture and reputation. Recently researchers have referred to these resources as human capital and organizational capital (Lahiri & Kedia, 2009). Human capital is tacit and involves work-based knowledge, experience, skills and abilities of employees at various levels. Organizational capital consists of codified and institutionalized knowledge (Subramaniam & Youndt, 2005). Resources enable a firm to conceive and implement strategies to improve its efficiency and effectiveness (Daft, 1983). Organizations can obtain above-normal returns if they can use their existing resources to sustain competitive advantage by exploiting opportunities in the market or neutralizing threats from competitors’ strategic resources.

Various criticisms have been advanced in reviews of the extant literature on the RBV (Wang & Ahmed, 2007). Starting from its tautological definition, the criticism has been on the clarity of the associated terminology, the static nature of the RBV, especially to address market dynamism, and its failure to define mechanisms that explain how resources are transferred to competitive advantage. However, central to the RBV is the fact that resources, per se, do not create value (Porter, 1991; Bowman & Ambrosini, 2000); value is created by an organization’s ability (or competence) to use and mobilize those resources. Setting aside the validity of the RBV as a framework of reference for organizational theory, we pursue how competencies can benefit firms. There are many definitions of competence, and a few are recalled here. Prahalad & Hamel (1990) have used the concept of capability, competence, and core competence as synonymous. Stalk et al. (1992) attempted to differentiate between core competencies and capabilities. For the purpose of this paper, we subscribe to the definition of Amit & Schoemaker (1993), because it is precise and fits with the distinction between the concepts of resource, competence, and capability. According to Amit and Schoemaker, competence refers to ‘a firm’s capacity to deploy resources, usually in combination, using organizational processes, to affect a desired end’, and thus represents ‘bundle of skills and technologies rather than a single, discrete skill or technology’ (Hamel & Prahalad, 1994). Competence can therefore be portrayed as the ability to deploy combinations of firmspecific resources to accomplish a given task (McGrath et al., 1995; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000).

Finally, organizational capability refers to the strategic application of competencies, that is, their use and deployment to accomplish given organizational goals (McGrath et al., 1995). Winter (2000, 2003) elaborates:

An organizational capability is a high-level routine (or collection of routines) that, together with its implementing input flows, confers upon an organization’s management a set of decision options for producing significant outputs of a particular type.

Some researchers refer to this capability also as management capability (Desarbo et al., 2005). In relation to the service provider, the concept of management capability specifically refers to the provider’s capability that integrates and supports various resources and firmlevel capabilities, or competencies (Amit & Schoemaker, 1993), for managing human resources, diverse project requirements, information systems, technology-related changes, and satisfying the client’s contractual requirements (Lahiri & Kedia, 2009). Using the extant RBV-based literature (Armstrong & Shimizu, 2007), capabilities are portrayed as collective, complex, and dynamic as compared to resources that are relatively independent, simple, and static.

The BPO service providers treat an outsourced process as a strategic resource by which they can enhance their core competence. The process that is outsourced by the client carries with it domain knowledge. Here we consider the process domain knowledge that is tacit, codified, and institutionalized, that it belongs to resources according to RBV, and it is embedded in process competence. These resource-rich and competence-based processes are outsourced to the service provider either because they do not belong to the client’s core competencies or some service provider can deliver them better as they have already become part of the service provider’s core competencies.

With this strategic view of the processes, BPO service providers are addressing the question that many client organizations are asking. The client organizations are asking how to outsource a ‘process’ (embodiment of an organizational capability) to a service provider who is more capable in that process? This newly emerging philosophy of outsourcing is called ‘global capability sourcing’ (Gottfredson et al., 2005). Greater focus on capability sourcing thus has specific impact on an organization’s strategic position by reducing costs, streamlining the organization, and improving quality. Thus client organizations can enhance their core capabilities that are the base for their businesses by finding more capable service providers as outsourcing partners to provide critical business process operations and drive competitive advantage in their industries. This capability sourcing view is validated through selective case studies by Gottfredson et al. (2005). A larger section of clients has yet to endorse this view, especially in the BPO domain.

## Conceptual framework

Our objective here is to develop a conceptual model for building a successful relationship in BPO. The model, thus, should address organizational or management capability that encompasses various competencies required in both the client and the service-provider organizations for building a successful relationship. The model should evaluate the required competencies from the client’s perspective in building a successful relationship. Figure 1 and Table 1 give the key constructs and theoretical orientations. Thus we address the following research questions:

1. Which competencies of service providers of BPO from their client companies’ perspectives contribute to building successful relationships?

Requirements of outsourcing management capability that encompasses corresponding competencies at the client end are crucial, since these are needed at the least to supplement service providers’ capabilities. Thus we address also the following research question:

2. Which competencies are required at the client end for building a successful relationship?

Finally we research the results of BPO outcomes to the future of a business relationship by addressing the question:

3. What is the influence of BPO outcome towards building a successful relationship?

## Successful relationship

Any BPO initiative starts with the BPO client expecting a value delivery (such as cost reduction, process performance improvement, etc.); the service provider also expects a business value addition (such as business growth, longer client retention, etc.) (Saxena & Bharadwaj, 2007). However, the BPO project may provide the expected outcome only to the client, or only to the service provider, or to neither of the two. In such a case the BPO project is considered as failed. However, if both the client and the service provider are able to realize the BPO outcome as expected, the project is considered successful and may lead to building a successful relationship between the client and the service provider. Relationship is a state involving mutual dealings, which evolve with time between people or parties (Mirani, 2006). Thus with time, the service provider and client become more valuable to each other. Anderson & Narus (1990) have identified a business relationship as a ‘process where two firms form strong and extensive social, economic, service, and technical ties over time with the intent of leveraging total cost and/ or increasing value, thereby achieving mutual benefit’. Thus we define a successful relationship as a long-term relationship in which the client is willing to renew or extend the contract with the service provider and/ or the client is willing to extend the scope of the work outsourced to the service provider. However, the other side of the coin is a losing relationship, which is defined as the termination of the contract by the client before its expiration date (Caldwell & McGee, 1997), reduction in the scope of the contract, or reduction in the volume of business to the service provider.

## BPO outcome

The BPO outcome refers to realizing the intended benefits of outsourcing. The intended benefits desired by the client may vary from cost efficiencies to quality services to process innovation. The intended benefits vary with the type of business processes that are outsourced.

We have defined the BPO outcome according to the categorization of BPO. The transactional outcome is derived from the transaction cost theory, or TCT (e.g. Williamson 1975, 1981, 1985; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993a, b, c; Lacity & Hirschheim, 1995; Aubert et al., 1996; Ang & Straub, 1998). Successful BPO outcomes are the ability to deliver cost efficiencies and better service quality. The transformational outcome is based on objectives of business transformation outsourcing, where the benefits include rapid improvement in enterprise-level performance, reduced time to market, increased innovation through access to world class skills, resources, industry knowledge, enhanced core capabilities, and so on (Linder et al., 2002). The strategic outcomes provide the client firm with sustainable competitive advantage and complementary skills to transform its business to more flexible, integrated services (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994). In this paper, to make our results rigorous, BPO outcome is confined to the transactional category. We were motivated by the presumption that most organizations would not be in a position to make a strict differentiation between transactional and other categories of BPO. We also aimed at contributing to the knowledge of BPO in its nascent stage. Thus we state our first research hypothesis as:

## Research H1: BPO outcome positively affects the likelihood of building a successful relationship between the client and the service provider.

## Competencies of the service provider

Business process management competence of the service provider (SPBPMC) Process management is a set of process-maintaining and improvement techniques coupled with organization mechanisms. Successful management of a business process in the outsourcing environment requires knowledge about the following three process attributes, and effective control structure for knowledge transfer (Kim & Kim, 2008):

(a) Process goals: the business purpose the process intends to fulfil.

(b) The interface the process has with other business processes in terms of information and knowledge flows, so that outsourcing of one process may not affect the operations of other interfaced processes.

(c) The actual process procedure being used in its operations.

Management of outsourced process enables a company to:

(a) achieve cross-organization efficiencies, gained through a common set of business processes and supporting information systems;

(b) satisfy the unique needs of different market segments and business units.

Unfortunately, the knowledge about the process may not be easily available to the service provider, either because it has never been shared by the client organization or because it is tacitly embedded in the minds of a large number of process actors and can only be acquired through a sophisticated process of knowledge acquisition methods and techniques. The more complex the process to be outsourced is, the more difficult and complicated would be the process knowledge discovery. Thus it is obvious that service providers must have the relevant level of competence in this area, without which the service outcome of the outsourced process will not be successful.

Thus business process management competencies are at the heart of the BPO service provision. In BPO, business process is the object of outsourcing and competent process is an embodiment of capability. So the service providers are required to have competence in processes that are outsourced to them. The various activities that constitute the business process management competence are as follows:

\- Process knowledge discovery. It is a well-known fact that when a process is in operation internally for a long time and is not being formally managed, the very knowledge about how the process actually operates is scanty and distributed across the entire set of process operators (Bititci & Muir, 1997; Harmon, 2003). Obviously such an unmanaged process, if outsourced, is unlikely to deliver any of the benefits of outsourcing. Therefore, when a process is considered as candidate for outsourcing, the first thing that needs to be acquired by the service provider is the knowledge about the process in its insourced form.

\- Process measurement and monitoring. It is also wellknown that in an unmanaged or informally managed process environment, even if process knowledge is available, performance measurement metric (such as process cost, process cycle time, etc.) is either missing or informal (Valiris & Glykas, 2004). Thus, the next critical requirement for process outsourcing is that the service provider has a process performance measurement system in place, which can ensure that the process performance standards defined in the service-level agreements indeed provide performance benefits of an acceptable degree to the client organization.

\- Process performance exploitation. The last and most critical competence for the service provider is the capability to exploit a process, and operating it at the desired level of performance in order to deliver the business benefits it was intended to give (Malone et al., 2003).

In short, the candidate process to be outsourced must be formally managed to yield its intended business impact. All the above three competencies may or may not exist with the client. If they do not exist with the client, the service provider has to be competent in providing them to the client, in addition to the following competencies that the service provider should also possess.

\- Process transition. Process transition or migration refers to moving the insourced version of a candidate process from the client site to the service provider site in an (improved) mutually agreed upon outsourced form, and to demonstrate that it performs satisfactorily at the agreed upon performance level. Process transition is a complex activity. Its complexity comes out of the following implicit activities: (i) acquiring the process knowledge; (ii) improving the candidate process so as to bring its performance to the agreed-upon level; (iii) implementing this improved version of the process at the service provider’s site.

\- Process performance management. This competence refers to the capability of improving the operations of the outsourced process to the agreed-upon level or even better. The performance measures that need to be managed fall in three categories: (i) process cost performance, which is required to ensure that process operation cost results in expected level of profitability for client; (ii) process output performance, which is often specified in the form of key performance indicators in the service-level agreements of the contract; (iii) process outcome performance, which refers to the effectiveness of the process in meeting the intangible business impacts, which were intended through the outsourcing of the process.

Based on the above requirements on process-related issues at the service provider’s end we formulate the following hypothesis.

Research H2: Business process management competence of the service provider positively affects the likelihood of building a successful relationship between the client and the service provider.

Information technological management competence of the service provider (SPITMC) Is IT infrastructure an enabler or a competence for BPO service provider? In order to explore this question we can look into the following issues. BPO requires skilful management of both people and technology (Click & Duening, 2005). Management of technology further requires technically skilled people to manage the infrastructure. Saxena & Bharadwaj (2007) have argued that IT infrastructure and management of the same together constitute IT management competence.

For a BPO service provider a tangible asset like IT infrastructure is a resource of the company (Wernerfelt, 1984). The IT infrastructure includes the hardware, software, people, knowledge, and management capabilities required to manage the same. The people here are different from those who work directly with client business processes. Apart from this, BPO requires huge investment in technology infrastructure. The technology infrastructure has to be reliable and scalable. BPO service providers need to develop redundant and robust infrastructure so as to provide 24/7 services to the client. This type of investment and ability to manage it makes it an important resource for the BPO service provider. Another important requirement is to integrate the technology infrastructure of the service provider with the client’s infrastructure and to extend it to provide automated monitoring of processes to the client. Thus building a successful relationship involves IT infrastructure and its management.

Research H3: Information technology management competence of the service provider positively affects the likelihood of building a successful relationship between the client and the service provider.

## Competencies of the client

Outsourcing management competence of the client (COMC) Feeney & Willcocks (1998a, b) listed nine in-house core capabilities needed to govern and manage any IT sourcing arrangement. They referred, among others, to informed buying, contract facilitation, contract monitoring, vendor development, and making technology work as delivery-related issues of IS core capabilities.

On the presumption that the environment of ITO and IT-supported BPO is the same, we have employed these capabilities, to BPO. We refer to this set of capabilities as outsourcing management competence with business systems thinking. Client organizations undertaking outsourcing require specific expertise in outsourcing management. The principal tasks of outsourcing management vary among organizations, but there are many common ones that are given below:

\- process due diligence, that is, identifying which processes to outsource;

\- in case of outsourcing a number of processes, setting up an appropriate structure for managing the outsourcing activity;

\- service provider due diligence, that is sourcing service providers and appraising them for selection;

\- negotiating service provision levels of price, quality, and delivery;

\- coordinating outsourced processes with the organiza tion’s other core and critical processes;

\- monitoring the impact of process outsourcing;

\- assessing outsourcing risks and contingency planning

Research H4: Outsourcing management competence of the client positively affects the likelihood of building a successful relationship between the client and the service provider.

## Empirical research

## Methodology and instrument development

Instrument development first consisted of reviewing the literature so as to identify measures for each dependent and independent variable. As very little published work is available, in particular about BPO, such measures were not available for some competencies of our model. Hence new measures were developed based on consultants reports and items appearing in news dailies. Previous research in general outsourcing and ITO was reviewed in order to specify the variables and measurements wherever applicable. Later a verification of the measurement identified in the previous step was carried out and a list of items was generated. In addition, verification of the measurement with some service providers in India was useful for generating a new list of variables and their measurement with clients. A comprehensive discussion between the researchers regarding their experience in the survey with service providers, and professors from European business schools, as well as three doctoral candidates working on ITO/BPO, helped evaluate the accuracy of the terminology towards client organizations. In order to validate the constructs and the derived measurement questions, the questionnaire was presented to one client organization in the Netherlands.

The complete instrument with items is given in Appendix, with the competencies defined as variables.

Various items mentioned under each variable define the measures of the competence. Literature evidence available was satisfactory to support the items of the variable BPO outcome. For business process management competence and IT management competence of the service provider references were taken from Hyder et al. (2004). For the outsourcing management competence measures we have referred to Feeney & Willcocks (1998a) and Kern & Willcocks (2001). We have also included the items that we discovered during the time of initial interviews with some service providers.

## Data collection

The questionnaire consisted of identification whether the offshored business process was outsourced or done in a captive centre of the firm, followed by identifying the type of business processes that were outsourced and the destination. Subsequent questions were equipped with multiple sub-questions. These sub-questions were used to measure the items of the variables on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 for complete disagreement to 5 for complete agreement. The last question referred to the name and address of the firm, and an optional question regarding the respondent’s name if he/she wished to receive a copy of the analysis of the survey.

To start with, we used the database of Rotterdam School Management of Erasmus University to identify possible client firms that would be outsourcing their business processes. Initial data collection that started in the beginning of May 2006 consisted of e-mailing a request for participation, with a link to the web survey. Out of 300 companies in the U.K. and the Netherlands, 190 confirmed having received our questionnaire. It was not always evident on the basis of the e-mail address whether a firm was located in the U.K. or in the Netherlands. There was also a certain degree of uncertainty whether our e-mail would be directed to an office outside these two countries. In spite of two reminders by e-mail during the subsequent 16 weeks, there were only 11 responses. This poor response prompted us to search for other firms that were not included in the initial survey. In order to assure a higher response, 148 firms in the Netherlands were contacted in the beginning of September 2006 by phone prior to mailing the request form. After getting assurance that the questionnaire would be filled in, the on-line survey was mailed to 75 firms. Lacking contact details by phone or unable to reach a suitable person, a request mail with a link to the web survey was sent to additional 90 firms both in the Netherlands and the U.K.

The link to the web survey facilitated access to the questionnaire both in English and Dutch languages; thus a respondent could choose the preferred language. ## Data analysis and results

The results of reliability and validity analysis of the variables are shown in Table 2. Internal consistency as measured by Cronbach’s alpha for all the variables ranges from 0.645 to 0.903. Since it is an exploratory study, we accepted an alpha value of more than 0.6. Convergent validity is evaluated by measuring the correlation of each item representing the variable with the aggregate measure of the variable less the than focal item (Grover et al., 1996; Kerlinger, 2000). This approach assumes that the total score is valid; thus, the extent to which the item correlates with the total score is indicative of the validity of the item. An item value less than 0.333 is deleted; however, for defining the variable BPO outcome an item value of 0.3 is accepted on the basis of the exploratory nature of this study. Discriminant validity is the degree to which a variable differs from other variables, and it is usually verified through factor analysis. Factor analysis for discriminant validity was performed with respect to each domain of the research model. In each case, discriminant validity was confirmed when items for each variable loaded on to a single factor with loadings of greater than 0.50. This excludes, however, one item of BPO outcome variable with a factor loading of 0.356; this item is the same as mentioned earlier for convergent validity.

Table 3 shows the correlation between the various variables. The correlation is primarily concerned with finding out the extent to which two variables are correlated. It also determines the magnitude and direction of the same.

The results show that a successful relationship is highly correlated with BPO outcome, with a correlation coefficient of 0.642, and correlation is significant at the 0.01 level (2-tailed). The BPO outcome is highly correlated with business process management competence and the IT management competence of the service provider, with a correlation coefficient of 0.543 and 0.467, respectively, and correlation is significant also at the 0.01 level (2-tailed). The BPO outcome and the competence of the client, that is, outsourcing management competence, are moderately correlated, with a correlation coefficient of 0.303, and correlation is significant at the 0.05 level (2-tailed). Hence we carried out simple regression between these variables. The results of this simple regression are shown in Table 4.

For Research H1, the R-square value is 0.413 when BPO outcome was regressed against the successful relationship. Thus BPO outcome can explain 41.3% of the variance in the dependent variable successful relationship. The computed F statistic is 43.542, with an observed significance level of less than 0.001. Hence we confer that there exists a linear relationship between the independent variable BPO outcome and dependent variable successful relationship. Also beta score ¼ 0.809 (t ¼ 6.599, Po0.001) is positive and statistically significant. Thus higher BPO outcome would lead to successful relationship. For Research H4 R-square value is 0.175 (F ¼ 12.060, Po0.001). This indicates that though the independent variable outsourcing management competence of the client is significant for a successful relationship, it can only explain 17.5% of the variance in the dependent variable successful relationship. Research hypotheses H2 and H3 could not be proved with the given data.

## Conclusions, limitations, and suggestions

Business process outsourcing involves continuous decision making throughout the contract life cycle of outsourcing. During the pre-contracting phase decision making includes: to outsource or not, the choice of the service provider, and duration of contract. During the post-contract period, a client company has to monitor the service provider’s performance continuously and decide on rewards and/ or punishment. The client company should also decide whether to extend the outsourcing contract for another period, and whether to enhance the scope of work. In the offshore outsourcing environment the risk exposure to the client company often is enhanced (Shi, 2007). During the post-contract period, when a client company decides to continue with the same service provider and renew the contract and/or enhance the scope of work, the indications are towards building successful relationships. Dibbern et al. (2004) point out that this is an area of research where gaps in knowledge of outsourcing exist and where further research is needed.

Thus understanding of various issues is valuable for the practitioners responsible for managing outsourcing relationships with clients as well as service providers. Building a successful relationship results in a ‘win-win relationship’ between the service provider and the client. Thus a ‘win-win relationship’ also emerges as a zero risk outsourcing philosophy. The causes of a failed relationship explored by Shi (2007) relate to knowledge transfer difficulties and process calibration difficulties, apart from lack of process maturity in the client and a competence gap at the vendor’s end. The results of our exploratory study indicate that only delivery of the intended BPO outcome would contribute to building successful relationship. There is a clear indication that if the service provider delivers the intended BPO outcome, a successful relationship can be built. However, this research does not throw much light on the competencies of the service provider and the client for building a successful relationship. There is weaker support from the client’s perspective for the same.

Prior to discussing the implication of these findings, it is appropriate to discuss the limitations of this study. Even though scrupulous procedures were followed to develop and test the measures, it should be stressed that this is an initial step only. While we believe the items presented here are suitable for testing the variables, there could be others as well, which may qualify as additional items to describe the variables.

The sample size of 65 is relatively smaller than we would prefer, making the generalizability of our findings in the broader BPO domain weaker than ideal. We have confined our claims in the present work to those of an exploratory study and would like to explore the model with more data in future.

In many outsourcing contexts such as service outsourcing (e.g. janitorial services outsourcing, food service outsourcing), contract duration tends to be shorter and relationships tend to be functional (Goo et al., 2007). Goo et al. have further identified knowledge acquisition, relationship-specific investment, and the extent of substitution by the vendor as related factors, which have a positive influence on ITO duration. However, in the BPO context, when business process management competence was studied, which includes the process knowledge discovery and acquisition by the vendor, the results were inconclusive. Hence we have gone back to the process classification.

The outsourced processes are low both in criticality and complexity; thus the primary objective of outsourcing is the cost arbitration rather than capability sourcing. In other words, most of the processes outsourced by majority of the clients are of support or non-core type. These fall under the category of transactional BPOs, where the value proposition is transactional in nature and outcome delivery is cost efficiency, which can be achieved relatively easily. Such BPO outcome delivery can be monitored easily by the client through service-level agreements. Client companies then need not go beyond that for probing and understanding the service provider’s competencies.

Such processes can be transitioned from one service provider to another (in the same or another low-cost destination country) without incurring much switching costs. Thus the client’s expectations of building a longterm relationship with the service provider are superfluous when the fact is that the client can switch the service provider without incurring much cost. This in fact may further reduce the cost.

Clients hedge themselves against outsourcing risks by adopting a portfolio approach to outsourcing. This is seen from the cases of clients who are largely successful in their BPO initiatives, such as Barclays Bank, ABN-AMRO Bank, Cendant Corporation, Elsevier, who use a ‘process portfolio’ approach for managing their business processes. This also allows them to move their processes from one service provider to another if BPO outcome does not seem to be realized. Similarly, it is also clear that many service-provider organizations have very strong formal process transition/migration methodologies, which enable them to migrate most of the processes outsourced to them successfully.

Thus until the outsourced processes are high on criticality and /or complexity, the competence of the service provider would not play a visible role in delivering a successful BPO outcome. However, to meet the ever increasing expectations of the client once cost efficiencies have been achieved, the service provider has to differentiate as well as innovate his service delivery to maintain the revenue growth, either through more business from the same clients (often called farming), or business from new clients (often called hunting), or both hunting (new business development) and farming (sustaining existing clients). In all scenarios, the service providers can no longer compete on cost efficiencies alone. They have to compete on their capabilities. This has to be appreciated by their clients if they plan transformational or strategic BPO.

## Suggestions for future research

Modifications to our competence model to incorporate process types are feasible. If the processes are support or peripheral in nature, delivery of the intended BPO outcome is a sufficient condition in building a successful relationship. For other categories of processes and related intended benefits, the capability of the service provider would probably play a crucial role.

This research opens up several potential avenues for future research. The focus of this research is BPO from the client perspective. The data were collected from European clients, but no data were compiled from BPO service providers. The authors are separately carrying out research with a similar framework with service providers in India. The results of such a study would complement the framework used in this research.
