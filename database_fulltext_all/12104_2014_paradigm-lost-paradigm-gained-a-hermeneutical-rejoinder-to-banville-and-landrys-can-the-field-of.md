RESEARCH ESSAY

# Paradigm lost … paradigm gained: a hermeneutical rejoinder to Banville and Landry’s ‘Can the Field of MIS be Disciplined?’

Nik R. Hassan

University of Minnesota Duluth, Duluth, MN, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Nik R. Hassan, University of Minnesota Duluth, 1318 Kirby Drive, LSBE 335Q, Duluth, MN, 55812, U.S.A. Tel: +218 726-7453 Fax: +218 726-7516; E-mail: nhassan@d.umn.edu

## Abstract

This study offers an alternative interpretation to Banville and Landry’s (B&L, 1989) Can the Field of MIS Be Disciplined?, the canonical text that argued persuasively against the adoption of the Kuhnian view of scientific progress for the information systems (IS) field. Much has transpired in the quarter of a century since its publication, which provides us with new sources of understanding about paradigms and how they relate to the challenges faced by the IS field. On the basis of the hermeneutical principles of tradition, prejudice, temporal distance, history of effect and application, this study describes the context from which B&L was written, its dependence on Whitley’s (1984) The Intellectual and Social Organization of the Sciences, and examines several of its claims and assertions. In contrast to B&L, this study finds the Kuhnian model of scientific progress well suited for a multidisciplinary and pluralistic field like IS and concludes with guidelines on how to reclaim the more transformative aspects of the paradigm concept, engender a culture of contextual borrowing from reference disciplines, and encourage conceptual development and autonomous theory construction. European Journal of Information Systems advance online publication, 15 October 2013; doi:10.1057/ejis.2013.29

Keywords: information systems (IS) disciplinarity; IS theory; IS philosophy; hermeneutics; Kuhnian paradigms and normal science; sociology of science and knowledge.

## Introduction

Questions surrounding the disciplinary status of the information systems (IS) <sup>fi</sup>eld have been asked since its inception (Ackoff, 1967; Dearden, 1972; Keen, 1980; Bjorn-Andersen, 1985; Wand & Weber, 1986; Mumford, 1991) and the status of the <sup>fi</sup>eld continues to be a major concern for the IS community (Mingers & Stowell, 1997; Introna, 2003; Adam & Fitzgerald, 2000; Paul, 2002; Avison, 2003; Robey, 2003; Lange, 2005a, b; Klein & Hirschheim, 2006, 2008; Hassan, 2011). These questions are complex and range from whether the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is capable of being relevant and in<sup>fl</sup>uential (Keen, 1991; Hirschheim et al, 1996; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999), how it relates to other <sup>fi</sup>elds of study (Baskerville & Myers, 2002; Grover et al, 2006; Wade et al, 2006), what forms the basis of its identity and legitimacy (Benbasat & Zmud, 2003), whether or not it has a ‘core’ (Alter, 2003; Galliers, 2003; Gray, 2003; Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Lyytinen & King, 2004; Weber, 2006), which research paradigms are most suited for IS (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1981; Goles & Hirschheim, 2000; Mingers, 2004) to the need for a distinctive body of knowledge (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003; Iivari et al, 2004). It is not within the scope of this study to address all these issues. The goals of this study is more modest and limited only to the question of the application of the

Kuhnian paradigm to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, as articulated by Banville & Landry (1989) in the landmark article, ‘Can the Field of MIS Be Disciplined?’ (henceforth B&L).

A long list of research-on-research studies followed in the wake of B&L (Introna, 2003) re<sup>fl</sup>ecting its considerable in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Any number of these researchon-research studies could have been chosen for analysing how the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s view of the Kuhnian paradigm impacts its disciplinarity. B&L is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally chosen because: (1) it is among the earliest works that address the issue of disciplinary paradigms in such a comprehensive manner; (2) it is among the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential articles on IS disciplinary paradigms; (3) its characterization of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld as a ‘fragmented adhocracy’ has become a received view of the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s disciplinary state; and (4) although many have addressed the general question of IS disciplinarity, none has analysed B&L’s claims and assertions themselves. Focusing on B&L speci<sup>fi</sup>cally provides a concrete way of analysing not just the epistemological questions that are raised by B&L’s claims, but also the political, social, and institutional arrangements that factor into the establishment of its truth regime (Introna, 2003). The results from this analysis will have implications on the broader discourse surrounding the disciplinarity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

This study begins by recognizing the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of B&L on the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and its characterization of the Kuhnian paradigm. B&L’s characterization is then contrasted against well-known criticisms of Kuhnian theories on the progress of science. The comparison highlights major gaps between B&L’s assertions and what Kuhn and his critics actually say about his theories. To understand how B&L reached such differing conclusions, the study draws from Gadamerian hermeneutics to uncover its productive and negative prejudices, and fuse the differing horizons of the traditions behind B&L’s assertions that culminate in its particularistic view of the Kuhnian paradigm. The analysis suggests that instead of helping the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld progress, the legacy left behind by this received view is holding the <sup>fi</sup>eld back. After reiterating why the Kuhnian paradigm is well suited for a pluralistic <sup>fi</sup>eld like IS, the study concludes with several recommendations on how to reclaim the more transformative aspects of the paradigm concept that have helped many other pluralistic and interdisciplinary <sup>fi</sup>elds progress.

## The influence and contributions of B&L

For decades since its publication, B&L engaged the attention of IS researchers concerned about the progress of their <sup>fi</sup>eld. As required reading in doctoral IS programmes, it occupies a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant position as a historical reference on the disciplinary nature of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Google Scholar reports over 380 publications citing B&L, making it a cited classic article in IS. Although it is ranked 307 in the Communications of the ACM according to Publish or Perish (Harzing, 2007), it is the second-highest ranked article in the research-on-research genre after Attewell and Rule (1984). On the basis of a survey of the top 500 most cited articles in the Communications of the ACM, which are primarily computer science-related articles, there are only 40 IS-related articles, in which B&L ranked 16th. In an era with few research-on-research articles about IS, the publication of B&L at the end of the early developmental phases of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld exerted considerable in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the thinking of the time.

Before its publication, IS authors were busy de<sup>fi</sup>ning the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Kennevan, 1970; Emery, 1973; Davis, 1974), developing frameworks (Blumenthal, 1969; Gorry & Scott Morton, 1971; Mason & Mitroff, 1973; Zachman, 1978; Ives et al, 1980; Nolan & Wetherbe, 1980), searching for paradigms (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1981; Galliers, 1985; Wand & Weber, 1986; Weber, 1987; van Gigch & Le Moigne, 1989), and responding to Keen’s (1980) exhortation for the <sup>fi</sup>eld to develop a cumulative tradition (Culnan, 1986; Culnan & Swanson, 1986). B&L arrived on the scene with a highly developed series of arguments that not only addressed these concerns, but also coloured much of the discussions that followed. Like many other <sup>fi</sup>elds of study, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld sought inspiration from Kuhnian thought to understand how the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld was supposed to progress. Moreover, like many other <sup>fi</sup>elds of study, the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld interprets Kuhn through its collective efforts in its own way. One of the goals of this study is to examine such interpretations of Kuhn within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

Evidence of B&L’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence comes from two sources: (1) spillover effects from Kuhn’s critics into the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and, (2) evidence from articles and publications within the research-on-research genre of the IS community. Regarding the former, interpretations of Kuhn were extraordinarily varied (Hoyningen-Huene, 1993), in part engendered by his expository style and by his own later attempts to explain the misunderstandings and distortion of his most famous work, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (henceforth Structure). Traces of these spillover effects into the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld can be seen in the preface to Ein-Dor & Segev’s (1981, p. vii) A Paradigm for Management Information Systems.

It is with some trepidation that we adopted the use of the word ‘paradigm’ to describe our work … Our trepidations increased when one of our colleagues let it be known that he ‘wouldn’t be caught dead using the word’ – a re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the recent disrepute into which this word has fallen.

Most ‘research-on-research’ studies in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld concur with the opinions of B&L and collectively reinforce certain interpretations that have since become the received view. Many agree with B&L that the ‘establishment of an MIS paradigm could actually restrict, rather than enhance, the progress of MIS as a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discipline’ (Cushing, 1990, p. 48). These claims saying that the Kuhnian model is monistic, too restrictive, and hands over IS to the ‘rigour’ and ‘objectivity’ of a hard science like physics are repeatedly emphasized in many IS studies (Klein et al, 1991; Hirschheim et al, 1996; Avison, 1997; Jones, 1997; Khazanchi & Munkvold, 2000; Mingers, 2001).

At the same time, B&L contributed critical insights to the discussions concerning the progress of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. It highlighted the then growing preoccupation within mainstream IS research towards constructing the ‘perfect’ IS framework or discovering the single grand paradigm that would de<sup>fi</sup>ne the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. The article also drew the attention of the IS community to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the Kuhnian model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress, albeit with its own particular interpretation. B&L emphasized not only the need to understand the importance of the disciplinary and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c status of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, but more importantly, how the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is to realize its disciplinary and scholarly goals. Should the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld emulate the pattern of progress shown by the natural sciences or is it closer to the more pluralistic social sciences? B&L categorically favours the latter and argues against the imposition of any natural science paradigm over the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. B&L’s proposal towards plurality and to ‘let the <sup>fl</sup>owers bloom’ was enthusiastically embraced by leaders within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Klein et al, 1991; Mingers, 2001; Kaplan et al, 2004). Despite its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and wide in<sup>fl</sup>uence, very little has actually been written about its claims and assertions. A detailed exegesis of B&L is therefore instructive and useful in informing our understanding of disciplinary paradigms in IS, and how its legacy has impacted and will impact the future of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## The controversies surrounding the Kuhnian model: will the real Kuhn please stand up!

Before Kuhn’s Structure, science was believed to be a logical and rational enterprise. Underpinning all sciences was the Baconian and positivist belief of a value-free process of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and repudiation that made scienti<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>ndings objective. Kuhn’s penetrating analysis in Structure turned this belief upside down and threatened the foundations of the authority of the sciences by suggesting that science is nothing more than what scientists do. For Kuhn, scientists perform and evaluate research in reference to rules, exemplars, and models that are acquired during their training. This conduct of ‘normal science’ re<sup>fl</sup>ected the social practice of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c work, which, although carried the progress of science forward, often obscured the scientists from seeing beyond their existing world views. Extraordinary scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discoveries require changes or ‘paradigm shifts’ to existing world views that resembled ‘revolutions’ in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress.

Kuhn injected a sociological perspective into the logical view of science implying that even the most rigourous natural scientist is merely part of a community that is equally open to ideological strife and controversy. Consequently, not only was scienti<sup>fi</sup>c objectivity put on trial and exposed to a level of scrutiny it did not experience previously, Kuhn’s work suggested that any activity could claim scienti<sup>fi</sup>c status as long as it is coherent within its own world view. The notions of differing paradigms and world views described in Structure were received with a sigh of relief especially by social scientists who had struggled for decades under the hegemony of the positivist ‘hard sciences’ and were therefore among the earliest to welcome the ideas propounded by Structure (Fuller, 2000). As Masterman (1970) puts it, ‘we are not going to be able to go back to where we were before Kuhn’ (p. 87). These Kuhnian notions are summarized in Table 1.

Kuhn’s theory was given so many varied interpretations, and in some cases outright distortions such that Kuhn (1970a) himself felt that there were two Thomas Kuhns: the Kuhn replying to all his critics and the Kuhn who <sup>fi</sup>rst wrote Structure in 1962. Among the earliest to disagree with Kuhn was the ‘realist’ camp, represented by Karl Popper, that views reality as existing independent of observation, not as a world view. Popper (1970) considers Kuhn a ‘relativist’ (despite his protests) for saying that different frameworks and world views can never be compared unless ‘we have agreed on fundamentals’ (p. 56). This difference in perceptions of reality creates a kind of ‘incommensurability’ between groups each founded on their own world views. Toulmin (1970) disagrees with the use of the term ‘revolution’ in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress and instead describes the progress of science in terms of a conceptual evolution instead of a revolution of human understanding. Many critics disagree with Kuhn’s notion of ‘normal science’ claiming that it either does not exist or at least demeans the integrity of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c method (Watkins, 1970).

Despite their criticism, as Bernstein (1983) insightfully explains, there is a lot of common ground between Kuhn and his critics. A deeper reading of Kuhn’s critics shows that they agree with Kuhn more than they care to admit. For example, although Toulmin (1970) disagrees with Kuhn’s use of the term ‘revolution’, he is in agreement with Kuhn’s concept of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community as ‘men uniquely responsible for the pursuit of a set of shared goals, including the training of their successors’ (Kuhn, 1970b, p. 177). Most of Kuhn’s critics focus on four major elements of his model (see Table 2): (1) his purported relativism, (2) the vague nature of the paradigm, (3) the banal state of normal science and, (3) the magnitude of the ‘revolution’ or change in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress.

While B&L agrees with a few of the criticisms of Kuhn, in particular, the vague nature of the paradigm and the banality of normal science, it departs sharply from the others and offers its own particularistic and rather odd interpretation. While Kuhn’s critics consider his theories relativistic, B&L considers them monistic. Moreover, while most scholars acknowledge Kuhn’s sociological contributions, B&L claims Kuhn’s model is sociologically inadequate.

Combining well-known criticisms of Kuhn with its own peculiar views, B&L offers the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld a persuasive criticism of Kuhn that can be summarized into three main points, culminating in a rejection of Kuhn and a recommendation for a model of progress based on Whitley’s (1984) The Intellectual and Social Organization of the Sciences. First, B&L claims that Kuhn’s model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress is monistic and restrictive, based on a concept – the paradigm – that is vague and ambiguous. Second, B&L claims that the Kuhnian model imposes a natural or hard science model, the model of physics, which is unsuitable for IS. Third, B&L views the Kuhnian model as being epistemological and rational, therefore deterministic and lacking important sociological perspectives that are so crucial to a correct understanding of the sociology of science.

## A critique of B&L’s assertions

B&L bases part of its argument for the <sup>fi</sup>rst point on Kuhn’s (1970b, p. 19) statement: ‘The new paradigm implies a new and more rigid de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the <sup>fi</sup>eld. Those unwilling or unable to accommodate their work to it must proceed in isolation or attach themselves to some other group’ (cited in B&L, p. 49). Admittedly, Kuhn’s reference to rigidity contributed to this interpretation, implying as a result that (1) the IS community needs to choose a single paradigm from existing competing paradigms, and (2) only a single paradigm will triumph and hold sway over the other schools and paradigms, perhaps unjustly so. Because the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is known to be made up of several sub<sup>fi</sup>elds, B&L convincingly argue, restricting it to any one paradigm or attempting to impose a grand uni<sup>fi</sup>ed theory is not only misplaced but futile.

This argument against monism hinges on the assumption that Kuhn does support a rigid single paradigm requirement. A closer reading of Structure suggests otherwise. Although Kuhn (1970b) insists on the importance of reaching that ‘<sup>fi</sup>rst’ paradigm, he makes it clear that the view of one total dominating paradigm is not the norm:

What has been said so far may have seemed to imply that normal science is a single monolithic and uni<sup>fi</sup>ed enterprise that must stand or fall with any one of its paradigms as well as with all of them together. But science is obviously seldom or never like that. (p. 49).

In the postscript of Structure, Kuhn asks ‘What do its members share that accounts for the relative fullness of their professional communication and the relative unanimity of their professional judgment? … the answer, a paradigm or set of paradigms’ (p. 182, emphasis added).

The assumption that the Kuhnian model requires the IS community to choose one paradigm from existing ones takes a generous amount of space in B&L’s argument against Kuhn. After emphasizing the pluralistic nature of IS, B&L predicts that by following Kuhn ‘[E]ach researcher would bring along concepts and methods from his background discipline and, often, would continue doing research which is closely related to it’ (p. 49) and:

[I]t seems dubious that we could end up with a paradigm that could include the different approaches currently found in the MIS <sup>fi</sup>eld. A large number of MIS members would face the choice of either leaving or converting (in its religious sense) to the emergent paradigm. (p. 49)

B&L’s conclusion is founded on the assumption multidisciplinary <sup>fi</sup>elds like MIS can only progress by adopting the paradigm of one its referent disciplines, which according to B&L, will result in a ‘break-up of the <sup>fi</sup>eld into rather hermetic factions and the consequent loss of the creativity generated by exchanges about research topics and research methods’ (p. 51). Kuhn had anticipated such a misconstruction and addressed it in the postscript of Structure. As Kuhn (1970b) emphasizes, the emergence of a paradigm is not the result of one subject matter overpowering another, or one competing school decimating the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of another, but rather, several threads of thought or discourses synthesizing and providing solutions to relevant problems. This synthesizing process offers solutions to the whole community, not just for certain parties at the expense of others. Kuhn (1970b) provides a few examples of this process of synthesis:

When, in the development … an individual or group <sup>fi</sup>rst produces a synthesis able to attract most of the next generation’s practitioners (p. 18) … discovery involves an extended, though not necessarily long, process of conceptual assimilation (p. 56) … chemical phenomena exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed laws different from those that emerged with the assimilation of Dalton’s new paradigm … Dalton was neither a chemist nor interested in chemistry. Instead he was a meteorologist investigating the, for him, physical problems of the absorption of gases by water … he approached these problems with a paradigm different from that of contemporary chemists (pp. 132–133) … There was, for example, no physics community before the midnineteenth century, and it was then formed by the merger of parts of two previously separate communities, mathematics and natural philosophy (p. 179, all emphases added).

Contrary to B&L’s assertions and what is perhaps understood by the IS community, Kuhn’s model is not against pluralism or encouraging ‘letting the <sup>fl</sup>owers bloom’. Pluralism was and has been a major theme of the Kuhnian model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress. As Hirschheim & Klein (2003, p. 247) explain, there is a difference between a <sup>fi</sup>eld becoming fragmented and being pluralistic.

Pluralism – for us relates to diversity of ideas, perspectives, research approaches, paradigms, etc. But there is at least some underlying core set of knowledge or beliefs that all in the <sup>fi</sup>eld share. There is a sense of shared belonging and empathy to others in the <sup>fi</sup>eld. This is different from fragmentation.

A more nuanced elaboration of this process of disciplinary progress and synthesis, which is consistent with Kuhn’s, is offered by postmodern philosopher Foucault (1970, 1972) in his differing structures of knowledge: (1) the episteme, or form of knowledge, (2) the positivity, or characteristic of discourse, and (3) the theme, or untested theory. At the very abstract level, the episteme of a culture or epoch in history is analogous to Kuhn’s description of paradigms as world views. Foucault’s positivity is analogous to Kuhn’s description of paradigms in the sense of recognized scienti<sup>fi</sup>c and intellectual achievements. Finally, within positivities, it is possible to settle on ‘themes’ or certain hypotheses as a result of some observables and structures such that we can point to vitalist themes (e.g., life force, pneuma, epigenesis) in biology, or mercantilist themes (e.g., bullionism, zerosum economics, intrinsic value of money) in economics. Foucault’s highly nuanced descriptions of episteme, positivities and themes demonstrate that embracing the Kuhnian understanding of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress is not opposed to pluralism, but rather, encourages it.

On B&L’s second main point it argues that IS researchers ‘should not long for a paradigm as it rests upon assumptions of the Kuhnian model of science which imparts value to knowledge on the basis of the conformity of its methods and results to an explicit standard: physics’ (p. 50). Owing to the many references to physics in Structure, it is no surprise that Kuhn’s critics make this claim; and Kuhn (1970b, p. 160) himself anticipated how this model of progress could be misconstrued to favour certain disciplines like physics:

Nowhere does this show more clearly than in the recurrent debates about whether one or another of the contemporary social sciences is really a science … Men argue that psychology, for example, is a science because it possesses such and such characteristics. Others counter that those characteristics are either unnecessary or not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to make a <sup>fi</sup>eld a science. Often great energy is invested, great passion aroused, and the outsider is at a loss to know why. Can very much depend upon a de<sup>fi</sup>nition of ‘science’? Can a de<sup>fi</sup>nition tell a man whether he is a scientist or not? … Probably questions like the following are really being asked: Why does my <sup>fi</sup>eld fail to move ahead in the way that, say, physics does? What changes in technique or method or ideology would enable it to do so? These are not, however, questions that could respond to an agreement on de<sup>fi</sup>nition.

As this passage explains, it was not Kuhn’s intent to impose a physics model to his own nor will any de<sup>fi</sup>nition of science be able to rescue the <sup>fi</sup>eld concerned from its disciplinary problems. What does matter is whether or not the <sup>fi</sup>eld has made progress, regardless of whether it is physics, psychology, the arts or philosophy (pp. 161–163).

As far as Kuhn is concerned, focus should be placed on whether or not (1) the research community agrees on its <sup>fi</sup>rst principles, and (2) as a result of this level of agreement, the <sup>fi</sup>eld will demonstrate progress in the form of discoveries, solving of problems and the invention of new concepts and theories. Incidentally, consensual agreement on <sup>fi</sup>rst principles is arguably not the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld’s forte. Even the most basic of concepts in IS, such as that of ‘information’, ‘systems’, and ‘use’ have found little agreement community wide (Boland, 1987; Mingers, 1995; Falkenberg et al, 2000; Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006), and with this lack of agreement, as Kuhn predicts, progress will continue to remain elusive.

In many ways, some of B&L’s claims are not inconsistent with Kuhn’s view of progress. For example, citing Chalmers (1998), B&L asserts that progress should be judged on its merits by evaluating each <sup>fi</sup>eld’s aims and goals. Kuhn (1970b) concurs with this assessment and says that ‘part of the answer to the problem of progress lies simply in the eye of the beholder’ (p. 163). The other part of the answer lies in the community’s agreement of <sup>fi</sup>rst principles, which frees them from the distracting tasks of constantly re-examining them and needing to respond to questions from the laity, thereby freeing the community to be more effective and more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in solving new problems. This state of being effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient is what Kuhn refers to as the state of ‘normal science’. A practical manifestation of this progress can be seen in the standard textbooks of mature <sup>fi</sup>elds, which contain the canons of the <sup>fi</sup>eld that are critical to the apprentice interested in qualifying as a member of that intellectual community.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal point that deserves examination in B&L is the notion that Kuhn’s model is rationalistic, cognitive and epistemological and lacks the sociological dimension of the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community. In introducing Whitley’s (1984) sociological model, B&L suggests Kuhn himself admits to this inadequacy: ‘If this book were being rewritten [B&L’s emphasis], it would … open with a discussion of the community structure of science’ (Kuhn, 1970b, p. 176). They support their claim with Kuhn’s (1977, p. 296) writings in The Essential Tension citing: ‘To him, A scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community consists … of the practitioners of a scienti<sup>fi</sup>c specialty’ and ‘is a factual observation, not an a priori determination’ (Banville & Landry, 1989, p. 52, original emphasis). B&L’s observation is odd given that on the same page in Structure, Kuhn (1970b) writes ‘Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c communities can and should be isolated without prior recourse to paradigms; the latter can then be discovered by scrutinizing the behaviour of a given community’s members’ (p. 176), alluding to the a priori emphasis Kuhn places on the sociological dimension of paradigms.

The sociological dimension is so important to Kuhn that in The Essential Tension, Kuhn (1977, p. 294) practically equates paradigms with communities: ‘In the book [referring to Structure] the term “paradigm” enters in close proximity, both physical and logical, to the phrase “scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community” (pp. 10–11). A paradigm is what the members of a scientific community, and they alone, share’ (emphasis added). If anything, Kuhn’s Structure actually supports B&L’s claims that scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress is largely sociological rather than epistemologically rational. B&L’s renunciations are better suited towards Popperian views rather than Kuhn’s, because it was Popper (1959) who insisted on the logical and epistemological source for scienti<sup>fi</sup>c discoveries. In fact, an early review of Kuhn’s work states: ‘Kuhn’s account of science is, then, as both he and his critics recognize, intrinsically sociological’ (Perry, 1977, p. 40).

Kuhn’s sociological emphases were so signi<sup>fi</sup>cant that many credit him for inspiring their own studies in the sociology of science and knowledge. King (1971), emphasizing how Kuhn resisted the rationalization of science, traced the convergence of thought in the sociology of science between Merton (1977) and Kuhn (1970b). Berger & Luckmann (1966) credited Kuhn for inspiring their understanding of the social construction of reality. Ritzer (1980), cited by B&L to support their claim that Kuhn’s model is restrictive, is indebted to Kuhn for his multiparadigm perspective of sociology. The works of these scholars repudiate B&L’s claim saying Kuhn lacks the sociological dimension.

These three main points in B&L demonstrate glaring disconnections with what Kuhn wrote in Structure, prompting the following questions: How did B&L arrive at these interpretations and what can we understand from them? Why did B&L say that the Kuhnian paradigm is monistic, vague, and therefore restrictive when Kuhn himself repudiates it? Why did B&L insist that Kuhn only view progress through the template of physics when Kuhn welcomes progress for the arts and humanities? Why did B&L view the Kuhnian model as bereft of any sociological dimensions when other scholars acknowledge its sociological contributions? Given B&L’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence, what legacy has it left the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, if any? What conclusions can be drawn for the future of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld? These questions are the target of hermeneutical analysis in the foregoing sections.

## Gadamer’s hermeneutical philosophy

In order to address the questions raised earlier about the disconnections in B&L and what Kuhn wrote in Structure, we turn to hermeneutics, in particular Gadamerian hermeneutical philosophy. Gadamerian hermeneutics is chosen for various reasons: (1) it is less conservative and objectivistic than the versions of (Palmer, 1969) and Hirsch (1967), (2) it is an advancement over the attempts of Schleiermacher’s (Palmer, 1969) universalistic dialectics and Dilthey’s (1883/1989) efforts to <sup>fi</sup>nd a human science based on hermeneutics, (3) the elements of Gadamer’s interpretive methods are retained in postmodern versions of hermeneutics of Ricoeur and Merleau-Ponty (Madison, 1988), (4) it contributes to the modest list of mainstream hermeneutic research in IS (Lee, 1994; Butler, 1998; Klein & Myers, 1999; Cole & Avison, 2007; Lee & Dennis, 2012), and most importantly (5) the ontological foundations of Gadamer’s hermeneutics allow us to infer the impact of B&L on the state of IS research. As B&L offers an understanding of the disciplinarity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, a hermeneutical examination of B&L therefore includes a hermeneutics of disciplinarity itself. Students reading B&L, or any other in<sup>fl</sup>uential text, have not only produced within themselves the possibilities of the future of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, they tie their own history and experiences with the history of the <sup>fi</sup>eld and are actively forming a mode of being for themselves and for the <sup>fi</sup>eld in the present.

Given B&L’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and its impact on the future mode of being of the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s scholars and apprentices, examining the legitimacy, the ‘origin and validity of the fore-meanings’ dwelling within B&L becomes critical (Gadamer, 1975, p. 270). Following the exemplars set by Lee (1994), Butler (1998) and, Klein & Myers (1999), this study applies Gadamer’s (1975) Heideggerian existential grounding of the hermeneutic circle, de<sup>fi</sup>ned in terms of his principles of tradition, prejudice, temporal distance, history of effect (fusion of horizons), and application. The interpretative circle begins when the interpreter projects onto the text as a whole based on some initial emerging meaning or ‘fore-meaning’. These foremeanings are what need to be worked out in order for the meaning of the text to fully develop. Some of these foremeanings come from tradition – elements handed down in the form of writings, beliefs, propositions, teachings, and customs, often from some form of authority. Unlike the natural sciences, hermeneutics considers them, not an antithesis to objective understanding, but as part of human life and, as one of the objects of human science, should be embraced and applied to direct understanding.

For Gadamer (1975), the goal of hermeneutics is not necessarily to ‘objectively’ validate the text as is the case with Betti and Hirsch’s versions (Palmer, 1969), it is to fully realize understanding as it ‘distinguish[es] true prejudices, by which we understand, from the false ones, by which we misunderstand’ (Gadamer, 1975, p. 298 original emphases). This method allows for a plurality of understanding, all of which may be correct, and at the same time protects the interpretation from arbitrary, incorrect relativistic interpretations of the text (Palmer, 1969; Weberman, 2000). Understanding is fully realized when the fore-meanings of both the text and the interpreter are worked out. These foremeanings take the form of prejudices, which are often mistaken to mean false judgements. Prejudices are not to be understood as judgements that are biased, they are just judgements to be studied as conditions for understanding. Gadamer’s (1975) hermeneutics puts them into full play in order to experience the other’s claim to truth. Hermeneutics asks how ‘productive prejudices’ (p. 295) can be separated from the ones that lead to misunderstanding by foregrounding these prejudices.

However, foregrounding prejudice is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult while it is operating. This is where temporal and historical distance between the text and the context of the interpreter comes to the rescue. This temporal distance, which Lee (1994) calls ‘distanciation’, was assumed by historical studies to have offered closure that enabled a more objective knowledge of what transpired. Instead, Gadamer (1975) argues, the discovery of true meaning is never <sup>fi</sup>nished and temporal distance allows for new sources of understanding, pushes out sources of error and lets the true meaning emerge fully. Such an emergence is the result of the hermeneutic circle, which is neither subjective nor objective; rather, it describes the understanding as the interplay of the movement of tradition and the movement of the interpreter (Gadamer, 1975, p. 293). The positive and productive conditions for understanding the text from this temporal distance allows interpreters to interpret the text in their own way and according to their own age. Hence, in light of the rapid changes overtaking the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, its continued fragmentation for a quarter of a century since B&L’s publication, and the many transformations in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that have transpired during that time, the principle of temporal distance broadens the range of vision available to us, opens up new avenues of understanding, and helps resolve misunderstandings, and separates the productive from the false prejudices (Gadamer, 1975, pp. 296–297).

By asking the right questions and including all possible alternatives, what Gadamer (1975) calls the ‘horizon’ or range of vision (p. 301), this combining of the past and the present into a living situation will result in ‘fusing the horizons’ and uncovering the tension between the text and the present. The task of hermeneutics is to expose this tension, by being aware of it, by projecting the historical horizon and superseding it using the process of ‘application’. The principle of application is about applying the text to be understood to our present situation. Interpretation, understanding, and application together comprise one uni<sup>fi</sup>ed process. Therefore understanding is always application. ‘[T]he text, if it is to be understood properly – i.e., according to the claim it makes – must be understood at every moment, in every concrete situation, in a new and different way’ (Gadamer, 1975, pp. 307–308).

## The hermeneutics of B&L

The critique of B&L in the earlier section raises questions about B&L as a whole and highlights several disconnections between their assertions and Kuhn’s model. The task of hermeneutics is to understand how these disconnections came about. As Butler (1998, p. 289) notes ‘Prejudices need to be isolated and their validity needs to be suspended’. What sources of validity conditioned B&L to reach these conclusions? After uncovering the fore-meanings and prejudices, the hermeneutical process of application highlights, for each of the three major disconnections, their impacts and collective legacy on the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## Why B&L says the paradigm is vague and restrictive – the legacy of throwing the paradigm baby with the bath water

The vague and monistic nature of the paradigm, the most well-known and not surprisingly, the most criticized (Shapere, 1964, 1971), feature prominently in B&L’s rejection of the Kuhnian model and becomes the basis for B&L’s later arguments. B&L’s language clearly suggests Kuhn perhaps quibbling with or retracting from one of his major concepts (‘paradigms as dogmas … revolution … exemplars … evolution’; p. 49). Indeed, Kuhn admitted his dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in de<sup>fi</sup>ning the term (Kuhn, 1977; Kuhn et al, 2000); however, as Foucault’s (1970; 1972) elaboration of disciplinary progress suggests, the notion of a paradigm, regardless of how it was coined, is nevertheless instructive and useful. This productive prejudice of B&L is supported by most of Kuhn’s critics, but obscures several subtle prejudices that are uniquely B&L’s.

According to B&L, Toulmin (1972) presents ‘<sup>fi</sup>ve distinguishable phases’ (p. 49) in Kuhn’s use of this term between 1957 and 1970 implying that Kuhn struggled with <sup>fi</sup>ve different meanings to the term. There is an important difference between saying that the term ‘revolution’ went through five different phases, which is what Toulmin (1972) meant, and saying that it was the term ‘paradigm’ that went through those changes. What Toulmin (1972) criticized is Kuhn’s concept of revolution, not his concept of paradigm. Toulmin’s (1972) contention was not about the multifarious nature of the term ‘paradigm’ but rather about Kuhn’s thesis that science experiences abrupt, discontinuous, or ‘revolutionary’ transformations as an essential part of its progress. Toulmin (1972) made it clear that ‘[T]he two key notions in Kuhn’s account – ‘paradigm’ and ‘revolution’ – are in fact separate and independent’ (p. 106). The notion of the ‘paradigm’ was never novel. Toulmin (1972) explains how ‘paradigm’ was used in the past in the same way that Kuhn had applied it, to mean ‘conceptual system’, ‘stereotypes’, or ‘fundamental patterns of explanation’ (pp. 106– 107), either in a limiting sense as Rhees, (1968) had once used it or in a liberating sense as was used by Lichtenberg, a mid-18th century philosopher.

In the same passage, B&L applies Masterman’s (1970) oft-cited claim that Kuhn uses the paradigm term in no less than twenty-one difference senses. Not only is the term unclear, B&L goes on to provide a persuasive argument that because ‘a paradigm should dually indicate problems and methods not belonging to a discipline’ therefore use of such vague paradigms restricts that discipline. However, what B&L and others who repeatedly cite this observation consistently neglect to add is that Masterman (1970), after listing all 21 senses, quickly concludes that they can be grouped into three main categories: (1) metaphysical paradigms, (2) sociological paradigms, and (3) construct (conceptual) paradigms. Supporting Kuhn, Masterman (1970) accepts the usefulness of the paradigm concept, especially as a guide by which scientists are still able to perform their research in the period in which theories are absent (the pre-paradigm period). Kuhn’s critics who selectively choose parts of evidence that only support their contention often omit Masterman’s (1970) positive evaluations of the paradigm concept that forms the bulk of her commentary on Kuhn’s work. Masterman (1970) elaborates favourably on the ‘originality of Kuhn’s sociological notion of a paradigm … paradigms as a puzzle-solving device … paradigm as a way of seeing’ (p. 59).

The more concrete forms of the paradigm – Masterman’s (1970) sociological and conceptual forms – represent the view of paradigms that are most useful. As Eckberg & Hill (1979) argue, these two categories, which are closer to the meaning of ‘exemplars’. were Kuhn’s original formulation of the paradigm. As a result of the criticisms he received, Kuhn was compelled to <sup>fi</sup>ne tune what he meant by the paradigm, but the essential nature of the paradigm concept remains unchanged, useful, and productive for research, and not as B&L might imply – a vague and confusing term to be held in contempt.

What B&L correctly highlights was the misplaced preoccupation of the IS community on searching for the single ‘true’ paradigm in order for their <sup>fi</sup>eld to attain maturity. B&L states that ‘one should not distract researchers from their daily activities and ask them to try to set up a set of rules to be called a paradigm; rather, one could observe how these researchers proceed, elaborate a model and propose it as a paradigm’ (p. 50). B&L is correct in saying that paradigms are hic et nunc; the result of the work of the scientists and conclusions from data (Lyytinen, 2009), not the result of the search for a paradigm, and in this sense, is in agreement with Kuhn. His writings do not in any way propose that researchers go searching for a paradigm in order to bring their <sup>fi</sup>elds to maturity. Rather, like B&L, Kuhn (1970b) proposes that researchers continue to solve problems in their <sup>fi</sup>elds and it is the more successful rules, theories, and practices – the concrete elements of the paradigm – that will emerge as new paradigms.

Unfortunately, in arguing against searching for the single uni<sup>fi</sup>ed paradigm, B&L throws the baby with the bath water by rejecting all that has to do with the paradigm concept. For instance, to support their criticism against paradigm searching, B&L enlists De Mey’s (1982) book, The Cognitive Paradigm and selectively chooses a quote to suggest that De Mey discourages such activities. De Mey (1982) classi<sup>fi</sup>es paradigm-searching activities into three groups (pp. 104–105) whose activities are ‘paradigm-hunting, paradigm-detection and paradigm-dissection’. The paradigm-hunters are those ‘enthusiastic Kuhn followers who hope to remedy a deplorable state in their <sup>fi</sup>eld by providing a paradigm or by promoting the search for it’ (B&L, p. 49). However, by highlighting only the <sup>fi</sup>rst group, which De Mey (1982) admits is the least productive use of the paradigm concept, B&L ignores De Mey’s (1982) detailed description of the other two categories, which he considers are more productive activities of paradigm searching. De Mey (1982) notes that paradigm-detection studies, which today take the form of scientometrics (Price, 1963), have ‘developed into an area that greatly contributes to our understanding of the social side of the paradigm concept’ (De Mey, 1982, p. 105). For example, scientometrics and the concept of the ‘invisible college’ (Price & Beaver, 1966) owe a debt to paradigm-detection studies. Paradigm-dissection studies, which focus on understanding the concrete mechanisms of the paradigm’s functioning, inspire cognitive psychology and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence. B&L rejection of all of paradigm searching obscures De Mey’s debt to Kuhn, his defence of Kuhn’s paradigms and De Mey’s acknowledgement of Masterman’s (1970) positive appraisal of the paradigm concept.

Except for several notable exceptions (Iivari et al, 1998; Goles & Hirschheim, 2000; Khazanchi & Munkvold, 2003; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Mingers, 2004; Richardson & Robinson, 2007; Moody et al, 2010), the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld has abstained from actively engaging in a healthy debate on the subject of paradigms, at least in the concrete forms that Kuhn envisioned. In addition to the earlier evidence provided (e.g., ‘recent disrepute into which this word has fallen’ (Ein-Dor & Segev, 1981, p. vii)), further evidence suggest that for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, the Kuhnian paradigm remains a dubious undertaking. Citing B&L, authors in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld ‘question whether such a paradigm is feasible, or, indeed, desirable’ (Adam & Fitzgerald, 2000) and opine that ‘[T]he concept of paradigm, as Kuhn de<sup>fi</sup>nes it, is derived from research in the physical sciences. This perspective may not serve well in the social sciences, where pluralistic models are more appropriate as the basis for understanding and analysis’ (Larsen & Levine, 2008, p. 25). The Kuhnian paradigm is understood in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld in such a pejorative way that some consider traces of Kuhnian thought something worthy of disclosure. In a response to Benbasat & Zmud’s (2003) proposal for an intellectual core for the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, one commentator remarked ‘[T]here is a hidden “early” Kuhnian aspect to Benbasat’s and Zmud’s argument, which I believe, needs to be surfaced’ (Galliers, 2003, p. 339).

Such disparaging reactions to the Kuhnian paradigm in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld can be contrasted against the positive opinions of numerous scholars in other social and natural science <sup>fi</sup>elds who have bene<sup>fi</sup>tted from transformative aspects of the paradigm concept (Barnes, 1982; Bird, 2000). Friedrich’s (1970) A Sociology of Sociology and Ritzer’s (1980) Sociology: A Multiparadigm Science, both contributed to a rede<sup>fi</sup>nition of sociology as a multiparadigmatic and rich <sup>fi</sup>eld of study. As a result, sociology was able to free itself from the Parsonian functionalist grand theory programme. The sociology of technology studies was able to escape the clutches of the natural sciences, which, through political support, were grooming that <sup>fi</sup>eld to become their foot soldier in accomplishing their model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress. Kuhn’s Structure inspired top universities around the world including the University of California, Berkeley and Cornell University to found policy-oriented programmes that brought multiple perspectives from historians of science and philosophers to join their new Science and Technology Studies programmes (Sismondo, 2003).

The sociology of knowledge and scientometrics, both owe their debt to Kuhn (Price, 1963; Merton, 1973). Bloor (1997), in defending Kuhn, states ‘Perhaps the most shameful of all misunderstandings was the idea that, for Kuhn, science has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contact within independent reality’ (p. 124). Perry (1977) lists political scientists, religious scholars, and artists who have bene<sup>fi</sup>tted from the Kuhnian model. Cognitive historian Boring (1964) analogizes Kuhn’s paradigm and incommensurability thesis to the notion of ‘cognitive dissonance’ in cognitive psychology. In the natural sciences, Minsky (1975), among the pioneers of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence, admits his debt to Kuhn for his frame theory. Minsky (1975) writes ‘The basic frame idea itself is not particularly original – it is in the tradition of the “schema” of Bartlett and the “paradigms” of Kuhn’ (p. 113). All of these sociological and conceptual versions of the paradigm applied in other <sup>fi</sup>elds demonstrate its usefulness.

## Why B&L says that the paradigm imposes a physics model on the social sciences – the legacy of the banality of normal science

In addition to the sources of authority (e.g., Chalmers, Toulmin, Masterman, De Mey) mentioned examined earlier, the one that provides the strongest methodological justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for B&L is Whitley’s (1984) The Intellectual and Social Organization of the Sciences. Because of the overwhelming in<sup>fl</sup>uence of Whitley on B&L, a second-level hermeneutical examination of Whitley’s works becomes necessary in order to fully understand B&L. This examination takes the form of

Whitley’s (1984) interpretation of Kuhn’s model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress and the traditions surrounding it. The disconnections and incompatibilities between Kuhn’s critics and of B&L are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in Whitley’s (1984) introductory sections. Whitley (1984) <sup>fi</sup>rst gives credit to Kuhn for transforming the view of science from ‘an epistemologically rational process’ (p. 1) to one ‘to treat the natural sciences less reverentially and more of a social phenomena’ (p. 2). However, in the next paragraph, he accuses Kuhn of superimposing a ‘unitary view of the ‘mature’ sciences and his apparent belief that once a <sup>fi</sup>eld became ‘mature’ through unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed processes, it follows the same pattern of intellectual change regardless of its external circumstances’ (p. 3) and that it follows ‘modern physics and inevitability of its pattern of intellectual change’ (p. 3). Here, Whitley (1984) equates being epistemologically rational and deterministic with the methods of the hard sciences. B&L extends Whitley’s (1984) assessment of the hard sciences and concludes that Kuhn’s paradigmatic normal science, which follows from this rationalistic character, is uncreative, banal, and prosaic. They question why any <sup>fi</sup>eld would want to work hard towards acquiring a paradigm when the normal science associated with this paradigmatic phase is characterized by ‘mopping-up operations and puzzle-solving that produce no major novelties’? (B&L, p. 51).

First, the idea that the physical sciences, Whitley’s example of monism and uni<sup>fi</sup>ed science, is misplaced. Kuhn’s (1970b) description of the physical sciences clearly admits pluralism and differences in opinions:

Each member of that group is taught the laws … and most of them employ those laws at some point … But they do not all learn the same application of these laws … What quantum mechanics means to each of them depends upon what courses he has had, what texts he has read, and which journals he studies. (p. 50)

Moreover, his description of paradigm battles (e.g., the discoveries of the wave theory of light, dynamical theories of heat and electromagnetic theory, pp. 66–68) taking place during periods of normal science show that such periods are not without its share of excitement. These periods of normal science are far from being ‘quiet’ and are often punctuated with novel discoveries, both within and outside its paradigmatic boundaries. In fact, it is research performed without paradigms that will hold back the field because all the facts collected would all seem equally relevant and there is no set of agreed beliefs on which any evaluation of progress can be compared. Second, with the establishment of a paradigm or several paradigms, the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community no longer needs to build the <sup>fi</sup>eld anew, which is a common disadvantage for students in pre-paradigmatic <sup>fi</sup>elds. Therefore, with the guidance of paradigms, students can focus on the more esoteric aspects instead of wasting time on repeatedly building the research from <sup>fi</sup>rst principles. Knowing that one is on the research front is exciting and adventurous, but this excitement is only possible if the researchers in the <sup>fi</sup>eld are certain they are indeed on the fringes of their <sup>fi</sup>eld, which requires, a priori, an establishment of normal science.

In addition, it is also during these periods of stability that the professional aspects of the <sup>fi</sup>eld have the most opportunities to grow and in<sup>fl</sup>uence society. Although on the surface, the activities of normal science might be described as ‘mopping-up’, it is these activities that allow scientists to investigate part of the <sup>fi</sup>eld in enough ‘detail and depth, which would otherwise be unimaginable’ (Kuhn, 1970b, p. 24) and consequently be seen as signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant by society. The paradigm also guides researchers towards problems that are more likely to have solutions because paradigms come ‘with a criterion for choosing problems’ (p. 37). Whatever the motivation for the scientists, whether to be useful, to explore exciting new areas, to <sup>fi</sup>nd order in apparent chaos, or to establish knowledge, normal science provides the necessary rules by which progress can be evaluated. Unfortunately, this legacy of shunning normal science is holding back the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld and preventing its scholars from building the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s cumulative tradition.

## Why B&L says the Kuhnian paradigm is not sociological – the legacy of a disproportionate focus on reputations

Reasons as to why B&L insists that Kuhn’s model ignores the sociological dimension of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress can also be traced to Whitley (1984) and other publications in the same research programme. To build its argument against the Kuhnian model, Whitley (1984) creates a dichotomy between the social study of science and the alleged hard science of Kuhnian model by saying that ‘it follows the same pattern of intellectual change regardless of its external circumstances’, which ‘prevented social scientists from studying variations between the sciences and investigating the social conditions in which radical intellectual change will occur’ (p. 3). He draws on Bloor’s (1976) ‘strong programme’ (p. 4) who proposes that success of discoveries comes not from the scientists’ superior access to the ‘truth’ but from their ability to demonstrate their superiority in relation to existing scienti<sup>fi</sup>c ideals. Elsewhere, in his strongest criticism of Kuhnian and Mertonian tradition, Whitley (1983) re<sup>fl</sup>ects on why few studies ‘have concerned themselves with the sociological inadequacies of his [Kuhn’s] account of communities and of maturity’ (p. 683).

Whitley’s use of the ‘strong programme’ is puzzling given that the founders of the strong programme, in particular, Bloor (1997) and Barnes (1982), are well known Kuhn supporters. Whitley’s (1983, p. 689) assertion that science is ‘anything which a culture collectively endorses as such’ is a statement that any Kuhnian would agree with. As Introna (2003, p. 236) notes, drawing from Foucault, ‘the status of IS as an academic discipline is not an ontological question or an epistemological question as such, but rather a political one from the start’ (original emphasis). In fact, Whitley (1984) acknowledges Kuhn’s sociological roots when he refers to Fleck (1935/1979), Bachelard (1938/2002) and Polanyi (1958) as major in<sup>fl</sup>uences on

Kuhn. In the 1930s, Fleck (1935/1979) developed sociological notions that Kuhn would later make famous: notions that scienti<sup>fi</sup>c cognition is a social activity taking the form of a ‘thought collective’ with its own ‘thought style’, which when suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently developed will manifest itself in the form of an ‘esoteric circle’ that become ‘incommensurable’ with other circles. Kuhn would later develop these concepts into the idea of the paradigm, the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community and incommensurability. Bachelard (1938/2002) introduced the concept of the epistemological break and the discontinuity demonstrated by scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress, which Kuhn would describe as scienti<sup>fi</sup>c revolutions. Polanyi (1958) (misspelled ‘Polyani’ in Whitley (1984)) deliberated on the tacit nature of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge grounded in ‘tradition’ and constructed via consensus in the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community, handed down in conceptual frameworks that hold their own world view, presuppositions and vocabulary.

Whitley’s insistence that Kuhn ignores the sociological dimension can be traced back to the context and background of his research programme. Whitley was publishing during an era in which the sociology of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge, and social constructivism in general, was undergoing rapid growth as a result of the works of Merton (1973), Berger & Luckmann (1966), and Latour & Woolgar (1979). Criticizing Kuhn’s approach, Whitley (1984) writes ‘It is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to see how a genuine sociology of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge can be produced, though, without considering how intellectual production and assessment can change in different ways in different circumstances’ (p. 4). On the basis of such statements and his other publications working closely with Elias et al, (1982), it would appear that Whitley sought to contribute to this burgeoning area of study. Whitley’s contributions to the sociology of science programme are based on two main arguments: (1) the need to frame his sociology of science on the combination of ‘social structures with changes in intellectual development’ (Whitley, 1984, p. 3), continuing the efforts he began in the 1970s (Whitley, 1974) and, (2) his claim that Kuhn’s work ‘favoured state intervention in the direction of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research’ (Whitley, 1984, p. 3). The former basis for Whitley’s research programme was inspired by the tradition of Latour & Woolgar (1979), Knorr-Cetina (1981), and Mulkay (1979), which emphasize the socially contingent nature of intellectual development as opposed to what he considers to be Kuhn’s philosophical and epistemologically rational theories. This research programme is clearly documented in his earlier publications, which characterize academic <sup>fi</sup>elds as ‘reputational organizations’ (Whitley, 1982, p. 315).

The second justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for Whitley’s (1982, 1984) research programme and his views on research policy comes from his hermeneutical application of Kuhn to the ‘Finalization Thesis’ of the Starnberg group in Germany in the early 1970s. At the time, the Max Planck Institute for the Study of Living Conditions in the Industrial World, based in Starnberg, Germany, initiated a discussion on how society was to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research (Pfetsch, 1979; Schopman, 1980). In building their thesis, they appropriated part of Kuhn’s theory to propose a threestage model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c growth: (1) pre-paradigmatic stage, (2) paradigmatic stage, and (3) post-paradigmatic stage. The <sup>fi</sup>rst pre-paradigmatic stage is represented by the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c community’s efforts in collecting data and building theories, followed by a paradigmatic stage when a dominant theory becomes the paradigm, causing the <sup>fi</sup>eld to become a ‘normal science’. Up to this point the group remained faithful to Kuhn’s ideas. It is in the third phase where the group diverges from Kuhn’s model of progress.

Whereas Kuhn’s model focuses on the anomalies that can be expected to occur during normal science, hence, resulting in crises and revolution, the Starnberg group expanded on the notion of a third post-paradigmatic phase. According to the Starnberg group, three major notions characterize this third phase: (1) a sense of completeness in research, from which the term the ‘Finalization Thesis’ was derived, is reached, where all fundamental problems in the research area are resolved and puzzle solving in the Kuhnian sense becomes routine, (2) instead of discovery, the third phase is characterized by application of the ‘completed research to bene<sup>fi</sup>t society, and (3) a change from an internal autonomous force guiding the direction of research, to an external societal force planning and directing science. This third politically charged characteristic became a thorn on the side of the Starnberg group’s thesis because it implied a loss of autonomy for the scientists and a leaning to a more leftist stance of government control. This governmental imposition was the central argument used by Whitley (1984) to suggest that Kuhn’s model promoted an Orwellian approach towards science as a national resource that needed to be managed and controlled.

Notwithstanding Whitley’s (1984) criticism of Kuhn, the language used to describe his sociological view of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress is not surprisingly, Kuhnian. For example, these two passages in Whitley (1984) describe the Kuhnian paradigm and the formation of a ‘normal science’:

Thus, scientists who seek the highest reputations as novelty producers have to convince powerful colleagues of their competence in following standard procedures and applying shared skills and of the signi-<sup>fi</sup>cance and relevance of their work for collective use … Much literature, for instance, is based on a shared set of assumptions, linguistic conventions, and commonly recognized achievements which underlie reputations and assessments. (p. 12)

Whitley’s research programme was therefore an extension of Kuhn’s theory rather than a contravention. In fact, Whitley (1984), p. 13) applied the title of Kuhn’s re<sup>fl</sup>ec-Structure The Essential Tension: Selected Studies in Scientific Tradition and Change, 1977) in describing the ‘essential tension’ between novelty and tradition’.

In the end, Whitley’s (1984) doctrine of academic <sup>fi</sup>elds as reputational organizations, where employment and science itself depended largely on ‘enhancing one’s own reputation’ (p. 25), engendered a predominantly externalist view of progress where legitimacy and maturity hinges more on the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld maintaining a close collaboration with its reference disciplines rather than a concerted effort towards consensus within the IS community itself. This externalist view encouraged a culture of borrowing from reference disciplines over that of autonomously working out the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s own endogenous concepts and theories. Hence, writing on how fragmented adhocracies should strategize, Whitley (1984) describes that, ‘reputations in sub-units are usually complemented by attempts to gain the approval of scientists in other areas … through publishing in the more central journals’ (p. 173). Coupled with the belief that any internal efforts to put the IS house in order ‘rests on shaky ground’ (B&L, p. 51) and will result in one group imposing its views on another, the legacy of Whitley’s sociology of science in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is a disproportionate focus on reputations and an overreliance on the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s reference disciplines.

## Practical recommendations and future research

Harvesting the power of the original paradigm concept The paradigm concept, in its original sociological or construct versions (Eckberg & Hill Jr., 1979), offers the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld several advantages that will help take the <sup>fi</sup>eld out of its current disciplinary cacophony. First, unlike the metaphysical version, these more concrete views of the paradigm are not bound by any metaphysics because they do not take sides on whether a particular research method or topic is uni<sup>fi</sup>ed or diverse, monist or pluralist, positivist or interpretivist. Just like how it is applied in Mertonian sociology of science, Berger and Luckmann’s social construction of reality, science and technology studies, Ritzer’s sociology, and the cognitive sciences, the freedom from any philosophical or epistemological baggage will enable the IS community to invent its own theories and concepts based on exemplars of its best research.

Second, the paradigm concept frees the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld from the dependence on its reference disciplines by freeing the community from any rigid rules inherited from them, but at the same time enables the community to share new paradigms as a source for coherent research. As Kuhn (1970b, p. 42) summarizes this character of paradigms, ‘Rules, I suggest, derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research even in the absence of rules’. The IS <sup>fi</sup>eld is unique because it is situated within the gap between the organization sciences and the technical sciences. Unfortunately, paradigms that have been adopted by the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld in the past were mostly those taken wholesale from one side of that gap, the organizational sciences. For example, the psychology/management paradigm of the early 1970s, the strategic management (or competitive advantage) paradigm of the 1980s, the adoption/social psychology paradigm of the 1990 s, all demonstrate how reference disciplines dictate the kinds of research undertaken in IS. A <sup>fi</sup>eld that depends on the paradigms of its reference disciplines can never be expected to go beyond the limits and blinders imposed by those paradigms. The original

Kuhnian paradigm allows the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld to escape these blinders to <sup>fi</sup>nd its own concepts, models, and theories.

Finally, the paradigm concept offers the future IS research the necessary tools for a serious engagement in the depth otherwise not possible. As a result of this dependency, IS scholars are able to only research at a super<sup>fi</sup>cial level, dabbling in psychology, sociology, economics, marketing, engineering, management, and other disciplines without building the required depth within the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld or the discipline that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the contextual environment. The power of the paradigm is demonstrated by several noteworthy exceptions. Iivari (1991) and Hirschheim et al, (1996) organized the very messy jungle of IS development methodologies by de<sup>fi</sup>ning several paradigms, which these methodologies follow. Goles & Hirschheim (2000) propose a modi<sup>fi</sup>cation to the incommensurable metaphysical– sociological paradigm of Burrell & Morgan (1979). The ontological (Wand & Weber, 1995) and the artifactual paradigms (Weber, 2003) developed in IS have captured the attention of other <sup>fi</sup>elds. Other paradigms relevant to the study of IS, such as the cognitive paradigm, the network paradigm, and the sociomaterial paradigm, are just recently being explored. The paradigm concept opens the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld to a plurality of subject matter while at the same time maintaining the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s cogency.

## Building cumulative tradition by reaching consensus on the most basic IS concepts

Following the route of normal science prevents the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld from falling into the trap of unproductive dabbling and commits the IS community to engage in its subject matter at the level of depth that will make cumulative tradition a reality. Normal science builds the bridges across the ‘communication gaps’ (Hirschheim & Klein, 2003, p. 241) that exist not just among the many sub-<sup>fi</sup>elds within IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, but also between the <sup>fi</sup>eld and its practitioners. As Kuhn (1970b) explains, it is not at all necessary for everyone in the <sup>fi</sup>eld to agree on all matters; what is important is for the community to agree on the most basic elements and concepts of their <sup>fi</sup>eld, because these basic elements and concepts form the foundation for cumulative tradition. Citing Polanyi, Kuhn (1970b, p. 44) argues that ‘[I]ndeed, the existence of a paradigm need not even imply that any full set of rules exists’. Is it too much to ask the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld to all agree on what they mean by the two words used in its name, ‘information’ and ‘system?’ Often it is the simplest terms in the <sup>fi</sup>eld that requires the most creative thinking to de<sup>fi</sup>ne, words such as ‘data’, ‘use’, or ‘technology’.

These basic concepts can be expected to differ from similar terms used in other <sup>fi</sup>elds. For example, the term ‘organization’ or ‘structure’ in IS can be expected to differ from the same term used in the organization sciences and become new concepts belonging to the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Using Foucault’s (1970) terminology, these new concepts emerge as a result of the synthesis of the various reference disciplines into the unique positivity of the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. These new concepts become possible because of a deeper self-understanding, exploration, and synthesis of the potential and limits of the reference disciplines and as a result, new questions that were unavailable to the contributing disciplines are addressed.

An example of this unique conceptual development process is the <sup>fi</sup>eld of women’s studies. It emerged as an academic <sup>fi</sup>eld because there was a strong sense on the part of many people that there were many aspects of the human experience that were not being given suf<sup>fi</sup>cient attention. It did not mean that women were not being studied in the sciences. Concepts related to women were studied in sociology, psychology, and even political science. And yet, women’s studies still emerged. Why? As it offered concepts about women, not as a by-product of social forces as in sociology, or a kind of mental object as in psychology, or the centre of feminist political activity, much less as a confounding or ignored variable in scienti<sup>fi</sup>c studies; it offered compelling concepts about women that were not offered by any other <sup>fi</sup>elds. Only when the <sup>fi</sup>eld does so can it demonstrate progress and cumulative tradition.

## Escaping the reputation trap: a moratorium on excessive borrowing

The externalist view of progress where legitimacy and maturity relied on borrowing from reference disciplines has not appeared to pro<sup>fi</sup>t the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld. Instead, it may have encouraged a culture of excessive borrowing from other disciplines (Nerur et al, 2006) without exerting much in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the opposite direction (Wade et al, 2006). Consequently, the growth of the <sup>fi</sup>eld has been largely the result of direct and uncritical borrowing of concepts and theories from other disciplines (Markus & Saunders, 2007; Grover et al, 2012). The paradigm concept offers the epistemological basis for a critical and novel way of borrowing. As a metaphor or exemplar, the paradigm concept enables a researcher to describe an unfamiliar object of study using one that is already well-known. Hesse (1967, p. 356), Oxford philosopher explains this power of the paradigm or theoretical model:

Basically, the theoretical model exploits some other system … that is already well known and understood in order to explain the less well established system under investigation … the theoretical model carries with it what has been called ‘open texture’ or ‘surplus meaning’, derived from the familiar system. The theoretical model conveys associations and implications that are not completely speci<sup>fi</sup>able and that may be transferred by analogy …

An example of exploiting an already familiar system is the model of the ‘wheel’ (Kaplan, 2001). In its speci<sup>fi</sup>ed form, the wheel as a machine that turns around and forms the necessary part of a moving vehicle is well-known. In its paradigmatic form, the wheel as a theoretical model has a great deal of plasticity and ‘surplus meaning’ that allows people to use it to explain other phenomena. The expressions, the ‘wheel of life’ and the ‘wheel of fortune’ exploit the constant turning of the wheel bringing change to our lives in unexpected ways. The expression ‘A <sup>fi</sup>fth wheel’ explains yet another aspect of the wheel implying an image of something unnecessary since customarily, four wheels is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for any vehicle. In each case, although the qualities and models from the wheel are borrowed, no details are assumed, and each expression takes on its own novel meaning and varied implications. The same process has been historically the hallmark of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress. The discovery of oxygen and the nature of light depended on the generous borrowing of metaphors and models from other more familiar phenomena without necessarily picking up any speci<sup>fi</sup>c detail (Kuhn, 1970b). In the social sciences, biological metaphors form the basis for major theories of sociology (Spencer, 1897). In the same way, as a multidisciplinary <sup>fi</sup>eld, IS has available to it a wealth of metaphors and models from other disciplines. At the paradigmatic level these metaphors and models provide for a rich source for inventive conceptual activity without the stigma and limitations of direct borrowing.

## Conclusion

This study examines several generally accepted assertions in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld concerning the use of the Kuhnian model for understanding how the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld might progress. It begins by highlighting some disconnections between the generally accepted criticisms of the Kuhnian model of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c progress with arguments presented by B&L, including its purported monism and rigidity, ignoring the sociological dimension of science, therefore unsuited for a pluralistic <sup>fi</sup>eld like IS. Instead, the Kuhnian model is found welcoming not only of diversity in concepts, theories, and methods, but also different schools of thought and multiple paradigms based on the sociology of science. Indeed, Kuhn warned against necessarily following the model of physics and favoured a more egalitarian approach such that his critics labelled him a relativist.
