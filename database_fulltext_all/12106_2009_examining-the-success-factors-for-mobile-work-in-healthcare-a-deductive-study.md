Keywords: Mobile work Healthcare IS success Mobility

Mobile work is emerging as an area of major importance in healthcare. However, past literature on this topic remains largely anecdotal, fragmented, and atheoretical. In this paper, we address this gap and adapt the DeLone and McLean model of IS success to the context of mobile work in healthcare, and articulate speci<sup>fi</sup>c propositions. We then deductively evaluate each proposition based on studies reporting mobile device use in the healthcare context. Through this rigorous evaluation process, we are able to deliver a revised theoretical model that presents a consolidated view of the literature in the area of mobile work in healthcare.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid development of mobile information and communication technologies has led to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant innovations in the healthcare industry, which have potential to improve care for patients [78]. It has been argued that “mobile technology can provide a number of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to organizations, such as connectivity, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, interactivity” [76, p. 273], capabilities which can be especially valuable to healthcare professionals. As noted by Ammenworth et al. [3], mobile work in healthcare can encompass different kinds of tasks:

• Routine use, such as the day to day activities for healthcare professionals (e.g. getting regular updates regarding patients).

• Mobile communication, such as the ability for a healthcare worker to be reached anytime and anywhere (e.g. a physician driving home can be reached on his/her PDA for consultation).

• Mobile information access, such as the possibility of healthcare workers to access patient medical reports from their mobile devices.

• Mobile documentation, such as the possibility of avoiding “double documentation” and have all documents in one centralized place, which could be updated/accessed through the mobile devices.

Thus, it is understandable that much research has been conducted in the health informatics arena to understand the impact of mobile technologies on providing effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient healthcare services to patients, while reducing the costs of doing so [49,82].

Recent work on mobile technologies point to radically different possibilities of conducting work, enabled by technology mediated mobility or “nomadicity” of individual agents [8,50]. The inherently <sup>fl</sup>exible nature of the healthcare workspace which, requires that healthcare workers be constantly on the move [34], makes the ability of individual agents to work anytime and anywhere especially relevant.

Even though the notion of mobile work in healthcare has gained importance, the literature remains largely anecdotal, fragmented, and atheoretical [20,49]. A review of the literature reveals that mobile work in general, and mobile collaboration in particular, has been under-researched, especially in terms of developing a theoretical understanding [28,63,74], with a high proportion of studies in healthcare exploring context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c or application-speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues.

In this paper, we take a <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward addressing this above mentioned limitation in the literature. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we adapt a wellknown model of IS success (the “DeLone and McLean model”) to the context of mobile work in healthcare, and articulate speci<sup>fi</sup>c propositions that may be discerned from the literature. We then deductively evaluate each proposition based on studies reporting on mobile device use experiences in the healthcare context. Through this rigorous evaluation process, we are able to deliver a theoretical model that presents a consolidated view of the literature in the area of mobile work in healthcare.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: First, we present our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of mobile work in healthcare, followed by a discussion of our theoretical model, and justi<sup>fi</sup>cation behind our propositions. Next, we discuss our research methodology, data analysis technique, and results. Finally, we conclude with a discussion of the results, and recommendations for future research.

## 2. Mobile work in healthcare

Adapting from Martins et al. [52], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne mobile work as the usage of mobile technologies in varying degrees to accomplish tasks, across locational, temporal, and contextual boundaries. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition subsumes mobile collaboration, which refers to cooperation among two or more individuals, synchronously or asynchronously, anytime and anywhere [62], and in varying contexts [36].

Mobile work within hospitals is complex and intricate [4,68] and is undertaken by individuals with varying degrees of expertise [4] using the information and resources that are often locationally distributed [68]. As noted by Bardram and Bossen [4], mobility of resources and individuals are at the very heart of patient diagnosis, treatment, and care. Today's healthcare work scenarios are increasingly characterized by greater <sup>fl</sup>exibility [89], and may be viewed as loosely coupled distributed resources and individuals ([4,64] that work in unison through an intense information exchange [68].

## 3. Theoretical model

The primary objective of this study is to identify the key factors suggested in the literature that influence the “success” of mobile work within the health care context. Given our focus on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of information technology (mobile ICT), in developing our theoretical model, we drew inspiration from DeLone and McLean [16], p. 11], whose “multidimensional” and “interdependent” view of IS success has been adopted and applied by IS researchers in a variety of contexts, including mobile commerce [23]. DeLone and McLean [15] proposed six different dimensions of IS success. They are: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impacts and organizational impacts. Further, they suggested that system and information quality affects use and user satisfaction. Use and user satisfaction on the other hand were proposed to affect each other, and also the individual impacts of the use of the system. Finally, individual impacts were proposed to affect organizational impacts of the new information system. As a follow up of their pioneering work on IS success, DeLone and McLean [16] presented a revised model to account for recent changes in technologies, by speci<sup>fi</sup>cally including “Service Quality” as an antecedent of use and user satisfaction (in addition to system and information quality), proposing “intention to use” as an alternate measure of use, and combining the “impacts” variables into one unifying construct termed as “net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.” System quality, refers to the characteristics of the information system as well as the “processing” of the system, the <sup>fl</sup>exibility offered by the system, the amount of information/resources it accesses, etc. DeLone and McLean [16, p. 25] refer to information quality as a “content issue.” In our study, we view this more broadly as the nature of work that is mediated through the technology. Use refers to the extent to which the system has been adopted, while user satisfaction refers to the extent to which the user is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with his/her interaction with the system. Finally, net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is considered to be an overall measure of the <sup>fi</sup>nal outcome of the use of the information system, and includes a myriad of subconstructs ranging from the impacts on customers, suppliers, organizations, and work groups [16,39].

DeLone and McLean [15, p. 80] suggest that the choice of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c dimensions of the IS success measures (e.g., dimensions of system quality) “is often a function of the objective of the study, the… context… etc.” Thus, in deciding on the appropriate set of system quality related, information/content quality related, and service quality related variables for our study, we turned to our context, mobile work in healthcare. Mobile work in healthcare is enabled by the ability of healthcare professionals to seamlessly “access” (input) patient and medical information from (into) centralized databases, irrespective of current location or context of the healthcare worker [29,33]. Thus, technology characteristics such as the data processing capability of the mobile technology (that enables processing of large amounts of data), the ability to access information from centralized resources, and the portability of the technology are key components of the technology that should affect the use/effectiveness of the mobile technology. Further, given that collaboration within the healthcare industry is enabled by the ability of the healthcare workers to communicate and coordinate actions with each other [12], the availability of multiple channels of communication and coordination, and the capability to deliver reliable service and support could also be argued to play an important role in the success of a mobile technology. Rodriguez et al. [68] note that healthcare work is characterized by a fair degree of mobility given that “a hospital's staff might be distributed in space (i.e., different location within the settings) or time (i.e., working different shifts) and their information needs and communication patterns are highly dependent on their location and other contextual conditions such as their role or time of the day” (p. 448). Thus, we believe that in addition to the features of the system, the characteristics of work (i.e., the structure of the task being performed by the collaborators, the time pressure under which the workers perform the task, complexity of the content being dealt with by the collaborative partners, the mobility requirement of the collaborators, and the asynchronicity/synchronicity of the communication required) would also play an important role.

Drawing on the DeLone and McLean [16] model, we argue that system quality, information/content quality, and service quality would affect the use of the mobile technology and the satisfaction of the collaborators with the mobile technology. Finally, use and user satisfaction would affect net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. DeLone and McLean [16, p. 22] argue that the assessment of “net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts” is often challenging, since the phrase itself raises questions such as “what is a bene<sup>fi</sup>t,” bene<sup>fi</sup>t “for whom,” and “at what level of analysis?” Seddon [75, p. 246] de<sup>fi</sup>nes net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as the “idealized comprehensive measure of all past and expected future bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, less all past and expected future costs, attributed to the use of an information system application.” It has also been suggested that net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts should be assessed by observing what is valuable to the different stakeholders [16,75]. Our review of the literature suggests a myriad of outcomes that may be relevant to stakeholders within the healthcare industry, ranging from faster/more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient delivery of care to patients, to better monitoring of diseases, to increased productivity of the healthcare service providers [39]. Also, a recently published bene<sup>fi</sup>ts evaluation framework for health information systems in Canada has included healthcare quality, productivity and access as the key measures of net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [43]. Given that each of these above mentioned perspectives of net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is critical for different stakeholders of healthcare, we used all of these indicators to capture “net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts” in our study, without making distinctions among them. We provide the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of our key constructs in Table 1. In the next section, we present our hypotheses.

## 4. The theoretical propositions

## 4.1. System quality

## 4.1.1. Extent of data processing

The data processing capability of an information system, i.e. the extent to which the system a) is able to effectively integrate data from different places, and b) has the necessary tools to support decisionmaking, has been associated with higher use and increased satisfaction amongst the users (e.g., [15,58]). The effect of the data processing capability of mobile handheld devices on the use of such technologies has also been acknowledged in prior literature (e.g. [14,42,49]). In the context of healthcare, Baumgart [6] notes that PDAs that provide higher levels of decision support to health care professionals play an important role in addressing the work related needs of these professionals. Lee et al. [46] conclude that the ability of the mobile technology to provide relevant data integration and decision support can increase its use and satisfaction of the users. Finally, Mitsa et al. [56] argue that health care units are characterized by “chaotic environments that feature high activity, excessive input, uncertainty, interruptions, and competing responsibilities.” In such contexts, “point-ofcare Decision Support System” or technologies with high quality data processing capability can help address some of the challenges related to decision-making, therefore positively affecting the use of (and satisfaction with) such technologies. Thus, we can argue:

P1.1a. The extent of data processing capability of a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

## 4.1.2. Extent of information access

Prior literature suggests that the extent to which the information system is able to provide users with access to data stored in centralized databases can enhance the use of (and satisfaction of the users with) these technologies (e.g., [23,58]). In the context of mobile technologies, Lee et al. [46] conclude from their empirical study that the ability of the technology to enable users to “download” “relevant information” from “corporate databases” increases the use of the technologies. Within the context of mobile healthcare work, Bates and Gawande [5, p. 2527] suggest that errors are often associated with inadequate access to medical and clinical data, and thus, the use of technology that includes “computerized coverage systems for signing out, handheld personal digital assistants,” and “wireless access to electronic medical records” would improve the exchange of information, especially if links between various applications and a common clinical databases are in place.” Kuziemsky et al. [39] also argue that PDAs that enable on time access to centralized patient records can “reduce medical errors in different areas of healthcare,” and therefore increases its use and satisfaction with its use. Thus, we argue:

P1.2a. The extent of information access supported by a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

P1.2b. The extent of information access supported by a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

## 4.1.3. Communicability

Prior literature argues for the positive relationship between the number of communication channels provided by an information system and its subsequent use (e.g., [59]). Riemer and Frobler [67] argue that given the dispersed nature and the “virtualization” in the workplace, most users or work groups prefer to use technologies that offer multiple channels of communication. Based on Gebauer and Shaw [23], we can argue that in any setting (including a healthcare one), a mobile device would be useful if it supports both synchronous and asynchronous communication. Scholl et al. [73] speci<sup>fi</sup>cally note that healthcare workers prefer integration of paging, voice and text (email) services into one single device, especially since they operate in environments characterized by “high activity” [56, p. 740] and increased “<sup>fl</sup>uidity of interactionwith others” [67, p. 206]. From an empirical study investigating the effect of wireless devices offering multiple channels of communication in a Chicago hospital, Minnick et al. [55] concluded that such devices signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase nurse's satisfaction. Based on the above discussion, we have:

P1.3a. The communicability of a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context

P1.3b. The communicability of a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

## 4.1.4. Portability

The portability of a technology has been associated with the use and effectiveness of a technology [22]. Lyytinen and Yoo [50] argue that today's organizational work-life re<sup>fl</sup>ects “nomadic information environments,” which are characterized by high levels of mobility of the different stakeholders. In such contexts, it is important for an information system to “support all forms of mobility,” by being smaller in size, more “ergonomic,” and “stylistic.” With respect to mobile devices too, the extent of portability has been argued to be a key factor affecting its use (e.g. [23,83,85]). Kuziemsky et al. [39] categorically mention that portability prompts greater use and satisfaction with the use of a PDA. Others researchers suggest that within the context of healthcare workers, portability of a technology would increase its use [49,56]. Thus, we argue:

P1.4a. The portability of a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

P1.4b. The portability of a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

## 4.2. Content quality – The nature of work

## 4.2.1. Structure of the task

Task structure has been viewed as an important variable affecting both the use of an information system (including a mobile system), and the <sup>fi</sup>nal outcomes associated with the use of an information system (e.g., [23,24,40]). Task structure has been assessed in different ways. Within a group environment, researchers (e.g., [80]) have examined whether tasks are additive (i.e., each collaborative member contributes a part to the entire decision-making process), disjunctive (i.e., each group selects an optimal solution from a myriad of choices proposed by each group member), or conjunctive (each group member possesses unique information, which needs to be pooled to generate a group decision). In a broader sense, task structure has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which the tasks have “exceptions,” and the extent of the “analyzability and predictability” of work [24, p. 595]. Shirani et al. [77] view task structure as the extent to which tasks are “routine with wellestablished methods for handling them” (i.e., high task structure), or “require a great deal of decision maker insight, judgment, and evaluation and have no de<sup>fi</sup>nite boundaries or well-understood procedures” (i.e., low task structure). While earlier research seems to have suggested that information systems support both structured and unstructured tasks, and were found to be more valuable in the context of unstructured tasks (e.g., [61]), recent studies suggest that unstructured tasks tend to lower the use of an information system, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally has a negative effect on the user satisfaction associated with an information system. We believe that such a relationship is also likely to exist in the context of mobile technology use in healthcare and satisfaction with its use as well. Since mobile devices have shortcomings such as problems of connectivity, relatively small screen size, dif<sup>fi</sup>cult interfaces and input mechanisms, lack of availability of a wide range of software or programs (e.g., [70]), it may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use them in contexts where the tasks are unbounded. Thus, we argue:

P2.1a. The extent of structure in the task being performed by the user will positively affect the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context. P2.1b. The extent of structure in the task being performed by the user will positively affect his/her satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

## 4.2.2. Urgency of information

The choice/use of a particular technology is often associated with the extent to which the users require “immediacy of feedback” or feel the need to interact “on-demand” (e.g., [17,23,35]). In today's hypercompetitive and turbulent environment, most organizational workers operate under time pressure and experience (and participate in) ondemand task performance/collaboration (e.g., [50]). Nowhere is the urgency of the tasks more evident than in healthcare, where workers often need to perform under intense time pressure, and deal with the “emergence of contingencies that require ad hoc and pragmatic responses” [[7], p. 90]. Prior research argues that such work environments often require the use of ubiquitous technologies such as mobile devices (e.g., [79]). Others argue that mobile technology “better emulates face-to-face discussions by delivering … in devicescaled form, to the participants in real time wherever they are [[30], p. 56]. Similar sentiments have also been expressed by other researchers who argue that tasks requiring such immediate access can be best performed through the use of mobile devices [13,36], due to the ability of such technologies to support “anytime, anywhere” task performance [62]. Consequently, we argue:

P2.2a. The extent of urgency in the task to be performed by the user will positively affect the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context. P2.2b. The extent of urgency in the task to be performed by the user will positively affect the satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

## 4.2.3. Mobility of the healthcare workers

A key factor that can potentially affect the use and satisfaction of mobile technology within any setting is the mobility of its users [23]. In a healthcare setting, this refers to the mobility of the primary caregivers and stakeholders. Healthcare providers such as physicians and nurses are typically very mobile, constantly moving between different wards, departments and outpatient clinics [3], and shifts [57]. Kakihara and Sorensen [[37], p. 1] de<sup>fi</sup>ne mobility as “humans' independency from geographical constraints,” and argue that in the current age, to be mobile is “not just a matter of people traveling but, far more importantly, is related to the interaction they perform and the resulting communication pattern.” They propose three different types of mobility: spatial, temporal, and contextual.

Spatial mobility refers to the “human geographical movement, or nomadicity” [37, p. 2], and can be of three types [38,70]: traveling, wandering, and visiting. According to Kristoffersen and Ljungberg [38], wandering refers to “local mobility in a building or local area,” while traveling and visiting signify more extensive mobility where members may be going from one place to another (i.e., “traveling”) or going to a particular location and being stationary for a certain time in that location, say during a vacation (i.e., “visiting”). Kakihara and Sorensen [37] argue that mobile technologies such as PDAs and cell phones are likely to be perceived as valuable and used in contexts where users experience high levels of spatial mobility. Kafeza et al. [34] argue that healthcare workspaces are by nature spatially mobile as the physicians and nurses do not have <sup>fi</sup>xed workspaces (e.g., one physician or nurse may need to attend a number of different types of wards). This creates the need for handheld mobile devices that allow access to clinical information irrespective of the physical location of the healthcare worker [19,49]. From this it may be concluded that in healthcare contexts where users experience high degree of spatial mobility, mobile technology is more likely to be used, and also more likely to increase the satisfaction of the users.

P2.3.1a. The extent of spatial mobility of the user will positively affect the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

P2.3.1b. The extent of spatial mobility of the user will positively affect the satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

Temporal mobility within organizations refers to the “temporal order of the workplace,” and re<sup>fl</sup>ects both the “pace of work” and time [37, p. 3]. Temporal mobility consists of both structural and interpretive components. Structural aspects of temporal mobility “are measured by largely objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed parameters, among which, sequence, duration, temporal location and rates of recurrence” are particularly relevant [37, p. 3]. On the other hand, the interpretive aspects of temporality refer to “how people in the workplace interpret the change of those structural parameters.” Different types of technologies often have the ability to change the “structural allocation of events” within organizations, which in turn changes the interpretation of the frameworks related to temporality [37]. In the context of healthcare (i.e., radiology), Kakihara and Sorensen [37] particularly state that temporality in<sup>fl</sup>uences the use of different technologies. We believe that healthcare contexts wherein users experience high levels of temporal mobility (i.e., where the sequence, duration, temporal location, and recurrence rates are not clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned), mobile technologies are more likely to be used, and more likely to increase users' satisfaction. For example, in cases of emergency (a patient in a critical condition), the mobile healthcare workers (e.g. physicians and nurses) might need to check in on a patient in an ad hoc manner, and administer different types of treatment (while checking his/her past medical history for assessing drug interactions, etc.) at indefinite time intervals and in different sequences. Such a situation re<sup>fl</sup>ects high temporal mobility, and would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the use of a mobile device which has the ability to provide anytime anywhere access to patient records and support collaboration among healthcare workers (such as between doctors and nurses) even when they are not collocated. Hence we propose,

P2.3.2a. The extent of temporal mobility of the user will positively affect the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

P2.3.2b. The extent of temporal mobility of the user will positively affect the satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

The concept of contextual mobility refers to the “situatedness of human action,” and stems from the belief that “human action is inherently situated in a particular context that frames and is reframed by his or her performance of the action recursively” [37, p. 4]. Questions such as “in what way,” and “in what particular circumstance,” are often asked to assess the contextual mobility of an actor. Contextual mobility can be unobtrusive/obtrusive (i.e., the extent to which “it imposes obligations to notice or react”) and ephemeral/persistent (i.e., the extent to which it “exists in the <sup>fl</sup>ux of unfolding activities”) [48 p.125]. It has been argued that the degree of contextual mobility signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects the use and user satisfaction related to technologies. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, Kakihara and Sorensen [37] suggest that contexts in which people “continuously reframe their interaction” bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the use of sophisticated ICTs, such as mobile devices and context-based services [53]. For example, a physician may want to check up on a hospital inpatient's medical records in order to prescribe some new medication to the patient, while having dinner with family and friends at a restaurant. Such a situation re<sup>fl</sup>ects high contextual mobility, where the physician needs to perform his/her professional responsibilities (i.e., context #1) while at the time ful<sup>fi</sup>lling his/her social responsibilities (i.e., context #2).

P2.3.3a. The extent of contextual mobility of the user will positively affect the use of a mobile device within the health-care context.

P2.3.3b. The extent of contextual mobility of the user will positively affect the satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

## 4.2.4. Information complexity

The complexity of information required by users has also been viewed as an important variable predicting the usage of mobile devices. It has been argued that medical and clinical information is typically complex, consisting of audio, video, and textual data which, require sophisticated information technologies to capture, display, analyze, and comprehend [5]. However, the handheld devices suffer from inherent problems of screen size, bandwidth, input mechanism, processing power, thereby reducing their ability to represent and process complex medical information [18,49]. Kuziemsky et al. [39, pp. 336–337] also suggest that PDAs often suffer from connectivity related problems, resulting in “slow speed of images being obtained, as in radiology images.” We thus believe that in contexts requiring complex information, users will be likely to use the more traditional technologies such as desktop work-stations, while in situations of low information complexities mobile technologies will be more likely to be used. Similarly, the use of mobile PDAs in high complexity situations is likely to be detrimental to user satisfaction, since users may get frustrated with the speed at which audio/video or radiology images load, and with the lack of suitable display and analytical tools. From this, we argue:

P2.4a. The complexity of the information required by the user will negatively affect the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context. P2.4b. The complexity of the information required by the user will negatively affect the satisfaction associated with the use of a mobile device within the healthcare context.

## 4.3. Service quality

## 4.3.1. System reliability

The reliability of a system and the availability of consistent service to its users have been associated with its use and the satisfaction of the users in the prior literature (e.g., [16]). Nelson et al [58, p. 217] concluded from their empirical study that “reliability appears to have the strongest in<sup>fl</sup>uence” in the context of the system's use and satisfaction. Existing research also suggests that the reliability of a mobile device has been a key factor determining use [82]. Issues such as delay of data propagation and loss of data in propagation are important concerns for mobile computing using handheld devices [86]. Lee et al. [46] concluded from their empirical study that “reliability,” de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the user's ability to “depend on the system to complete the task without system problems and system breakdowns,” is an important predictor of the use and effectiveness of PDAs.

In the healthcare industry, the critical and time sensitive nature of medical information makes reliable performance critical to their deployment [31]. This is exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by rigorous clinical studies evaluating such performance. System performance issues (such as delay, traf<sup>fi</sup>c) and the reliability of the mobile technologies with respect to these issues have notable implications for technology adoption behaviors of healthcare workers [49] and for patient care [10,47,84]. Thus, we argue:

P3.1a. The reliability of service provided by a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

P3.1b. The reliability of service provided by a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

## 4.3.2. System support

System support is another critical factor affecting the use and satisfaction of an information system [15,16,23]. Researchers argue that “perceived continuity in the availability of technical support from the vendor (or appropriate agents)” is an important antecedent of stakeholder perceptions and use of the system [71]. DeLone and McLean [16, p. 25] contend that in the current age, “users are now our customers and poor user support will translate into lost customers…” In the context of mobile technologies, such as PDAs, commercial support of PDA software has been viewed as important for usage [25]. Lu et al. [49] argue that a lack of support for the mobile technologies (e.g. absence of technical support, expired warranty, unavailable features) inhibit their actual use by healthcare workers such as physicians. We thus argue:

P3.2a. The extent of system support available for a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

P3.2b. The extent of system support available for a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

DeLone and McLean [15] argue that use of an information system will affect the user's satisfaction with the information system, and vice versa. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they state “USE can affect the degree of USER SATISFACTION—positively or negatively—as well as the reverse being true” (p. 83). DeLone and McLean [16 p. 23] state that “‘use’ must precede ‘user satisfaction’ in a process sense, but positive experience with ‘use’ will lead to greater ‘user satisfaction’ in a causal sense. Similarly, increased ‘user satisfaction’ will lead to increased.. ‘use’.” In subsequent empirical tests of the DeLone and McLean [15] model, Rai et al. [66] con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that user satisfaction has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the use of the system. In the context of mobile technologies, the relationships between use and the user's satisfaction with the technology have also been highlighted in the literature (e.g., [72]).

We believe that such a relationship is likely to exist in the context of mobile work in the healthcare setting as well, and argue:

P4a. Use of a mobile device will positively affect the satisfaction associated with its use within the healthcare context.

P4b. The extent of user's satisfaction with a mobile device will positively affect its use within the healthcare context.

DeLone and McLean [16, p. 83] make the assertion that “as a result of … ‘use’ and ‘user satisfaction,’ certain ‘net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts’ will occur.” In studies about mobile technologies (e.g., PDAs), the linkage between use of the system and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts have also been suggested in the literature (e.g. [46]). Within a healthcare setting, quality of patient care has been considered to be the most important outcome of medical applications [32]. As noted by Varshney [84], better patient care often refers to the lack of delay in critical care delivery. Since mobile handheld devices owing to their portability, communicability, and ability to provide anytime anywhere access are able to reduce the delay in service, it may be argued that appropriate use of a mobile handheld device would lead to a higher level of patient care [43]. On similar lines, Mitsa et al. [56, p. 741] argue that the use of mobile technologies by nurses could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase “the accuracy and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of patient care delivery,” and “enhance patient safety,” both important “net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts” within a healthcare setting. Thus, we argue:

P5a. Use of a mobile device will positively affect the “net benefits” within the healthcare context.

P5b. The extent of user's satisfaction with a mobile device will positively affect the “net benefits” within the healthcare context.

## 5. Methodology

We see our empirical approach directed toward consolidation of the literature as consisting of two layers [54]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst layer deals with the epistemological concerns such as the nature of reasoning, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, deduction that underlies scienti<sup>fi</sup>c thinking (e.g., [44,69]). The second layer is concerned methodological practices that support the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c reasoning process, by developing acceptable levels of credibility of the evidence (e.g., [27]).

## 5.1. Nature of reasoning

In empirically evaluating the propositions related to mobile work implied in the IS literature, we use the hypothetico-deductive (H-D) logic, the validity of which has long been recognized in Information Systems and related disciplines [2,44]. A deductive approach involves starting with an already formulated theoretical proposition and using empirical evidence to assess the validity of the proposition [69]. We note that within this perspective, evidence that is consistent with the predictions “can only temporarily support” a proposition or a theory, since subsequent tests “can always overthrow it” [65], p. 136]. Popper adds that “So long a theory [proposition] withstands detailed and severe tests…, we may say that it has ‘proved its mettle’ or that it is ‘corroborated by past experience.” If the evidence is not found to be consistent with the proposition in question, it casts doubts on the proposition [2].

## 5.3. Analysis

In order to assess the impact of the independent variables on the dependent variables in the model, we conceptualized each relationship in the model (e.g., portability→use) as a variance theory relationship, wherein independent variables are individually considered as necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient [51]. As an illustration, —in the case of portability→use proposition, the relationship implies that high portability of the devices should be associated with high use, or low portability of the devices should be associated with low use. Evidence in our “texts” pointing to high use for low portability or low use for high portability would cast doubt on (or discon<sup>fi</sup>rm) the proposition's validity. It is useful to note that many scholars believe that even a single instance of evidence that is inconsistent with a proposition is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to falsify it (e.g. [44,65]). While we wish to assess propositions using high standards, we believe that discon<sup>fi</sup>rming a proposition based on one instance may be too stringent in a study of this nature, given that some of the “texts” may have been reporting on very unique (“outlier”) situations, and the relatively open ended coding process that involved signi<sup>fi</sup>cant judgment on part of the coders. We wanted to avoid rejecting valid propositions due to spurious data or so called “measurement error” to the extent possible. Further, noting that the number of articles coded was not very high, we believe that a reasonable standard for casting doubt on a proposition is if greater than 10% of the articles presented inconsistent with the proposition.<sup>4,5</sup> We summarize our results in Table 3 and provide a detailed discussion in the next section.

## 6. Results and discussion

Our results, shown in Table 3, reveal mixed support for the propositions suggested in the literature. Interestingly, we see that results were similar in the case of use and user satisfaction. In other words, when the effect of a particular construct on use was discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed, the same effect on user satisfaction was also discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed (except for the effect of task structure). This pattern is not surprising given that Wixom and Todd [88] have argued as to how both use (adoption) and user satisfaction are closely related to each other. This is further reinforced by DeLone and McLean's [16] argument that use and user satisfaction mutually inform each other (a relationship acknowledged in our model).

First, the effect of data processing on use and user satisfaction was not supported, suggesting that data processing is not a key determinant of handheld mobile device usage. In other words, even in the absence of high data processing capability of a mobile device, usage could be high. One possible reason might be the fact that using such mobile devices is the norm among many healthcare professionals, irrespective of the data processing capability. Further, our empirical analysis reveals that in many cases, healthcare workers use mobile devices for simply communicating with each other, which do not require high data processing. For example, a pager, which is a mobile device, used extensively (and with great satisfaction) in the healthcare industry, has very low data processing capabilities. What our empirical examination suggests is that the need for high data processing capability of a mobile device usage depends on the context of use. As noted before, healthcare professionals undertake various tasks like communication, documentation, information access, with the help of their handheld mobile devices. Thus, different kinds of healthcare tasks may not need a high amount of data processing (e.g. paging an on-call doctor), and devices that are low on such capability may still be used.

Our proposition on the effect of the extent of information access on both use and satisfaction was not supported. As mentioned earlier, in a large number of contexts, healthcare workers use mobile devices simply for communicating with other stakeholders, which rarely requires the need for downloading/uploading data from/into centralized databases. The reasoning behind the invalidated effect of communicability on use and user satisfaction also follows a similar line of thought. Given that within healthcare settings mobile devices are frequently used for synchronous one-way communication only (e.g., paging an on-call doctor in a hospital), a single channel is often adequate, and may be used extensively and with great satisfaction.

Our hypothesis regarding the effect of task structure on mobile device use was supported, while the hypotheses regarding its effect on user satisfaction was not. Indeed, results of our study suggest that within healthcare settings, mobile devices are used more when the task involved have a high structure, validating earlier <sup>fi</sup>ndings on this issue (e.g., [61]). The lack of an effect of the task structure on the user's satisfaction with the usage of a mobile device could suggest that satisfaction with usage depends less on the nature of the work, than on the features of the technology itself. Healthcare workers may use mobile devices for their structured tasks (either because they are mandated or because everyone they interact with uses one), but may still be dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed owing to poor features. The discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of the positive effect of task urgency on both use and user satisfaction with mobile devices is an important one. As per our methodological stance of searching for evidence that falsify a given proposition (i.e. searching for evidence that the antecedent is not a necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for the predictor to occur), we can argue that our results show that greater use of, and satisfaction with, mobile handheld devices is not necessarily predicted by task urgency. In other words, a healthcare worker may use a mobile handheld device even when task urgency is absent. For example, a physician may still carry a mobile handheld device in order to call the nurse in order to receive regular updates on a patient, and, s/he may be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed that they are able to receive these regular updates, even though none of these updates are of an urgent nature. Again, the lack of support for this hypothesis may also be explained by our previous observation that healthcare tasks, especially using mobile handheld devices, are quite varied. Thus, the urgency and the consequent use due to that urgency could be contingent upon the task at hand, and also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the context of such tasks. For example, Abraham et al. [1] note that mobile devices are useful for reviewing patient history, a task which is often urgent in a healthcare setting. As they note, if the patients are HIV positive, they might not declare it immediately, hence the mobile system helps the healthcare workers review their medical history without asking them. However, other patients might be only too willing to share their medical history. Thus, even the same task urgency would necessitate different levels of mobile system use, depending upon the context.

Our results also indicate that in the context of healthcare, any kind of mobility, i.e. spatial, temporal, or contextual is not relevant to either the use or the satisfaction of using the mobile device. This is actually a surprising <sup>fi</sup>nding because one would argue that healthcare workers' mobility should be an important consideration in the adoption and use of the mobile devices (e.g. [19,34]). One way to interpret these results is that within healthcare settings, most stakeholders are heavy users of (and/or mandated users of) some form of a mobile device owing to the demands of their task, and the convenience of the technology (e.g., portability), irrespective of the level of mobility they experience while performing their job. The lack of support for the effect of information complexity on use and user satisfaction is not surprising given the current state of mobile technologies. Nevertheless, we believe that appropriate handheld devices are developing gradually such that they are able to process healthcare-related multimedia information. In fact, in our database of 70 articles, at least 21 of them suggested the presence of high information complexity, and the ability of the mobile device to support such complexity.

Our empirical analysis results also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a small set of necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient technology speci<sup>fi</sup>c and service-related factors predicting mobile handheld device use and user satisfaction in the healthcare context. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that portability is a necessary and suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for use of (and satisfaction with) mobile device usage within the healthcare industry. It can be argued that if a device needs to be used for mobile work, such as in healthcare, it needs to be of a size that is portable. It is also evident that the reliability of these mobile devices, and the available organizational support for these devices, are important indicators of the use and user satisfaction. In fact, in healthcare settings, where timeliness and accuracy are critical, reliable performance of any technology, including mobile devices, is an imperative. Further, as discussed in the literature, one of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant barriers to using mobile devices in the past has been the limited support available in case of software or hardware errors/breakdowns. Thus, it is not surprising that both use and user satisfaction are strongly predicted by the quality of service offered by the mobile technology [23].

Finally, both the mutual effect of use and user satisfaction on each other, and their effects on net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts were supported (that is, not discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed). This is to be expected, given that these relationships have been hypothesized as per the DeLone and McLean model, and has been validated in prior research. Our results indicate that these relationships are fairly robust, and hold true for mobile device usage in the context of healthcare as well.

## 7. Contribution and future implications

This paper contributes on two fronts. First, it provides an integrative theoretical understanding of mobile work within the healthcare industry. While mobile devices have been studied in healthcare settings before, the knowledge has largely been atheoretical and fragmented [74]. This paper, drawing on the DeLone and McLean [15,16] model of IS success presents a theoretical framework to better understand and clarify the success factors associated with mobile healthcare work. Related to this issue, the study highlights the fact that the DeLone and McLean [16] model in general, is a powerful theoretical framework for investigating mobile device usage within healthcare. A number of hypotheses were “not discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed” in the study, suggesting that the corresponding relationships proposed in the DeLone and McLean model are applicable to healthcare as well. To our knowledge, a similar validation of the DeLone and McLean's [16] model has not previously been attempted by mobile healthcare researchers. Past research in this area has typically been organized around case studies, with little attempt at theoretical assimilation. We believe that our study provides a consolidated view of the key factors affecting mobile device usage in healthcare, thereby providing a foundation for future research on mobile healthcare.

Related to this above issue, the second important contribution of this paper is that it showcases a leaner and more parsimonious model than the DeLone and McLean model, and identi<sup>fi</sup>es the “particular” factors that are important to achieve success in mobile healthcare. Consistent with the sociotechnical paradigm is IS, the DeLone and McLean model proposes both social and technological factors as relevant for IS success. However, our results showcase that, at least in mobile healthcare work currently, it is the technological characteristics that assume greater signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. It is possible that such a technologically deterministic <sup>fi</sup>nding has emerged due to the lack of maturity of mobile technology in healthcare; as mobile technologies in the healthcare sector develop further, we believe that the focus will shift on the adaptation of technology to the nature of task and mobility from just the technically oriented issues that emerge as salient in this study. Perhaps, the sociotechnical paradigm will more accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the situation then. For implications, we argue that this proposed model begins to address a major gap that exists at present in understanding the nature of mobile work in healthcare. While this proposed theoretical framework of mobile work in healthcare based on the DeLone and McLean IS Success Model appears to hold promise, further investigation, using a variety of approaches, is needed. For example, a more extensive set of articles from the PubMed, Ovid, and other databases could be included to test the validity of the factors in the model and the strengths of the relationships among these factors. One could also conduct <sup>fi</sup>eld studies in selected healthcare organizations where mobile technology is deployed to determine if its adoption, use and impact can be adequately explained by this theoretical model. Third, future research can also help to develop a more general theoretical perspective of mobile work, drawing from this research, and empirically evaluate such a theory, both quantitatively and qualitatively. This would lead to a better understanding of mobile work as a phenomenon.

This study also informs future practice in multiple ways. First, healthcare organizations should consider portability, task structure, spatial mobility, and system reliability and support amongst the key success factors when incorporating mobile technology into their overall information systems strategy. As shown from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, these factors can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the use of mobile technology by healthcare professionals, their degree of satisfaction with the technology, and realization of the net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.
