# Exploring users’ appropriation and post-implementation managerial intervention in the context of industry IOIS

Juan Rodon,\* Feliciano Sese\* & Ellen Christiaanse<sup>†</sup>

\*Department of Information Systems, ESADE, Ramon Llull University, Spain, email: joan.rodon@esade.edu; email: feliciano.sese@esade.edu and <sup>†</sup>Department of Business Studies, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands, email: P.W.Christiaanse@uva.nl

Abstract. Although the literature has repeatedly shown that inter-organizational information systems (IOIS) are prone to abandonment and low levels of adoption, a great deal of research examining such unsatisfactory outcomes has focused on phenomena occurring in the early stages of the implementation, thus, leaving the post-implementation period under-explored. This paper presents a conceptual lens through which we study two interrelated post-implementation phenomena: managerial intervention and users’ appropriation. Specifically, we develop a struc turational model to examine IOIS management intervention to boost IOIS assimilation, the situated and emergent appropriations of the IOIS that followed and the ongoing adaptations by those intervening in these processes. Actors shape the context for others’ actions by offering modalities of structuring – i.e. new meanings, new procedures, new software applications – which are then taken up in practice by adopters, giving rise to both intended and unintended outcomes. The paper contributes with a multi-level process-based study that (1) conceives IOIS assimilation as an episodic process in which there are dialectical tensions between users and IOIS management; and (2) identifies two forms of managerial intervention targeting diverse aspects of the institutional context that lead to assimilation.

Keywords: post-implementation, appropriation, intervention, assimilation, inter organizational information systems, structuration

## INTRODUCTION

While information systems (IS) literature has long acknowledged the role of management during the implementation of IS (Leonard-Barton & Deschamps, 1988; Cooper & Zmud, 1990) or the role of users during the post-implementation (Orlikowski, 1996; Lapointe & Rivard,

2005), the joint study of managerial intervention and users’ appropriations in the postimplementation period remains an under-explored area (Jasperson et al., 2005). This topic is particularly relevant in the context of inter-organizational information systems (IOIS), where the practitioner literature shows that initial adoption does not necessarily lead to assimilation (Jap & Mohr, 2002; Day et al., 2003). A great deal of research literature, however, primarily focuses on phenomena related to adoption (Robey et al., 2008). This literature provides models tha support management for controlling and shaping the implementation (Iacovou et al., 1995; Gallivan & Depledge, 2003; Teo et al., 2003), but largely neglects the agency of managers and users during the post-implementation period.

The purpose of this paper is to explore two interrelated post-implementation processes in the context of industry IOIS: managerial intervention and users’ appropriation of the system. Specifically, we examine how intervention by IOIS management nurtures the assimilation of the industry IOIS. To explore both processes, we adopt a conceptual lens that applies structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) for studying the link between IOIS management interventions and users’ appropriations of the IOIS and the realization and modification of the intended social structure.

The paper is structured as follows. We begin by providing a definition of industry IOIS and reviewing prior literature on information and communication technology (ICT) appropriation, mediation and assimilation, in order to identify the most important issues that have not been addressed. The research methodology is introduced in Research Site and Method section. Results section follows with the presentation of the results from the case study. In the Discussion section, we discuss the findings of the case. Lastly, the paper ends with contributions and further research.

## LITERATURE BACKGROUND

## Industry inter-organizational IS

An industry IOIS is a socio-technical infrastructure that underpins cross-company relationships and embeds a set of business rules – called an IS industry standard – that are defined by members of the same industry. The implementation of an industry IOIS entails first choreographing cross-company business processes and then drawing up common data definitions to create the IS industry standard (Markus et al., 2006; Bala & Venkatesh, 2007; Boh et al., 2007).

An industry IOIS is usually set up, organized and used by organizations in the same industry that create a consortium (van Baalen et al., 2000; Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 2001). This excludes IOIS owned and/or controlled by dominant members of the industry to support their interactions with non-dominant trading partners. In those consortia, the industry members cooperate in the development of the IOIS in order to increase the efficiency of the whole industry while competing for the same customers.

Given the ownership structure, IOIS management fiat cannot be assumed in this interorganizational context. This means that IOIS management can rarely rely on authority to boos the adoption and use of the system. Hence, users have a great deal of freedom to adopt o reject the system.

Finally, an industry IOIS has to underpin multiple realities for a given practice. That is, in an inter-organizational context, a given practice (i.e. the submission of a transport order) may be performed in a variety of ways (i.e. each pair of firms may understand and execute it differently) (Feldman & Pentland, 2003).

A recent review on IOIS research (Robey et al., 2008) shows that mainstream literature has mainly focused on phenomena related to the implementation (e.g. antecedents to adoption, use) and outcomes (e.g. organizational change, financial performance, benefits) but few IOIS studies have paid attention to phenomena at the post-implementation period. Moreover, although some studies show that industry IOIS implementations are prone to abandonment and low levels of adoption and assimilation (Kumar et al., 1998; Allen et al., 2000), they neithe explore the micro-strategies through which IOIS management copes with unintended outcomes that emerge during the post-implementation, nor the changes in the system that resul from those coping strategies.

## Post-implementation: users’ appropriation and managerial interventions

Assimilation of the IOIS is expected to occur during the post-implementation stage. In this paper, by industry IOIS assimilation we mean the routinization of the use of the IOIS in the processes it supports such that it is woven into the work systems of the diverse adopters. ICT assimilation usually involves two sets of processes: users’ structuring of ICT and mediations from others that influence users’ structuring (Sharma & Yetton, 2003).

The first process includes actions taken by users to appropriate technology features and to adjust them to accomplish their work. There is a stream of literature that shows that ICT appropriation is likely to be painful and problematic and that technology is not always used as intended by designers and management (Markus & Robey, 2004). This vein of research draws attention to the processes of structuration (Orlikowski, 2000) through which users produce and reproduce existing institutional contexts or work around the intended ICT uses (Orlikowski, 1996; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2004; Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Azad & King, 2008). This stream of literature recognizes that the appropriation of ICT involves consequences that may not necessarily be predictable at the start of the implementation (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1996), hence, mutual adaptation between the ICT and the existing institutional context becomes essential (Majchrzak et al., 2000). These studies, however, do not emphasize the deliberate interventions from others (i.e. managers, designers) to shape users’ appropriation of the system, nor the changes in the features of the ICT that result from such interventions.

These shortcomings link with the second process involved in ICT assimilation: the actions from mediators to facilitate assimilation by resolving the potential misfits between the users institutional context and the structures embedded in the new system. Although some of these misfits can be anticipated during the implementation (Soh et al., 2003; Wei et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2006), the diversity of actors and interactions among actors in the context of industry

IOIS makes such anticipation very complex (Hanseth et al., 2006). Therefore, mediations in the post-implementation period are likely to be necessary for the assimilation to occur.

A great deal of papers examining these post-implementation mediation processes draws upon the concept of ‘meta-structuring’ (Orlikowski et al., 1995), which is built on the theory o structuration (Giddens, 1984). ‘Meta-structuring’ refers to the deliberate ongoing alterations of the users’ own structuring of the technology (Orlikowski et al., 1995); in other words, it is a second-order structuring of technology. The utility of the meta-structuring concept stems from its explicit acknowledgement that technology adaptation and assimilation is a gradual process that occurs primarily after the implementation, and is facilitated by ongoing proactive interventions (mediations).

There may be diverse sources of meta-structuring actions: the community of users associated with the system, the peers of users either within the organization or from other organizations, either internal or external IS experts, and managers of users’ organizations (Jasperson et al., 2005). Earlier research has drawn upon the concept of meta-structuring to develop process models (Orlikowski et al., 1995; Yates et al., 1999; Bansler & Havn, 2004; Davidson & Chiasson, 2005) as well as variance models (Purvis et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Sharma & Yetton, 2003; Liang et al., 2006). Within the process research, studies usually show how different types of mediating activities are sequenced in an ongoing process of meta-structuring (Orlikowski et al., 1995). On the other hand, studies that develop variance models examine the impact of diverse types of meta-structuring actions – i.e. management championship, support – on specific constructs: implementation success (Sharma & Yetton, 2003), web assimilation (Chatterjee et al., 2002), enterprise systems assimilation (Liang et al., 2006) and assimilation of case tools (Purvis et al., 2001). These studies, however, do not attend to what mediators and users do with ICT in practice over time, nor pay attention to the situated interactions between mediators and users that shape and are shaped by the ICT features and the institutional context.

## Conceptual lens

Although both post-implementation processes – the users’ structuring of technology and the interventions of mediators – are widely recognized in the literature, they are rarely studied together. Considering the interrelatedness of both processes, this paper examines how each shapes the other during the post-implementation of an industry IOIS. Specifically, the main research issue covered by this paper is how the interactions between IOIS management and users lead to assimilation. To study such issue, we use the conceptual lens in Figure 1, which rests on structuration theory (Giddens, 1984) and adapts existing structurational models in the IS literature (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski et al., 1995; Orlikowski, 2000) to the context of industry lOIS. We consider that structuration theory is a valuable interpretative lens because it allows one to study the link between agency – IOIS management interventions and users appropriations of the IOIS – and realization and modification of the intended social structure.

The conceptual lens contains two layers: context for action and interaction. The context for action refers to the modalities of structuration–interpretive schemes, norms and resources – that users and IOIS management draw upon in their social interactions (Giddens, 1984). For instance, in the context of industry IOIS, a firm workers’ knowledge about the business, the existing procedures and exchange systems (i.e. fax, EDI, email) are the means through which they interact with partner firms. For the purposes of this study, we split the context for action into the users’ existing institutional context and the IOIS’ institutional features.

Figure 1. Conceptual lens.

The users’ existing institutional context refers to the meanings, norms and work procedures, power relations, control and coordination mechanisms, and habits that users instantiate in their daily practices (Orlikowski et al., 1995). Users’ existing institutional context will give them inertia that will influence the assimilation of the IOIS. For instance, Bala & Venkatesh (2007) found that the ability and willingness of firms to overcome inertial mechanisms influence the level of assimilation of inter-organizational business standards that firms reach. On the othe hand, the $| \bigcirc | \bigcirc '$ institutional features embed the intended new social structure that managers expect users will enact in the use of the IOIS. This includes: the official vision and goals underlying the industry IOIS, the technical features of the IOIS and the conceptual schemes and processes embedded in the IS industry standard. All these elements bring meaning to interactions between firms and function as a way of legitimizing the IOIS as they supply a normative frame for appropriate behaviours (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Griffith, 1999). Contrary to users’ institutional context, the institutional features of the IOIS are a force fostering change. Therefore, during the post-implementation, both parts of the context for action will compete for domination.

The layer of interaction refers to the actions and interactions in which users and IOIS management engage in the day-to-day basis (Giddens, 1984). It encompasses the users appropriation of the IOIS as well as the interventions from IOIS management to shape users appropriations. Within the context of industry IOIS, IOIS management and users/adopters draw upon the users’ institutional context and the institutional features of the IOIS as they act, simultaneously producing and reproducing the structural conditions that make action possible.

When users try to appropriate the IOIS, they draw on the characteristics of their institutional context (arrow 1 in Figure 1) and the institutional features of the IOIS (arrow 2). In turn, users actions can have several effects: either serving to reinforce the existing institutional context, slightly modify it or radically change it (arrow 3). Although users tend to perpetuate their institutional context – especially at the outset of use – because they have internalized those institutions as guiding principles of action (Giddens, 1984), sometimes their action may have unintended consequences on their institutional context. On the other hand, users’ actions may also have some effect on the institutional features of the IOIS (arrow 4). For instance, users may adjust the IOIS in light of their work contingencies (Orlikowski et al., 1995). Or users refusal to use an IOIS may damage its credibility. When the users’ existing institutional context is aligned with the IOIS’ institutional features, users will probably enact the intended socia structure of the IOIS, and hence, it will be reinforced. However, if the structure enacted in the use of the IOIS contradicts the existing one, users will probably circumvent the intended uses of the IOIS – either ignoring or working around certain technical features, conceptual schemes or processes (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994).

On the other hand, IOIS management tries intentionally and reflexively to shape the assimilation process by influencing the users’ structuring of the IOIS. Those managerial interventions may be constrained or enabled by the users’ institutional context (arrow 5) as well as by the IOIS’ institutional features (arrow 6). For instance, users’ habits may condition management decision to launch a new functionality. When the structure enacted in use is not the intended one, IOIS management can intervene to alter or reinforce either the users’ institutional context (arrow 7), or, should their prior assumptions prove wrong, IOIS management can also alter the institutional features of the IOIS (arrow 8). The literature on strategy formation has identified two forms of managerial interventions to shape the strategy process, (Jarzabkowski, 2008)namely, interactive and procedural strategizing. We integrate these two forms of intervention in our conceptual lens as they are also valid in the context of industry IOIS. Specifically, interactive interventions consist of framing and sense-giving encounters (i.e. meetings, presentations, training sessions, press articles), through which IOIS management tries to shape users’ interpretations (arrow 7). On the other hand, through procedural interventions IOIS managers aim to change the institutional features of the IOIS that users draw upon in the use of the system (arrow 8). In procedural interventions IOIS management does not have a direct contact with users.

We contend that the need for IOIS management interventions will largely depend on the degree of alignment or congruence between the users’ institutional context and the institutional features of the IOIS. The more contradictions that exist between both, the more likely that tensions and conflict – for instance, in the form of rejection to use – will emerge, and thus the more the interventions will be required. Therefore, in using the conceptual lens, we adopt a dialectic perspective (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) on assimilation, in which users’ institutional context and the institutional features of the IOIS will compete for domination and confront each other. They are expected to evolve throughout the post-implementation period and collide to produce a synthesis (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995) – the institutional features of the IOIS become part of the users’ institutional context. Otherwise, assimilation will not occur.

## RESEARCH SITE AND METHOD

The aim of the study is to explore how users’ appropriation of an industry IOIS as well as ongoing IOIS management interventions during the post-implementation period lead to assimilation. The achievement of this goal requires observing and interpreting actors’ constructions and accounts, and looking at processes of change rather than snapshots, which suggests the use of intensive qualitative methods. Accordingly, we chose to conduct a process-oriented case study that emphasizes the agency of individual actors and the situated nature of action during the implementation process. This paper is concerned with discovering processes, not in terms of stages following a given chronological order, but rather as chains of changes in the patterns of action between actors and in the context for those actions (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Because the research focus was management and user agency, the research was conducted from an interpretive perspective (Walsham, 1995).

## Research setting

The research setting selected for this study was the seaport of Barcelona, and the focus of analysis was that of the implementation and post-implementation of an industry IOIS. This setting was well suited to explore the links between ongoing managerial interventions and assimilation because (1) the promoters of the IOIS – the set of organizations that institutionally and economically supported the creation of PortIC: Port Authority, the Chamber of Commerce of Barcelona and the trade associations representing the private companies that operate in the port: stevedores, freight forwarders, clearing agents and shipping agents – had admitted that the implementation process has not turned out as expected; (2) we had good access to longitudinal data from several sources; and (3) we had been following the implementation process since its inception in 1999.

PortIC is an industry IOIS at the seaport of Barcelona that was launched in mid-1999 afte several attempts in the 1990s to boost the use of ICT to support the interactions among companies in the Port Community of Barcelona. According to its promoters, PortIC aimed to coordinate the activity of the firms in the port’s landside transport network (which encompasses the transport of goods between the port and any place in the hinterland and vice versa) and to integrate all the information being exchanged between the various port agents. PortIC design would be based on the standard defined by the Telematic Forum (TelFor). TelFor is a consortium that was formed in 1994 to coordinate the development and maintenance of a vendor-neutral standard for the inter-organizational processes as well as the syntax and semantics of the messages exchanged in those processes within the seaport of Barcelona.

PortIC was expected to integrate the diverse agents in the Port Community with three main goals: (1) reduce the operational costs of port agents; (2) speed up the passage of goods within the port; and (3) provide transparency to the document exchanges and to the movements of cargo in order to reveal inefficiencies. To achieve these goals PortIC would (1) capture the information produced in any exchange within the seaport, thus, avoiding the need to retype data, substituting paper and reducing the errors and processing costs; (2) centralize all the information of the seaport; and (3) provide real-time information to facilitate the documentary track and trace of goods.

PortIC is characterized by the fact that it is owned and used by the members of the port community. These stakeholders set up a company in 1999 to manage the operation of PortIC. The company offered three types of services: (1) private-to-public exchanges – exchanges between a private organization and a public body – e.g. cargo manifests, customs request; (2) private-to-private exchanges – e.g. booking, gate-in notification; and (3) information services that allow the real-time documentary track and trace of goods. This case focuses on the study of private-to-private exchanges. Although the development of PortIC started in early 1999 and it went live with some services 1 year later, the speed of adoption and use of the private-toprivate exchanges has been much slower than promoters expected (Figure 2).

## Data collection and analysis

Our data collection involved interviews, meetings observation and documents. We followed in real-time the post-implementation of PortIC for 5 years (from 2001 to 2005) and used 8 years of archival data (1993–2000) about the initiation and development phases. The first author conducted 27 semi-structured interviews (each about 1 hour long, see Table 1), observed users interacting with PortIC in their daily work, and attended meetings over a 9-month period (March 2005–November 2005). The first author also collected a wide variety of documents for analysis: meeting minutes, press articles, internal notes and mailing lists.

We started with an open sample, which consisted of 11 port agents operating in the seaport of Barcelona that were accepted to participate in the study, plus the company managing PortIC and the Port Authority of Barcelona. We selected those port agents based on the following criteria: (1) companies that had been successfully using the IOIS as well as companies tha had failed or rejected the use of the IOIS; (2) companies that played various roles (e.g. freight forwarders, haulers, shipping agents, inland terminals) in the transport chain; and (3) companies that had business relationships among themselves, and hence were supposed to use the IOIS coordinately to carry out their operations.

Data collection aimed at identifying and understanding the relevant events that shaped the post-implementation. Each interview focused on the interviewee’s company, background and experience in the company, his/her vision on the IOIS and the relevant events and problems that shape his/her views and actions during the post-implementation. Some of these events and problems were repeated by more than one informant, thus, we could compare the various interpretations. We traced those events, the actions in response to those events adopted by the diverse actors, especially those of the IOIS management, and the consequences of those actions. Likewise, regular visits to the diverse sites provided observation and background data on the post-implementation period.

The main unit of analysis of our case was the post-implementation period. We carried out data analysis in two main stages. First, we used the coding for process method proposed by Strauss & Corbin (1998) to analyse the data. This coding technique involves the analysis of data in terms of conditions, actions and consequences. Four major themes emerged from this first stage (see Rodon & Pastor, 2007 for the details on the coding process). These themes referred to sets of interventions from IOIS management that proved effective in increasing the use of the IOIS. We note that in this paper we do not judge whether the IOIS was used effectively or in a complete way, for instance, in terms of number of functionalities or level of process integration.

In the second stage, we re-analysed those four themes according to the conceptual lens proposed in this paper (see Figure 1). We called those series of interactions episodes of assimilation and represented them with diagrams (see Figure 3). In those diagrams we bracketed the action/interaction and context for action into diverse time periods to analyze the shifts between the two (Barley & Tolbert, 1997). ## RESULTS

This section presents the four episodes of assimilation. For reasons of word count, we only provide quotations for the first episode. The four episodes may be seen as forming a continuum: the second, third and fourth episodes run simultaneously and follow the first one.

## Episode of assimilation 1: customizing the IOIS to adopters’ reality

One of the premises underlying the design of PortIC was that it was based on a standard tha had been agreed upon by the same potential adopters. PortIC would reflect a unique and common set of work practices and port agents would adapt to it and introduce organizationa changes to take advantage of PortIC when required. Accordingly, PortIC was designed following a set of business process and messages defined in the standard (a) (Figure 4).

![](/api/attachments/ZVJG696J/fulltext/images/8c67757042c64b12fe8d95c6024c7fc690af9d79b8c98d8d055e1233c1d61527.jpg)  
Figure 4. Customizing the IOIS to adopters’ reality.

However, the middle managers of adopting firms in this study (i.e. IT and operations managers) merely saw PortIC as a glorified mailman, which would receive messages and would forward them on to the right target. So these managers neither envisaged introducing changes to their organizational structures nor to their internal business processes. They expected PortIC would be a tool that simply replaced the existing electronic and paper-based exchange systems; therefore, these managers expected at the most that they would interface their IS with PortIC. Once users of these organizations started using PortIC, they realized that the practices supported by PortIC challenged the existing ones. Then they resisted the use of PortIC, moved back to previous systems, and complained to their managers, who in turn redirected the complaint to PortIC management, arguing that there was a gap between the content of the standard that was supported by PortIC – the flows and content of some of the messages – and their actual work practices (b).

IOIS management and promoters considered those complaints as damaging to the cred ibility of the whole project and had an adverse effect on their efforts to attract new customers (c). Because neither PortIC management nor promoters could impose the use of the system, they opted for a discursive strategy (d). PortIC management and some promoters (presidents of trade associations, president of the standardization committee, management of the Por Authority) wrote press articles, gave presentations and had numerous scheduled and informa meetings with top managers of potential adopters aiming to persuade and convince them to become active users of PortIC. In these interactions they stressed the idea that PortIC was a collective initiative at the port community that would benefit everybody: ‘We are all part of PortIC’ (president of TelFor) and at the same time they emphasized the need for change: ‘PortIC is a system that is easy for companies to use. But this does not prevent the fear of the unknown in the form of internal change and a change in the way we think about our daily activity’ (CEO of the Port Authority of Barcelona).

However, PortIC management realized that this discursive strategy seemed to have the intended effect on top management of potential adopters but that effect diluted in the transmission of the discourse from top management to their middle management (e.g. operations managers, IT managers, etc.) (e). The top management rarely deploy resources for the adoption of PortIC, so the integration with PortIC was perceived by middle managers as extra work: ‘[the integration with PortIC] is not in their work plan, and in addition, nobody prioritizes that task’ (PortIC manager). Later users discovered that some of the prior inconsistencies in the work practices supported by PortIC still remained. They strongly complained to their managers and to PortIC management, arguing that the use of PortIC had lowered their productivity and damaged customer service. Then, the top management of those firms reneged on the prior commitment to use PortIC. Because PortIC management noticed that the discursive strategy would require a lot of time to permeate the middle managers and end users, and hence, usage would not rise in the short-term, they adopted a new strategy: customize PortIC to users’ reality (f): ‘the main change has been that we do not insist so much in that our customer adapts to PortIC, rather PortIC adapts to the customer . . . at least with those customers that are worth the effort’ (PortIC manager). For instance, in the case of exports procedures, TelFor members had agreed that truck drivers would not need to show any paper-based documentation to enter the inland terminal provided that the hauler had previously specified the driver identity in electronically submitted ‘pre-arrival notification’. However, once PortIC had implemented the procedure, inland terminals objected to it arguing they had never worked that way. Then PortIC made some additional changes, which lay outside TelFor’s scope, to persuade inland terminals. A manager at PortIC who participates in TelFor notes:

There was initial resistance from inland terminals to accept this procedure. They argued that with the ‘acceptance order’ message was enough. But that means that the truck driver wil have to bring a paper-based copy of the ‘acceptance order’. Finally we agreed with inland terminals that haulers could submit the ‘pre-arrival notification’ and that PortIC would translate that message into the ‘acceptance order’ format before submitting it to the inland terminal . . . In that way the inland terminal did not have to adopt a new procedure for pre-arrival notification messages.

This customization of PortIC to the adopters’ practices allowed the adopters to avoid making changes to their business processes and databases (g). Interoperability between PortIC and adopters’ systems was mainly accomplished through conversion tables, implemented either by PortIC or by the adopters, but never by changing the adopters’ data models. Adopters basically interfaced their systems with PortIC. A systems developer of an inland terminal notes:

We use the call-sign to identify a vessel. PortIC, however, uses the Lloyds number. In the case of ‘acceptance order’ I receive the Lloyds number from PortIC. Then I periodically upload the master tables of vessels from the Port Authority to our AS400 and cross those tables with ours, in order to retrieve the call-sign, and store it in our system.

Although this customization was successful because it led to a considerable increase in the number of messages exchanged through PortIC (i), it also caused new concerns to IOIS management (h). First, as a result of the several customizations, the procedures implemented at PortIC started deviating from the standard as approved by TelFor. Second, these customi zations to users’ reality jeopardized the adaptability of PortIC to future changes. And finally, the maintenance costs of PortIC increased.

## Episode of assimilation 2: mediating the agreement between adopters on the use of the IOIS

In the scenario prior to PortIC, the systems of different companies were seldom automatically integrated with one other. The interconnection of those systems was usually mediated by people who retyped incoming messages in their systems. In doing that task, they interpreted the content of those messages, and if there were any mistakes they corrected them. In the scenario with PortIC, the different systems were supposed to integrate with PortIC once for all the partners. In doing so, users that received a message from PortIC avoided having to retype it, as it would be automatically stored in their systems. PortIC was supposed to check the syntactical and partially the semantic correctness of messages. However, the fact that a message is syntactically correct does not mean that it is semantically correct. For instance, data may be located in other fields, rendering the message syntactically correct but semantically incorrect. These inconsistencies occurred quite often because senders used message fields for purposes other than the standard set. So what happened was that as firms gradually started using PortIC the number of mistakes related with the processing of messages rose, and in turn this led to more errors in the physical dispatch of goods and the customer service of those firms (a) (Figure 5).

Then users complained to their managers and to PortIC management about the inconsistencies of some messages that passed through PortIC and the subsequent negative impact on customer service. Users noted they had always worked that way, so PortIC should suppor them (b). These complaints damaged the credibility of the project and threatened the capacity to gain new customers (c). The PortIC implementation team, however, had no way of detecting most of these inconsistencies because controls usually worked at the syntax level. Fo instance, PortIC could not control errors in text fields that were used for physical addresses or free text. So they became aware that the way a sender – either a human or a technological system – generated a message hindered the receiver from automatically integrating that message into his in-house systems. Then PortIC management considered that these anomalies in the functioning could be largely solved if the diverse users (which belong to differen companies) agreed on the way they filled and processed the messages. Then PortIC management selected set of interrelated user firms of different type (e.g. inland terminal, shipping agent, hauler and depot) that had business with each other – namely, constellations – and held meetings with them, where they discussed, negotiated and agreed on the semantics and pragmatics of message exchanges among those firms. Afterwards, the implementation team adapted PortIC to the new agreed work practices (d).

Figure 5. Mediating the agreement between adopters.

This agreement at the level of use within constellations helped substantially reduce the number of errors in messages but also had several unintended consequences for users (e). First, end users have more and better knowledge about the work of their partners, as well as about the impact of their actions on their partners. Second, the integration with new partners is now easier because, after each agreement, firms get some knowledge that will prove useful in subsequent integration projects. Thus there is an ongoing learning on how to integrate with partners. Third, when fax was the normal exchange mechanism, addressees (clerks) had to retype messages; during the retyping they interpreted the content of the messages and if there was a mistake they corrected it. However, once a firm integrates its systems with PortIC, clerks do not have to retype incoming messages but check whether they are semantically correct. Therefore, the nature of the clerks’ work has changed – from data entry to message checking on the screen. Some of the companies in this study recognized they had to train their employees into the new way of working. On the other hand, this agreement within constellations also allowed the PortIC implementation team to speed future integrations of new users with PortIC (f).

## Episode of assimilation 3: extending the service to non-adopters

As firms gradually started interfacing and using PortIC, they realized that only a few of thei partners were already using PortIC. This meant that these early adopters of PortIC had to maintain several systems (e.g. PortIC, fax, other EDI systems) to interact with partners. Then these firms expressed this new requirement (a), and PortIC management proposed making a slight change to the standard. This change consisted of adding an optional field to all the messages for the email of the addressee (b). This would allow PortIC users to send messages the same way whether the addressee had adopted PortIC or not. PortIC would check the identity of the addressee. In the event that the addressee was a non-user of PortIC, the PortIC system would forward a message to the email address instead. This intervention pleased current users as they expected to see an increase in the use of the PortIC (c) (Figure 6).

![](/api/attachments/ZVJG696J/fulltext/images/84bb288dcd4e288dae18ffdcd79262294312bfb7693300bf3a0d73132366fe9a.jpg)

However, most of the non-users of PortIC gave the emails of middle managers (i.e. sales, IT, export and import) but not of the person-in-charge of message processing (usually a clerk in the import, export or transport departments). The first outcome was that these middle managers usually did not forward the message to the right person inside their companies, did not read it or deleted it by mistake. Hence, the intended targets of those messages, the clerks, neither received them nor were aware of what their managers did. Later on, the clerks called the sender to chase things up. Then senders felt surprised and worried because although they had specified the right email address in the message, their intended target had not received the message. This created uncertainty to senders with regard to their communications with partners through PortIC. First, they felt that through the intermediation of PortIC the communication with partners had become depersonalized. Second, they considered that the use of PortIC displaced the responsibility for the smooth running of the workflow from the addressee to the sender. Before the IOIS was in place, once a sender has submitted a message, he/she has freed herself from monitoring the workflow. In case of an event, the addressee would contact the sender who would resubmit the message. However, once the IOIS was in place, senders felt that after having submitted a message they still had to follow the process to make sure that the addressee had received it. Lastly, they considered they had less control over the business operations, which, in turn, had a negative impact on the service they provided to customers (d).

These outcomes created uncertainty among users (senders) and led them to start mistrusting the system and the PortIC management, who had offered a solution to their partners non-adoption of PortIC, which had only made things worse (users felt vulnerable). So when these errors occurred, senders managed to find a solution by working around PortIC. For instance, they resubmitted the message through other channels (fax or email), but did not notify their management or PortIC management about the solution they had chosen (e). Thus the entire workflow was partially fulfilled through PortIC and partially through other channels (fax, email, phone or paper-based). As PortIC had no proof of the messages sent through other channels, it could not coordinate and synchronize the flow of exchanges. PortIC was not able to track the status of document exchanges, which sometimes generated error messages to the parties (users) who then got more confused and distrustful of the system (f). A set of problems was reported as a result of this duplication of messages. For instance, some shipping agents complained to PortIC because they sometimes received duplicate ‘gate-out notification’ messages – messages with the same ‘release order’ number – from depots. After some analysis, the PortIC implementation team discovered that the reason was that haulers, in order to make their work easier and faster, made photocopies of former ‘release-order’ messages and gave them to depots. Later the depots, in order to submit the ‘gate-out notification’, used the ‘release-order’ number that the hauler had used.

Then PortIC managers responded (g) by introducing two measures. First, they added automatic mechanisms in the application to control inconsistencies over flow of the messages – i.e. duplication of messages. Second, they defined new working procedures and created a new role within the implementation team to ensure that any company, which was not integrated with PortIC but wanted to receive messages by email, had internally organized their procedures so that the right addressee (clerk) was the one receiving the message. This new role monitored those new adopters until there were no errors in the exchanges. Because these measures succeeded, the new role became permanent (h). On the users’ side, there was a decrease in the number of error messages generated by PortIC as a result of inconsistent exchanges (i), and those users moved back to PortIC to send their messages.

## Episode of assimilation 4: capitalizing on unintended appropriations of the IOIS

Another premise underlying the design of PortIC was that it should provide access to all the firms in the Port regardless of their in-house systems (a). Hence, PortIC’s design was based on the Internet. Firms could send and receive messages in the formats defined by TelFor (EDIFACT, XML and flat file) by using various services (FTP, email). Likewise, companies that were not ready for these services or data formats should be able to use PortIC. Accordingly, a web-based application, namely FrontEnd, was designed for these cases. FrontEnd run on personal computers and was used for the generation and reception of messages. It was designed to facilitate the transmission of information between agents that were not able to integrate their systems with PortIC, and was expected to be used marginally (b) (Figure 7).

However, when firms started using FrontEnd, things turned out differently. Most of the firms used their in-house applications only for message generation, but they used FrontEnd (1) to check the status of their outgoing messages, for instance, to check whether those messages

That is, FrontEnd became the preferred medium for users even though they had an alternative in-house (d). We identified two reasons for this massive use of FrontEnd that was not intended nor anticipated by FrontEnd’s designers. First, most of the users felt more confident doing a double check on messages: FrontEnd on one side and their in-house application on the other. Second, IT managers of these firms did not want to do extra work by integrating all incoming messages, given that they considered some of those messages had little value to their firms and moreover, their superiors (top management) had not asked them to do so. They believed the cost that end users manually process these incoming messages was much less than the effort of integrating them into their systems. Accordingly, the use of FrontEnd became routinized (e).

However, given that FrontEnd had been designed assuming a small group of users, this unexpected success of FrontEnd worsened its performance, which in turn penalized users’ work productivity (f). Then users started complaining about the speed of FrontEnd and the subsequent worsening of the service provided by PortIC (g). Consequently, PortIC managers, perceiving that those complaints damaged their reputation (h), first decided to increase the processing capacity of the system in order to accommodate a larger number of users. Then they considered redesigning FrontEnd in order for users to easily and rapidly view messages (i). The first results from resizing the hardware were (1) better response times of the FrontEnd; and (2) a perception by users and developers alike that the system was more stable than hitherto.

## DISCUSSION

The purpose of this case was to examine the ongoing interplay of managerial interventions and users’ appropriation during the post-implementation of an industry IOIS. Through the conceptual lens in Figure 1, our results show how the assimilation process is both shaped by users as well as IOIS management.

The four episodes of assimilation illustrate the tensions between users and IOIS management derived from the contradictions between the users’ institutional context and the institutional features of the IOIS, the deliberate interventions from IOIS management to influence users’ ongoing behaviour, the situated and emergent change processes that followed, and the ongoing adaptations by interveners to those processes. Interveners shape the context for others’ actions by offering modalities of structuring (Giddens, 1984) – i.e. new meanings, software applications – which are then taken up in practice by users to produce intended as well as unintended outcomes.

## Triggers for managerial interventions

The interventions from IOIS management are triggered by (1) users’ resistance to the institutional features of the IOIS; (2) unintended appropriations of the IOIS; and (3)

undesired unintended outcomes on users’ institutional context that result from IOIS appropriation.

While the IOIS management may have an intended structure they wish to bring about, the shaping of action is by no means a deterministic process. Users as knowledgeable and self-reflexive actors, enact a process of sense making through their institutional context that may differ from those of IOIS management. Accordingly, users may resist the institutional features of the IOIS or appropriate it in unintended ways. In the first case, from the outset the official vision of the IOIS was that it would support a common set of practices that were part of the standard that port agents had agreed by consensus and the IOIS would be designed in the belief that adopters would change their working practices accordingly. This official vision, however, overestimated the commonality of the work procedures embedded in the standard, and underestimated the subjective understandings of adopters and how they would adjust and adapt to those procedures. Accordingly, after the first 2 years, the IOIS was perfunctory used. As time passed and adopters persisted in their former work practices, the official vision of a unique, common reality that everybody would adapt to was progressively replaced by one that was closer to tailored standardization, and the customization of the IOIS to adopters’ reality took precedence. Therefore, users’ resistance to a core aspect of the institutional features of the IOIS – the official vision – caused IOIS management to intervene.

On the other hand, in the fourth episode, the generalized success of FrontEnd was unintended for the IOIS management. FrontEnd was intended to support those who lagged behind in terms of technological capabilities. However, the use of FrontEnd increased the sense of confidence and security of users in the practice of checking the status of messages or the retrieval of old messages, and accordingly, they massively used FrontEnd in their daily work. FrontEnd became the preferred medium for monitoring messages. Then, the IOIS management intervened to capitalize on this unintended outcome by adapting the FrontEnd application in order to support this routinized practice.

Finally, users’ unacknowledged conditions or the impossibility to reshape their institutional context resulted in appropriations of the industry IOIS that produced undesired unintended consequences on their own institutional context, which in turn, triggered management to intervene. For instance, in the second and third episodes, users did not work around the intended uses of the IOIS. But their use of the IOIS had negative unintended consequences on their institutional context – in the form of damage to customer service – because addresses did not accommodate their working procedures and technological systems. This in turn generated some conflict between those adopters, and between adopters and IOIS management, who intervened again into the institutional features of the IOIS and the users’ institutional context in order to resolve the conflict.

## The focus of interventions

From the case we identify multiple intervention actions: changes in the content of standard (i.e message, processes), changes in the technical features of the IOIS (i.e. software routines, processing capacity), changes in the procedures (i.e. integration for a new customer), use training, setting up new roles in the IOIS to support adopters’ integration, mediating sensemaking meetings between adopters, presentations (i.e. of new services, yearly wrap-up) or launch of new services to fulfil user-demands. With regard to the focus – elements of the contex for action – these intervention actions may be regarded as either interactive or procedural.

The interactive interventions enable IOIS management to reinforce their own interpretations of reality as-to-be as well as negotiate these interpretations with adopters. For instance, in the first episode, IOIS promoters and management intensified the persuasive actions aiming to get the commitment from the top management of potential adopters in order to become active users of the IOIS. In this sense-giving process they had direct, face-to-face interactions with top managers where they emphasized the idea that the IOIS supported a common reality that would benefit all. With these interventions management sought to unify users’ interpretive schemes regarding the need to adapt to that reality and the use of the IOIS. In the second episode IOIS management mediated the sense-making and negotiations within constellations of firms – sets of interrelated firms that have business with each other. Through IOIS management mediation, these firms were able to reach a commonality of meanings in their work practices within their constellation.

On the other hand. the procedural interventions. which shape the institutional features of the IOIS, may be located at the level of work practices (i.e. content of the standard, roles of people, skills) and technical features (i.e. capacity of the hardware, software, telecommunications). For instance, in the second episode, once firms agreed on the work practices at the constellations level, the software applications of PortIC supporting those practices had to be changed. In the third episode, they adapted the standard and the design of PortIC to enable non-adopters to receive emails, and later they defined new procedures, job positions and roles to support the integration of non-adopters. In the fourth episode, the web-based application was redeveloped to accommodate the unintended uses.

## Interventions, malleability of institutional context and assimilation

The four episodes led to an increase in the assimilation of the industry IOIS, that is, the industry IOIS became part of users’ behavioural routine. Such assimilation depended on the interventions from IOIS management to shape the users’ institutional context and the institutiona features of the IOIS.

On the one hand, interactive interventions proved effective to shape some interpretive schemes, re-establish trust on the system and rebuild IOIS management reputation, but proved insufficient to shape other aspects of the users’ institutional context – i.e. working procedures, habits – that were necessary for assimilation to occur. For instance, in the first episode of assimilation (see Figure 8 below), IOIS management first persuaded top management of adopters to get their commitment to use the IOIS (arrow 1). These top managers then passed that request to their middle managers and end users, but did not deploy allocative resources – i.e. time, extra budget – to support them. They simply drew upon authoritative resources (arrow 2). Later, in the daily use of the IOIS, end users found inconsistencies that challenged their work practices and they complained to their managers arguing against the use of the IOIS as it had a negative impact on their productivity and customer service (arrow 3), thus, they influenced their management by drawing on allocative resources. Then top management interpreted that the use of PortIC penalized their business, and accordingly, reneged on the commitment they had made to IOIS management (arrow 4). Then, given that interactive interventions alone did not succeed – that is, they were not sufficient to increase the malleability of users’ institutional context – the IOIS management decided to combine them with procedural ones – i.e. customizing the IOIS (arrow 5).

![](/api/attachments/ZVJG696J/fulltext/images/352a1d67bbea448be7d21254826bed669c845df45d31f2959c026313b7e94fc0.jpg)  
Figure 8. Combining interactive and procedural interventions (in italics the main modalities of structuration that actors draw upon in their actions).

From the case we provide an explanation for why interactive interventions alone were insufficient to bring assimilation. Some inconsistencies in work practices are difficult to detect only through interactions at the level of interpretive schemes – for instance, in plenary meetings between IOIS management and adopters. Therefore, those inconsistencies appear later in using the IOIS. Then IOIS management, being aware of their lack of power over adopters and the ineffectiveness of those interactive interventions in increasing the malleability of users’ institutional context, opted for adapting the institutional features of the IOIS – that is, they performed procedural interventions.

On the other hand, the second and third episodes show that crafting the malleability of users institutional context is difficult because of the multiplicity of independencies between firms. That is, the malleability of users’ institutional context is contingent upon the interdependencies that a firm may have with partners. This entails that IOIS assimilation occurs at various levels:

firm, constellation and community-wide. For instance, the first episode shows assimilation a the firm level as the IOIS was customized to the needs of individual firms. In the second and third episodes, assimilation changed from firm to constellation level as new meanings and procedures were mobilized at that level. Assimilation in the fourth episode occurred at the firm and community-wide levels.

We also note that the three levels of assimilation recursively depend on each other. In the fourth episode, individual firms adopted the web-based application for control purposes regardless of others doing it. However, such firm-level assimilation was conditioned by others submitting messages through the IOIS. That is, community-wide assimilation of the web-based application resulted in positive network effects that influenced the firm-level assimilation. Regarding constellation-level assimilation, it depends on the coordinated efforts of the members of the constellation to assimilate the industry IOIS. For instance, individual firms assimilation is shaped by how the other members of the constellation assimilate the IOIS.

## CONTRIBUTIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

In this paper we suggested a conceptual lens to study managerial interventions and users appropriation of industry IOIS that extends the literature. First, we add to the technology mediation literature as it has argued that ICT that are more specialized and fixed may need less mediation than more generic, open-ended technologies (Orlikowski et al., 1995). This paper, however, demonstrates that mediation for already contextualized information systems, the case of industry IOIS, is also essential for assimilation to take place. There are two explanations for this. On the one hand, although industry IOIS strongly inscribe practices that are tailored to a given context, and hence, which are supposed to limit users’ potentia workarounds, the multiplicity of actors and interactions among them makes unacknowledged conditions and unintended appropriations pervasive. On the other hand, the power relationships between IOIS management and adopters are very diffuse, which means that it is unlikely that the initial intended institutional features of the IOIS will be the final outcome and hence adopters have a great margin of manoeuvre in the way they adopt and use the IOIS.

Second, technology mediation literature acknowledges that the assimilation of technology can be facilitated by the ongoing interventions of mediators on the users’ structuring of technology. We add to this literature (Orlikowski et al., 1995; Davidson & Chiasson, 2005) as we not only focus on the agency from mediators – IOIS management – but also on the ongoing interactions between users and mediators; how each actor shapes others’ actions, the emergent change processes that subsequently occur, and how this leads to assimilation. On the other hand, these authors (Orlikowski et al., 1995; Davidson & Chiasson, 2005) related mediation activities to the moments in which they occur – establishment, reinforcement, adjustment and episodic change. In this paper, we view technology mediation not as linear stages but as episodes involving dialectical tensions and accordingly we contend that an assimilation path can be unlikely preset. We classify interventions according to their target into interactive and procedural. Interactive interventions aim to shape users’ interpretive schemes by targeting their institutional context. Procedural interventions aim to change the institutional features of the IOIS. We concur with prior literature that the effectiveness of mediation wil depend on the malleability of structure (Majchrzak et al., 2000). In our case, the effectiveness of managerial interventions largely depended on the malleability of the institutional features of the IOIS and hence, interactive interventions that target users’ institutional context, proved insufficient. Assimilation resulted from the combination of both forms of interventions. Moreover, we suggest that in the context of industry IOIS, the malleability of a firm’s institutional context depends not only on that firm’s willingness to adapt its institutional context, but also on the coordinated effort of firms that form a constellation.

Third, a mainstream research on ICT assimilation has studied the impact of various forms of mediation on assimilation (Purvis et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Sharma & Yetton, 2003; Liang et al., 2006). These studies propose variance models that examine the effects of concrete managerial interventions – i.e. management championship, knowledge embeddedness, instituting performance controls, establishing coordination mechanisms – on ICT assimilation. That is, they usually model assimilation as a linear function of managerial interventions. Moreover, the level of analysis of these studies has been that of the firm or the group – aggregate of individuals within the firm. In contrast, through the lens proposed in this paper, we develop an account that locates and explains the assimilation process and that allows us to study it at multiple levels — firm. constellation and community-wide. We conceptualize assimi lation not as a direct function of managerial interventions but as the ongoing interplay between users and managers, and thus, both collectives are proactive shapers of the assimilation process. Accordingly, we show that while some technology mediation actions may be generalized and determined ex ante, the dynamics of assimilation may be better viewed as situated episodes involving dialectical tensions between users and mediators.

This paper raises a number of topics for further research. First, future studies should explore other sources of mediation within the context of industry IOIS. For instance, in our research setting, we have observed that mediations from middle managers of adopting organizations are relevant as those actors are the ones that actually interpret the potential changes in the users’ institutional context that the new IOIS entails, hence, those middle managers can increase the malleability of the users’ institutional context. Second, future research is needed to understand the influence of mediations on the degree of assimilation – for instance, habitual, extended or innovative forms of assimilation. Finally, we have shown that assimilation of IOIS occurs at multiple levels. Hence, another avenue for further research is the analysis of the diverse levels of assimilation and the relationship between them.

To sum up, we believe this study enables a rich reading of agents’ – users and managers – implication in the post-implementation of industry IOIS (Jasperson et al., 2005). Specifically, this paper shows the dynamic interplay amongst users and IOIS management during the post-implementation period and provides a view for the study of assimilation that complements prior process models (Orlikowski et al., 1995; Davidson & Chiasson, 2005) and variance models (Purvis et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Liang et al., 2006). Our data suggest that, in the context of industry IOIS, the post-implementation period is characterized by episodes in which the confrontation between users’ institutional context and the institutional features of the

IOIS generate dialectical cycles. We consider that this practice-oriented study about interventions and assimilation offers new possibilities for conceptualizing the management of IOIS pos implementation.
