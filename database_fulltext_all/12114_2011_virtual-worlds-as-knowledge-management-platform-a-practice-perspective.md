# Virtual worlds as knowledge management platform – a practice-perspective

Julia Mueller,\* Katja Hutter,<sup>†</sup> Johann Fueller<sup>‡</sup> & Kurt Matzler<sup>§</sup>

\*Innsbruck University School of Management, Department of Strategic Management, Marketing and Tourism, Universitaetsstr. 15, A-6020 Innsbruck, Austria, email: julia.mueller@uibk.ac.at, <sup>†</sup>Innsbruck University School of Management, Department of Strategic Management, Marketing and Tourism, Universitaetsstr. 15, A-6020 Innsbruck, Austria, email: katja.hutter@uibk.ac.at, <sup>‡</sup>HYVE AG, Schellingstr. 45 80799 Munich, Germany, email: johann.fueller@hyve.de, and <sup>§</sup>Innsbruck University School of Management, Department of Strategic Management, Marketing and Tourism, Universitaetsstr. 15, A-6020 Innsbruck, Austria, email: kurt.matzler@uibk.ac.at

Abstract. Virtual worlds, as electronic environments where individuals can interact in a realistic manner in form of avatars, are increasingly used by gamers, consumers and employees. Therefore, they provide opportunities for reinventing business processes. Especially, effective knowledge management (KM) requires the use of appropriate information and communication technology (ICT) as well as social interaction. Emerging virtual worlds enable new ways to support knowl edge and knowing processes because these virtual environments consider social aspects that are necessary for knowledge creating and knowledge sharing processes. Thus, collaboration in virtual worlds resembles real-life activities. In this paper, we shed light on the use of Second Life (SL) as a KM platform in a real-life setting. To explore the potential and current usage of virtual worlds for knowledge and knowing activities, we conducted a qualitative study at IBM. We interviewed IBM employees belonging to a special workgroup called ‘Web 2.0/ virtual worlds’ in order to gain experience in generating and exchanging knowledge by virtually collaborating and interacting. Our results show that virtual worlds – if they are able to overcome problems like platform stability, user interface or security issues – bear the potential to serve as a KM platform. They facilitate global and simultaneous interaction, create a common context for collaboration, combine different tools for communication and enhance knowledge and knowing processes.

Keywords: knowledge management, organisational learning, information and communication technologies, virtual worlds, second life

## INTRODUCTION

An organisation’s knowledge and its capabilities to absorb knowledge are regarded as decisive sources of competitive advantage (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Grant, 1996; Davenport & Prusak, 1998) leading to a focus on knowledge management (KM) (Alavi & Leidner, 2001). In the past, knowledge was regarded as something possessed by individuals and groups tha could be captured and saved in databases (‘epistemology of possession’; Cook & Brown, 1999). However, not only the form of knowledge is decisive for understanding KM; equally, the process of knowing, i.e. how individuals and groups ‘make use of knowledge’ is important (‘epistemology of practice’; Cook & Brown, 1999). Therefore, processes of social construction are taken into consideration (Polanyi, 1966; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Nicolini et al., 2003; Gherardi, 2005) and knowledge is regarded inseparable from social relationships (Sawhney & Prandelli, 2000). The practice-based and socially constructed nature of knowledge raise considerable challenges for designing information technology (IT)-based KM systems. Although several virtual communities and networks exist, many studies have discovered that real-life interactions are still needed for ongoing knowledge exchange and creation (Wasko & Faraj, 2000; Tagliaventi & Mattarelli, 2006; Mueller et al., 2008).

The emergence of new virtual technologies like Linden Lab’s Second Life (SL) offers new promising possibilities of multimodal and rich interactions, and new ways of connecting (Schroeder & Axelsson, 2006; Kohler et al., 2009). These platforms emphasise the virtual dialogue between people and offer companies the opportunity to create global networks of employees (Castronova, 2005; Rymaszewski et al., 2007) in an interactive three-dimensional (3D) environment which satisfies the social and visual natures of human beings (see e.g. Castronova, 2005; Borremans, 2007). It is acknowledged that ‘virtual worlds can influence how and where work gets done’ (Messinger et al., 2009) by bringing people virtually together. Consequently, employees use SL for several purposes such as knowledge and information sharing. First research has dealt with the potential of virtual worlds as a learning environment suggesting that they allow for new methods, simulations and experiments, and can furthermore reorganise, empower and open up learning processes (de Freitas & Veletsianos, 2010). Simulations and models within the virtual environment allow illustrating complex and highly abstract topics in a visual way (Thomke, 2003). Also, innovation processes are enabled by collaboration in virtual worlds by accessing customers’ innovative potential (Kohler et al., 2009), and users are empowered to experience and modify innovative products and product features long before they really exist (Füller & Matzler, 2007).

Up to now, research has not considered the impact of virtual worlds on knowledge and knowing processes. Thus, this study aims at closing this gap. The specific focus of this exploratory study is the investigation of the use of SL as a KM platform from a practice perspective. Specifically, we analyse how knowledge and knowing processes take place in SL, which differences to traditional information and communication technology (ICT) (e.g. the use of email, telephone, chat, wikis, blogs, etc.) can be observed, and how current users experience knowledge and knowing activities in SL. Whereas explicit knowledge can be documented, stored and easily communicated via the use of ICT, tacit knowledge is invisible, hard to articulate, embedded in practice, ‘sticky’ and hard to exchange (Polanyi, 1966; Nelson & Winter, 1982; Szulanski, 2000). These properties of knowledge pose a serious challenge for KM. However, the characteristics of virtual worlds, which are multi-user, synchronous and media-rich environments to mention just a few (see e.g. Castronova, 2001; Schroeder & Axelsson, 2006), facilitate knowing processes among employees. We therefore also explore whether virtual worlds have the potential to support and enable the creation, acquisition and sharing of tacit knowledge among geographically and hierarchically dispersed people. To study these research questions, an in-depth analysis including 16 interviews and observations was conducted at IBM. We interviewed and observed SL users and members of the special workgroup called ‘Web2.0/virtual worlds’, who make use of SL as an experiment space for knowledge sharing activities. Results show that SL has great potential for serving as a KM platform. SL enhances the creation and exchange of knowledge through its media richness. It allows for simultaneous interaction and creates a common context, often called ‘ba’, that allows the creation and exchange of tacit, context-bound knowledge. SL also facilitates the creation of a shared language and shared codes, which have been identified as important enablers of knowing processes. Users also report flow-like experiences and a sense of community among participants – important antecedents of knowing processes.

## KNOWLEDGE MANAGEMENT FROM A PRACTICE PERSPECTIVE

The knowledge-based perspective considers knowledge a company’s most important resource (Grant, 1996; Spender, 1996) because potential competitive advantages are derived from this asset. KM includes the processes of knowledge creation, acquisition, storage and sharing (see e.g. Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Hendriks, 2001; Butler & Murphy, 2007). However, KM is a difficult task as it comprises different forms of knowledge as well as the process of knowing (Cook & Brown, 1999).

Knowledge, consisting of a tacit and explicit dimension, is ‘something that is possessed’ – either by individuals or collectives. The process of knowing does not refer to knowledge being used in action, but to knowledge ‘that is part of the action’ of individuals and groups (Cook & Brown, 1999). Focusing on the explicit dimension of knowledge, the use of ICT for its diffusion and storage becomes vital (‘computational paradigm’; Hazlett et al., 2005). ICT facilities include for example databases, the Web and intranets. However, explicit knowledge only represents the tip of the iceberg of the entire body of potential knowledge (Ikujiro Polanyi, 1966; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Beneath the surface, the tacit knowledge resource exists, which is invisible, hard to articulate, embedded in practice, ‘sticky’ and hard to exchange (Polanyi, 1966; Nelson & Winter, 1982; Szulanski, 2000). The focus on tacit knowledge led to the growing understanding that the mere introduction of ICT cannot sufficiently facilitate knowledge sharing and creation. ICT can only act as a catalyst for KM (Davenport & Prusak, 1998; McDermott, 1999). Consequently, KM initiatives become more dynamic approaches drawing the attention to people-centric and cultural prerequisites (‘organic paradigm’ von Krogh et al., 1996; Hazlett et al., 2005).

Another distinction of knowledge types focuses on the unit that possesses knowledge, which can be the individual or the group (Cook & Brown, 1999). If the individual is regarded the holder of knowledge, it is assumed that knowledge exists in the minds of the people, who are responsible for sharing knowledge with others (Ikujiro Nonaka & Takeuchi. 1995). Organisa tional knowledge then is the sum of individual knowledge that might be accessed by email, phone or knowledge maps (Wasko & Faraj, 2000). If knowledge is regarded as embedded in groups, the ‘social practice of knowing’ (Schultze, 1999) becomes prevalent, i.e. the interrelatedness of knowing and human activity, which can be facilitated by discussion groups, chat rooms or listservs. (Wasko & Faraj, 2000). So, companies deliberately connect their employees, e.g. by establishing electronic communities and knowledge networks using email, mailing lists, discussion fora and other collaborative software (Jarvenpaa & Staples, 2000; Wasko & Faraj, 2000; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2007). The advantages of these networks are that geographic distance, organisational boundaries and time differences can be overcome and that access to distributed knowledge can be facilitated leading to increased sharing activities (Lesser & Storck, 2001; Verburg & Andriessen, 2006; Zammuto et al., 2007).

Taking the tacit dimension and social construction of knowledge seriously sets the focus of KM equally on the process of knowing, which describes how knowledge is used in practice as part of the action (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Cook & Brown, 1999; Orlikowski, 2002). Research about communities of practice found out that the process of knowing is vital as individuals and groups use knowledge in interaction with others (‘cognition in practice’; Lave, 1988) and finally construct knowledge (Cook & Brown, 1999). Collective knowing processes comprise sharing identity, interacting face to face, aligning effort and supporting participation (Orlikowski, 2002). Sharing identity, which can be achieved by common trainings and socialisation, enhances the tacit knowledge of the organisation. Interacting face to face provides a knowing of the members of the organisation, which increases trust, commitment and knowledge sharing processes. Using the same models and standardised methods determines how efforts are aligned, which affects the knowing how to coordinate within the company and enhances tacit knowledge about procedures. Supporting participation, e.g. by distributed R&D activities, involvement and in decision-making processes, enhances the knowing on how to innovate. Individual knowing processes include face-to-face interaction and learning-by-doing processes (Orlikowski, 2002). Employees achieve a knowing of how to develop capabilities by learningby-doing practices, which is part of their tacit knowledge, and increase their explicit knowledge by learning facts.

The question now arises how these knowledge and knowing processes can be enabled in today’s organisations. As many organisations are globally dispersed, these practices often take place in an online and virtual environment. Virtual collaboration offers the possibility to include more employees in activities and decision-making processes, especially from other countries or different departments. Additionally, activities can easily be monitored leading to capturing decision rationales from which individuals can learn for future activities (Zammuto et al., 2007). Furthermore, groupware and intranets facilitate knowledge sharing in virtua settings (Chudoba et al., 2005; Bélanger & Allport, 2008; Wang & Haggerty, forthcoming). The emergence of new virtual worlds, such as SL, which are described in the next section, provides new features for online activities. These might offer opportunities for knowledge and knowing processes, which we will discuss at the end of the next section.

## VIRTUAL WORLDS AS KM PLATFORMS

The second generation of the Web (Web 2.0, NextGenNet, Social Net) has emerged to facilitate new online possibilities and has changed and challenged the perceptions and expec tations of its users encouraging a more human approach to interactivity and participation on the Web (O’Reilly, 2004). Besides Web 2.0, new technologies emerged supporting human-tohuman communication by using 3D space and via graphical embodiments called avatars (Schröder, 2002; Schroeder & Axelsson, 2006). Virtual worlds can be described as: ‘any computer-generated physical space, represented graphically in three dimensions that can be experienced by many people at once’ (Castronova, 2005, p. 22). Characteristics of virtual worlds are (see e.g. Castronova, 2001; Schroeder & Axelsson, 2006):

1 multi-user – facilitating the interaction of multiple, geographically dispersed users;

2 synchronous – enabling users to interact with each other in real time;

3 media richness – offering a variety of communication channels including 3D visuals, voice, text and body language;

4 embodied – representing the user by digital proxies called ‘avatar’;

5 physicality – providing a shared 3D interaction context; and

6 persistence – the program continues to run whether anyone is using it or not; it remember the location of people and things as well as the ownership of objects.

Besides Kaneva, There and Entropia Universe and more than 100 virtual worlds (de Freitas, 2008), the Linden Lab’s SL has received considerable attention. SL is a ‘3D online digital world imagined and created by its residents’ (http://www.secondlife.com). Although SL was mainly a phenomenon of consumer markets like other virtual worlds (see e.g. Catterall & Maclaran, 2002; Kaplan & Haenlein, 2009), it is also integrated into the intranets of organisations (McAfee, 2006; Silva et al., 2009). Virtual worlds help to reinvent many business processes, services and industries because strategies are first tested in the virtual world. Particularly, the improved interactivity and social nature of 3D virtual worlds promise an enhanced experience for many applications and ‘transform social interaction online’ (Hof, 2007). Applications can range from conferences, simulations and training sessions to collaborative work task (Schroeder & Axelsson, 2006).

Since virtual worlds seem to satisfy the social and visual natures of human beings better than existing ICT solutions, we assume that these environments could also support KM activities by influencing the social dimension of KM and the process of knowing. So far, research has acknowledged that virtual worlds bring people virtually together and provide shared voice in order to talk, avatars as shared representations of the person involved, shared active databases, shared computer-aided design systems and shared additional technological facilities such as photo, videos and repositories for digital files. This makes it possible to teleport avatars to meetings and collaborate virtually (Messinger et al., 2009). Therefore, virtual worlds can be the basis for new learning approaches and customer co-creation processes (Kohler et al., 2009; de Freitas & Veletsianos, 2010). However, there are still open questions, such as how new technologies, such as SL, can foster knowledge and knowing processes in companies. We assume that the social interaction of avatars, as a quasi faceto-face interaction and a direct experience, fosters individual and collective knowing processes. Furthermore, the representations via avatars may help to share identity, which enhances the knowing of the organisation, especially in a dispersed organisation. Virtual worlds may also support participation processes and the knowing how to share and create knowledge because they are accessible from all over the world. As virtual worlds do not only provide explicit knowledge and facts, but creates learning environments, learning-by-doing processes might be enhanced. In order to analyse if and how virtual worlds can contribute to social aspects of KM, an exploratory study was conducted, which is described in the following section.

## THE EMPIRICAL STUDY – SL AS A KM PLATFORM AT IBM

The study aims to shed light on the following questions:

1 How do knowledge and knowing activities take place in SL?

2 Which differences between traditional ICT and SL do users experience regarding knowledge and knowing activities?

3 How do current users experience knowledge and knowing activities in SL? What are the drawbacks, respectively, which improvements are necessary to use SL for KM?

Therefore, an exploratory qualitative research design was developed in order to discover how employees of IBM currently use and experience SL as a KM platform.

## Research field

As qualitative research cases are not selected based on statistical issues but according to theoretical considerations (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Eisenhardt, 1989), we selected an extreme case, where the researched issues can be observed ‘transparently’ and thus contribute to theory generation (Pettigrew, 1990). One workgroup at IBM was selected as the research object due to the following considerations: IBM, which is regarded as a knowledge intensive firm, (Newell et al., 2002) can further be seen as an early adopter of KM strategies as the company makes use of new technologies in order to expand further and improve traditional KM tools. IBM pushed into the virtual world with an investment of roughly \$10m (Reuters, 2006) and is moving to dominate what is called virtual business just as it championed the electronic business during the dot.com boom (Borremans, 2007). A survey among 330 000 IBMers showed that the employees themselves consider virtual worlds as one of the key future topics:

‘. . . we view SL at a very early stage and only at the first step in the direction of the evolutionary phase of the Internet – the goals go far beyond it. SL is a good place to start and to gather experiences since it is one of the most popular and distributed 3D words. (interviewee #7)

However, the focus of our study was not the whole company as not all employees are familiar with virtual worlds like SL, but rather we interviewed participants of the workgroup ‘Web 2.0/virtual worlds’. The purpose of the studied workgroup was to analyse the business value that virtual environments can bring to the future regarding unified communication and collaboration. Therefore, team members, who worked in different subsidiaries of IBM in Germany, gathered experience within virtual worlds by using the emerging 3D environments for online interaction and collaboration. The work team was founded in 2006 and consisted of about 50 members. We selected this workgroup at IBM because they have already gathered experiences in activities in SL. Furthermore, access to rich data (‘thick description’; Geertz, 1973) was granted as one of the researchers was working on a related research project at IBM during the period of the empirical study and another one is quite familiar with IBM and its KM practices as he previously worked for this company. This allowed us to spend significant amount of time online, especially in the research setting SL, observing, participating and taking field notes regarding knowledge and knowing activities among the Web2.0/virtual world workgroup members.

## Methodology

For this research, we selected a methodology based on qualitative research to gain deeper insights into the experiences of employees regarding SL knowledge processes. This method ology is appropriate if no data is available regarding the research question (Eisenhardt, 1989; Denzin & Lincoln, 1994; Yin, 2003). More precisely, observations of current SL activities, qualitative interviews and secondary data analysis about IBMs activities in various journals, newspapers and web pages were used. These types of data collection aim for triangulation across sources and methods, which enhance the trustworthiness and increase the reliability of information gathered by the semistructured qualitative interviews (Denzin & Lincoln, 1994). Over a period of 2 weeks, 16 in-depth interviews using a semistructured interview guideline were conducted. The sample of interviewees was selected by the researchers in order to provide a broad spread of qualifications ranging from people with profound technical and IT knowledge to IBM employees in all kinds of divisions with less or no IT affinity (see Appendix 1).

As the interviewees were located throughout Germany, the majority of these interviews (11) were carried out via telephone. Two of the interviews were held in SL and three were held as personal interview. The interviewees were notified and an appointment was made in advance in order to guarantee that the interviews could be conducted over the full length of 30–40 minutes. An interview guideline (see Appendix 2) was prepared in advance, which sought to examine the perception of KM in light of the ongoing evolution of the internet into a 3D internet.

We asked the interviewees questions regarding how and why they use SL for work, which role the avatar takes, how communication and interaction work, as well as regarding their experiences compared with traditional ICT tools like the use of email, telephone, chat, wikis or blogs, and scope for improvements. The semi-structured interviews were conducted in accordance to suggestions on empirical social research and study design (Eisenhardt, 1989; Denzin & Lincoln, 1994; Yin, 2003). Each interview was recorded, transcribed as well as anonymised.

In accordance with this qualitative research design, the collected data (i.e. transcribed interviews, field notes of observations and secondary data) were imported into the software QSR NVivo 7 (QSR International Pty Ltd., Doncaster, Victoria, Australia). This data management tool enables a logical and well-constructed analysis that is reliably related to the research questions (Richards, 1999). First, the interview data were analysed. Therefore, the research team, consisting of two persons, took over the coding of the interview data individually. One coder was an insider working at IBM at the moment of the data collection. To ensure that the insider’s perspective did not bias the results, a coder external to IBM coded the interviews independently. Both interviewers agreed on established concepts derived from the literature (Maxwell, 2008) such as findings of previous knowledge/learning studies in virtual environmen (e-learning, presentations, meetings) and knowledge and knowing practices (sharing identity, supporting participation). Furthermore, we also inductively developed categories from the data (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). In order to ensure inter-rater reliability, the meanings of the categories were continuously negotiated. Thus, the relevant topics regarding the research questions were identified, compared and cross-checked in observations of SL interaction and the obtained secondary data.

## Results and insights from the 3D environment SL

The analysis of the interviews starts with a description of the applications for which IBM uses SL as a KM platform. Afterwards, we deal with our research questions where the results are highlighted by adequate quotes from the interviewees. Due to anonymity issues, no further information on the interview partners can be provided at this point.

## How do knowledge and knowing activities take place in SL?

The findings of the interviews show that there are various ways to engage in individual and collective knowledge and knowing processes in virtual worlds. We have observed that the interviewees use SL for three different purposes: (1) holding virtual presentations; (2) taking virtual courses; and (3) arranging virtual meetings. One way to exchange explicit knowledge with a big audience is to hold presentations. The workgroup ‘Web 2.0/virtual worlds’ organised presentations, which were held live in an IBM location in front of a real audience. These presentations were streamed into SL where interested parties – most not located in the same subsidiary – could follow the presentation within the virtual 3D environment. Rather, differen tools for communication and interaction were combined in order to foster collective knowing processes. The avatars communicated by using the chat function or VoIP to share opinions and align their understanding. After the presentations, people in the audience could ask questions, which were streamed into SL by a moderator. These presentations were enhanced by videos, PowerPoint slides and whiteboards. However, the focus was not only on passing on explicit knowledge. The avatars’ questions were asked using the chat function; the moderato read them aloud in order to make them understandable to the physically present audience.

The second purpose for SL activities are learning processes. The interviewees took part in learning, training and employee orientation sessions. In order to acquire basic knowledge and skills, IBM newcomers receive initial trainings via SL where they can watch presentations, read important documents and accompany a mentor (see Figure 1). On the one hand, these activities enhance explicit knowledge about the company’s structure and performance; on th other hand, newcomers get to know the organisation, the procedures and important players.

Another example for individual knowledge and knowing processes are virtual language courses: with the introduction of the ‘talkademy’, employees can take part in language courses with native speakers without incurring any travel expenses. SL has several properties that make them suited to a more interactive learning atmosphere than studying vocabularies or doing some language book exercises by oneself and only acquiring explicit knowledge: the shared 3D environment, the simulation of different surroundings according to the studied language, the representation of course partners and teachers by digital proxies called ‘avatars’, and the real-time interaction provide opportunities for putting tacit knowledge into action. The need to collaborate within geographically dispersed teams increases the need for effective and efficient ways to communicate. Technologies like telephones, text messaging, email and classical video conferencing tools have already supported specific aspects of interaction among physically remote people. Instead of conference calls, different project teams of the workgroup ‘Web 2.0/virtual worlds’ teleport themselves into SL, experiment with the new technology and collaborate virtually from a true first-person perspective. Meetings are arranged, interaction is possible via verbal and non-verbal communication (e.g. the use of presentations, video streams, voice or chat) within a shared meaningful environment where the participants can naturally move around, talk, gesture and manipulate objects. These meetings are not limited to employees working at IBM, e.g. customers can also meet with their project managers in SL which supports participation by watching the progress of the project, sharing visual information and discussing further proceedings. The virtual IBM business centre offers a new place to present future products, e.g. by building 3D models and simulations to experiment with complex and abstract topics. This allows team members and customers or other project partners to interact with the objects through modifying colours, sizes and styles of every single element. The possibility to manipulate the virtual 3D objects and the chance to visualise materials, shaping or colours allow for high degree of interactivity. To ensure that the new product or the prototype functions properly before its introduction to the market, the company can further build mock-ups, test the first concept in SL, and gain feedback by engaging partners in SL.

‘The virtual prototype serves as a laboratory and is a good way to test-market the new device and rapidly prototype the evolving concept’. (interviewee #10)

Which differences between traditional ICT and SL do users experience regarding knowledge and knowing activities?

SL provides another means for developing and maintaining social relationships and sharing and creating knowledge. In the following, we describe the differences of traditional ICT, including email, telephone, chat, wikis and blogs, and SL regarding KM based on the inter views. The main differences are the 3D shared environments developed in SL as well as avatars who resemble the real persons and can interact directly. This brings about several advantages.

The network of SL is characterised by an augmented reality. Because of the 3D environment and the avatars that are visible images of the people involved, people can identify with their vis-à-vis. The use of gesturing and pointing as well as certain body posture creates a common experience. Stories can be shared, and a common history can be developed, which leads to a sense of commonness. This makes it easier for individuals to understand the new knowledge and combine it with the existing one. Furthermore, individuals are able to create a community and a sense of belonging to this group.

‘I think that the avatar makes it easier to imagine taking part in a common meeting. In conference call, I do not see whom I am talking to. I do not have visual contact. With avatars it is different. Although it is just an image and not always a perfect copy of the real person, it is easier to build bridges between what is happening in SL and the real-life activities’. (interviewee #6)

‘I like in SL that you are sitting there as a group and listen to the project’s progress. You just have more a feeling of being part of the group. You have the feeling you are fighting shoulder to shoulder. This is the prerequisite to help each other or to share know-how’. (interviewee #4)

In SL, different tools for communication and interaction can be combined. Virtual environ ments are media rich in the sense that they allow people to interact via several senses. At IBM, the avatars can communicate using a chat function or VoIP. Additionally, presentations can be enhanced via visual environments like films, PowerPoint slides and pictures. These tools and the fact that interacting avatars are present in the same room make knowledge exchange easier. By means of informal talk, which is also possible during meetings or presentations, this shared experience is enhanced; it enables the creation of relationships among participants. Additionally, after the actual meeting, informal talk is possible because avatars stay in the room and discuss what they have heard.

‘In contrast to conference calls, meetings in SL have the advantage that you have team feeling because other persons are present. And after the meeting you can stay in SL and talk to each other. In conference calls the connection is cut’. (interviewee #12)

The direct interaction makes it possible that rules, which are valid in real communication, also are observed in the virtual world. You can naturally look or walk around, walk up to and face each other, thus supporting a natural social behaviour. The interaction of avatars facili tates the development of proximity although the other persons are not present in real life. However, SL allows to share an interactive synthetic environment form a true first-person perspective, which is regarded as the second best alternative to the direct interaction of rea people. Although people know who the avatars are in real life, hierarchical structures are not as important anymore, making it easier for employees to exchange knowledge throughout the company.

‘The hierarchical structures are somewhat dissolved in the new virtual environment. The knowledge that can be distributed in IBM is – it’s just so easy to share and exchange this know-how with this new technologies’. (interviewee #3)

The presence of avatars facilitates the development of trust in two ways. On the one hand, the meeting in the virtual world enhances trust because in contrast to conference calls, the participants see who is in the virtual room and is listening to the conversations. On the other hand, people sometimes dare to say things they would not have said in the real world because only avatars are in the room and not real persons.

‘One is not staring or talking into a black hole like during conference calls, but one is rather feeling that somebody is there. During conference calls one often has the feeling of being alone. One does not know whether your counterpart is still there and pays attention, as many participants in teleconferences can put themselves on mute and continue working on something else. While within virtual worlds one can see the others sitting in the same room and dealing with the question or the problem. Speakers can observe the reactions from the audience and can directly respond to it’. (interviewee #4)

As already mentioned, SL has several properties that make them suited to collaborative work. However, critical for knowledge-sharing activities is that virtual environments include not just mechanisms for synchronous collaboration but also mechanisms to create knowledge repository to build upon existing work. Digital libraries, search programs, project managemen tools should support individual access to find, to further contribute and/or to comment on existing work. Such mechanisms are already quite elaborated in the context of computerbased knowledge repository systems but not integrated in the virtual 3D environment.

How do current users experience KM activities in SL (including drawbacks and areas for improvements)?

Within the following section SL users’ experiences and therewith related drawbacks and areas for improvements will be presented. Generally, the experiences range from positive and grea experiences to moderate ones. The so-called ‘technique nerds’, seem to be enthusiastic and stressed the positive experience of being totally immersed by the new technologies as well as participating in something great.

‘You can be part of something brand new; you can witness something that is not possible while surfing the usual Internet; it is like reading a great book, you just lose all senses of time and you are sucked into the 3D world’. (interviewee #12)

As the workgroup Web2.0/virtual worlds participants are interested in the new development of virtual technologies, they regarded using SL within the business context as challenging task. SL activities were plagued with the instability of the system, download problems, log-in failures, system shut-downs or similar technical problems. Furthermore, two interviewees mentioned that virtual worlds are not always easy to handle because one can easily become lost when entering such 3D environments. Even if you are able to navigate within SL, several activities like setting up meetings in SL are rather time consuming. However, after these technical issues were resolved, the SL activities became quite interactive and increased members’ awareness.

‘Right now it isn’t really a great enjoyment; it is really exertive to set up a meeting with all the different technical hurdles . . . But the potential is certainly there’. (interviewee #15)

Security is also an issue. This day, SL is not the place to discuss confidential business insights. High-security standards are expected to be the key characteristic facing organisations that want to use virtual worlds for daily business. Entrance restrictions for special areas, user logins, etc. would be useful. Poor graphics were also frequently criticised within the interviews, especially concerning the avatar who should be a 1:1 representation from real life. Currently, the human-like articulated character – the avatar – looks like a cartoon image and the interview partners mentioned that the identification of the partner especially in the business context is essential. Furthermore. travelling between different virtual worlds using the same avatar is not feasible. Contemporary 3D virtual worlds are distinct environments that do not touch each other in any way, therefore requiring users to download different software applications.

IBM is working on continuous improvements to further advance the 3D Internet. IBMers think, however, that we are in the early stages and that SL is only the beginning and some o the problems can be solved within the next 3–5 years.

‘After the technical issues are resolved the future developments could lead to an virtual environment that will be more realistic, more interactive, and more social than anything experienced through Web browsers. Particularly, the enhanced social nature of the emerg ing 3D Internet promises superior experiences for many applications’. (interviewee #3)

## DISCUSSION

With the development of virtual worlds, new ways of generating and sharing knowledge evolved offering possibilities to quasi face-to-face interaction and a direct experience. There fore, we conducted a first qualitative study at IBM. Our results confirm that virtual worlds can support knowing processes such as sharing identity, aligning efforts and supporting participa tion (Orlikowski, 2002).

Our study shows that virtual worlds offer new opportunities of knowledge as part of the action (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Cook & Brown, 1999; Orlikowski, 2002). Virtual worlds can be applied for dynamic, practice-based and experience-rich knowledge generation far above the pure collection of knowledge in databases. Interactive and multimedia-rich environments (Ward,

2007) allow to embed existing knowledge in relevant contexts, settings and timing, and further provide communication along many channels concurrently (media richness theory; Daft & Lengel, 1986; A.R. Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Dennis & Kinney, 1998). From the media richness perspective of Ngwenyama & Lee (1997), virtual worlds offer a shared context that allows a social interaction among organisational members. The media-rich environment fosters verbal and non-verbal communication, a certain gestic in real time in the case of SL, which may add to the pertaining learning capacity of the media. In cross- boundary communication, SL enables sender and receiver to play an active role in knowledge sharing (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). This leads to an action-based application of existing as well as generation of new knowledge. The easy to change environment and boundary conditions enable an iterative trial and error-based learning process. The group dynamic and interaction processes resemble more face-to-face workshops than knowledge retrieval from existing databases. The immediate joint-building capabilities and presence of avatars make it a more immersing activity than any other Web 2.0 platform or network allows. The learning-by-doing modus of virtual worlds allows to practice and get familiar with existing procedures and established methods but also to further advance those. Although SL still faces some technical problems like part-time breakdowns of streaming functions or VoIP, we propose:

P1: Because virtual worlds combine different tools for communication and interaction (e.g. 3D visuals, voice, text, body language, etc.), knowledge and knowing processes are enabled.

Virtual worlds are simultaneously accessible for many users all over the world (Castronova, 2001). Our interviewees confirmed that many organisational members need to interact with colleagues, who are not located within the same building (see also media synchronicity theory; Dennis et al., 2008). The ability to sustain the interaction between globally dispersed employees remains critical to the success of exchanging and creating organisational knowledge (Lesser & Prusak, 2004). Access to information alone is not enough or not even possible since knowledge is much more tacit and embedded in a social context (Ikujiro Polanyi, 1966; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Furthermore, hierarchical structures often hinder knowledge processes (Adler et al., 2008). Therefore, adhocracies become vital for knowledge sharing because employees regard this as their own responsibility (Schepers & Berg, 2007). As our study reveals, hierarchical levels become less important in virtual worlds due to the avatars, which resemble cartoon images. As no formal signs of hierarchical status are attached to the avatars, the interviewees dare to contact other avatars without constraints. We propose:

P2: Because virtual worlds can grant access to globally dispersed people also across hierarchical levels and allow for simultaneous interaction, the access to tacit knowledge can be granted and the sensation of new knowledge can be enhanced.

Receiving an immediate answer to a specific question might motivate individuals for further interaction (Wasko & Faraj, 2000). Virtual worlds may support motivational issues due to their newness and their ability to provide a game-like and intrinsically enjoyable experience (Castronova, 2001). Although our interviewees were sometimes frustrated with the platform’s stability, they are still curious about new technologies and about meeting new people, which might make them highly motivated to exchange their experiences. They report that they lose all senses of time and are sucked into the 3D world (interviewee #3). They experience a flow-like state. a state in which people are so involved in an activity that nothing else seems to matter’ (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990, p. 4). Hoffman and Novak defined flow in online environments as a state that is characterised by a seamless sequence of responses facilitated by machine interaction, intrinsically enjoyable, accompanied by a loss of self-consciousness and self-reinforcing (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). Flow has some important consequences, among them are increased learning and an exploratory mind-set (Hoffman & Novak, 1996). Explor atory behaviour, learning and creativity are important processes in knowledge creation and acquisition. Thus, we propose:

P3: Because of the multi-user, interactive, telepresence, media rich or game like characteristics of virtual worlds, users experience flow-like states that lead to important knowledge related activities.

Virtual worlds provide a shared 3D environment where the digital proxies, the avatars, can interact and freely navigate. The 3D virtual world can be regarded as a cyber-ba' – a commor space – to exchange, create and utilise knowledge (Ikujiro Nonaka & Konno, 1998). Nonaka et al. (2000, p. 8) emphasise the role of context for knowledge and argue: ‘knowledge creating processes are necessarily context-specific, in terms of who participates and how they participate in the process. The context here does not mean “a fixed set of surrounding conditions but a wider dynamic process of which the cognition of an individual is only a part” (Hutchins, 1996). Hence, knowledge needs a physical context to be created, as “there is no creation without place” (Casey, 1997).’ In virtual worlds, a context for exchanging experiences and sharing stories is created, which is important for learning from the experience of others (Orr, 1996). The context in which narratives are embedded helps others to understand the knowledge. Because this knowledge has been gained in a certain situation, it can also be transferred to other situations by making modifications (e.g. situated learning; Jean Lave, 1991). As the example of the ‘talkademy’ shows (where employees are able to learn French in ‘virtual France’) virtual worlds may offer this context to embedded knowledge. However, there are still problems to overcome, as 3D environments face problems, like platform stability, interoperability with the current Web, security issues and poor graphical representations. Nevertheless, our interviewees reported that technically skilled and intrinsically motivated users become excited about the new interaction possibilities and that they are working to overcome these hurdles. Thus, we propose:

P4: Because virtual worlds develop interactive 3D environments, a common context for interaction can be created, which increases the transferability of context-bound, tacit knowledge.

Furthermore, the shared 3D environment is essential for knowledge creation and complex knowledge sharing. Therefore, virtual prototypes can serve as proxies that enable interested users to experience innovative products via the internet, long before they really exist (Füller & Matzler. 2007). Users can learn how new products function. recognise their benefit. build judgements, come up with new ideas and provide additional requirements, all of which are valuable for innovation teams (Füller & Matzler, 2007). Unlike abstract symbols, words and numbers, virtual prototypes help users to draw from vivid mental imageries of future products and to take decisions based on actual and not on past experiences (MacInnis & Price, 1987). Clear and precise presentations of future products avoid ambiguity and multiple interpretations of its quality (Hoch, 2002). This is also important for interaction with customers. Consequently, interactive environments enable customers to make more realistic judgments based on tria and error learning and modify the virtual products according to their ‘real’ needs and perceptions (von Hippel & Katz, 2002). Therefore, we propose:

P5: Through the use of 3D simulations and models and the possibility to make immediate modifications, virtual worlds offers an environment that enables users to visualise ideas, solutions or concepts that are difficult to describe verbally, and therefore supports the creation and exchange of difficult to articulate knowledge.

Virtual worlds allow geographically separated people to interact verbally and non-verbally within a shared meaningful environment through shared information objects, and thus supports social human interaction and communication in a natural and intuitive way (Roberts et al., 2006). Through virtual worlds, a shared language is created. Shared language goes beyond the language itself; it also addresses the ‘acronyms, subtleties, and underlying assumptions that are the staples of day-to-day interactions’ (Lesser & Storck, 2001, p. 836). Shared language and shared codes facilitate interaction and communication and therefore contribute to learning and knowledge sharing (Chiu et al., 2006). With conventional ICT, often norms for the confidentiality of one’s data and ideas are missing (Pigg & Crank, 2004) and have to be developed in the real world first before they are continued online (Blanchard & Horan, 1998). In a virtual world, nearly real-life experiences can be created, which support the development and maintenance of social relationships. Thus, we propose:

P6: Through the combination of different kinds of media and through interactivity, virtua worlds facilitate the creation of a shared language and shared codes, and therefore positively influence knowledge-sharing activities.

Our interviews revealed that friendship, respect, trust and approval are formed as consequences of ongoing social interactions in SL. It seems that by increasing the richness o exchanged information and fostering cooperative behaviour, virtual worlds may positively influence group performance and innovation (Lesser & Storck, 2001). The use of body language of the avatars and a more real-life experience (Castronova, 2001) may make it easier to establish strong relationships and maintain them. Participants report that virtual worlds enable to create relationships among them, they feel part of the group and a sense o community emerges. Sense of community has been defined as a feeling that members have of belonging . . . that members matter to one another . . . and a shared faith that members needs will be met through their commitment to be together’ (McMillian & Chavis, 1986, p. 9). Sharratt & Usoro (2003) explicitly argue that sense of community positively influences knowledge sharing in online communities of practice. Chiu et al., (2006) found empirical support for their hypothesis that the individual’s sense of belonging and positive feeling towards a virtua community is a strong determinant of the quantity of knowledge shared among the members. Hence, we propose:

P7: Through interactivity, media richness and telepresence, virtual worlds create a sense of community among the participants, which is an important antecedent of knowledge sharing.

Based on these considerations, virtual worlds indeed bear the potential to improve KM and increase the ability of ICT to support the generation of new knowledge and the transfer of tacit knowledge. But not only knowledge sharing can be facilitated by virtual worlds. As the avatars have the ability to use body language for communication, the communication within virtual worlds ‘allow[s] social interactions that are not a simulation of human interactions; they are human interactions’ (Castronova, 2001, p. 12).

## IMPLICATIONS, LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE PROSPECTS

It is evident that virtual worlds bear potential for KM because they overcome several barriers of established technologies in order to facilitate interactive knowing and knowledge processes. The emergence of virtual worlds knits together knowledge workers in a way that was previously technically not feasible. In this study, we were able to shed light on the usage of SL as a KM platform at IBM. We could show that not only existing tacit and explicit knowledge can be accessed and shared (‘epistemology of possession’; Cook & Brown, 1999), but also new knowledge is generated and applied, thus supporting processes of knowing (‘epistemology of practice’; Cook & Brown, 1999).

The findings of this exploratory study are strongly linked to and corroborated by important theories in information systems research (e.g. media richness theory, flow theory), knowledge management theory (e.g. organisational learning, knowledge processes) and sociology (e.g. sense of community). Hence, the propositions developed in this study bring together empirical insights and theoretical knowledge from different fields that contribute to a better and more holistic understanding of the phenomenon of knowledge processes in virtual worlds.

We contribute to literature on the transfer of tacit, invisible, hard to articulate, ‘sticky’ and hard to exchange knowledge that is embedded in practice (Polanyi, 1966; Nelson & Winter, 1982; Szulanski, 2000) by showing how through its media richness, through the connection of geographically and hierarchically dispersed people and through the creation of a common space or context (a ‘cyber-ba’) the exchange of tacit knowledge is facilitated and supported through virtual worlds. We also show that flow-like experiences occur during the SL sessions when users are engaged in knowledge activities and identify herewith an important antecedent of learning and creativity.

Another important contribution of this study lies in the demonstration how two group processes are facilitated that can be considered as important antecedents of knowledge sharing: media richness, interactivity and telepresence lead to shared languages and codes as well to a sense of community, both are considered important prerequisites for knowledge processes.

Third, from a practical perspective, this study provides examples and use cases in which virtual worlds for KM may add value. It also shows which results a company may expect and which reactions and experience it may be confronted with. This study also gives some hints regarding the required personality and skill set of employees for which virtual worlds may be especially suitable.

However, this study also bears some limitations: First, from a methodological point of view, this study is limited by the fact that the interviewees all pertained to a single organisation and represent only a limited number of virtual world users. Nevertheless, we could formulate propositions how virtual worlds may facilitate knowledge and knowing processes that should be tested in larger samples. Second, for the purpose of this study, we took into account the perspective of highly active and active participants in SL. However, as IBM is a highly technology-based company, the results may not hold true in other industry settings where less ‘techies’ are working. Thus, further studies are needed assessing the perspective of employees in other industries and business settings. Here, time will tell if virtual worlds will be accepted in the future KM processes.

## Appendix 2. Interview guideline

## General Information

1. In which division are you working at IBM? Can you describe your job?

2. Do you know SL?

3. What’s the aim and purpose of IBM and their SL presence? Can you name any further reasons?

4. Which processes are supported by SL at the moment? Which not? And Why?

5. Who has SL access among the IBMers?

– Who should use SL and for which activities?

– Who is actually using SL?

6. Is IBM taking actions to enhance SL within the organization?

– Is SL promoted within IBM?

– Are there any training programs regarding SL usage?

7. Which KM-Tools are in use within IBM right now? (short description . . . )

## Usage

8. For which activities do you use SL? – Why? Do you use SL for your work at IBM?

9. Can you explain how you use SL providing an example?

10. How often are you in SL?

11. Length of stay?

12. What is your motivation to use SL?

## KM – SL – Proceedings

1. Do you use SL for KM?

2. Can you explain the KM event in more detail? We are interested in the process.

a. Where does the meeting take place?

b. Can you describe the location in more detail?

c. How many colleagues participate?

d. How do they participate? – Can everybody join?

e. What are the participants doing?

f. How do they find out about the event?

g. Which communication tools are used during the virtual meeting?

h. Can you describe the beginning of the event?

i. What about the roles among participants?

j. Is a moderator used during the event?

k. How is the discussion going on?

l. Which tools are used?

m. Can you describe the end of the event?

n. How does the information distribution work after the event?

3. Can you explain the difference between real-life and virtual meetings?

– In comparison to other KM-tools?

– SL – new KM-Tool?

– Are there any differences regarding the content of the meeting? (Knowledge Sharing / Knowledge Creation)

4. Compared to the previously announced KM-tools – what are the advantages / disadvan tages (risks) of SL?

## Avatar

1. Which role does the avatar play? – appearance / visual nature / clothes, . . .

2. Communication (eye-contact, behavior during meetings, . . . )

## Experience

1. Can you describe positive / negative experiences?

2. What constituted the experience? What creates the experience? How does it feel?

## Future

1. Forecast: How do you evaluate the future usage of SL for KM activities at IBM?

2. Which activities could be executed in SL?

3. Do you think this is realistic? Under which circumstances can these activities take place?

4. How long do you think it will take to put this into practice?
