# Metaphors and Methodologies: Living Beyond the Systems Machine

By: Julie E. Kendall
School of Business-Camden
Rutgers University
Camden, New Jersey 08102 U.S.A.

School of Business-Camden

## Abstract

Metaphors are the cognitive lenses we use to make sense of all situations. Intimately interconnected with the way we think, metaphors are fundamental in shaping reality. Building on work about metaphors in organizational life, this paper examines the language of information systems users in 16 different organizations. The results confirm the existence of six main metaphors (journey, war, game, organism, society, and machine) and adds three metaphors that also emerged from the language of IS users (family, zoo, and jungle). Dramatistic analysis was used to reveal that seven of these principal metaphors are found in commonly used systems development methodologies. For example, the systems development life cycle draws upon the “game” metaphor, and structured methodologies and CASE tools are akin to the “machine” metaphor. Analysts who are aware of the existence of these metaphors (both in the user organization and within the methodologies themselves) will begin to see the systems development process in an entirely different light. Caution must be undertaken, however, when using this approach. First, analysts should lead the systems development process by selecting a methodology to match user metaphors, not the other way around. Second, analysts must see, rather than suppress, the paradoxical richness of metaphors. Third, analysts should not limit the number of metaphors because it limits the usefulness of this approach. Fourth, analysts should be adequately trained in a variety of systems development methodologies. Finally, analysts should use metaphorical analysis in conjunction with other approaches. Using the recommendations and findings for guidance, analysts can begin to develop the power of metaphorical analysis to better understand and communicate with users during the development process.

Keywords: Systems analysis, systems design, IS development strategies, participative design, metaphors, socio-technical approach, user-analyst interaction

## Introduction

Using metaphors empowers the speaker and listener to transform reality from the pedestrian to the remarkable. Metaphors are like the magical incantations of old. By using words that people understand and believe in to make linkages with the new and unfamiliar, the speaker provides the ability to envision the world in a new way.

Invoking a metaphor means opening the door for a listener to enter a subject in a different way. If we rejoice in the fact that the company's reputation is soaring like an eagle, or warn that an executive will go down with the ship, or worry aloud that the competition is lean and hungry, a tiger in the jungle, we have used metaphors to expand the understanding of the listener and have empowered them to see the world differently. There is true power behind metaphors, power to shape reality and structure the thoughts of the people who are caught up in a particular metaphor and its entailments (Duncan, 1968; Graber, 1976; Lakoff and Johnson, 1980).

Metaphors are ubiquitous, but that should not diminish their importance. On the contrary, metaphors are so deeply embedded in our daily language that we become blind to the important ways in which they shape our thought and influence our behavior.

This paper takes as its starting point the importance of metaphors in organizational life. From there it examines the literature on metaphors in organizations as well as actual metaphors that information systems users employ. Following that, it examines nine main metaphors and the attributes that best define them. Different kinds of systems development methodologies are then examined in order to determine what main metaphors they entail. Finally, the relationships among metaphors are discussed to uncover relationships between metaphors and methodologies and to relate the theory of metaphors to IT practice.

## Metaphors

## Definitions of metaphor

Words spoken by organizational participants are important in shaping and sharing organizational reality, and as such they merit our attention (Graber, 1976). Much of what is spoken comes through expressive figures of speech, including metaphors. Ott (1989) notes:

Metaphors help organization members put meaning into things they experience and realize apparent contradictions and paradoxes they encounter. Metaphors help organizations tie their parts together into meaningful wholes (pp. 29-30).

A metaphor takes the form of saying that one object is another. For example, in the metaphor, "The company creaks and groans under its enormous cargo of debt," the company becomes a ship. It also enjoys all of the associations of a sea-going voyage or journey that a cargo ship conveys.

## Assumptions about metaphors

There are many assumptions that we make about the use of metaphors in organizational life. Based on the work of Koch and Deetz (1980) they include:

1. Metaphors are fundamental in the English language.

2. Basic metaphors entail others. (For example, the root metaphor, “Time is money,” entails the ideas that time is valuable, time can be spent, saved, lost, and so on.)

3. Complex networks of metaphors permeate our language, leading us to conceptualize certain things in certain ways.

4. Metaphors are usually coherent and can be traced back to fundamental forms of experience.

5. Inconsistent metaphors can be explained by tracing them to different realms of experience.

The true effectiveness of metaphors is their almost paradoxical ability to point up dissimilarities and contrasts between two objects while simultaneously demonstrating that there are considerable similarities between the objects being compared (Weaver, 1967). Specifically, there are four main functions for metaphors: supplying concreteness or actualization of an abstract idea, clarifying the unknown, expressing the subjective, and assisting thought (Weaver, 1967).

## Previous Research on Metaphors in Systems

## Importance of metaphors in organizations

In the past few years, organizational researchers have recognized the importance of metaphors. Morgan (1986) in his pioneering work asserts that:

Our theories of organizational life are based on metaphors that lead us to see and understand organizations in distinctive yet partial ways.... By using different metaphors to understand the complex and paradoxical character of organizational life, we are able to manage and design organizations that we may have not thought possible before (pp. 12-13).

Morgan states that metaphors provide “some different ways of thinking about organizations.” He examines eight metaphors and then outlines ways that organizational researchers can use them to diagnose and evaluate organizational life. Morgan suggests a two-step approach:

The first [step] is to produce a diagnostic reading of the situation being investigated using different metaphors to identify or highlight key aspects of the situation. The second step is to make a critical evaluation of the significance of the different interpretations thus produced (p. 322, emphasis added).

Morgan suggests a diagnostic reading that enables a researcher to use different metaphors as a colored lens to bring out different qualities in the situation. He then concludes that one can “explore the complexity of organizations in both a descriptive and prescriptive manner” (p. 322). His seminal work on metaphors in organizations opened the door for further study.

Although we draw from the work of Morgan, the approach presented here differs from Morgan's in two ways. This paper goes directly to the language of the organizational members and then uses their own language to identify the metaphors. Going directly to the users' language is an attempt to avoid the metaphorical traps described by Boland (1989).

Second, this paper takes organizational culture as a starting perspective. Morgan (1986) interestingly sees the concept of organizational culture as a metaphor itself. We believe, however, that metaphors are but one important part of organizational culture, along with myth, symbolism, and ideology. (See Deal and Kennedy, 1982; Trice and Beyer, 1984; Riley, 1983; Whorton and Worthley, 1981; and Pettigrew, 1979, for insightful discussions of the components of organizational culture.) All of these are important in conveying what an organization is.

## Using metaphors in systems research

Researchers in systems development have touched upon metaphors and their usefulness to systems analysis and design in a limited way for the last decade or so (Allen and Lientz, 1978; Hirschheim and Newman, 1991; Lanzara, 1983; Madsen, 1989; Mason, 1991; Schon, 1979; Vainio-Larsson, 1986; Walsham, 1991). Although none has attempted to develop a formal approach to using metaphors, their work has nonetheless yielded results.

Mason (1991) believes that metaphors play a crucial role in enacting corporate strategy and linking strategic thinking with IT planning. After examining works in the popular and academic press, he suggests that the war metaphor, which is so pervasive in strategic planning, has severe limitations for strategic information systems planning. He suggests alternative metaphors that might fare better, including organism, city-state (society), and team sports (game). Although it is not the purpose of this paper to explore the use of metaphors at the strategic level, it should be noted that this is an important area benefiting from the insights gained from research into metaphors.

In their managerial systems book, Allen and Lientz (1978) use the construction of the palace at Versailles as a metaphor for development of an information system. Complete with illustrations, the metaphor performs well, enabling the reader to understand distinct aspects of managing present-day users via complete immersion into the metaphor as it relates to the systems life cycle.

Madsen (1989) suggests the use of what he terms “metaphorical” design to supplement the traditional “literal” design. He also suggests several informal guidelines for using metaphors to inform the design process. Although Madsen suggests metaphors can arise from “concrete situations” in stories told by participants, he devotes more time to discussing how to introduce and extend “generic” metaphors. Madsen’s work differs from the approach taken in this paper in that: (1) Madsen suggests the usefulness of introducing alien metaphors to a user group rather than relying on the metaphors expressed in the language of participants that are discovered during interviews; (2) he does not discuss in any length the idea of commonly occurring metaphors (e.g., journey, family, etc.) whose identification and use may be beneficial to the analyst; and (3) he does not investigate the metaphors resident in systems development methodologies.

## Methodology

This exploratory study was undertaken to determine which metaphors are articulated in user groups. Two kinds of interviews along with Q-methodology were used to capture and understand main metaphors of users. Then, dramatistic analysis was used to investigate which metaphors are inherent in recognized systems development methodologies and approaches. Details of the methodologies used can be found in the Appendix.

## Interviews

The data for the study were collected over a period of three years from 16 different companies in North America and Europe. Companies included banks, financial services companies, health insurance organizations, regional blood centers, hospitals, franchise grocery stores, manufacturing companies, and one state police organization. All organizations studied had traditional management information systems and decision support systems in place. Three had executive information systems. Three others had competitive information systems.

The number of user groups from each company depended on the particular project, but each user group was composed of three to six members. Usable responses were received from 142 participants. Users ranged in age from 18 to 62, with 57 percent being female and 43 percent male. Users were drawn, from all levels of the organization, who were currently using and would continue to use the system to support their work. Interviewees were also purposefully chosen so that different perspectives, different levels of education, and both sexes were represented.

## Focus group interviews

Focus group interviews were conducted by one of the researchers speaking with three to six users at a time. Each interview required one hour. Interviewees were chosen in a purposive manner, rather than randomly, in order to assure a breadth and diversity of opinion regarding use of information systems in the organization. Because interviewees were chosen for the representativeness of their comments toward information system use, their responses were considered to be the domain of all possible responses to the questions (Stephenson, 1953).

Focus group interviews prove useful in generating metaphors because during the course of the interview, one participant builds on and adds to another's metaphor. Bormann (1975) calls this process of building upon each others ideas "chaining out" and suggests that the dramas built in small groups eventually "chain out" to the mass media.

## Q-methodology

Not wishing to exclude any metaphors that might prove important later on, our net was flung quite widely. What this meant was that metaphors that were extended in the interviews beyond a singular mention, and were used by two or more people, were included. For this research it was imperative to find metaphors that were shared, rather than unique to an individual. In addition, it was necessary to determine more about each metaphor's attributes. Q-methodology (Kerlinger, 1986; Nunnally, 1978; Rawlins, 1968; Stephenson, 1953) was extremely helpful in this regard.

Q-methodology is a qualitative and quantitative way of gathering and processing data (in this case responses to metaphors) that requires participants to perform a “relative rating task” (Nunnally, 1978). By requiring the participants to sort statements into a forced normal distribution, many of the problems associated with questionnaires (e.g., central tendency, leniency) can be avoided.

One of the main assumptions of Q-methodology is that taken together, all of the statements used in the Q-sort represent the possible domain of opinion on the topic existing in the organization. In this study, that meant that the metaphors identified in interviews were considered to be the main organizational metaphors then in use.

## Using dramatism to analyze methodologies

The next step was to examine the most widely used systems development methodologies and approaches in order to find out whether the researchers who created them used metaphors to elucidate their points. Each author's fundamental writings about his or her respective methodology were examined using a critical method called dramatistic analysis. This particular method of dramatistic analysis is rooted in the study of messages, “which dramatize events in the past, in the future, or at some other place” (Bormann, 1972, p. 400). One of the advantages of this approach to dramatism is that results of criticism can be replicated to some extent (Bormann, 1972).

## Common Organizational Metaphors

Many authors have attempted classifications of metaphors. It is not the purpose of this paper to make an exhaustive list of metaphors useful in systems development because the possibilities are literally limitless (given the complexity of language and the power of imagination applied to its use). Instead, this paper attempts to provide a classification of metaphors used in systems analysis and design in order to suggest possibilities for other researchers and IS professionals using metaphors for systems development.

For the study presented here, the six metaphors presented by Clancy (1989) were observed: journey, game, war, machine, organism, and society. Clancy examined the texts of speeches given by CEOs as well as the books they wrote. He presents a critical/historical analysis of metaphor usage in business.

In order to be comprehensive, it was necessary to include three additional metaphors found frequently and repeatedly in the statements of users. These were metaphors of family, jungle, and zoo. These metaphors were not typical of CEOs but were typical of some end users. Although not present in Clancy's (1989) work, it seemed essential to include them here. The nine main metaphors are pictured in Table 1 along with key attributes that differentiate one metaphor from another. Each of these metaphors is described in more detail in the following sections.

## Game

The “game” metaphor (usually a sports team playing a game) entails the ideas of goal, difficulty, known risks, fun, teamwork, and leadership. The company as a team means that each player has a specific position to play. The success of the team depends on the competency of each member at each position working with all other members toward a common goal. High praise from users enacting the game metaphor is to declare a group member “a real team player.” When the metaphor is the game, the goal is to win, which means that there will be winners and losers in the corporation’s experience. There is constant striving in the organization, along with a great deal of pressure to perform.

## Machine

The major entailment of the “machine” metaphor is its predictability. The machine has a purpose or goal, and its performance can be calculated. The machine was created or designed by an individual. The machine metaphor may entice a leader into believing he or she is “omniscient,” knowing “all [of the organization’s] operations and all its potential” (Clancy, 1989, p. 89). Omniscience can be highly undesirable. Clancy notes that this idea of the “leader” as creator/designer is unique to the machine metaphor.

A critical component of the machine metaphor is its indifferent handling of employees, who are often viewed as interchangeable cogs in the machine. Each part (employee) has its function or partial function to perform. Individual parts are never important in and of themselves; they are subordinate to the functioning of the whole. The machine metaphor overemphasizes rationality.

When users enact a machine metaphor, they often describe the system's trouble-free processing as "running like a well-oiled machine." When problems are encountered by the organizational machine, they are attributed to the worker's inadequacy, not the machine's, e.g., "He threw a wrench into the works," or, "He's all geared up."

## Journey

The key entailments of the “journey” metaphor are the leader; his or her team or crew; unpredictability, including the possibility of danger and risk; and, potentially, adventure. Most often the journey is a sea voyage. A journey requires a strong leader who can lead a cooperative, tightly knit crew to do his or her bidding to complete the journey. The captain and crew focus on what they can do to further the voyage. The journey can be highly unpredictable. As such, the captain and crew run considerable risk in undertaking the voyage. To enact this metaphor, users accept the idea that the organization has a goal, although it may be distant.

Even though captain and crew embark on a journey with a plan, existence at sea is not without its perils. During the metaphorical sea journey of a business, waters of the economy can grow rough and choppy. The ship can actually be so damaged as to sink. If the captain's charting is imperfect, the ship of the organization may run aground.

## Jungle

For the organizational “jungle” the key entailment is unpredictability. The idea of “every man for himself” is clearly operating. An organizational member who awakens as a stranger in the organizational jungle cannot share a common goal with others. The use of any means to ensure survival is permissible and understandable (jungle aggressions would doubtless be unacceptable behavior in other metaphors). Jungle inhabitants actively negotiate with the environment to hold danger at bay.

The jungle is thick with chaos. There is an over-riding perception of danger and being imperiled at every turn. What once seemed a clear path (say, maximizing profits or increasing market share) is now eclipsed in darkness and uncertainty. Danger and chaos equal unpredictability of the most-feared kind. Those stranded in the jungle metaphor are constantly on the lookout for what may be the next danger to befall them.

In the jungle metaphor, no leader arises who will take charge. The only hope is for a guide to route the group away from danger and eventually out of the jungle. The journey metaphor (which shares the entailment of danger with the jungle metaphor) can be characterized as an adventure. By contrast, the jungle is a nightmare, pitting organizational members against nature.

## Family

The “family” metaphor features people coming together to share each other’s company. This affiliation provides comfort and friendliness for its members. People using this metaphor typically claim, “We’re all one big happy family here.” The organizational family need not be the stereotypical nuclear family. It often includes an extended family of organizational members who are supportive of one another. Members in the family metaphor display order; each member has a role to play, and there is an internal focus on keeping the group together as a cohesive whole.

In the family metaphor, people with different goals co-exist. Political negotiations are strongly associated with this metaphor. Siblings, while often solidly supportive of each other, still disagree. In their search for need fulfillment they often vie for the attention of the head of the family. For organizations enacting a family metaphor, the organizational leader is the head of the family who makes balancing decisions regarding the fulfillment of the disparate needs of the siblings and adults in the extended family. Interestingly, family negotiating does not preclude protecting one another from outside forces. Also entailed in the family metaphor is an element of sacrifice for another family member in need. There is unpredictability in the life of an organization enacting the family metaphor. With each member reacting to his or her own needs, it is not clear how or if the needs will be met.

## Zoo

The organizational “zoo” is a metaphoric reality often born of frustration. The zoo reflects a chaotic existence where the participants are not fully in charge of the unnatural, hectic habitat superimposed upon them. Unpredictability and chaos are the key entailments of the zoo metaphor.

In the zoo metaphor, the orientation is toward what goes on internally rather than on the outside. Animals are in cages, so no danger from natural predators exists. The organizational zoo is a collection of people who do not belong together. For example, in a real zoo, visitors view African elephants and Indian elephants together. Since they are “all just elephants” to the casual onlooker, convenience groups them together in one location. The people caged in the metaphorical zoo are similarly without a common goal. The animals require a keeper to oversee their survival because they are not free to do so themselves. They may negotiate with the keeper for a different space or with each other over resources. However, this maintenance does not entail a role for a true leader.

## Society

The “society” metaphor means that the organization using it is alternative-oriented. The society is not dedicated to any one goal but stresses the political interaction of its members who engage in negotiations to achieve their own individual goals. (For further discussion of alternative versus goal-oriented groups in systems development, see livari, 1989). The society runs according to its own set of rules and regulations. In the society metaphor the organization is made up of diverse groups.

The society possesses an internal orientation, with members preoccupied with their own needs. It is built up in an orderly way to proceed with its daily conduct of business. The society, organized according to rules, tends to be predictable. The leader of the society is the head of state, who executes the law and implements the rules that arise from political negotiations.

## War

The “war” metaphor always assumes a purpose for waging the war, so having a goal is a primary entailment for war. Put colloquially, “War is hell.” War requires the best leaders and troops, strategy, and material in order to fight and win against the enemy. War is also unpredictable and risky, especially if miscalculations are made or information is incomplete. Waging war is rigidly controlled through rules for engaging the enemy, disengaging, retreating, and handling of prisoners. Gathering of intelligence about enemies (competitors) is important.

The strategy plotted by the highly skilled general is an entailment that separates war from other metaphors, such as journey. As Clancy (1989) notes, “Just as in war, the business leader assesses his own situation, his strengths and weaknesses; he judges the enemy’s (competition’s) position, strengths, and weaknesses; and then he thinks through the opportunities that are present and the threat that must be dealt with” (p. 68). The general differs from the captain of the journey in his or her aloofness from the fate of those sent into battle. Instructions from the top down are one-way communiqués to be carried out, not participative decisions to consider jointly. Information is available to participants on a need-to-know basis. The leader is clearly in command.

## Organism

The “organism” metaphor stands in animated contrast to the lifeless machine metaphor recounted earlier. The mechanical organization is replaced with the living one, meaning the organization can now be born, grow, become diseased, and even die. The organism has a structure and an orderliness to it. It is not chaotic or random in the way its functions are put together, but it is not totally ordered either. Its growth depends on external forces that may not be controllable. The organism must survive and adapt to its external environment.

The organism is neither fully goal-oriented nor fully alternative-oriented. At different stages of its growth it can be either. Inherent in the organism is the propensity for change in order to adapt. Therefore, at one stage it may agree upon one goal, while at another it may be more propitious to harbor a multitude of alternatives.

Entailed in the metaphor of the organism is the necessity of a leader who is able to innovate and care for the organism. $^{1}$ Innovation is the genesis for the capability to adapt, but the leader also nurtures the organism (as a gardener tends to the garden). How the organism will develop and adapt to the environment is ultimately unpredictable.

When an organization uses the organism metaphor, rather than the journey or machine metaphor, it is reflecting the involvement and ambiguity of the world of business. Enacting the living metaphor means that the corporation has potential to grow. Self-knowledge of its own vulnerability and transience may empower the corporation to take calculated risks and to fashion changes that would be unacceptable if organizational members were wedded to the status quo.

After identifying these nine main user metaphors, the next step was to see whether the same metaphors also appeared in systems development methodologies. We hypothesized that matching user metaphors and a systems methodology might contribute to the development of successful information systems. The next part of this study uses dramatism to examine different systems development methodologies.

## Systems Development Methodologies as Metaphors

Shortly after the dominant systems development methodology began shifting away from the traditional SDLC toward structured techniques (see, for example, Colter, 1984; Gane and Sarson, 1979; Jackson, 1983; Yourdon, 1976; 1989) and CASE tools, new methodologies and development approaches were also being introduced (Mahmood, 1987; Necco, et al., 1987). Prototyping, established in other disciplines and applied to IS (Alavi, 1984; Avison and Wilson, 1991; Naumann and Jenkins, 1982), was offered as a response to long development times associated with the systems development life cycle approach, as well as the uncertainty inherent in user requirements. ETHICS (Mumford, 1983; Mumford and Weir, 1979) was introduced as a socio-technical methodology to combine social and technical solutions. Project champions (Beath, 1991; Howell and Higgins, 1990a; 1990b; Maidique, 1980) was offered as a participative approach for involving one key person to ensure systems success. Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1981) was envisioned as a way to model a world that is often chaotic. Multiview (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990) was proposed as a way to organize competing methodologies.

Dramatistic analysis was used to find out whether the methodologies displayed any of the essence of the user metaphors identified previously. The findings are displayed in Table 2, which maps the various metaphors onto commonly used methodologies. The rationale for each mapping is explained in the following sections.

## SDLC as a game

Most familiar, and most widely taught, is the traditional systems development life cycle (SDLC), wherein the analyst proceeds systematically through several predetermined phases in the life cycle of systems. The SDLC took a powerful hold in the 1960s and remained strong throughout the 1980s. The SDLC embodies a game metaphor, with the discussion of the team of systems analysts being first and foremost. Kendall and Kendall (1988) discuss analysts as “team members” who are selected for their “competency” and “compatibility.” They also discuss setting proper “goals” for project productivity and “motivating” the team to achieve them. The game metaphor includes the concepts of team members chosen because of their competency, shared team goals, and the need for inspiring those playing the game.

If a team wants to win, it must follow the rules of the game prescribed in the SDLC. Elaborate diagramming, as well as the strategic information systems plans drawn up before a system is built, is closely akin to the game plan drawn by the coach revealing team possibilities for future plays. The goal is to win by designing and implementing the system.

Winning or losing in the SDLC game is mentioned in terms of information systems successes or failures. Opposition is often in the form of organizational members who will eventually use the system. Whitten, et al. (1989) discuss this as an “us-versus-them” environment, with analysts and programmers aligned against users. Arguing for more successful systems development made possible by including end users on the same team, they note involving and educating users will “minimize such misunderstandings,” and Laudon and Laudon (1988) comment that “education is also crucial to winning end user acceptance of new ideas and change” (p. 488).

## Structured methodologies and CASE tools as a machine

Analysts today are steeped in the tradition of the machine metaphor. It is so prevalent today (see, for example, Yourdon, 1989) that readers would scarcely remark on it. One researcher has characterized structured methodologies in the same vein as tools, stating that, “Methodologies are just the way we fix things up, when they are broken or don’t work right” (Cornford, 1991). The Gane and Sarson (1979) approach (called STRADIS) is a prime example of a structured methodology, although there are many others, all equally caught up in the machine metaphor.

Clearly, the roles of analysts and programmers in STRADIS are as designers of machine-like, smoothly running systems. User participation is limited to early phases and final stages of the methodology. That aside, professionals work in isolation conceiving logical and physical designs for the automated system.

Another popular structured methodology that uses the machine metaphor is Jackson Systems Development (JSD). Jackson (1983) notes that the system itself may be thought of “as a large program” that must be developed as if it were a machine to be maintained. Jackson Systems Development was created with a machine, not an organization, in mind. It entirely omits the topic of user participation. JSD’s strength as a methodology has been described as lying in its treatment of “concurrency, timing and process scheduling” (Avison and Fitzgerald, 1988, p.216). As a methodology, JSD is widely used and prized for its machine-like precision.

CASE tools seem to be virtually incapable of being discussed without the help of the machine metaphor. CASE is an acronym for computer-aided (or assisted) software engineering; both computers and engineering are closely associated with machines. Additionally, the word “tools” in the name of the approach contributes further to the strong link with machines.

Early synonyms for CASE tools include the phrases “analyst/programmer workbench” (Whitten, et al., 1989) and “workbench technologies” (Kendall and Kendall, 1988), which conjure up a picture of the analyst as tinkerer who puts together a machine on a workbench. The accounting firm of Coopers & Lybrand discusses its CASE tool as a “standardized tool kit” (Laudon and Laudon, 1988), emphasizing the importance of standardization while downplaying human uniqueness or analyst subjectivity. The company also describes the capabilities of its CASE tool as capable of scanning diagrams for “accuracy” and “completeness,” all automatically and more accurately than a human. CASE tools purportedly “increase productivity,” as should the use of any new piece of machinery. Some CASE tools include “code generators to eliminate or reduce programming.” These expressions further contribute to the machine metaphor, suggesting that, once designed, the machine can work unaided.

## Prototyping as a journey

Prototyping demands rapid development of some system features, high analyst and user involvement, the ability to incorporate changes quickly according to feedback, and a willingness to scrap what is undesirable. The analyst goes through several iterations of use and feedback, refining features until the user is satisfied with the system (Alavi, 1984; Avison and Wilson, 1991; Naumann and Jenkins, 1982). Prototyping is rife with uncertainty about user requirements, user behavior, and the feasibility of technical design solutions. It is an understatement to say that user needs and behavior are “not entirely predictable” (Gould and Lewis, 1985). Uncertainty about the future is a fundamental part of both journey and prototyping.

Perhaps when we make a quick prototype of the system we are making a crude map suggesting that which is to come. As users experiment in finding their way through the system, they necessarily want to modify the course. "As they react to and refine each version of the prototype, users become more intimately involved in the design effort" (Cerveny, et al., 1986), as does the crew of a ship adapting to the changing sea. Additionally, Naumann and Jenkins (1982) mention that during the prototyping process, problems and misfits with the prototype are "discovered" and then addressed. This is similar to the process of discovery in the journey metaphor.

In the end, analysts and users are responsible for reaching the goal of the final system, just as in the journey metaphor, in which the captain and crew must safely reach their destination. The analyst knows that the eventual outcome of the journey is reaching an agreed-upon goal (the system). However, what will occur during the process is full of the unknown.

## Project champion as the guide out of the jungle

Borrowing from the marketing and management literature, the product champion becomes the project champion for information technology (Beath, 1991; Howell and Higgins, 1990a; 1990b; Maidique, 1980). The champion is one user chosen by the organization who is seen as pivotal in driving the acceptance of a new technology. Project champions are enthusiastic, knowledgeable about the technology, have influence with key user groups, and basically stake their future careers on the success of the new technology by actively promoting its acceptance in the organization. IT champions strongly value: “(1) assistance in developing information to help sell their IT vision, (2) a flexible implementation process, and (3) legitimation of their ideas for using information technology” (Beath, 1991, p. 355).

The project champion possesses particular personality characteristics, leadership behavior, and career experience (Howell and Higgins, 1990b). A few of those characteristics identified as special to the project champion include: self-confidence, persistence in the face of doubt and negative reaction, high energy within organizational relationships, readiness to take risks, the capability to express a captivating vision to the technologically unaware in the organization, willingness to pursue unconventional action plans, ability to develop others' potential, gives recognition to others, has a long tenure in the organization, is likely to be in a middle-management position, has decision-making authority, has in-depth knowledge of the industry, and has experience in many divisions and locations.

It is possible to view the project champion as the guide who will enable users to get out of the jungle in which they find themselves. Lost in the jungle, users feel that nature (or information technology) is against them. They require a guide (project champion) to route them away from danger and eventually out of the jungle. Those involved in the jungle metaphor respond instinctively to a rapidly changing environment. In the case of the project champion, users may fear or not understand technology and falter in their abilities to cope with it. The project champion serves as a guide out of the nightmarish jungle and into a technologically superior civilization.

## ETHICS as a family

The ETHICS method (Mumford, 1983; Mumford and Weir, 1979) has a “participative nature” emphasizing user involvement. The socio-technical approach upon which ETHICS is based “improves the relationship between the groups and its work” (Mumford, 1983, p. 8). ETHICS examines the quality of work life, helping users design solutions to problems that not only fulfill technical objectives but also give consideration to social interactions. Mumford (1991) points out that the ETHICS method attempts to fulfill users' information needs in a social context, whether the solution is a manual procedure or the use of a computer. A work group needs to spend time together in order to recognize its true needs.

In the family metaphor, members are grouped together through the sharing of resources, even though they hold differing views. The situation is strikingly similar when using ETHICS. Group work is to be improved in the context of the organization's life, not just the individual's. Supportiveness is a key element of the users who compose a work group using this method. By using ETHICS, users have helped "design the system which fitted their job-satisfaction needs and to which they felt a sense of personal commitment" (Mumford and Weir, 1979, p. 295). This closely resembles the commitment that families feel toward their members. Group members using ETHICS are alternative-oriented, just as what occurs in the family metaphor. They are working toward achieving balance among multiple social and technical objectives in the design of a system. As they redesign their work, they are searching for ways to improve their shared work their comfort within the group. Mumford and Weir (1979) state:

Each “solution” may include a whole range of options . . . [which] may be extremely important in the evaluation of a particular system . . . The process of investigating alternatives and working through the technical and social advantages of these tends to suggest other alternatives, which then have to be considered (p. 294).

We find in the ETHICS method, as in the family metaphor, a strong sense of group supportiveness for improving their life together, personal commitment to values, an alternative orientation, and concern for the group's overall lifestyle.

## Soft systems methodology as a zoo

Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) created by Checkland (1981; 1989) and discussed by Atkinson (1989) is concerned with helping the organization learn by prescribing general problem formulation approaches rather than tools and techniques. At its core is the “root definition” that spells out verbally the critical aspects of the organization. It concerns itself with wholes rather than parts and “denies the existence of organizational objectives, needs, and performance objectives” (Atkinson, 1989, p. 44). Checkland believes that human activity systems possess goals that are not quantifiable and describes organizations wrestling with these goals as going through an “organizational conversation” (Checkland, 1991).

Soft Systems Methodology can be viewed as a zoo metaphor. Users believe they are grouped in their organization in an illogical way, much as animals are grouped together in the zoo, i.e., they cannot relate in anything but a chaotic way because of their groupings of convenience. Checkland (1989) describes what led to the development of soft systems methodology:

In our research we found ourselves seeking an approach to problem solving which would cope with messy situations in which objectives were themselves problematical (p. 275).

Further considering SSM in the chaotic context of the zoo metaphor, we find this comment by Checkland (1989) on the ongoing nature of problems:

Although management “problems” may occasionally be temporarily “solved” out of existence, this is only a special case of the continuing process. Choose the right time frame and all is seen to be in flux.... Given this broad view of managing, SSM assumes that different individuals and groups, being ultimately autonomous, will make different evaluations leading to different actions (p. 278).

In the zoo metaphor, there is not just one commonly agreed-upon goal; rather, there is an alternative orientation. Checkland (1989) notes that:

SSM . . . accepts the real-world action will be much messier than these pure models, and uses the models to structure a debate in which different conflicting objectives, needs, purposes, interests, values can be teased out and discussed. It thus seeks to articulate a process in which accommodation between conflicting interests and views can be sought, an accommodation which will enable action aimed at feasible improvement to be undertaken (p. 288).

Soft Systems Methodology discusses the chaos (messiness) of the organizational situation in which users and analysts find themselves. It also shows concern, as the zoo keeper does, and exhibits the same alternative orientation as the animals in the zoo metaphor.

## Multiview as a society

The Multiview methodology (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990) discusses defining an information system as a “social process.” Avison and Wood-Harper also state:

The process of going from “thinking about the content of the information situation” by the information systems development team to the “perceived content” of the situation (including users) . . . can be examined within different social theories relevant to information systems definition (p. 269).

Multiview sees analysts as acting in a society, or social system, with the possibility of taking on several different societal roles, e.g., either as a technical expert, a liberal teacher, an agent for social progress, or a change catalyst (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990).

Multiview maintains an alternative-oriented perspective, which is a hallmark of the society metaphor. Avison and Wood-Harper (1990) write:

Multiview is a flexible framework which provides an alternative to choosing between different methodologies. The techniques and tools available within the Multiview framework are chosen and adjusted according to the particular problem situation. Multiview is a blended methodology drawing from a number of major methodologies already in use or proposed (p. 17).

Even with this professed flexibility, much of Multiview is taken up with rules on how to diagram, among other things. Rules are a very important aspect of the society metaphor as well. Further, Multiview is explained in terms of the negotiating aspects of the society metaphor:

Information systems emerge as a result of negotiation between managers, operators, analysts, and suppliers, and as a result of the interplay of the functional, social and political systems (p. 18).

Multiview can usefully be seen as employing the society metaphor. It exists in a society where each person has his or her own role. Numerous rules must be followed, and multiple alternatives are decided upon via negotiation.

## IS development deteriorating into war

Often in discussing development, analysts and users recount their struggle as if engaged in an arduous war. Other researchers (Hirschheim and Newman, 1991) comment that information systems development is often characterized by both users and analysts as a “battle.” It is highly interesting that both “sides” enact this metaphor. From the perspective of systems analysts, they assert that they are “doing what is right” (e.g., installing a system by waging war). Analysts maintain the attitude that technology is good and that they will win the war because good is on their side. When this enactment of the war metaphor is taken to extremes, there is no request for user participation. In fact, just the opposite occurs, with systems analysts blitzing the organization with changes, firm in the belief that pressing on without interaction will assure that the new system triumphs.

Users' enactment of the war metaphor is different from that of analysts, since users talk of being besieged. One assistant managing director of a health insurance company commented bitterly that the expensive new system developed for them had so many problems and shortcomings that it was “littered with mine fields.” Carrying through on the metaphor, he mentioned that, “No one ever asks where the dead bodies are” when coming to assess the satisfactoriness of the new system. He added that, “There are plenty if evaluators would just bother to ask.” Absence of invited user participation results in users finding their own unorthodox ways to get involved, sometimes through sabotaging the new system. Using a variety of means, users attempt to win a small battle against the occupying force of analysts, even though users feel they cannot win the war. Examples range from users refusing to sign off on changes and deliberately missing meetings to rejecting the new system without trying it.

Hedberg (1975) extends the war metaphor to two types of dysfunctional user participation, which he labels “hostage” and “indoctrination.” In hostage participation the user becomes overwhelmed by technical jargon and withdraws. When indoctrination is used, users are indoctrinated with the creed of the analyst and thereafter cannot provide useful input. Both types of dysfunctional participation are engendered by the war metaphor, as is sabotage (the most extreme response). Mason (1991), working on strategic applications of information technology and IT planning, offers sound insights into the pervasiveness of the war metaphor in strategic circumstances. He suggests that it imposes severe restrictions on those who use it, and he offers alternative metaphors for consideration, including the organism and the sports team. He does not propose a formal methodology to change an organization’s metaphor, but he does recognize serious drawbacks with using the war metaphor in strategic situations.

## Absence of the organism metaphor

The closest we have come to experiencing the widespread use of the organism metaphor is through the use of general systems theory. However, one of the most commonly voiced complaints in systems is that general systems theory is so general that it cannot be applied in any formal way.

We can only speculate as to why an organism-based methodology has not developed. Firstly, the organism metaphor requires a great deal of flexibility and adaptability to flourish. Creating a methodology that is adaptive and flexible while still retaining enough material that can be codified and taught becomes paradoxical. Typically, flexibility is sacrificed to concreteness, in the interest of being able to convey material in a structured way. Secondly, using an organism-based metaphor would make us confront our own worst fears, i.e., that systems, like other organisms, mature and die. Most of the development methodologies are biased toward assuming that new systems are superior and should be built while also implying that aged systems, well past their prime, are dark secrets whose demise should not be discussed in polite company.

## Discussion

This paper identified the existence of nine major metaphors employed by user groups in 16 major firms. The paper has described the distinguishing attributes of each metaphor, whether they exhibit order or chaos, whether they are alternative or goal-oriented, and the type of leadership they portray. The following sections attempt to put the findings into perspective, specifically relating metaphors to one another, relating metaphors to systems development methods, and relating theory about metaphors to the development of information systems by IS professionals.

## Relating metaphors to one another

Figures 1 and 2 organize the metaphors and their corresponding methodologies, first separating them according to their orientation (goal versus alternative) and then by their other attributes. Notice that four of the metaphors (game, journey, machine, and war) are goal-oriented. Four others juggle conflicting alternatives and are not oriented to a common goal. The organism metaphor appears more balanced in this regard than the other eight metaphors, being somewhat goal-oriented but alternative-oriented as well.

The metaphors are also split over the attribute of environment, with four metaphors (journey, jungle, zoo, and war) having a chaotic environment. The game, machine, family, and society metaphors all have ordered environments. The organism exhibits both characteristics: ordered growth with external, sometimes chaotic influences.

The last attribute, that of “leader needed” provided some interesting texture. Non-participative leaders were to be found in the machine metaphor, which has a designer who builds the machine; the society, which has a head of state who executes the rules; the zoo, whose inhabitants have a keeper; and the war metaphor’s general, who commands the troops from afar. These metaphors have leaders who favor consultative participation, where users give input to the project but systems analysts make the decisions (Land, 1982).

Four other metaphors suggest that responsible participation (as defined by Land, 1982) is the norm. This is where users are involved throughout the entire development process and assume full responsibility and authority for making and implementing decisions. The journey metaphor requires a captain who is willing to go down with the ship and crew; the game requires a coach who is supportive, whose success depends on the achievement of the entire team; the family needs a head who balances the requests of other family members; and the jungle hopefully can produce a guide to lead the group out into civilization. Again, the organism stands alone because the gardener nurtures the garden, encouraging it to grow, but does not have full control over growth. This leader cultivates a balance between consultative and responsible participation.

Many more questions concerning metaphors and their meaning to analysts and users remain. One central question is what will happen if analysts consciously choose a metaphor that is opposite the one found in the organization (e.g., if they find a zoo metaphor and use a traditional SDLC approach to develop a system). Will it spell failure from the outset? Conversely, if the analyst matches the methodology to the users' metaphor, such as ETHICS to a family metaphor, will the probability of IS success be increased? Or does this mean that particular systems are best developed with a particular metaphor (Kendall and Kendall, 1993)?

This also brings about the question of whether analysts should attempt to change the metaphor of the organization. Although this remains to be researched, at this point we do not believe analysts know enough or have it within their expertise to change the users' metaphor by introducing an alien metaphor into the organizational setting. Perhaps this is the work of organizational developers or other types of organizational interventionists but not an appropriate role for systems analysts to play.

## Relating metaphors to methods

In this paper, methodologies have been characterized as the enactment of a particular metaphor. The methodologies chosen for analysis are popular, widely used, and offer a variety of approaches to developing systems. The processes mapped out by methods are shaped by the metaphors that inform them. Metaphors in methodologies influence the way that analysts interact, spelling out how they should interact, how they should proceed, and how they should document and diagram their interactions. Metaphors in methodologies tell analysts how to think about an organizational situation, how to characterize it, and how to act on those assessments.

Figure 1. Goal-Oriented Metaphors and Corresponding Methodologies Along User Participation and Environment Dimensions

Thus, we assert that the metaphors embodied in methodologies are important and of genuine interest because they guide the analyst about appropriate ways to interact and behave. For example, an analyst using the ETHICS methodology, which manifests the family metaphor, would place value on spending time with users because within this metaphor each member is unique and contributes something to the group. By contrast, an analyst using a CASE tool approach, such as Excelerator, enacts a machine metaphor that features roles for the analyst and programmer as designers of the machine. The analyst fulfilling a role in the CASE tool approach behaves differently toward users, requiring only limited participation from them at the beginning and end of the project.

Metaphors present in methodologies paradoxically present opportunities for action, as well as constrain the analyst from seeing and enacting certain possibilities. For instance, an analyst using a prototyping methodology (which we have found enacts the journey metaphor) will seize chances to interact with users whenever possible, together plotting the course to reach their destination. However, the analyst using the journey metaphor in prototyping would not be able to capitalize on the efficiencies present in the machine metaphor of CASE tools, which emphasizes standardization, downplaying interaction with users, human uniqueness, and analyst subjectivity.

We suggest, therefore, that the choice of a development methodology makes a difference. It means choosing the metaphor from which an analyst will approach the organization, understand the organization, and, in turn, be understood. Why then is there such a proliferation of systems development methodologies? One reason is that analysts believe in them; another is that we need a diversity of ways to cope with a diversity of situations. We hypothesize that different methodologies embody different metaphors and that by using an appropriate methodological metaphor in concert with the organization, chances for systems success may be enhanced.

## Relating theory to IT practice

Systems developers should be educated to enter an organization with the awareness that users will be enacting metaphors. Awareness is the first step. Next, the systems analyst should identify the metaphor or metaphors in use among users. (The analysts can accomplish this through observations and interviews. It need not involve asking someone to explicitly discuss their metaphors.) Members of the systems group, along with users, should then interpret and attempt to understand how the metaphor is being used. We then recommend that the nine prevalent metaphors identified serve as a checklist. Are any of the nine metaphors (journey, war, game, organism, society, machine, family, zoo, or jungle) being used? Are any paradoxes present in these metaphors? What are they? How does the client organization's interpretation agree with or depart from characteristics discussed here?

Identification of a user metaphor may guide analyst behavior in choosing an appropriate systems development methodology. In the sense of choosing one methodology rather than another, analysts may want to choose a development approach in response to metaphors that are present. Experience tells us that the odds of successful systems development and implementation are much higher if the analyst can share the world view of users rather than trying to superimpose an unfamiliar metaphor. As a significant part of language, we believe metaphors both shape and reflect the views of reality held by organizational members.

By their very presence in the organization, analysts change it. It is not necessarily appropriate for analysts to set out to change the organization's metaphor. This would mean changing a substantial portion of an organization's culture, and analysts have lamentably little expertise in this. Further, it is not something that should be undertaken on a superficial basis. Rather, an experiential approach might be preferable, one that takes as its first step an attempt to understand what metaphors are in use, what they mean, and how they are serving users. Analysts can interpret metaphors along with other information gathered about users. Then, analysts can choose a methodology that mirrors, extends, or incorporates a prevalent metaphor. The authors' organizational experience and exploratory studies suggest that analysts diminish their chances for a successful system when they adopt a metaphor devoid of values articulated in the metaphors of the client organization. Specifically, adopting a systems development methodology that embodies a metaphor contrary to what has been found in the organizational narrative will make the success of the system less likely.

Awareness of user metaphors is one way analysts can begin effecting change. Feeding back perceived metaphors/paradoxes to users is yet another. Extending, building upon, incorporating, or actively exploring corporate metaphors through choice of methodology is another way to effectuate change. However, the analyst's choice of methodology is but one of many factors in facilitating change through awareness of metaphors.

What this means to IS professionals is that they should take seriously what is being said by participants in systems projects. They must pay close attention to how users express their views of organizational life through different metaphors. We do not recommend acting upon metaphors as a lone data point, but we do believe that within the organizational context analysts should examine them carefully when making decisions about how to proceed.

## Caveats and Contributions

There are five caveats about our work with metaphors that should help other researchers and IS professionals put it into perspective:

1. Analysts should lead the systems development process by selecting a methodology to match user metaphors, not the other way around.

2. Most metaphors are paradoxes, and analysts must use, rather than suppress, this paradoxical richness.

3. Analysts should not limit the number of metaphors because it limits the usefulness of a metaphorical approach.

4. Analysts should be trained in a variety of systems development methodologies.

5. Metaphorical analysis must always be used in conjunction with other approaches. Use of multiple methods is critical to the success of information systems development.

The first caveat involves the question of causality. Some critics have raised the idea that selection of a systems development methodology may actually drive the user selection of metaphors. However, we observed that when an analyst enters an organization there are metaphors already in use that can be identified and interpreted. Metaphors are ubiquitous, and they say something about the people who are using them. We recommend selecting a methodology that incorporates, reflects, or extends the identified metaphors in a useful way. It is traditionally the analyst who is expected to organize and lead the process of systems development.

A second caveat is suggested by the capability of the systems analyst to grasp the full meaning of the metaphor. It is important for analysts using metaphors to remember that groups and their expressions are subject to paradoxes. The classic paradox of the group is that although members are dependent on the group for identity, members feel compelled to express their individual independence (Smith and Berg, 1987). Some metaphors seem straightforward (for example, the war or journey) while others are puzzling and paradoxical (family, zoo, and jungle). The family implies caring, sharing, and closeness, but it also reveals tension, competition, and compromise. The zoo appears orderly while the keeper protects the inhabitants—yet chaos dominates. The jungle is mysterious, challenging, and at times alluring, while at the same time it is dangerous, frightening, and chaotic. A thoughtful analyst should recognize these paradoxes, seeing that each metaphor is a many-faceted entity rather than a collection of attributes. Identifying and exploring the paradoxes residing in user metaphors (with the help of a development methodology that incorporates or extends the metaphor) allows the analyst to help user groups become more deeply involved in systems projects.

The third caveat is that we are limiting the systems analyst because there are simply many more metaphors than are discussed in this paper. While we make no claims that we have found the complete set of archetypal metaphors, two ideas weigh in our favor. First, critics have suggested other metaphors that are neglected here, e.g., religion, circus, and factory. Religion, however, has similarities to the jungle, in which the guide saves the people as long as they put their faith in a savior; the circus is quite similar to the zoo, albeit more active; and factory is an elaboration of the machine. We believe that the nine metaphors are as complete as possible because they are the only metaphors found in the organizations visited for this study. $^{2}$ We could not justify adding other metaphors unless there was evidence that they really existed in the corporation.

A fourth caveat is the limitation that one systems analyst might not be comprehensively trained in all of the systems development methodologies discussed here. This has certainly been true in the past. Future systems analysts should be exposed to a full palette of systems development approaches. Also, IS professionals and organizations should remain flexible in their approaches to problem solving (Kendall, et al., 1992). By remaining flexible, the odds of choosing a useful approach to developing a system increase.

The fifth and final caveat is that analysis and use of metaphors in developing information systems is but one small part of many other methods that the analyst uses. We encourage the use of multiple methods to ensure the richest possible approach and the deepest possible understanding of what is happening in the organization. Awareness of metaphors can be successfully incorporated into many different approaches, but in and of itself it cannot yet be considered a development methodology.

Given these not inconsiderable caveats, it is hoped this paper has accomplished the introduction of a new subject, drawing attention to the relationships among metaphors, behavior, and methodologies and hypothesizing some of their effects. Because this was an exploratory study drawing from many different disciplines, possibilities for answering research questions raised here are many and promising. One research avenue could lead to investigations of how metaphors and methods affect one another. A different sojourn could doubtless be made to identify and interpret more metaphors in users' language and systems development methodologies. A third research path could lead to devising empirical and theoretical ways to test the hypothesis that congruence between user metaphors and the system development methodology produces better project outcomes.

Why then is the understanding of metaphors in systems development worthwhile? Perhaps awareness of them can help prevent the numerous systems failures that are all too common. A plethora of well-recognized methodologies is available, but there are no clear-cut indications of which methodology to use in a given situation. Important metaphorical data are basically of little or no cost to the analyst because it can be gleaned from standard interview data. Metaphors matter, and yet they are so ubiquitous as to be invisible to many analysts. What metaphors reveal can be used when analysts make decisions about the types of methodologies to use in a particular situation. Analysts are required to design a wide variety of systems compared to expectations of just 20 years ago. Examining user metaphors and then checking whether development methodologies exhibit those same characteristics is only one of many ways to facilitate successful development, but we believe it is a highly useful one.

# Appendix Methodological Details

## Details of Interviewing

Both researchers were present for individual interviews with a purposive sample of organizational members in each of the 16 companies. Each interview lasted about 45 minutes. Interviews were structured but consisted of many open-ended questions (Cash and Stewart, 1986). The importance of asking open-ended questions for this kind of exploratory study cannot be over-emphasized because when interviewees elaborate, their language reveals attitudes, morals, beliefs, opinions, feelings, and, of special interest here, metaphors. In addition to questions about their information systems, users were asked questions about their leadership (for example: “Describe who you think is the leader of your group,” “What are some of their important characteristics?”); questions about their groups (e.g., “How would you describe your group and their interactions?”); and questions about the goals of the organization (e.g., “What goals do you believe the organization has?” “How does what you do on your job contribute to their achievement?” “Can you provide an example of that?”).

The researchers did not ask people about their use of metaphors in an isolated context. Rather, the questions were part of a traditional information requirements determination phase of either an information analysis project or a systems analysis and design project. Open-ended questions allow metaphors to surface. Because metaphors occur naturally when people speak, they do not need to be prompted to use them. This approach examines the metaphors that users employ as part of their typical means of expression and attempts to understand what the metaphor means to them in the organizational context of information systems usage.

## Details of Focus Group Interviews

Focus groups were composed of three to six participants drawn from a purposive sample covering different departments in each of the 16 companies. The pool for the sample was based on referrals. Questions asked of participants in the focus group interviews were similar to those asked in individual interviews with two important differences. Because of the highly dynamic nature of focus group interviews, it is not possible (or necessary) to ask as many questions. So fewer questions were asked, and the volume of response was typically much greater than in individual interviews.

A second important difference between individual and focus group interviews is that questions asked of the focus group are more concerned with group interactions as they collect, store, disseminate, share, and make meaning of the information received from the information system. Examples of focus group interview questions asked are: "What are typical meetings where you come together as a group?" "Describe what happens during these meetings," and "Do people have set roles that they play during the meeting?" Focus group interviews each lasted for about one hour, allowing time for story telling and chaining to one another's comments. Transcripts of the interviews were then analyzed for predominant metaphors.

## Details of Q-Methodology

In this study, a Q-deck of 27 relevant statements (including metaphors) was constructed from interview data. Each statement was put on a separate card to build the deck. It was critical for this study that the language included in the Q-deck statements be that of the users. In the organizations studied here, the language of the Q-statements was drawn from the words of the users found in interview transcripts. Statements in the Q-deck included comments about the organizational climate (e.g., “Most days are utter chaos, with everyone running in different directions. We are grouped together for the convenience of management, and we never have enough time, money, or information”); comments about the leadership (e.g., “Our leader looks out for the welfare of all of us”); or simply a direct metaphor (e.g., “We function like a well-oiled machine”).

For ease in sorting the Q-deck appropriately, a Q-sort board was devised. The right hand side of the Q-sort board was labeled “strongly agree,” and the extreme left was labeled “strongly disagree.” Using the board to guide them, participants were asked to sort the deck of 27 cards into piles of one, three, five, nine, five, three, and one card(s) respectively, a forced normal distribution. Once Q-sorting were complete, the strongest statements from the extreme ends of the deck were identified and examined using the methodology outline by Rawlins (1968). In all cases Q-methodology helped identify which metaphors people in organizations used. Because our participants were from every level of each organization, it was surmised that the metaphors identified were representative of the organization as a whole.

It was possible to have nine people sort Q-decks simultaneously. The number of people sorting the decks was small enough for the researchers to answer questions on sorting when they arose and large enough to gather data efficiently. Each group of Q-sorts took about one half-hour to complete. For further details about using Q-sorts in IS research, see Kendall, et al. (1989).

## Details of Dramatism

To get an overall picture of how the respective authors vividly depict their methodologies, their writings were examined using a type of dramatism based on the work of Bormann (1972), asking, “What practices and actors are considered good in this methodology?” “What and who is castigated as evil?” “When things go wrong with the project, who is to blame?” “What roles do the systems analyst and the end users play?” “What metaphor serves to crystallize and codify role expectations and ways of behaving for analysts and users in a particular methodology?” Rather than use a traditional method of content analysis, which precisely counts occurrences and positions of words and phrases but may miss larger meanings, dramatism was used to acquire a deeper understanding of the attitudes, values, and beliefs of the author as dramatized in his or her unique vision of how systems development should proceed.

Dramatism was used to critically examine each author's work for consistent, overarching metaphors that were repeatedly returned to in the course of the explanations of their own methodologies. Bormann (1972) believes that by examining the written text, a rhetorical critic could use a humanistic approach to understanding and interpreting the rhetorical vision present, complete with any metaphors used to create the vision. For a thorough discussion of the use of dramatistic analysis and its use in studying organizational communication, see Kendall (forthcoming 1993).

In order to supplement the insights about what the authors of the methodologies were saying, one of three things was done for the methodologies studied. We either (1) attended formal lectures about the methodologies given by one of the authors; (2) participated in a methodology workshop with the authors; or (3) conducted informal interviews with the authors about their methodologies.
