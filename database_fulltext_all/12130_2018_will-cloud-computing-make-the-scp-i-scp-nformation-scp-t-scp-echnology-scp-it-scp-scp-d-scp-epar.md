# Will cloud computing make the Information Technology (IT) department obsolete?

## Joseph Vithayathil

Computer Management and Information Systems, School of Business, Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, Campus Box 1106, Edwardsville, IL 62026‐1106, USA

## Abstract

The rapid adoption and growth of cloud computing is creating unprecedented change in the manner in which IT services are procured, managed, and deployed. Cloud computing is forcing firms to rethink traditional IT governance practices while raising new and fundamental questions for scholars and practitioners. This paper identifies the major areas of change and highlights governance issues that arise with the adoption of cloud computing. The focus of this paper is on the organizational impact on IT governance under cloud computing. The paper posits (1) that successful IT departments under cloud computing will transform into new roles that address internal customer‐facing issues and external cloud‐facing issues, (2) firms that mitigate information asymmetry under cloud computing will show higher firm performance, and (3) firms that offer superior cloudsourced IT service attributes of internal prices, quality, variety, and competition in the cloud will show higher firm performance.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION TO CLOUD COMPUTING AND THE ROLE OF THE IT DEPARTMENT

The late pioneer of artificial intelligence and the L ISP programming language John McCarthy has been widely creditec with proposing computing as a utility in 1961, and these ideas were later explored by Parkhill (1966). Cloud‐shaped figures were used in depicting communication networks and the Internet, and the term cloud computing may hav arisen from this practice (Fogarty, 2012). Cloud computing is analogous to the concept of service bureaus such as the IBM subsidiary Service Bureau Corporation that rented time on its tabulating machines well before the advent of the modern server (Scavo, 2005). Time‐sharing was one of the terms used to describe early versions of cloud com puting. Application Service Providers (ASP), an early incarnation of cloud computing services, were prevalent in the late 1990s by providing externally hosted applications to clients over a variety of communication services. Practitioners argue that the ASP model failed and was successfully replaced by what is known today as the Saas (Software‐as‐a‐Service) model of cloud computing. The ASP model lacked some of the key attributes of modern day cloud computing such as multi‐tenancy, where infrastructure resources are shared among users. Hence, it took a process of fits and starts to arrive at the widely available and rapidly growing business and technology model of cloud computing that consumers and organizations deploy today

The rapid adoption of cloud services has raised the fundamental question of the existence of the traditional IT department. Under cloud computing, IT departments may need to address the question of what additional service or value‐added attributes to IT services it can provide that are unavailable from cloud vendors. This paper discusse these questions and argues for transformed roles for the IT department under cloud computing. The consequence to the IT department are captured in a set of opinions that form the basis of a research agenda.

## 1.1 | Cloud computing today

Cloud computing as we know it in the 21st century has emerged from the combination of high bandwidth communi cations, ubiquitous and low‐cost computing and storage. A broad view of cloud computing is captured by the charac teristics of on‐demand computing and storage, with shared resources between multiple customers. The sharing of computing and storage resources enables operational efficiencies and scale efficiencies. Such characteristics led to a more formal definition of what constitutes cloud computing by Armbrust et al. (2010) and Mell and Grance (2011) based on five essential characteristics. These are: (1) on‐demand self‐service, (2) broad network access, (3) resource pooling, (4) rapid elasticity, and (5) measured service. Cloud computing eliminates the upfront capital costs of a captive, or in‐house IT infrastructure installation and the associated costs of maintaining and upgrading this infrastructure.

Cloud computing has three basic service models (Mell & Grance, 2011): (1) Infrastructure‐as‐a‐Service (IaaS), (2) Platform‐as‐a‐Service (PaaS), and (3) Software‐as‐a‐Service (SaaS). Infrastructure‐as‐a‐Service offers raw computing and storage services, whereas PaaS adds a layer of basic software such as an operating system, and SaaS include one more layer of software applications. Examples of IaaS and PaaS are Amazon's Elastic Cloud (EC3) or Google Drive and examples of SaaS are the cloud services provided by Salesforce.com or Google Docs

The cloud computing model is also decomposed into three types (Mell & Grance, 2011): (1) public or community cloud where the services are offered to the public, (2) private cloud where the services are offered within an organi zation, and (3) hybrid cloud where some services are procured from a public cloud, whereas other services are from a private cloud. Henceforth, all cloud computing references will be about the public cloud. Private clouds essentiall constitute captive or in‐house IT services albeit with the characteristics of cloud computing.

Cloud computing is different from traditional IT outsourcing, which is characterized by (1) a 1:1 relationship between the client and vendor, (2) longer duration of contracts, (3) the nature of contracts where pricing and terms include the use of incentives, or specific performance directives, or the use of Time‐and‐Material (T&M) pricing. Such contracts are typically negotiated and customized for the specific client‐vendor outsourcing relationship, and (4) th outsourced work is specific and customized for the client (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013).

Furthermore, cloud computing is also different from on premise, in-house, or captive IT services with the terms being synonymous. Traditional computing services and IT departments evolved as captive departments grown within the organization that incurred the capital expenditures and operating costs for the IT department. In contrast, cloud computing obviates the requirement for capital and the services are on‐demand and metered. The customer pays only for those services used and for the duration of use. The cloud vendor offers a public pricing schedule, not unlike the pricing schedules offered for renting an automobile. The terms are typically covered by Service Level Agreements (SLAs), the commonly used, acronym for the agreement that covers quality, reliability, and other performance attributes. SLAs might include guarantees of availability of resources, their uptime, performance, or responsiveness of the IT resources, among others (Baset, 2012). The cloud vendor serves a broad marketplace with divergent interests between the cloud vendor and a specific customer. The cloud vendor's services are likely to have the dimensions of price, quality, and other attributes to satisfy aggregate demand and maximize the cloud vendors profits or revenue instead of a specific customer's preferences. The cloud vendor develops efficiencies through multi‐tenancy and sharing of IT resources. Multi‐tenancy implies that resources such as servers and storage are shared among multiple customers. In contrast, the defining characteristic of traditional captive IT is that of a monopoly and the demand for IT services is modelled as being driven only by internal use

The adoption of cloud computing introduces a new party, the cloud vendor, resulting in a layer of external separation between the firm and the provider of IT services, which is now the cloud vendor. This external layer of separation did not exist with an in‐house or captive IT department. While cloud computing has its advantages, there are also disadvantages that are described in Section 4. Therefore, cloud computing may not be the best IT solution for all organizational needs. Indeed, for some organizations, it may not even be a suitable means of procuring IT services Primault (2011) and McKendrick (2012) provide examples of environments that should not use cloud computing. Nevertheless, cloud computing is an important and disruptive technology that is upending the status quo in th delivery and deployment of IT services.

## 1.2 | The existentialist question for the IT department

The advent of cloud computing and its rapid adoption has raised the existentialist question regarding the IT department in the minds of many scholars and practitioners. The advent of cloud computing obviates the need for captive IT departments because the cloud vendor installs and manages the infrastructure, applications, and services. Hence, the existentialist question arises because cloud computing brings utility‐type IT services (Carr, 2003) to all organizations from a rapidly growing number of cloud vendors, and these services can, in theory, displace the traditiona captive IT department.

The idea of the “death” of the IT department may be greatly exaggerated. Industry reports such as Deloitte (2013) indicate that cloud computing creates opportunities for IT departments to grow and increase the relevance of their function. The article suggests that to do so requires IT department personnel to learn new skills that enhance busines processes and operations to generate business value. Likewise, a KPMG (2013) survey shows that corporate culture and change management is the second greatest challenge for organizations after security. McKinsey and Compan (Manyika et al., 2013) describe transformational, disruptive technologies that include cloud computing

Research on the impact of cloud computing on organizations is just emerging, and this paper identifies the ramifications of this fundamental change in the way IT services are procured, deployed, and managed through cloud com puting. More specifically, this paper asks whether the traditional captive IT department can exist under cloud computing and whether the IT department will need to transform itself to grow and thrive in the emerging cloud era. Netflix is an example of a company that transformed itself using cloud computing (Izrailevsky, Vlaovic, & Meshenberg, 2016). Neil (2013) provides another example of a major firm transforming the IT department under cloud computing.

This paper posits that rather than becoming obsolete, the traditional manner of organizing, staffing, and operating the IT department will be subject to transformation and the IT department will face new governance issues. The emerging, transformed IT department under cloud computing will not resemble the traditional, captive IT department The transformation required by the IT department and the implications to firm performance are developed throug arguments and a set of assertions stated formally as opinions in Section 7 of this paper.

I use principal‐agent economic theory–based arguments and organizational theory–based arguments to conceptualize and discuss three significant issues that arise as a consequence of the adoption of cloud computing. The dis cussion and arguments include areas where IT departments could augment the standardized services offered by cloud vendors. Going beyond prior literature (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013; Gurbaxani & Kemerer, 1989), I stipulate that these emerging issues are: (1) information asymmetry under cloud computing and the associated problem of IT governance under such information asymmetry, (2) the transformation of the role of the IT department under cloud computing into an external‐facing role and an internal‐facing role, and (3) the key attributes required for IT services under cloud computing. The discussion of these issues includes a proposed research agenda based on the assertions prof fered as opinions backed by arguments.

## 2 | EMERGING RESEARCH ON CLOUD COMPUTING

Recent market research reports and forecasts indicate a major transformation of traditional or legacy IT spending by a shift to cloud computing. Gartner (2016) predicts that over \$1 trillion in IT spending will be affected by cloud computing by the year 2020. Colombus (2016) offers a comprehensive analysis of cloud computing forecasts and his report notes that IDC predicts cloud services will enjoy a compounded annual growth rate (CAGR) of 19.4% from 2015 to 2019. He further reports that SaaS has the largest market share within the cloud computing market. Additionally, IaaS is predicted to grow at a CAGR of 27.0%, and PaaS is predicted to grow at a CAGR of 30.6% during this period Furthermore, cloud computing spending is reported to be growing at 7 times the rate of other IT spending (Paul, 2017), and such dramatic changes lead to organizational challenges.

The benefits of cloud computing are well discussed (eg, Armbrust et al., 2010). Mell and Grance (2011) hav developed models of cloud computing, and scholars in other disciplines (Vecchiola, Pandey, & Buyya, 2009) have addressed, and continue to address, technical issues in hardware, software, and security, provisioning, metering, bill ing, and related areas. De Assunçao, Di Costanzo, and Buyya (2009) and others have analyzed capacity and pricing models. In contrast, literature on the organizational implications of cloud computing is just emerging (eg, Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013; Marston, Li, Bandyopadhyay, Zhang, & Ghalsasi, 2011). ## 3 | INFORMATION ASYMMETRY AND CLOUD COMPUTING

Information asymmetry occurs when two parties who may have disparate objectives possess private information. For example, the IT department has information on the costs, new products, and the efficacy of technology that is unknown to the business manager. Similarly, the business manager has information regarding business problems, the demands, and the requirements of business issues that are unknown to the IT department, but could potentially be addressed by IT solutions. Furthermore, the business manager may have a private value or willingness to pay for IT services that address business issues. Prior IT governance literature (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013; Gurbaxani & Kemerer, 1989; Wang & Barron, 1995) has highlighted the role of asymmetric information on IT governance. Th adoption of cloud computing brings new dimensions to information asymmetry because of the injection of the cloud vendor as a new party to the firm and to the internal IT department.

## 4 | ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF CLOUD COMPUTING AND CAPTIVE IT

Captive IT, also referred to as on premise or in‐house IT, represents the traditional IT department that resides and operates within the firm. While resources may be shared among the internal users, captive IT is typically an interna monopoly supplier that makes capital investments for IT infrastructure and bears the cost of maintenance and upgrades. Captive IT infrastructure offers many advantages, though some practitioners argue that some of these advantages are simply perceived advantages. First, control of intellectual property and the security of data and the quality of IT services may be stronger, or perceived to be stronger, under captive IT because the firm controls these elements. Second, the firm has the ability to select and install best‐of‐breed applications and determine the quality o services. The captive IT department can develop proprietary software applications to meet unique requirements that could result in hard‐to‐replicate organizational knowledge and competitive advantages. Third, when a firm has homog enous and standardized IT service requirements, a captive IT infrastructure could provide economies of scale and effi ciencies through multi‐tenancy with a sufficiently large internal user base.

A major disadvantage of a captive IT department is the necessity for large capital expenditures. This requirement for capital investment is inexorable because of rapid technology obsolescence. Hence, the firm will need to renew IT infrastructure every few years. Because captive IT services are likely to have many customized applications and ser vices, changing applications or services will result in high switching costs. While certain firms may have economics of scale, in general captive IT services are likely to be inefficient because many firms offer captive IT services as a corporate resource, which results in inefficient consumption (Brow, 2015). Captive IT departments are viewed as a support function and career prospects may be limited because of the support nature of the department.

Cloud computing eliminates many of these disadvantages by virtue of its defining characteristics. The cloud vendor has large economies of scale and greater efficiencies from multi‐tenancy because the cloud vendor serves the broad external market for IT services, and these benefits pass through to customers. The cloud vendor can generate efficiencies even when clients have heterogeneous IT needs because of the large market served. Cloud services are offered at market‐based pricing that may result in efficient consumption of IT services. The cloud vendor bears the capital cost of IT infrastructure and the cost of infrastructure replacement from technology obsolescence. IT personnel issues of growth and retention may be mitigated as IT infrastructure and IT services are the mainstream business of the cloud vendor. The standardized nature of cloud computing implies lower switching costs between vendors. Such lower switching costs are a benefit to the user, particularly for basic services such as IaaS and PaaS.

Higher‐level cloud service vendors such as SaaS vendors offer Application Programming Interfaces (APIs), such that third parties or the firm's IT department can add customized software modules or applications on top of the basic service from the cloud vendor

Cloud computing has several disadvantages. First, a notion of reduced security surrounds cloud computing. Similarly, disaster recovery, backup, and mirroring will typically be the customer's responsibility. Significant investments in time and effort may be required by the customer to overcome such concerns (Chow et al., 2009) which diminishes the advantages of cloud computing. Second, standardized cloud services may be considered undifferentiated and clients may be unable to leverage cloud services for competitive advantage. Third, customization of cloud services through APIs effectively ensures switching costs and the propensity for lock‐in with a specific cloud vendor.

Fourth, the cloud vendor is an independent profit‐making entity and as such, agency theory would suggest that the cloud vendor will not make all decisions on IT service attributes such as security, quality, pricing, reliability, and others based on optimizing a customer's objectives. The cloud vendor will maximize its profit based on its aggregate market demand. Hence, the services and attributes offered will reflect this objective and will not reflect the maximi zation of an individual customer's benefit from cloud services. Finally, information asymmetry between the cloud vendor and the client firm will not be eliminated even though pricing is public information

The adoption of cloud computing raises organizational issues that firms will need to address because of the differences in the service models between captive IT and cloud computing. The elimination of capital expenditures on infrastructure will create an instant and dramatic mismatch between the skills of a good portion of the incumbent IT personnel who would traditionally be responsible for procuring and maintaining IT infrastructure. Such personne and their skills are immediately of no value to the firm. Other personnel with technical ability, who would traditionall acquire and maintain enterprise application software, will be redundant under cloud computing

Skills immediately lacking under the adoption of cloud computing will be in the following areas: (1) procuring and monitoring a new set of services under cloud computing SLAs, (2) providing a consulting role that matches cloud services towards addressing and solving business requirements and business problems, and (3) cloud computing opens up a totally new sourcing model where the internal users bypass the IT department and procure IT services directly from the cloud vendor.

I describe two cloud computing sourcing models and discuss the issues that arise from the two models in Section 5. The implications of these sourcing models for the role of the IT department under cloud computing are discussed in Section 6. These implications are subsequently stated as refutable assertions in the form of opinion statements in Section 7.

## 5 | CLOUD COMPUTING SOURCING MODELS

While the emergence of cloud computing is unlikely to eliminate the IT department, the future IT department in a cloud environment is likely to be dramatically different. ZDnet (2012) notes that this is the new cloud computin world and in this new world, IT departments and firms will need to learn to manage this new entity, the cloud vendor.

Cloud computing creates the option for the internal consumer to source IT services directly from the cloud ven dor, bypassing the IT department. Many industry reports point out that cloud computing breaks down the traditional monopoly role enjoyed by the IT department. Indeed, this is the motivation for the title of this paper because the internal users have the ability to source IT services directly from the cloud vendor and bypass the IT department. Suc circumventing of the IT department might be the proverbial death knell of the captive IT department as we know it.

TechRepublic (2014) reports that 61% of business units use IT services procured directly from cloud vendors When cloud computing services are passed through the IT department, the services may or may not be enhanced by the IT department. ZDNet (2012) points out the ways in which the IT department can add value such that users are better off by sourcing cloud services through the IT department instead of direct sourcing from the cloud vendor Figure 4 illustrates the possible flows of IT services from the cloud vendor to customers.

Figure 4 raises the question of the relevance of the IT department under cloud computing when the internal user bypasses the IT department. I argue that value provided by the IT department is not necessarily in technology unde cloud computing, but will be superseded by management functions. In this context, it is relevant to note that there is a cloud ecosystem that surrounds cloud computing as described in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (www.nist.gov/). There are several ways in which the IT department can add value in this ecosystem.

The firm will need the cloud vendor evaluated before signing on, and the IT department may be the best suited to accept this role because the technology aspects are familiar. However, the IT department will be required to manage procurement, metering, billing, and related activities with the cloud vendor. The firm will need to ensure that the cloud vendor is in compliance with quality, reliability, and other service attributes or features of its cloud services. Hence, monitoring and maintenance of the cloud vendor is a new responsibility that the IT department could take on. The IT department could add technical value by enhancing the cloud services with higher value‐added features such as custom reports, or dashboards among others. Customization and proprietary add‐on services may increase switching costs as described in the previous section. The IT department can mitigate such switching costs by developing internal platform‐independent standards for customized software. Using platform‐independent standards enables easier migration to another cloud vendor. These new roles of the IT department are mostly external facing, that is, towards the cloud vendor.

A second significant role for the IT department is internal facing towards the internal IT users. Internal users have business problems that can be addressed with the appropriate cloud computing solutions. Hence, the IT department can act as an internal consultant that understands user needs and adapts the cloud computing ecosystem to solve business problems and satisfy business needs.

I use these arguments to make the assertion that the advent of cloud computing will offer IT departments a unique strategic opportunity to increase their value added to the firm by elevating their capability to a higher level Industry reports such as PWC (2011) and Deloitte (2013) support this assertion. Hence, I posit that the IT department will not only continue to exist but will become more critical to the firm compared to the pre–cloud computing captive IT department. The post–cloud computing IT department that has transformed to adapt to the cloud will allow the firm to extract the highest level of benefit from IT. However, cloud computing will require that the IT department transform itself. The manner in which the IT department adds value in the areas noted earlier will require change i the role and expertise of the IT department

## 6 | TRANSFORMED ROLES FOR THE IT DEPARTMENT

I decompose the transformation necessary for the IT department as a consequence of cloud computing and the new sourcing structures, into two fundamental transformed roles. These roles are denoted as the external‐facing role and the internal‐facing role.

## 6.1 | External‐facing role of the IT department

The transformation to an external‐facing role will require the IT department to be an intermediary between the cloud vendor and the internal consumer. The two new roles arising out of the discussion on cloud sourcing structures described in the previous section, which are not exhaustive, are those of an intermediary. In this role of an intermediary, the IT department relieves the internal user from managing processes that are necessary but typically not within the user's competency. The internal business user of IT services will have to apply resources to manage the cloud vendo and cloud services if the user sources IT services directly from the cloud vendor. Whereas, if the IT department managed the cloud vendor, the internal user can focus such resources on business issues instead of managing the cloud vendor. Functions such as cloud vendor evaluation, metering, billing, monitoring of the cloud vendor, and maintenance of IT ser vices and processes required to procure and deploy cloud services constitute activities within an external‐facing role

## 6.2 | Internal‐facing role of the IT department

The second transformation will require the IT department to develop skills in understanding the business needs and problems faced by the internal consumer. First, under cloud computing, the IT department and the firm are relieved of many of the traditional functions such as installing, maintaining, and upgrading IT infrastructure because the cloud vendor takes on these functions. Second, as a consequence, IT personnel will be relieved of these functions and th alternative is to (i) downsize, or (ii) help the internal customer to increase their business value from IT services

The internal‐facing role is a more difficult transition than the external‐facing role. The IT department may have had prior external‐facing experience from traditional IT outsourcing and infrastructure procurement and installation. The emerging internal‐facing role may be alien to the captive IT department because the role requires substantial engagement and understanding of the business environment and business issues, which is not its traditional competency. In essence, the IT department needs to take on the role of a consultant that is well versed in IT as well as in business problems. Within this role, the IT department can (1) assess the external cloud computing technology landscape and its evolution (2) understand the business needs and any changing reguirements depending on the competitive landscape and the firm's product, market, and production technology environment, and (3) identify the best ways to exploit the external cloud services to improve business functions, processes, and operations, and thus add business value

The internal‐facing role for IT personnel presents opportunities and challenges for the functions of business analysts and systems analysts in the era of cloud computing. The internal‐facing role with its need for addressing business issues and problems suggest that the need for business analysts is likely to grow under cloud computing (Barrios, 2016). In a similar vein, the Bureau of Labor Statistics under the United States Department of Labor reports that the job outlook for systems analysts is projected to grow at a higher than average growth of 21% per year between 2014 and 2024 (BLS, 2015). However, the transition to cloud computing will require a shift in the capabilities of systems analysts from captive IT to cloud computing (Chou & Chou, 2011). Hence, the familiar Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model will need to adopt cloud computing into the process (Krishna & Jayakrishnan, 2013).

## 7 | OPINION STATEMENTS ON IT GOVERNANCE UNDER CLOUD COMPUTING

I formalize the discussion and arguments to develop a set of assertions stated as opinions. The previous discussion lead to the two transformational and fundamental roles of the IT department under cloud computing. Additionally, the new layer of information asymmetry under cloud computing brings new challenges to IT governance and to IT service attributes. Hence, I proffer opinions in three areas: (1) the transformed role of the IT department under cloud computing, (2) the effect of mitigating information asymmetry under cloud computing, and (3) the attributes of IT services under cloud computing. These opinions fall into the realm of IT governance issues, and these emerging gover nance challenges embodied in these assertions are developed into a proposed research agenda in Section 8.

## 7.1 | Opinion statements regarding the transformed roles for the IT department

The two transformed and fundamental roles for the IT department described earlier in Sections 6.1 and 6.2 are illus trated in Figure 5.

Agency theory suggests that unless the IT department can credibly add value in the new world of cloud comput ing, the internal consumers of IT services will bypass the IT department. It is likely that for certain commodity service such as email or desktop computer file backup, the IT department's intermediary role is very minimal. Higher value cloud services such as SaaS offer greater opportunities for the IT department to increase its value. The role of the IT department under cloud computing portends significant change. I use these arguments in conjunction with prior literature to examine the potential for growth or shrinkage of the IT department.

At first blush, it would seem that the adoption of cloud computing will obviate the need for many traditional internal IT functions, and IT departments will shrink if nothing else changes. The traditional role emphasized procuring and maintaining IT infrastructure, installing and running enterprise software applications, software development, develop ing or modifying software applications, and ensuring that such applications were provisioned and functioning for th internal user. Under cloud computing, this traditional role of IT in managing IT infrastructure will be subsumed by the cloud vendor (Armbrust et al., 2010), which suggests a shrinking IT department under cloud computing

FIGURE 5 Transformed roles for the IT department [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

However, I have argued that cloud computing opens up new possibilities for the IT department to redirect resources that become free as a consequence the adoption of cloud computing. The free resources could focus on enabling increased business value to the internal customer. Increase in business value may be generated through adeptly adding value to cloud‐based services to address firm‐specific problems and opportunities. Choudhary and Vithayathil (2013) describe this opportunity by modelling the value‐addition mechanism as a quality feature. For example, value could be added through customization, or providing new services such as Business Analytics (BA) or Business Intelligence (BI) services. Such BA or BI services may help the firm identify new market opportunities or new product opportunities.

I assert that IT departments that leverage cloud computing to increase their value will enjoy growth even when many traditional IT functions are being eliminated through cloud computing. Unlocking such value will be one ke differentiator between IT departments that enjoy growth under cloud computing and IT departments that shrink under cloud computing. An example from practice is described by Neil (2013) where the IT leader transformed the IT department to be internal‐facing service providers. The previous arguments and this discussion lead to Opinion 1 regarding growth or shrinkage of the IT department under cloud computing.

Opinion 1a. IT departments that add value in an internal‐facing role and an external‐facing role under cloud computing will grow in spending and staff levels compared to pre–cloud computing levels.

Opinion 1b. Conversely, IT departments that do not add value in an internal‐facing role or an externalfacing role under cloud computing will shrink in spending and staff levels compared to pre–cloud computing levels.

The opinions above may appear obvious because of the intuitive connection between adding value and growth. Keep in mind that IT spending has been a growing phenomenon across the board. However, while this growth story ma continue with those IT departments that adapt to the new cloud computing environment, those IT departments that do not adapt will shrink and reverse the growth environment that IT departments have enjoyed in the past

Next, I refine the growth or shrinkage of the IT department under cloud computing, by connecting the outcome to the two fundamental transformed roles of the IT department described in Section 6 and illustrated in Figure 5

First, I argue that the external‐facing role is primarily administrative and includes activities such as vendor selection, management, monitoring of SLAs, and related activities. If the IT department is primarily external‐facing, and mostly providing such administrative services, I suggest that the value to the firm is minimal. Such an external‐facing IT department is mostly providing necessary, but basic services under cloud computing. Hence, I further suggest that shrinkage of the IT department will be the outcome. However, the external‐facing role will provide certain opportunities for delivering increased value. For example, the IT department may identify new and innovative cloud services that competitors have not yet adopted, and enable unique value generation for the firm. The extent of unique value generation may be limited because cloud services are openly available in the market, and examples such as early adop tion of new cloud services may only offer limited opportunities for firm‐specific business value generation. Conse quently, opportunities for growth for an external‐facing IT department may be limited and the best case may be maintaining the size of the department as measured by personnel and budget.

Second, I argue that the internal‐facing role offers a rich set of opportunities for the IT department to leverage cloud computing and enable unique firm‐specific value, by adapting cloud‐based IT services to the firm's unique needs. Such firm‐specific value generation requires the IT department to become familiar with the firm's business needs such as the competitive environment, market and product positioning, and strategies. This in turn requires the IT department to develop an internal‐facing role where the IT department faces internal users as unique customers and serves as internal consultants. Examples of firm‐specific adaptions of cloud services may include customized services built on top of the cloud-based services. Other examples noted earlier include the introduction of new services such BA or BI. Prior literature (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013) describes such addition of value to cloud‐based services as internally enhancement of IT service quality. These examples of firm‐specific value‐added services may enable market share growth or enable identification, development, and effective deployment of new products. Consequently, such firm‐specific opportunities for adding value may enable continued growth for an inter nal‐facing IT department. These arguments lead to the following opinions regarding the impact of the two fundamen tal transformed roles described in Section 6 on growth or shrinkage of the IT department under cloud computing.

Opinion 2a. IT departments that transition only to the external‐facing role under cloud computing wil either (i) maintain spending and staffing levels, or (ii) shrink in spending and staffing levels

Opinion 2b. IT departments that transition only to the internal‐facing role under cloud computing wil either (i) maintain spending and staffing levels, or (ii) grow in spending and staffing levels.

Opinion 3a. IT departments that transition to the internal‐facing and the external‐facing (dual) role unde cloud computing will grow in spending and staffing levels.

Opinion 3b. Firms with IT departments that transition to the internal‐facing and external‐facing (dual) role under cloud computing will be associated with increased firm performance, compared to firms with IT departments that do not make this transition to dual roles

Opinions expressed in 2a and 2b relate to the IT department's role transformation and its relation to growth or shrinkage of the IT department. The external‐facing role transformation is not as radical as the internal‐facing transformation Hence, I associate potential growth with the internal‐facing transition, and I associate potential shrinkage with the external‐facing transition. The opinions expressed in 3a and 3b describe IT departments that manage dual transitions to external‐facing and internal‐facing roles. Such a dual transition is clearly the desired outcome under the arguments offered in this paper. While it is not immediately and intuitively clear that IT departments that make the dual transition will grow, I posit that IT departments that make the dual transformation will grow. This assertion is based on the argu ment that the internal facing role the IT department will generate new avenues for business value by adapting cloud based services to solve firm‐specific business problems, with IT personnel serving as internal consultants straddling business and IT. Additionally, the IT department also performs the functions of procurement and monitoring of cloud services, which would otherwise be undertaken by the internal user. Hence, the dual role will lead to business value and operational efficiencies, which I argue will lead to increased internal demand for IT services. The opinion stated in 3b extends this argument to firm performance improvement when the IT department undertakes a dual role transi tion. Performance may be measured in various ways including accounting measures such as sales or output, Retur on Assets (ROA), market measures such as market value, and forward‐looking measures such as Tobin's q

The changes wrought by the adoption of cloud computing and the new roles for the IT department fall within the area of IT governance under cloud computing. However, the adoption of cloud computing raises additional, related IT governance issues which are discussed next.

## 7.2 | Opinion statements regarding information asymmetry

Organizational theory includes organizational structure and processes that improve organizational performance. Therefore, IT governance issues under cloud computing can be described and analyzed by these two areas withi organizational theory: organizational structure and organizational process attributes of the firm. Structure issues include the organization structure of the IT department, performance metrics and incentives, the mode of procure ment—direct from cloud vendor to user or through the IT department—and decision authority on attributes such as pricing, quality, and customization. Organizational process issues in IT governance and organizational theory include (i) the degree of information exchange between the IT department and business managers on procurement policies, demand, internal IT cost, emerging user requirements, and technology evolution, (ii) formal processes for development, implementation, monitoring, and review of policies associated with cloud computing, and (iii) informal processe for such policies that have been institutionalized over time

Prior literature (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013) informs us on the choice of organizational structure for the IT department under cloud computing. Effective use of performance metrics tied to incentives can mitigate informatio asymmetry. As an example, if the CIO was to be measured on business metrics in addition to IT metrics, she may b inclined to exchange information with the business manager. Conversely, the business manager could have IT metrics as part of her performance criteria. Hence, the use of the right structural mechanisms can help mitigate information asymmetry between the IT department and the business manager.

Formal and institutionalized processes that enable information exchange between the IT department and the firm can mitigate this important dimension of information asymmetry. A contract based on agency theory generall requires that the principal compensate the agent for the full value of her private information as shown in Wang and Barron (1995). In contrast, organizational theory provides opportunities for a process‐oriented approach to mitigate information asymmetry. Mitigating information asymmetry will enable each party to understand more about the other party's functional area. Lower information asymmetry would enable the IT department to understand the business processes and business needs better. Likewise, lower information asymmetry would enable the business man ager to understand the technologies, services, and opportunities offered by cloud computing. Hence, IT governance under cloud computing may require that firms ensure that there are formal and institutionalized processes for information exchange between the IT department and the firm. Examples of such processes include formal, regularl scheduled meetings between a committee of IT personnel and business personnel. Prior literature such as Weill and Ross (2004) refers to the existence of an IT‐business steering committee that helps improve information flow and can therefore, mitigate information asymmetry.

These arguments lead me to posit that IT departments can mitigate information asymmetry between the cloud vendor and the internal business users of cloud computing services by using organizational structure and organiza tional process governance mechanisms. Firms having IT departments that mitigate such information asymmetry are likely to improve performance and leads to the following opinions.

Opinion 4a. Firms with IT departments that mitigate the information asymmetry between the cloud vendor and the internal user will be associated with increased firm performance compared to firms with IT departments that do not mitigate information asymmetry.

Opinion 4b. IT departments that mitigate the information asymmetry between the cloud vendor and the internal user will grow in spending and staffing levels compared to firms with IT departments that do not mitigate information asymmetry

Opinions expressed in 4a and 4b rely on agency theory and organizational theory–based arguments along with support from prior literature, which suggests that mitigating information asymmetry allows the firm to extract increased benefits from cloud computing. As noted earlier, performance may be measured in various ways including accounting measures such as sales or output, ROA; market measures such as market value, and forward‐looking measures such as Tobin's q. Next, we examine the attributes that characterize IT service under cloud computing

## 7.3 | Opinion statements regarding IT service attributes

I use attributes from economic theory as a framework for describing the critical attributes necessary for IT services under cloud computing. While alternate frameworks are possible, in this paper, familiar attributes from economic theory are explored. Important attributes include the internal price at which cloud services are offered to users, the quality of IT services from the cloud vendor and internal IT service quality, the number or variety of IT services offered, and the competitive environment that the cloud vendors are facing

Under cloud computing, even though the firm pays the cloud vendor for IT services, it can choose to offer these services to the internal consumers as a corporate resource at no cost to the internal user. These are decisions that the firm will have to make. Quality can be interpreted in a variety of ways and has a number of dimensions. Quality ca include reliability or uptime, disaster recovery provisions, software bugs, and service latency. While internal con sumers may desire a large number of services, the firm or the IT department may choose to limit the offerings based on cost, value, and demand. The cloud vendor's competitive environment will not only determine price and quality of the cloud‐based IT service offerings, but it is also a signal of the nature of the services. For example, a highly compet itive environment may signal commodity‐type services. I argue that the firm will need to take all of these attribute into account when making decisions on IT governance. These arguments lead to Opinion 5.

Opinion 5. Firms with IT departments that offer cloud‐based IT services with the attributes of: (i) lowe internal IT service prices, (ii) higher quality IT services, (iii) greater number of IT services, and (iv) source IT services from cloud vendors facing higher competitive intensity will be associated with increased firm performance compared to those firms that do not have one or more of these attributes

Opinion 5 relies on prior literature and on economic efficiency arguments to posit that when cloud‐based services have attributes that imply efficiency and quality, which leads to greater surplus generation for the internal user, the firm is likely to enjoy improved performance compared to those firms that are unable to offer these attributes of cloud‐based IT services. Future work could include further refinement to understand the number of attributes needed to improve firm performance. As before, performance may be measured in various ways including accounting measures such as sales or output, ROA; market measures such as market value, and forward‐looking measures such as Tobin's q.

## 8 | DISCUSSION AND A RESEARCH AGENDA ON CLOUD COMPUTING

The era of cloud computing has emerged and the existentialist question regarding the IT department under cloud computing has been raised in the trade press and is a provocative question. Cloud computing is a new and disruptive technology development within IT with burgeoning adoption. Furthermore, cloud computing is a significant area for venture capital investment as reported by Gigaom (2012) with about a third of the total venture capital investment in 2011 being directed towards cloud computing. Even though the relevance of the IT department is being questioned by some, industry reports such as from Deloitte (2013) indicate that cloud computing could increase the value of the IT department to the organization. Under this backdrop of unprecedented growth and impact of cloud computing, this paper identifies IT governance and organizational issues that arise as a consequence of the adoption of cloud comput ing. The discussion and arguments made in this paper, provoked by the existentialist question regarding the traditional, captive IT department, form the basis of formally stated opinions that constitute a proposed research agenda

Research based on the assertions in Opinion 1 would examine the manner in which IT departments change themselves to add value in the face of a structural transformation on how IT services are procured and delivered to interna business users. I assert that some IT departments will make a successful transition whereas other IT departments wil not adapt well to cloud computing. Consequently, the former will grow and the latter will shrink in terms of budgets and staffing levels. Euture research that analyzes this assertion will uncover the ways in which IT departments unlock business value under cloud computing.

The next set of assertions embodied in Opinions 2 and 3 drill down into the two new roles that define the IT department of the emerging cloud computing era. These opinions posit that it is a necessary condition the IT depart ments pivot to dual internal‐facing and external‐facing roles to be relevant and grow under cloud computing. Further more, Opinion 3 asserts that firm performance will improve when IT departments pivot to the dual roles. Opinions 2 and 3 provide a rich area for future research to analyze such IT department role transitions and the effect on the IT department in terms of budget growth and staffing levels as well as on firm performance. A variety of measures of firm performance such as output, accounting measures such as profits and margins, market measures such as firm value, and forward‐looking measures such as Tobin's q may be useful in future research

Internal IT service quality, variety, and price are attributes of IT services procured from cloud vendors that Opinion 5 asserts will be associated with firm performance. Future research could examine whether one or more of these attributes are associated with improved firm performance when such services are sourced through cloud vendors. The overall thrust of this paper is that IT departments that adapt to the advent of cloud computing by transforming themselves into new roles that serve the organization, will thrive, and grow. Understanding the players and factors that drive th change and the value derived from such change in the role of the IT department will be useful to scholars and prac titioners. The IT department has to deal with two communities under cloud computing, the external cloud vendor and the internal users. The IT department's traditional captive role of a monopoly IT service function is rapidly changing. IT departments will need to adapt to such change and ensure that they deal with the two communities in a manner that ensures relevance and increases value to the users and to the firm.
