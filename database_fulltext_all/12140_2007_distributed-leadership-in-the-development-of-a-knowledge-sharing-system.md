# Distributed leadership in the development of a knowledge sharing system

Jing Zhang<sup>1</sup> and Sue R. Faerman<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Graduate School of Management, Clark University, Worcester, MA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Public Administration & Policy, University at Albany,SUNY, Albany, NY, U.S.A.

## Abstract

This paper studies leadership dynamics in the development of a knowledge sharing system. We apply the concept of distributed leadership, examining how leadership roles are distributed across different individuals in the organisation during developing and implementing a knowledge sharing system. The results of a case study demonstrate that the leadership was fulfilled by the interdependent and emergent roles played by several individuals: the spearheading and coordinating roles of a knowledgeable and persistent project leader, the supporting and steering roles carried by a group of perceptive and collaboration-inclined executives, and the knowledge sharing and momentum driving roles performed by knowledge champions. Each of these leadership influences was indispensable. None of the leaders could have accomplished this task individually, without the active involvement of the other types of leaders. This research makes its contribution to the understanding of the emergence and dynamics of distributed leadership in the information system environment, and demonstrates the importance of a full awareness of distributed leadership as tasks are carried out in developing a knowledge sharing system.

Keywords: IS leadership; project leadership; distributed leadership; knowledge sharing

## Introduction

Over the past few decades, organisations all over the world have experienced forces for change that have required them to examine the very notion of organisation, and within it the role of leaders in organisations. Information and knowledge intensive organisations, in particular, have experienced radical changes in communication and information technologies (ICT) that have required information executives to fundamentally alter their approach to leadership (McLean & Smits, 2003). As organisations in the public and private sectors develop alliances and networks across both internal and external organisational boundaries, there has been an increased questioning of the traditional models of governance as well as of management and leadership (Roussel, 1999). At the same time, demographic, economic and technological forces have required organisations to change the ways that they interact with both external and internal constituencies, which has similarly raised questions about the type of leadership that is needed to accommodate these new organisational changes and restructuring imperatives (Gronn, 2000, 2002; Schneider, 2002). Many researchers argue that classic theories of leadership do not provide adequate direction for the type of leadership approaches that are needed in these new settings (Huxham & Vangen, 2000; Brown & Gioia, 2002; Pearce & Conger, 2003; Day et al., 2004).

As changes in information technologies that allow information to flow more freely alter the relationships between managers and subordinates or between leaders and followers in organisations (Drucker, 1993), information technology and systems executives recognise that new styles of leadership may be necessary for interacting with external constituencies (McLean & Smits, 2003), and new approaches to organising may be necessary for the successful development and implementation of new technologies such as knowledge sharing systems. Dyson, a leading authority on high-technology business and organisational culture, observed ‘Information-age technology won’t fly if it is hobbled by industrial-age organisational culturesya better computer won’t buy you instant collaboration if there is not a culture of sharing knowledge’ (Dyson, 1999, p. 144). Not satisfied with the classic approaches to leadership (Gronn, 2000), new approaches, focusing on models of distributed and/ or shared leadership, which better fit the necessary technological and organisational changes (Fletcher & Kau¨fer, 2003), are being developed.

This paper examines the dynamics of leadership in the development of a knowledge sharing system applying the concept of distributed leadership. The research question is how leadership roles are distributed among multiple individuals in the process of developing and implementing a knowledge sharing system. In the following sections, we review the research literature by defining leadership, contrasting traditional models of leadership with more recently developed models of distributed leadership. We then discuss the important leader roles required for the successful development of knowledge sharing systems, and thus the need for considering distributed leadership in carrying out these roles. This is followed by the review of research methodology. Next, we present the case results. Finally, we discuss two properties of distributed leadership based on case result and their theoretical and practical implications.

## Changing views of leadership

Traditional models of leadership have been based on the assumption that the leadership role is played by a single individual (Pearce & Conger, 2003), or by multiple individuals with separate responsibilities for different organisational units following the institutional arrangement in an organisational hierarchy. Initially, these models focused on what traits an individual needed to be successful as a leader (Yukl, 2002). In the 1950s, the research focus was moved from traits to behaviours, and leadership models were developed that emphasised what leaders do to motivate subordinates and to get the task done (Fleishman, 1953, 1957; Hemphill & Coons, 1957). In the 1960s and 1970s, contingency, or situational, models of leadership emerged. These models emphasised the need to exhibit different behaviours in different settings and situations (Fiedler, 1964, 1967; House, 1971; Hersey & Blanchard, 1977). In the 1980s and 1990s, several models of leadership emerged that recognised the importance of leaders’ interactions with a variety of constituencies, and so placed greater emphasis on the nature of the relationship between leaders and others both internal and external to the organisation (Vaill, 1989; Kouzes & Posner, 1993; Kanter, 1996).

In the field of information systems and technology (IS), McLean & Smits (2003) developed a model of IS leadership that focuses on the changing role of information executives. Their model recognises that as the nature and availability of information technologies have changed, information executives have needed to alter their emphasis as organisational leaders, moving from the role of technologist to the roles of enabler and innovator, and ultimately to the role of strategist. As strategists, IS leaders must ensure that their organisations are prepared to play a strategic role in enhancing the competitiveness of its parent organisation in the market.

Most of these models assume, however, that leadership responsibilities are vested in a single individual or individuals reporting through a hierarchical structure (Gronn, 2000). In the 21st century, organisations have attempted to increase their learning and innovation capacities through a variety of collaborative approaches and an increased attention to sharing knowledge and information distributed throughout the organisation and across organisational boundaries. As new approaches to organising have emerged, new leadership models that consider the need for a different type of leadership when projects involve multiple units across the organisation or multiple organisations have also emerged. As Gronn (2000) suggests, to ensure its creditability, applicability and usefulness of leadership model, it is necessary to reconceputalise the appropriate unit of analysis by taking consideration of a distributed view of leadership.

Such models of distributed leadership (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2001; Pearce & Conger, 2003) reject understandings about leadership that rest largely upon the concept of a single leader within a single organisation, and recognise that as cross-boundary relationships become more common, it will become increasingly important to acknowledge that leadership need not be tied to particular person, nor to a particular organisation or unit (Huxham & Vangen, 2000). Rather, it is more likely that multiple individuals and organisations will emerge as leaders as a result of their knowledge, skills and abilities, in the case of individuals, or by convening the collaboration, being designated by a policy maker or funder, or by providing the institutional home for the interorganisational operations, in the case of organisations. This research thus take advantage of the growing recognition of the existence and importance of distributed leadership, and explore further about how task performance and leadership practices are distributed in the context of information system development, and the nature and the intensity of such distribution.

In this research, we adopt Huxham & Vangen’s (2000) definition of leadership, which focuses on leadership as ‘making things happen’ (p. 1160) and Tichy’s (1997)

notion that ‘leadership is aboutyhaving smart ideas and getting them implemented well’ (p. 22). By focusing on the activities and influences of leaders, the observation of leadership can be made to the emergence of multiple individuals exercising leadership roles. Following Gronn (2002), distributed leadership is defined in this study as direct or vicarious influences attributed voluntarily to one individual, separate individuals, collections of individuals in collaboration, or organisational units by members in the organisations.

## Leadership influences in knowledge sharing systems development

Knowledge management has begun to recognise the importance of leadership in facilitating knowledge sharing activities (Senge, 2000; Brown & Duguid, 2001b; Nonaka et al., 2001). In developing knowledge sharing systems, leaders can directly influence the likely success of the system through the commitment of resources and personnel, as well as by shaping organisational structures and processes. Equally important, leaders indirectly promote new organisational technologies by communicating their vision and cultivating norms and values in favour of sharing, thus shaping how users perceive the potential usefulness of the technology (Pinchot, 1996; Purvis et al., 2001). It may not be possible, however, for a single leader to be capable of performing all these tasks in a fast moving and dynamic environment. As Yukl (1999) observed,

Some leadership functions (e.g., making important decisions) may be shared by several members of a group, some leadership functions may be allocated to individual members, and a particular leadership function may be performed by different people at different times. The leadership actions of any individual leaders are much less important than the collective leadership provided by members of the organization (pp. 292–293).

Therefore, it is quite likely that different leaders have different areas of influence. For example, the influence of a positional leader (manager) will be very different from that of an external facilitator, yet each of these influences may be necessary. Moreover, each will carry different weight at different stages in the process of leading and participating. Thus, in the following section, we discuss a set of essential leader influences in a knowledge sharing environment – providing vision, reconstructing organisational structures and processes, and coordinating the sharing of knowledge; and within these themes, we further examine the distributed view of leadership.

## Providing vision

One of the most essential tasks for leaders is to create, articulate and build the foundation of shared purpose, vision, and core values that govern the knowledge sharing activities (Senge, 2000). The vision defines fundamental questions such as ‘who are we,’ ‘what we do,’ ‘why do we share,’ and ‘how we do it’ (Nonaka et al., 2001).

Moreover, a visionary credo can be a resource for superior performance for many long-lasting companies, because it generates spontaneous commitment, provides direction for the organisation to evolve that transcends environment changes, and builds a fundamental principle upon which all decisions can be based (Collins & Porras, 1994). In the case of knowledge sharing initiatives, the leaders or leading groups must articulate and build a shared vision about the purpose of sharing the knowledge by incorporating the reality that the participating groups encounter, but, more importantly, by envisioning what should and can be achieved collectively by sharing practices and know-how (Senge, 2000).

Developing such a shared vision is, however, particularly difficult for cross-boundary situations due to environmental uncertainty, cognitive constraints, and potential conflicting interests of the participating groups. The increasingly dynamic and interdependent nature of the economy makes it simply impossible for management to figure everything out at the top, necessitating approaches that integrate thinking and acting at all levels (Senge, 2000). In a knowledge-intensive society, acknowledging leadership in those who have the know-how is especially crucial. In elaborating on the knowledge creation process, the importance of distributed leadership – everyone in the organisation being responsible – is emphasised (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2001).

The effectiveness of strategic renewal or organisational learning in general is limited by individuals’ ability to recognise, construct and analyse problems in new and complex situations (Levitt & March, 1988; Senge, 1990; Argyris & Scho¨n, 1996). Thus, one line of leadership research focuses on the cognitive and behavioural complexity of individual leaders (Denison et al., 1995; Pitcher & Smith, 2001). In studying leader effectiveness, Denison et al. (1995) found that the most effective leaders have styles that reflect greater complexity in their thinking and variety in their behaviours. They are able to recognise paradox, contradiction, and complexity in their environment, and simultaneously attend to seemingly opposing and competing requirements, such as the need for integration and differentiation, and the demand for accountability and creativity at the same time. Alternatively, Pitcher & Smith (2001) examined the possibility of sharing strategic decision-making processes in teams of leaders with different leadership strengths. They concluded that top management teams that exhibit strong cognitive diversity – a balanced combination of different types of leaders – are more successful in producing long-term results, because they include diverse ideas stemming from different cognitive perspectives and permit a more comprehensive and creative analysis of strategic alternatives (Pitcher & Smith, 2001).

Finally, in a distributed setting, groups often bring with them diverse perspectives, divergent realities, and disparate goals. Thus, although this vision building often focuses on leaders’ influences in creating a fundamental shift of the collective mindset to help participating groups see factors that are not locally relevant in the near term but essential for long-term development (Senge, 2000), this process should not downplay the active influences from participating groups (Huxham & Vangen, 2000). Leaders can be effective in helping those involved to think creatively and discover the possibility of achieving a win-win situation only when they incorporate the interests, pronounced or latent, from the different participating groups (Huxham & Vangen, 2000). Thus, vision formation should result from mutual influences in the collaboration.

Reconstructing organisational structures and processes Once a shared vision has been developed, there must be a systematic approach to translating this vision into organisational structure and processes (Purvis et al., 2001). To facilitate knowledge sharing across boundaries, leaders can shape the structure and processes in several ways. First, they set the agenda by defining the problems or the gap between vision and reality, and design concrete strategies to tackle the problems (Brown & Duguid, 2001a). Second, leaders can influence the structure by selecting and empowering particular groups of participants (Huxham & Vangen, 2000; Faerman et al., 2001). Third, they should have the authority to define the modes and instruments for communication and decision making. For example, leaders may set up specific decision-making groups that may be advisory in nature or may have actual decision-making responsibilities. In cross-divisional transactions, centralised forms of decision making have been found to have a negative effect on knowledge sharing among units (Tsai, 2002). Thus, a shallow hierarchy, decentralised decision making, and cross-functional teaming may be the key design features for a supportive structure (Teece, 2001). Fourth, leaders may need to able to manoeuvre the incentive and educational system in order to enhance individuals’ motivation to use the new system (Teece, 2001). Finally, leaders are especially important in detecting and overcoming the organisational inertia that hinders the exploration of new knowledge (Nonaka et al., 2001). Leaders need to exert intentional effort to unfreeze the prevailing institutional structure, as well as to introduce and legitimise new structures and processes (Crossan et al., 1999).

As organisations grow increasingly complex, institutional arrangements as well as social structure are grounds for multiple leaders to emerge to co-influence the change processes during and after the implementation of a knowledge sharing system. Some new management innovations, such as self-management teaming, cross-functional teaming, and virtual teams, could all be seen as attempts to take advantage of distributed thinking, leading, and acting provided by a flexible structure (Brown & Gioia, 2002; Day et al., 2004; Yoo & Alavi, 2004).

## Coordinating knowledge sharing

A related task for leaders in knowledge sharing initiatives is to deal with knowledge coordination. We discuss it in a separate section to highlight the compounding factor of the nature of organisational knowledge, which is inherently distributed, segmented, and embedded in community practices in a sizable organisation (Kogut & Zander, 1992; Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Wenger, 1998). A central focus of implementing a knowledge sharing system is to equip the organisation with the capability to collect, share, evaluate, and apply this knowledge. In this paper we take as our definition of knowledge as distributed social expertise, knowledge-in-practice embedded in the historical, social, and cultural context (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Drucker, 1993). Knowing and learning are thus not only reflected in cognition, but also embedded in actions; a process of participation in communities of practice (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Lave & Wenger, 1991; Wenger, 1998). This dynamic, context-embedded nature of organisational knowledge often creates complexity and difficulties for developing and using knowledge sharing information systems (Kogut & Zander, 1992, 1996; Grant, 1996). Particularly, it creates pragmatic difficulties for two general tasks of knowledge coordination critical for the success of knowledge sharing systems. The first knowledge coordination task involves the sharing of knowledge regarding system requirements – what knowledge is needed, how the knowledge can be acquired and stored, and how it is applied and evaluated both in the current and future term. To accomplish this task, common language, understandings or mindsets may need to be established across geographically, professionally, or specialisation-divided knowledge domains (Cohen & Bacdayan, 1994; Carlie & Rebentisch, 2003; Carlie, 2004). Leaders need to rely on the leadership provided by other knowledge champions who have authority derived from their knowledge and expertise or boundary spanners who can bridge gaps between knowledge domains (Leonard-Barton, 1995; Carlie, 2004).

The second task is to promote the contribution to and utilisation of knowledge sharing systems. The elusive and complex nature of knowledge also makes managing and evaluating the value of contributions a difficult task. To create a positive momentum of knowledge sharing, it is critical for leaders to cultivate a culture and norms that favour sharing and trust through their words and deeds (Davenport & Prusak, 1998). Collectively, leaders can encourage as well as restrain input and quality of reflection (Pfeffer & Sutton, 1999; Edmondson, 2002). It is the leaders’ task to foster a cultural environment that encourages risk-taking and knowledge sharing (Leonard-Barton, 1995). As Japanese scholars maintained, ‘[f]ostering love, care, trust and commitment among members of an organisation is important because those qualities form the foundation of knowledge creation’ (Nonaka et al., 2001, p. 37).

As a whole, the literature has addressed the existence and importance of distributed leadership. However, the operation of the distributed leadership as a unit of analysis is still in the stage of defining. That is, how leadership roles are distributed and the way in which leaders emerge and interact with other leaders have yet to be adequately explored and conceptualised, particularly in the context of developing and implementing knowledge sharing systems. For example, recent discussions of distributed leadership in knowledge environments often depict the roles of project leader as the link that bridges the visionary ideas of the top managers with the practical understanding developed by the front-line employees (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2001). This may not give the complete picture of the dynamics interactions, particularly in a new collaborative setting where precedent relationships and structure have to be redefined. In a sense, we still have much to learn about the relationships, communication, and influences among senior management, the project manager and other employees involved. This case study applies the concept of distributed leadership and shows how the leadership roles required for developing and implementing knowledge sharing systems are distributed across different leaders in the organisation. It intends to sheds light on the richness and nature of the distribution.

## Research methods

This study is part of a larger case study investigating the dynamics of knowledge sharing in the development of the Multi-purpose Access for Customer Relations and Operational Support (MACROS) knowledge sharing system in New York’s Office of the State Comptroller (OSC). Using a case study approach, the original study identified influential factors that facilitated and/or impeded the development of the knowledge sharing system. The case study used two primary data collection tools – interviews and observations. This section first describes the case briefly, and then explains how the data were collected using the case study approach.

The case study approach is an appropriate approach for this research due to the nature and the purposes of the study. In general, this case study resembles the kind of case study that is categorised as instrumental (Creswell, 1998), which has as its purpose to develop a general understanding of other similar cases. There are primarily three reasons to use the case method in this research. First, case studies are well suited to answer ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions in investigating phenomena that are dynamic, complex, and multi-faceted (Benbasat et al., 1987; Lee, 1989; Creswell, 1998). This study investigates the dynamic interaction of leadership and knowledge sharing; thus, employing a case approach can facilitate the understanding of the complexity of the underlying change and reciprocal influences. Second, case research also offers the advantage of being able to observe the emergence of distributed leadership and its impact on the processes of knowledge sharing in its natural setting. The problem that we examine in this study – distributed leadership and its influence on the development of a knowledge sharing system – is situated in practices and embedded in the historical and social-material context. The experiences of the actors and the information about the context are critical for understanding the dynamic interactions and outcomes. To obtain meaningful results, Baroudi & Orlikowski (1989) advise researchers to adopt qualitative or interpretive research methodologies that offer the advantages of in-depth, non-deterministic, and contextual investigations. Finally, the case method offers multiple sources of information that may reveal different perspectives. Using interviews, observation, and also having access to organisational documents offers ways to validate the conclusions reached through data analysis, and minimises the chance of misunderstanding or misinterpreting the data; thus, enhancing our ability to draw lessons that can be applied in similar settings.

## The MACROS case

The inception of the MACROS project can be traced back to 1997, as OSC found itself struggling with problems of locating the right information in the midst of disparate sources of information. This problem was especially apparent in the Division of Municipal Affairs (MA), which is responsible for supervising the fiscal affairs of 3200 local governments in New York State (CTG, 2001). MA’s employees are located in eight regional offices distributed across the state, as well as in the central office. For many years, MA saw its primary responsibility to be the monitoring and controlling of financial operations of local governments, which often led to adversarial relationships between MA and local government agencies. In the early 1990s, however, the office began an effort to redefine its role from a regulating to a coaching position (OSC, 2002b), and, as part of this effort, developed the Municipal Government Partnership, which offers a range of resources, training and information, to increase the professional development opportunities for municipal officials (OSC, 2002a). Managing the information necessary for supporting the state–local partnership, however, is complicated because of the diversity of sources and users, the variety of channels of collection and distribution, and the geographical separation of the field offices from the central office.

Within this context, and with the help from Center for Technology in Government (CTG), an applied research centre located at the University at Albany-SUNY, MA decided in 1998 to develop a knowledge repository with information about municipalities and local officials, past services provided, and preferred channels of communication, resulting in the initial conceptualisation of MACROS. Motivated by the need to streamline the collection, organisation and distribution of financial and other information from and about local governments, the purpose of MACROS is to enhance communication with and services provided to local governments. In June 2000, MA awarded the contract to ComputerWorks, an Albany-based software development company, and began the process of building the

MACROS system, a Lotus Notes-based application powered by InterTrac (the software developed by Computer-Works). After a year of effort in setting up the infrastructure, MACROS serviced the first user in June 2001. Almost three years later, the system was available on the desktops of over 500 employees of the 2000 OSC employees. In its original plan, the MACROS system was intended to facilitate the information flow about technical assistance delivered to local governments by MA and between central and regional offices (Office of the State Comptroller Division of Municipal Affairs 1998). Later, however, it went beyond MA, and expanded to other divisions including Financial Services, Management Audit, and Administration, and has become the first enterprise-wide application in OSC (Office of the State Comptroller MACROS Advisory Committee 2001).

It was recognised that the development and the implementation of the system have been very successful across several dimensions. In addition to providing more accessible and accurate information about customers, the agency has saved \$100,000 per year in postage and staff costs, and has reduced response time by 20%. The impact of MACROS has gone beyond the information system. It also brought about changes to the structure and culture of the organisation, especially with respect to knowledge sharing practices. By 2003, knowledge sharing was embedded into several applications and the new practices were institutionalised into organisational artefacts – an information system, organisational rules, procedures, and culture, as well as professional networks. It was recognised that by institutionalising the MACROS system and other knowledge sharing mechanisms, the organisational context became more conducive for the continuous organisational learning and renewal.

MACROS was a new approach. Challenges arose as the team sought to make changes within the MA division and across divisions, between the MA division and the central IT bureau, across the boundaries of all divisions, between the central and regional MA staff, and among the regional offices themselves. During the course of system development, several constraints were prominent and challenged the legitimacy and sustainability of the new initiative and the concomitant changes. Its success, thus, has been dependent upon individual as well as collective efforts in overcoming the barriers to change and creating new models of thinking and acting. The research setting of this case involves multiple organisations, divisions, and units; and the transformation of knowledge across these boundaries is both an initial condition and an essential outcome of the development of the knowledge sharing system.

## Data collection and analysis

Semi-structured interviews were held with 19 participants across different units, offices, divisions and organisations. The participants in this study were individuals who were involved in major activities associated with the initiation and development of MACROS and the related knowledge sharing activities. To identify the most appropriate individuals for the interviews, the study used a combination of purposive and snowball sampling approach, with initial interviewees selected based on an interview with a key informant and additional interviewees either referred by other participants during an interview or an event or recommended by the project leader of MACROS based on their position. To ensure the inclusiveness of those whose involvement is cross-boundary by nature, and hence whose observations would be most relevant in order to examine the role of leadership in this context, the list of interviewees included a good representation of both central and regional offices, as well as six relevant divisions and two other organisations (See Appendix A for a list of interviews with the position or role and affiliation of all the interviewees). Observational data were collected at the training and rolling out sessions in three regional offices, two Reflection Workshops administered by CTG, three Expansion Committee meetings, and one workshop conducted jointly by OSC, CTG, the NYS Forum for Information Resource Management and ComputerWorks. In addition, during or after an interview or an event, relevant documents and archival data were solicited. These documents allowed us to track information about the history and background of this project, the legal and policy environment, the objectives, and other issues related to the project.

Each of the interviews followed a semi-structured interview protocol, and lasted for one and a half to two hours. All of the interviews were taped recorded with participants’ consent and transcribed verbatim. Observational data focused on information about relationships among organisations, incentives, external issues, conflicts and disagreement. In addition, observational data also recorded how and to whom people said things, where they sat, how they felt about the situations and other people, who did not get involved in the discussion, level of formality, and the physical environment. These observations allowed us to make a subjective judgement about who were leaders, key players, champions, and major opponents; and how the various types of leaders were able to influence the process.

After each interview and event, the interview transcription and observation notes were converted into electronic versions and saved as a Hermeneutics Unit using ATLAS.ti software. We identified key concepts and themes that emerged from the text and linked them to formal theories of leadership (Lincoln & Guba, 1985; Strauss, 1987; Charmaz, 2000). Following suggestions from both Miles & Huberman (1994) and Eisenhardt (1989), the initial coding was somewhere between open coding and deductive coding, in the sense that it was guided by general themes from the literature review. The data analysis was an iterative process as data were coded and the emerging themes and subthemes were explored immediately after several initial data collection activities. This allowed the gaps in the available data to be identified so that they could be addressed in subsequent data collection activities. In addition, new themes and sub-themes were developed as researchers conducted iterative analyses of the text. It should be noted that the text was coded according to the interpretation of the researchers, rather than by matching the codes with the exact words spoken by the participants. In the section that follows, verbatim quotes from participants will be presented as examples of concepts and theories.

## Results

The case results showed that during this process of developing and implementing the MACROS knowledge sharing system, leadership was not provided only by one person. A distributed leadership was crucial for its success. In the following sections, we examine the roles carried out by a number of leaders.

## Project leader

Many of those interviewed stated the primary factor leading to the success of the MACROS project was the project leader. As one participant pointed out, ‘she is the heart and soul of the project.’

The technical expertise of the project leader enabled her to effectively evaluate the software products and negotiate the contract with the vendor. She acted as a conduit between the organisation and the vendor in defining the system requirements and in managing the customisation process. Her suggestions for several enhancements were eventually incorporated into newly released versions of the software, and, as a result, those enhancements became freely available to OSC.

In addition to the project leader’s technical knowledge, she was characterised as having extensive knowledge and exceptional instincts about the organisation – how it works and how different people in different units connect with one another. Interviewees recognised that her knowledge and instincts about the organisation enhanced her ability to coordinate and facilitate the progress of the project. First, in spite of the prevalent segmentation of business processes and information usage in OSC, she was able to frame the nature of the information problem in a broader perspective and, further, to articulate and communicate the idea of sharing to the team, upper management, regional executives, and support staff, and thus gain their support and buy-in. Her knowledge about the organisation also enabled her to manage people skilfully. She brought the right people onto the team, ensuring the system would appropriately address the problem at hand. Finally, because each division has different rules and procedures, the consensus building and coordination required great effort. She effectively coordinated the diverse expectations and requirements of a heterogeneous group, making sure that the requirements of another division outside of MA were an integral part of the big picture.

Several interviewees particularly commented that her leadership ability was enhanced by her image and her reputation for trustworthiness in the organisation. She gained the leadership role not simply because she was appointed to this position, but because of what she had been able to achieve and the influence she had as a result of her expertise. Indeed, when the project started, she began taking on responsibilities for tasks that are generally assigned to people working in positions that are several levels higher than her formal assignment and this leadership role was not formally designated with an organisational title. Nevertheless, she emerged as the leader because she believed in the idea of a seamless organisation and because she was trusted by the executives and staff as someone who could make a difference. Her leadership ability became more important than her organisational position. As one facilitator from CTG who had worked very closely with this project observed, ‘she’s got the reputation of being a person who is doing the right things, has the support of the leadership, and that’s happened over time. y the authority and the responsibilities that she has been granted is a result of yearsy[where she has demonstrated] that she can do so much more, and wants to do so much more.’

Beyond the project leader’s knowledge and reputation was her perseverance and passion for MACROS. This project extended over a long period of time, during which several reorganisations and personnel changes occurred, which could have shifted the focus of the organisation of this system. In spite of many barriers that emerged over the course of the project, she persisted. One executive in MA commented,

y you can have somebody very technically proficient who just doesn’t have what it takes to get knocked down day after day, disappointed and frustrated, and it’s harder than it is supposed to be, and it is taking longer than it is supposed to be, and people are saying bad things to you, and they are hurting your feelings. Whatever the day-to-day aggravation, she dusts herself off and gets back on the horse and she rides on another day. That persistence and that passion are really the key to our success.

Although persistent, she was also seen as flexible with regard to the implementation strategy. When running into difficulties and stagnancy, she understood that the path to success is not always straightforward and deterministic. She was able to appreciate key elements of the environment, and take advantage of the dynamic nature of the situation, and modify steps along the way. Thus, when the implementation of one particular component of the MACROS system met substantial resistance, and another component emerged as a sensible step to proceed first, she swiftly adjusted the strategy, allowing the project to evolve down a different path, one that would lead to better progress. This variation in implementation strategy was seen as essential for achieving initial success of the technology implementation, which further drove the evolution of incentives, and reinforced the commitment of upper management and the champions.

## Upper management

Although the project leader was seen as pivotal in providing a vision and coordinating the project activities, many participants stated that her efforts would not have been effective without appreciation, encouragement, and nurturing from upper management. As one individual observed,

y So the combination of that, somebody who could formulate the issue and potential solutions and people who were willing to listen and [give] support to proceed. I am sure there are a lot of good ideas dying on the desk of the person who thought of them, because they can’t find somebody to go along with it. So they gave up. Getting something that is a significant change requires persistence and executive support.

Interviewees pointed out a number of individuals in top management who were crucial to the project’s success. In addition to the top management within MA, because of the cross-boundary nature of the project, top managers in other divisions as well as agency-wide top management were important in balancing the complicated relationships. Collectively, they contributed to the success by overcoming structural barriers, empowering the project leader, bringing the community together, dedicating resources, and creating a culture that encouraged collaboration.

Interviewees consistently noted that OSC upper management demonstrated true leadership in taking actions that demonstrated their willingness to work around the existing structure and bear the risk of undertaking a non-conventional course of action. The development of MACROS had implications for existing organisational structures and the relationships among important actors in the organisation. The greatest conflict arose over the lack of congruity between the traditional centralised IT resource allocation process and growing decentralised business requirements. Traditionally, IT resources in OSC had been rationalised and allocated in a centralised way. A central IT unit, the Bureau of Information Technology Services (BITS), had sole responsibility for system development and maintenance throughout the agency. In order to use the IT resources from BITS, a project was expected to go through a formal evaluation process, the Information Resource Management (IRM) planning process, held once a year. There had been areas of mistrust between BITS (traditional developers) and the business bureaus (users) in OSC. Division staff saw BITS as focusing on technical rather than business issues and not open to exploratory discussions of business problems. On the other hand, BITS was concerned, based on previous experiences with other divisions, that MA might produce a system solution that BITS would be responsible for maintaining over the long term. As a result, the request to have the MACROS project sponsored in 1999 was rejected on the grounds that the MACROS application was not seen as supporting mission critical operations in OSC.

After the MACROS project failed to gain support in the agency-wide IRM process, the leaders in MA became determined to fund this project using MA’s own budget and personnel. With no precedent for a project to be funded out of a budget other than the central IT resource, nor a system being run by staff outside of BITS, the initiative met tremendous resistance and could even have been stopped by a political battle. To overcome this major obstacle, MA leaders built a coalition with a number of Deputy and Assistant Deputy Comptrollers in other divisions and persuaded them that the concept embodied in the MACROS proposal would have the potential to solve agency-wide information problems as well. With the overwhelming support advocating that the business needs of the MA Division as well as MACROS’s potential for addressing agency-wide information needs justified taking an unconventional route, upper management found a way to alter the process and MA was given permission to proceed.

The strategic importance of information in MA was clearly reflected in its continued efforts to alter its organisational structure. In 1999, MA created its own IT unit and appointed a CIO in formal recognition of their IT dedication. This provided a constant structure from which MACROS can draw resources.

Another important action taken by upper management to foster the development of the project was to support and empower the project leader and, more importantly, provide coaching and opportunities for her growth. As noted above, when this individual became the leader of this project, she did not have a title that was associated with the responsibilities she had assumed and so she would not have been allowed to progress on a career path associated with information system development. Not only did the leaders in the MA division not limit her because of her title, they were perceptive to her potential as well as her professional growth needs and fought hard for a permanent position that would allow her to continue to grow in the future.

Upper management also recognised that the success of the MACROS project necessitated the involvement of a broad range of communities – regional offices and the central office, support and operational units, and MA and other divisions. Participants in the interviews consistently expressed that the constant and close engagement of those different knowledge actors could not have been provided without support from the executives who supervise those communities. For example, one participant noted,

I should have thrown in [one of the regional directors] too. He has responsibility to oversee regional offices. y So we can do this without him, but with him, it’s not a struggle. It’s a collaborative thing we should do. So it’s essential to ease the way with which we were able to proceed with the workflow piece. And he is also a champion of the technical assistance piece of MACROS.

Therefore, although a limited amount of resources were initially available to the project, the MACROS team was able to share resources and responsibilities with agency leaders from other business units and other divisions in order to proceed at a reasonable pace. In addition, some division leaders committed specific resources to enable continued progress. Comparing across divisions, our observations showed that those divisions that committed greater resources toward the implementation of MACROS were more successful in using the system for their own benefit. Conversely, those divisions or bureaus where there was a lack of commitment and resource dedication achieved fewer benefits from MACROS. As one participant commented,

We are committed to this and we’re devoting a lot of time to it. We may have another bureau that has other duties and responsibilities other than this, so it’s a very challenging prospect to think I’m going to have success in MACROS, and by the way, I have a full-time job that I have to satisfy too. We’re unique in some ways in that we can spend a lot of time on this thing because it’s so important to us.

Finally, a central role played by top management in the development of MACROS was to endorse and foster collaboration and knowledge sharing by defining norms and culture in the organisation. The Comptroller of OSC from 1994 to 2002 was strongly in favour of collaboration and partnership. In the mission statement of the agency – ‘partnership, excellence, respect, integrity, and leadership’ – partnership was listed as a leading principle for the agency. The Comptroller further institutionalised this value by selecting leaders at the top levels according to their ability and willingness to work in a collaborative environment. In addition, appropriate training and reorganisations were carried out to legitimise collaboration and cultivate trust throughout the agency. Some interviewees observed that the development of MACROS clearly benefited from its being seen as a means to strengthen the core values of the organisation; on the other hand, it is clear that MACROS would not likely have been carried out had there not already been systematic efforts to build collaboration and trust in the organisation. Thus, its progress was accelerated because it was both an input to and an output of an environment that appreciated collaboration, trust, and openness.

At this point it is important to recognise that, while the Comptroller had made much progress in changing the organisational culture, it was also evident during interviews and observations that the collaborative culture was not necessarily consistent throughout the organisation. As noted above, there were certainly some areas of mistrust between BITS and the business divisions in OSC. In addition, some mistrust appeared to be present between MA and other divisions, and some divisions were very sceptical about the functionality of MACROS. Even in divisions that fully embraced the concept of MACROS, there were concerns that the MACROS applications were advanced based on the MA division’s agenda, while the general progress of agency-wide applications was relatively slow. Nonetheless, trust built in the structure of the organisation, and long-term interpersonal ties and relationships, as well as the individual predispositions of a group of executives and champions, were highlighted as indispensable to enhancing the communication, alleviating the conflicts and easing the way for risk taking and organisational changes.

## Champions

The data from interviews and observations showed that many individual users throughout OSC, individuals who were directly involved in the development of the system – the first adopters – carried out a key leadership role. These individuals have different ranks and professions, ranging from Chief Examiners to liaisons in BITS to secretaries; yet they all contributed substantially to the development of MACROS by providing leadership within their own division, office, and units. They were chosen or volunteered because they have a strong appreciation of the technology and the idea represented by MACROS. They took an active role by providing detailed information about system requirements, promoting the information system, making incremental changes in their work units, telling success stories, and creating a momentum to drive the further adoption of the system. As the CIO mentioned, ‘[s]o it’s not just giving us our moral support to keep the project going but providing that leadership within their own divisions, saying ‘‘sharing information is a good idea, let’s figure out how to do it.’’’

In fact, disincentives to participation existed even within the MA division due to historical information sharing behaviours, personnel changes, and personal conflicts. The benefits to sharing knowledge through collaboration were not immediately obvious to the regional staff. Instead, they felt they had many reasons for not sharing knowledge. For example, regional staff were aware that they could potentially lose their professional autonomy, and need to give up their independent approaches to their job functions and conform to a uniform business process. In order to address these disincentives, the MACROS team recruited champions, those who could see the value and were willing to take a risk. Their success was told in stories that demonstrated the value of this system, which counteracted the effects of the negative attitude of those who were reluctant or indifferent about changes. One team member stated, ‘if we can get some success out of people who want it to be successful, then that can grow.

A severe challenge faced during the project was related to the different definitions and practices exercised by various offices. Initially, the plan was to build a component focused on ‘technical assistance.’ In preparation for developing this component, a 2-day workshop was held in the Spring of 1999 to define the term, ‘technical assistance’ and to examine the business processes involved in tracking technical assistance. Thirty-five potential users from central and regional offices participated in this workshop. One difficulty revealed in the workshop was the lack of a consensus on the types of technical assistance to be recorded and how tracking could be carried out. Considering that there were a large number of calls for technical assistance and they were of a varying nature, it was suggested that only ‘significant’ instances of technical assistance should be kept in the information system. This approach seemed like a reasonable business rule to all attending. However, it quickly became apparent that since each office uses different practices, participants would have difficulty reaching agreement on what types of technical assistance they considered ‘significant.’ The challenge had much to do with the nature of knowledge, which can be highly tacit and embedded in local community practices and context making the exchange of knowledge across boundaries more complex and difficult. Thus, to enable the system to handle the complexity and to meet crosscommunity information needs, several champions with experience and knowledge from several communities demonstrated leadership in working with the project team to develop ideas for the designing the system to mitigate the embedded nature of knowledge.

In summary, our case study shows that effective leaders were able to promote knowledge sharing and system development through their ability to shape the organisational structures and processes, mobilise the resources, legitimate the changes, and cultivate norms and values in favour of sharing. More importantly, leadership was carried out by distributed leadership consisting of a knowledgeable and persistent project leader who played a spearheading and coordinating role, a group of perceptive and collaboration-inclined executives who carried out supporting and steering roles, and grass-roots champions who provided knowledge and created momentum to drive the progress. ## Discussion

Based on the research literature and affirmed by our case study, Table 1 shows how leadership roles are actually distributed and how leaders perform interdependent roles to fulfil these tasks. In our description of the results, we focused on the organisational roles that leaders play. It is useful, however, to look at distributed leadership as a unit of analysis, describing how it operates. This can be done by focusing on two properties of a distributed leadership – interdependence and emergence.

Interdependence implies that the task execution of one actor is constrained by and dependent on the actions of others (Gronn, 2002), and the joint performance is determined by the reciprocal influences of two or more members in the organisations. Table 1 lists the different roles that the three types of leaders played in the development of the MACROS system. As has been indicated in various places in the case results, these leadership roles dynamically interact with each other. Clearly, each of these three different levels of leadership was necessary for the project’s success; one leader could not have achieved the same level of impact without the active involvement of the other types of leaders. A second property of distributed leadership is its emergent nature. The leadership roles may not be unambiguously designated and clearly delineated in a distributed environment. Our case shows that environmental complexity and the need for simultaneous collaboration require leaders to rise to the occasion and fill in gaps when needed. Furthermore, the introduction of interdependency and new requirements for coordination that result from new information systems and changes in management practices also necessitate that the division of labour remain relatively flexible and openended. The project leader was not initially designated as a formal project leader until the idea of the project grew to be a mature concept; some individuals in upper management were not involved until their support was required; and the champions volunteered to serve in important roles although their actions did not fall into the areas of their official responsibility. In addition, the project was not run as a traditional IT project in this organisation, and the participation of a central IT department was very limited. It was not immediately clear who should be doing what, and arguably, the division of labour was kept ambiguous and was constantly reconstructed throughout the project. Even at the end of this study, there remained uncertainty where this system and its key personnel are heading. This aspect of distributed leadership is consistent with leadership theory that influences and hence the potential for leaders to emerge in the flow of activities changed over time, particularly as organisations see the need for change and development (Tichy, 1997; Yukl, 2002). The emergent nature of leadership also suggests that some actors are likely to be more influential than others and that some are more influential at some points in time than at others because the consequence of their actions will bear differential weight in a chain of dynamic interactions of system development.

The emergent nature of distributed leadership thus can be crucial to state of change. But an important question arises: Is emergence of leadership roles a determinant characteristic of distributed leadership? Some might challenge the sustainability of changes. They might argue for the equilibrium of stability, an unstable roles slowly settling into a stable structure and routine. Although the existing structure may make the actions of agents in making changes more or less effective, when we examine distributed leadership as a single entity we become aware of implicit process of negotiation and redefinition of leaders’ roles.

This case study has significance to theory development of leadership in IS research. The conclusion and implications contribute to the development of an enriched understanding of the nature of distributed leadership. It provides a description of the set of roles distributed to individuals at different levels of the organisation in developing a knowledge sharing system, and identifies the interdependent and emergent properties of the role distribution, thus, responds to the call for a more refined understanding about how distributed leadership emerge and unfold in organisations (Pearce & Conger, 2003). In addition, the discussion on the interdependence and emergence of distributed leadership shed light on the kind of model that is possible of distribution. Previous studies revealed role sharing within top management team (Pitcher & Smith, 2001), as well as role distribution between project leaders and top managers, and between project leaders and the front-line employees (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka et al., 2001). This study offers a different type, arguably, a more integrated type of distribution. Dynamic influences are identified among members in top management, across organisational levels, and across work teams and organisational units. This enlarges the collection of distributed leadership models, and raises additional research questions. Future research may examine what other types of distributed leadership exist, and what would be the ideal models, and under what conditions one model might be preferred to others.

This case also provide practical guidance for practitioners such as CIOs and IT managers who are struggling to promote and evaluate leadership as well as executives who seek to design new structures and practices to facilitate distributed leadership in their organisations. First, organisational leaders and IT managers should recognised that the tasks involved in the knowledge sharing system development is more than a cumulative flow of discrete and task-oriented actions. Job responsibilities are shifting, and there is more work than any individual’s ability to cope with. The effectiveness of dealing with new and interdependent tasks relies on how well managers could harvest the energy yielded from the emerging and interactive dynamics of actors at all level. IT managers are not automatic leaders, nor are users only to be led. MACROS case provide the example of the proactive role played by an individual who is essentially a user (later becoming the project leader) in defining the organisational objectives and the spontaneous actions of the knowledge champions in defining the practice and knowledge. To manage such ambiguous and dynamic interactions may be challenging, but they are essential for a manager’s work. The interdependent and emergent nature of distributed leadership places a high priority on learning capability and adaptability of the IT managers.

Second, the study also has implications to the leadership styles associated with distributed leadership. In order for emergent and interactive dynamics to grow in a distributed situation, the demand for cognitive diversity and behavioural complexity is especially salient. We saw that with no precedence for such an agency-wide collaboration and with the existing organisational structure and processes that were often in conflict with the new practices and approaches that MACROS introduced, leaders needed to contend with the need for preservation and change, the obligation for integrity and collaboration, the requirement for adhering to the vision and being flexible with the implementation strategy, and the demand for leading and being led. Although they might seem contradictory, persistent commitment and flexible strategies are complementary behaviours and examples of the ability to respond to paradoxical demands simultaneously. By persistently adhering to the vision and objectives, leaders could be open to changes that would lead to the achievement of objectives; while by being flexible with respect to strategies and tactics leaders could drive the progress, endure the constraints, and maintain the vision over the course of the project. A lesson learned from this MACROS case is that IS leaders are more effective when they possess the capability to recognise a contradictory and complex environment, and they can respond to simultaneous paradoxical demands.

Finally, collaborative initiatives for building and using knowledge sharing systems need persistent leadership to fill in many interdependent roles. It may emerge naturally as it occurred in MACROS case. However, the evolution of such natural growth relies on the nourishment from the organisation. The sustainability of distributed leadership would benefit from an organisation where flexible structure such as self-management, empowerment, and collective efforts of senior management are supported. Organisations can take advantage of distributed leadership by building a structure and culture that will facilitate leadership to emerge at all levels, and select and reward individuals who are predisposed of collaborative actions.

We should note that there are limitations to this study. Case study is limited in its generalisability to other settings. The data collection and methodology might be idiosyncratic to the study or researcher. It might not always be clear how to test or replicate case analyses. Admittedly, the task environment and structure of this particular agency created a unique opportunity. How leadership emerges under these conditions may not necessarily reflect how it would emerge under other circumstances. The uniqueness of case context requires that readers apply caution in extending the findings to all other IT environments. However, the results from case study have naturalistic generalisability or transferability as opposed to propositional generalisation that is strength of good case studies (Stake, 1995). A case study can provide vicarious experience from which other researchers and practitioners can form generalised understandings about a similar set of problems, not to draw quantifiable correlations in the proposed relationships. The uniqueness of the social and historical context is not an external factor that should be controlled; rather, it is critical for understanding the dynamic interactions and outcomes. Therefore, the validity of case study can be judged in terms of empirical consistency and heuristic value. Moreover, the generalisability of case studies depends on the growth of the collection of cases developed in various settings. Accordingly, future research in different settings, both in the public and private sectors, may reveal further insights into the roles of different leaders in the implementation of knowledge sharing systems.

## Conclusion

In summary, the case of MACROS demonstrated that the tasks that formed the basis for developing and implementing a knowledge sharing system such as formulating vision, reconstructing organisational structures and processes, and coordinating knowledge sharing activities were distributed across various leaders in the organisation. To deal with the interdependent tasks and new requirements for coordination result from new information systems and changes in management practices, the collective efforts of a number of leaders were essential.
