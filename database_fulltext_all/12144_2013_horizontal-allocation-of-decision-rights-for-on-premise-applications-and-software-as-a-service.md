# Horizontal Allocation of Decision Rights for On-Premise Applications and Software-as-a-Service

Till J. Win kle r and Ca ro l V. Brown

Ab st ract : This study addresses a major gap in our knowledge about the allocation of information technology (IT) decision rights between business and IT units at the application level, including the governance of applications delivered on-premise versus those delivered with a software-as-a-service (SaaS) model. Building on the findings from a multicase qualitative study of organizations that had adopted the same SaaS application, we draw on three theoretical lenses (agency theory, transaction cost economics, and knowledge-based view) to develop a theoretically grounded model with three organization-level factors, three application-level factors, and application-level IT governance. Hypotheses derived from the model, as well as a set of differential hypotheses about factor influences due to on-premise versus SaaS delivery, are tested with survey responses from 207 firms in which application-level governance is operationalized with two dimensions: decision control rights (decision authority) and decision management rights (task responsibility). Three antecedents (origin of the application initiative, scope of application use, business knowledge of the IT unit) were significantly associated with application governance postimplementation, and the on-premise/SaaS subgroup analyses provide preliminary evidence for the mode of application delivery as a moderator of these relationships. Overall, this study contributes to a growing body of research that takes a more modular approach to studying IT governance and provides theoretical explanations for differing application-level governance designs.

Key w ords and phrases: agency theory, application governance, cloud computing, field survey, IT governance, knowledge-based view, multigroup analysis, softwareas-a-service, transaction cost economics.

The alloc at ion of informat ion t ec hnology (IT) dec ision right s between IT units and business units remains an important IT governance challenge [8]. Companies that do not design an appropriate accountability framework for IT run the risk of business losses due to poor management decisions and misaligned IT priorities [35]. The widespread phenomenon of “shadow IT,” for example, refers to the challenges that arise when business units operate applications without central approval and involvement of IT units [4]. In other words, while there is a consensus in the literature that certain IT decisions require strong participation and ownership of business managers (e.g., [3, 9, 10, 36, 37, 46]), the IT function has still largely been viewed as the focal point for managing IT delivery, including the coordination of external providers [28, 47]. However, the recent rise of Internet-based delivery models such as software-as-a-service (SaaS) challenges this traditional assumption [1, 52].<sup>1</sup> Given that today’s business applications are increasingly provided externally, there is a need to reexamine earlier rationales for assigning decision rights between business and IT units and thus to define (or refine) appropriate IT governance schemes for managing specific business applications, including SaaS.

Prior IT governance research has focused on the distribution of IT decision rights between corporate IT and business units (e.g., [10, 37, 46]) and the mechanisms that are needed to implement and complement effective decision-making structures (e.g., [9, 15, 46]). Some researchers have also investigated the role of business managers and their competencies in strategic IT planning and decision making (e.g., [3, 36]). While the preponderance of IT governance researchers have studied IT decision-making structures and governance mechanisms at the IT function level, IT governance has also more recently been examined for particular IT subfunctions such as system development [43, 44], enterprise IT architecture [7, 45], as well as IT infrastructure investment [23] and investment decision processes [51].

However, the prior IT governance research falls short in providing a theoretically grounded model of factors to explain the allocation of decision rights for managing a specific business application, including applications for which a third party is responsible for the delivery. In addition, the outsourcing literature that has addressed SaaS solutions has largely focused on governance issues related to the external party— such as contractual arrangements [41, 42], pricing [12], and coordination [16]—from a simplistic two-stakeholder perspective: the client firm and the supplier (vendor) firm [28, p. 149].

In this paper we therefore not only address the neglected question of how organizations allocate horizontal decision rights between business and IT units at the application level and what factors influence these choices but also whether these factors have greater or lesser influence on governance choices for applications with SaaS versus traditional “on-premise” (local) delivery models. Our overall research questions for this study are as follows:

## RQ1: Which factors explain the governance of a specific business application?

## RQ2: Do these factors differ for SaaS and on-premise applications?

Given the paucity of prior literature on this topic, an exploratory multicase study was initially conducted by the first author in which potentially influential antecedents for the application governance of a common SaaS application were identified [50]. Using qualitative methods, this initial study also yielded a conceptualization of IT decision rights at the application level that included two application governance subdimensions: decision authority and task responsibility. In the next section we therefore first describe the case study research findings and then take a multitheoretic approach to explain the organizational boundary issues relevant to the application governance phenomenon. In the subsequent section we then use these three theoretical lenses (agency theory, transaction cost economics, and knowledge-based view) to develop the research model and hypotheses, which include both organization-level and application-level factors, to address our two research questions. After describing our research methodology, we present the results of our quantitative analyses, including the study’s limitations. Based on the survey data collected from 207 large companies in Germany, 131 of which provided information about an on-premise application in use and 76 about a SaaS, we find support for two application-level and one organization-level antecedents as well as some preliminary support for the theoretical arguments for some antecedents having different influence for SaaS versus on-premise applications. The paper ends with a discussion of the results and both the theoretical and practical implications, in which we argue that our empirical findings not only provide evidence for the importance of a modular view of IT governance arrangements that captures differences due to external delivery models such as SaaS but also the importance of a multitheoretic approach to explain these arrangements.

## Precursory Case Study Findings and Theoretical Framing

Giv en t he lac k of researc h on t he gov ernanc e of SaaS applic at ions, precursory case study research was conducted by the first author under the positivistic assumption that multiple contingency factors influence application-level governance choices [50], as found in prior research on overall IT governance choices (e.g., [10, 37, 46]). Four organizations of different sizes and from different industries were selected that had implemented a SaaS application from the same vendor (Salesforce.com) and where the decision rights for the SaaS application in one pair of case sites resided largely with business managers and in the other pair with IT managers. Interviews were conducted with relevant managers (one business manager and one IT manager each for the first pair of case sites and only IT managers for the second pair) using open-ended questions to explore factors that influenced the governance of the SaaS application.

Utilizing a qualitative inductive approach, the study identified two categories of application-level decision rights: decision authorities (including application change decisions, financial decisions, and architecture decisions) and task responsibilities (including change implementation and user support responsibilities). One contingency factor, the origin of the application initiative (i.e., whether business or IT), emerged as a salient antecedent. Two types of absorptive capacities or knowledge-based contingencies (IT knowledge in business units or business knowledge in the IT unit, respectively) emerged as salient antecedents in the two cases that reported to have a highly effective SaaS application governance—one under the business unit and the other under the IT unit. In addition, two application-level characteristics emerged as potentially influential factors: the application’s specificity (e.g., expressed by the ease of customization) and its scope of use within the organization (i.e., within multiple or single units).

Building on these exploratory findings, the objective of the current study is to develop a theoretically grounded research model to explain application-level governance choices and to test hypotheses derived from the model with a population sample that includes different on-premise as well as SaaS-based applications to address our two research questions. In order to theoretically ground the emergent factor origin of initiative, we draw on agency theory (AT [20]) as an appropriate lens because of its focus on issues related to the self-interest and opportunism of different actors in governance contexts [14]. Second, we contend that the factors related to absorptive capacities (i.e., IT knowledge in business units, business knowledge in IT unit) can be best understood by drawing on the knowledge-based view of the firm (KBV [22]). In addition, transaction cost economics (TCE [48]) has been used in prior literature to explain application-level sourcing decisions in general [28], as well as for SaaS (e.g., [6]). We therefore use a TCE lens to theoretically ground the emergent factors that captured characteristics of the application (i.e., specificity and scope of use).

All three of these reference theories, AT, TCE, and KBV, have also been used by management researchers as alternative theories to explain an organization’s external or internal boundary choices [17, 32]. Given that the allocation of IT decision rights between different IT and business units is an internal boundary choice—a notion implied by some of the past IT governance literature [8, 10, 37]—we therefore argue that this multitheoretic approach is an appropriate one to hypothesize relationships between organization-level and application-level factors and the two-dimension application-level governance construct that emerged from the precursory study. The results of our hypotheses testing will therefore also enable us to assess the explanatory value provided by each of these theoretical perspectives.

## Application Governance

We define application governance as the locus of decision rights for a business application. A business application—such as a system for enterprise resource planning, customer relationship management (CRM), business intelligence and analytics, or collaboration and communication—is an IT artifact that is a subsystem of an organization’s IT architecture, runs on a physical infrastructure, and delivers certain business functionality [39, 45].<sup>2</sup> While decision making regarding the overall IT function is regarded as a core element of IT governance [10, 37, 46], decision making regarding a particular business application is an important aspect of application governance. Consistent with an earlier definition of IT governance [10], this construct is concerned with what are the major decision rights needed to manage a business application and who makes these decisions. In a simple view, IT decision rights are decentralized to business units or centralized to corporate IT units [10, 37]. In this research, we follow previous authors (e.g., [10, 23, 43]) and focus on the horizontal dimension and the relationship between business and IT units measured on a continuum.

For this study we operationalize application governance as consisting of the two dimensions of IT decision rights identified by the precursory case research (i.e., decision authority and task responsibility). According to an established classification, these two dimensions can be regarded as separate decision control and decision management rights [21]. In other words, decision authority is allocated to those who control application operations, and task responsibility to those who manage them. Accordingly, we have modeled the application governance construct as a second-order factor that contains decision authority and task responsibility as its first-order dimensions [19].

Furthermore, when an application is outsourced—as is the case for SaaS—large parts of task responsibility are delegated to an external party. For example, a task structure proposed for SaaS CRM services consists of seven tasks, out of which only two (mapping SaaS to business requirements and streamlining customer-facing activities) are performed by the user organization [42]. However, the client organization still needs to retain, or even build up, certain capabilities to coordinate and control the activities of the external provider [47]. Therefore, in our conceptualization of application governance we limit this dimension to a firm’s internal continuum.

## Agency-Theoretical Perspective

The agency-theoretical perspective provides a theoretical explanation for why there is a need to arbitrate a balance of rights and their respective accountabilities between relevant actors for application-level governance. Agency theory is concerned with problems that arise in asymmetric relationships where one party (the principal) delegates work to another (the agent) who is more specialized in this task performance [20]. Since problems arise when principals and agents possess conflicting goals, incomplete information, and different risk preferences, appropriate accountability schemes need to be established to generate the expected value from an agency relationship—such as the business/IT relationship.

## IT Governance (Organization Level)

IT governance has been defined as the overall accountability framework for IT decisions to enable desirable behaviors [46] and is viewed as a key responsibility of corporate management [15]. The design of an organization’s IT governance framework is recognized in the literature as involving key trade-off decisions. For example, when IT decision rights are exclusively allocated to an IT unit (agent), there is a substantial threat that the business (principal’s) interests are not sufficiently considered, resulting in a lack of business/IT alignment [15]. Conversely, if IT decision rights are allocated to business units, considerations from a technical (e.g., integration with other IT solutions) as well as an enterprise-wide perspective (e.g., the use of standards) are not sufficiently addressed [7].

Some of the prior IT governance literature emphasizes the design choices for application governance versus infrastructure governance [9, 37]. More recently, a widely recognized framework of five IT domains has been proposed: IT principles, IT infrastructure strategies, business application needs, IT investment and prioritization, and IT architecture [46]. The latter three domains are more likely to influence an application-level governance arrangement, whereas the former two (IT principles and IT infrastructure) are only indirectly linked with business applications. For example, IT infrastructure is frequently governed in a centralized mode while application-related decision making is more shared between business and (decentralized) IT units—a pattern that has also been recognized as a federal (or hybrid) archetype [9, 37].

Congruent with the two application-level governance dimensions in this study, we focus on the horizontal allocation of overall application-related decision rights between business and IT units operationalized on a continuum. In line with the prior literature, we also assume that each of the classes of decision rights represents a subdomain of IT governance on its own (e.g., for the IT investment domain [23, 51] and for the architecture domain [7]). Thus, we conceptualize the overall IT governance design as formed by these distinct classes of IT decisions rather than influencing them. Accordingly, for the purposes of this study, we define IT governance as the locus of application-related decision rights (i.e., on business application needs, IT investment, and IT architecture) at the level of the overall IT function, and we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: Overall IT governance is associated with application-level governance.

## Origin of Initiative (Application Level)

The precursory case study research suggests that the origin of the initiative to introduce a new application is a strong predictor for postimplementation application-level governance. We define the origin of initiative as the locus of the idea and driving force to introduce a business application prior to implementation. Agency theory informs that, especially in the absence of overall IT governance schemes, application governance can be the (beneficial or adverse) result of opportunism of agents manifested in the individual initiative. Such an initiative would emerge based on opportunism of business or IT actors, that is, it can be conceptualized analogous to the governance dimensions as a continuum between these.

In a procedural view, the locus of initiative may even be regarded as the exertion of a specific decision management right that is applicable during the first step in application-related decision making [21]. Initiation can therefore be an important predecessor of formal decision rights and can also outweigh overarching accountability schemes if there is a strong self-interest in agency. However, we delineate the initiative construct from application governance by two criteria that refer to time and voluntariness. In other words, while application governance refers to the ongoing and (presumably) more formalized arrangements for ongoing operations of a business application (e.g., by certain roles or task descriptions), the initiative takes place in a preimplementation context [51]. During this phase, parties giving input to investment decisions may be diverse and very distinct from those approving later decisions. In other words, project ideas can be brought in by business or IT stakeholders, while the roles for deciding on and executing projects might be more firmly defined. Still, depending on the degrees of freedom in defining these later roles, we hypothesize an association between the origin of the application initiative and postimplementation application governance due to the initiator’s self-interest in taking over later decision rights. Accordingly, we posit:

Hypothesis 2: The origin of initiative is associated with application-level governance.

## Differential Hypotheses Based on Agency Theory

The differences inherent to on-premise and SaaS delivery models lead us to also hypothesize different levels of influences (stronger, weaker) of the two agency-theoretic constructs on application governance arrangements. These differences primarily result from aspects related to lower observability [13], lower controllability [44], lower investment risks [5], and variable pricing [12] of SaaS.

On-premise applications are by definition operated within (or in proximity to) the user organization; therefore, observability of the agent, that is, the ability to gather information about his or her behavior [13], can be regarded as comparably high. In contrast to the SaaS model, which is characterized by subscription-based pricing models and low capital lockup [12], on-premise applications also typically imply greater investment risks in case of possible project failure. Therefore, organizations (or better, the principals) create a strong governance framework and according mechanisms, such as investment planning and decision procedures and centralized approval, to mitigate these risks. These mechanisms also imply a greater separation of responsibilities for initiation and later responsibility for application operation.

For SaaS, however, initiators (or agents) have a greater incentive to behave opportunistically, such as by circumventing centralized investment procedures. This is not only due to lower observability and external provision of SaaS but also due to the lower investment risk [5]. In other words, agents that possess the same risk attitude and have potentially conflicting goals with the principal’s governance framework will be more inclined to take this risk and introduce SaaS compared to a traditional onpremise solution. A principal (e.g., corporate management) may grant lower controls when sourcing SaaS in situations where the agent takes the residual risks (e.g., by paying fees from a business unit budget instead of using centralized resources). Thus, for SaaS applications, initiation and postimplementation application management are more likely to reside in the same unit. Consequently, we posit that:

ΔHypothesis 1: The association of overall IT governance with application-level governance is weaker for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

ΔHypothesis 2: The association of the origin of initiative with application-level governance is stronger for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

## Transaction Cost Perspective

The transaction cost economics (TCE) lens provides a theoretical explanation for the two characteristics of the application itself—that is, application specificity and scope of application use—that emerged from the precursory case research as potential predictors of the horizontal allocation of application-level decision rights. Information systems (IS) scholars have previously drawn on TCE to explain contractual arrangements and other governance phenomena in outsourcing relationships [28], including SaaS-based outsourcing [6, 41]. TCE puts transactions, defined as the ultimate unit of economic activity, as the focus of interest to explain the boundary of an organization or an organizational subunit [48]. Applying this view to application-level governance arrangements, business units either enter into a contract with (internal or external) IT units to economize on total cost for application-related transactions, or coordinate application operations hierarchically. Total costs are composed of classical production costs (e.g., for input factors such as licenses, facilities, and labor incurred) and transaction costs (e.g., for planning, adapting, and monitoring application operation). According to TCE, transaction costs are relatively lower under hierarchical coordination within business units, while production is more efficiently coordinated under an IT unit that is concerned with aggregating IT delivery for multiple business functions due to the economies of scale effects (e.g., through a better utilization of input factors). Thus, TCE posits that two important transaction characteristics determine the level of transaction and production costs: application specificity and transaction frequency, which can be related to the emerging application-level variable scope of use.

## Specificity (Application Level)

TCE informs that if the application is very specific to a business unit and its processes, the transaction costs to a business unit for “contracting out” the application to the IT unit increase—such as through greater efforts for communicating requirements, requesting system changes, as well as monitoring application operations [41]. In the outsourcing literature, application specificity has been conceptualized and defined as the degree to which an application is customized prior to its implementation in a user organization [6]. Customization takes place to adapt a business application to the organization and its context during the process of organizational embedding [39]. It increases specificity inasmuch as a highly adapted system can be less easily redeployed to an alternative user organization without sacrifice of its productive value [48, p. 105].

In terms of application governance, higher specificity is associated with greater decision rights from business units to economize on transaction costs for application operation, such as for planning, adapting, and monitoring. Consider the example of an application for business analytics, which is highly idiosyncratic, since the information provided (e.g., via dashboards and data cubes) needs to be individually adapted to the company’s specific business context [42]. Thus, operating this application requires significant business user involvement and managerial governance. In contrast, applications such as for communicating and collaborating (e-mail, conferencing, and word processing) have lower specificity [6] and would likely require less business involvement, and thus are better governed in an IT-centric form. Accordingly, we posit:

Hypothesis 3: Higher (lower) application specificity is associated with greater business unit (IT unit) application-level governance.

Scope of Use (Application Level)

Scope of application use can be related to the frequency construct in TCE [48]: The frequency of transactions with an application is directly determined by the number of users and business departments making use of this specific application. We define scope of use as the breadth to which an application is used within the organization. Prior research has differentiated four organizational levels of functional scope: departmental, interdepartmental, enterprise, as well as interorganizational scope [51]. For example, a business analytics solution will typically be used by a few experts residing in local departments [42], while applications for collaboration and communication may be used across the entire organization.<sup>3</sup>

The greater the scope of use of a system, the more transaction and production cost will be incurred due to higher efforts for managing that application. Based on the rationale of scale effects in the cost for controlling and managing firm-wide applications, applications with greater scope are more likely to be governed in a centralized, IT-dominated fashion, compared to applications of smaller scope that may be more dispersed across the organization. Accordingly, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4: Higher (lower) application scope of use is associated with greater IT unit (business unit) application-level governance.

## Differential Hypotheses Based on Transaction Cost Economics

There are also likely to exist different levels of influences (stronger, weaker) of the transaction cost theoretic constructs on the application governance for on-premise versus SaaS delivery models. This is because for externally operated applications (e.g., SaaS) the internal economies of scale rationale becomes much weaker, since the production cost advantages largely accrue on the provider side. In other words, for external delivery models such as SaaS, the production costs (expressed by the fees of the provider) are largely independent from the internal governance form. Therefore, the choice for the best internal form of coordination, that is, the mode of application governance, becomes merely dependent on the transaction costs incurred by specificity and scope of use.

Regarding specificity, SaaS applications are thought to facilitate easier customization compared to the bulk of on-premise installed software [40]. SaaS vendors try to avoid the need for tenant-based customizations in terms of code changes by providing a broad set of configuration options that are easily accessible to the user, such as to add data fields, set up reports, define business rules, and create workflows [40]. In contrast, customizations of conventional on-premise systems typically require adjusting complex configuration tables or even custom developments [39], which is likely to necessitate more IT involvement even to implement small changes to an application. SaaS applications with high specificity can be better governed by business units to avoid these types of internal transaction costs. Consequently, the degree of specificity of an application becomes a more influential factor for SaaS application governance compared to on-premise models. Accordingly, we posit:

ΔHypothesis 3: The association of application specificity with application-level governance is stronger for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

A similar argument can be made for scope of use. While applications with a large scope of use provide a rationale to allocate application governance to IT units (due to economies of scale effects in production and transaction costs), this argument is weaker for SaaS applications with a small scope of use (due to missing economies of scale in production costs). Thus, whether the scope of use is smaller (or larger) becomes a more influential factor for SaaS application governance compared to onpremise models. We hypothesize that:

ΔHypothesis 4: The association of application scope with application-level governance is stronger for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

## Knowledge-Based Perspective

The knowledge-based view (KBV) provides a lens to explain the findings related to the organization’s absorptive capacities in the precursory case research. The KBV, which has evolved from the resource-based view (RBV), focuses on knowledge as a unique and strategically important resource of the firm [22]. The primary purpose of the firm is to integrate the specialized knowledge that resides in its employees and is costly to transfer [26]. According to the KBV, centralized decision making is feasible when knowledge can be integrated to a single point of the organization; otherwise, bi- and multilateral decision making is required [22].

Prior literature that has investigated the role of knowledge in governance choices (e.g., [43]) suggests that the two absorptive capacities included in the precursory case research can be regarded as two complementary types of knowledge: IT-related knowledge and knowledge of the business domain. Due to the logic of specialized resources, knowledge about technologies, applications, and IT management typically resides in IT units, whereas business domain knowledge resides within business units. Under this premise, a joint governance form is required to facilitate effective decision making and, in the case of an overweight of complementary knowledge on either side, this party is likely to have a higher level of decision rights for specific application-related issues. Thus, the knowledge-based perspective posits that greater horizontal decision rights over an application will be allocated to the unit where the most pertinent knowledge for the application is present [26].

## IT Knowledge Within Business Units (Organization Level)

IT knowledge can derive either from internalization of external knowledge (e.g., on IT management practices, applications, and technologies) or from prior experience (e.g., from participation in IT projects) [3]. Accordingly, we conceptualize this as an organization-level construct to capture the extent of knowledge and experience in the IT domain in the business units.

Prior IT governance literature has found evidence that the more technical and ITrelated knowledge business managers and employees have collectively “absorbed,” the more inclined they will be to take over authority in application-related decision-making and operational IT tasks [10, 37]. For example, in one of the precursory case sites where the Salesforce CRM administration team resided within the business organization, this team had been complemented by former IT-knowledgeable system administrators so that it could better leverage the CRM application to automate workflows and organize data. This in turn might also have led the business managers and employees to seek higher involvement than the IT unit in decisions related to managing this application. Accordingly, we posit that:

Hypothesis 5: Higher (lower) IT knowledge in business units is associated with business unit (IT unit) application-level governance.

## Business Knowledge Within IT Units (Organization Level)

Complementary knowledge on the IT side refers to the IT employees’ collective knowledge of the business domain [43]. Business knowledge of IT professionals can be broadly conceptualized as organization-specific and interpersonal/management competence [2]. We focus on the first component and conceptualize business knowledge in IT units as the insight into the business processes and day-to-day activities, since business applications primarily support the procedural aspects of an organization.

Some of the earlier IT governance literature has not found evidence for the business knowledge within IT units being a salient contingency factor [10, 41]. However, other IS studies acknowledge this construct as an important competency associated with developing partnerships with the business [2] and engaging in strategic decision making [34]. According to the KBV, business-knowledgeable IT employees are more likely to make required business decisions that arise during the life cycle of a business application. Accordingly, we posit that:

Hypothesis 6: Higher (lower) business knowledge in IT units is associated with greater IT unit (business unit) application-level governance.

## Differential Hypotheses Based on the Knowledge-Based View

The choice of the delivery model and the inherent characteristics of SaaS as an IT artifact are also likely to affect the levels of influence of the knowledge-based antecedents on the allocation of decision rights at the application level. This is because for mass delivery models such as SaaS—where most technical details (the “physical structures” [39]) and implementation-related decisions are in the hands of an external party and cascaded behind a Web front end (the “surface structures” [39])—the need for distinctive knowledge requirements for application-related decision making may differ from applications for on-premise delivery.

First, business managers may require less IT knowledge in order to make informed decisions regarding SaaS. The reverse has been observed in research on custom software development projects, that is, in comparably complex on-premise software delivery contexts, where technical knowledge in a business unit is associated with more effective project performance [43]. Thus, for on-premise software delivery, higher IT knowledge of business units appears to be a precondition for application governance residing in a business unit. In contrast, for the governance of SaaS applications, we assume that IT knowledge of business stakeholders will be less influential. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

ΔHypothesis 5: The association of IT knowledge in business units with applicationlevel governance is weaker for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

Second, the use of external delivery models such as SaaS may also demand different skill requirements for IT employees [2]. Since technical tasks are largely performed externally, the IT team managing SaaS may take on more of a consultant role to the business and a service integrator [52]. For example, in one of the precursory case sites that reported highly effective SaaS CRM governance under a central IT department, the IT staff was expanded with new hires, including a former CRM process consultant. Conversely, if internal IT units do not provide adequate skills and insights in the business processes and day-to-day operations, there is a stronger rationale for business units to govern SaaS-based applications without involvement of IT units. Accordingly, we posit:

ΔHypothesis 6: The association of business knowledge in IT units with applicationlevel governance is stronger for SaaS than for on-premise applications.

## Control Variables

To account for the differences among organizations, we include four control variables in our research model relating to industry, overall firm size, relative IT organization size, and time since implementation of the application. Our rationales for their inclusion are described below.

## Industry

Earlier research on organization and governance phenomena has not found a significant association between a corporation’s industry and the overall level of decentralization of IT in the organization [8]. Given the lack of prior research on application-level governance, we control for the industry dimension by differentiating between manufacturing firms (selling physical products) and service firms (selling informational products). Since there are mixed forms of service and manufacturing businesses (e.g., manufacturing companies that generate significant revenues with postsales services), we operationalize this control variable by two bipolar scales: one referring to the industry and the other to the type of product.

## Firm Size

The influence of firm size on IT governance arrangements has been viewed ambiguously in the literature. While a number of earlier studies did not find a significant relationship between firm size and the adoption of a particular IT governance pattern [8], later studies have substantiated such a relationship due to the increased need for coordinating mechanisms in complex organizational structures [10, 37, 46]. We assume that the firm size will be largely reflected in the mode of overall IT governance, rather than having a distinguishable impact on the application-level governance outcomes, but control for this influence.

## IT Organization Size

Given that we are investigating a boundary issue that specifically addresses internal business and IT unit decision rights, we also consider the size of the internal IT organization as a separate control variable. Since absolute size of the IT organization is collinear with overall firm size, we operationalize this parameter as the relative size (i.e., percentage of IT employees as total employees of the firm).

## Time

We include the time since implementation of the application as an additional control variable. IT governance is commonly viewed as a rather static concept that needs to be well designed and implemented in an organization via certain mechanisms. This contrasts with the dynamic development of organizations. It is plausible that the factors that influenced governance arrangements that were made at the time of initially implementing an application may have changed (i.e., by the time the survey is completed) due to a learning process from using an application [29]. Thus, the time variable controls for the distorting effect of time in postimplementation developments.

## Methodology

We first operat ionalized t he proposed researc h model in Figure 1 in a multistep procedure and then conducted a survey with IT decision makers in 207 large firms to test our hypotheses.

## Construct Operationalization and Scale Development

The items to measure the constructs were based on prior literature where possible (i.e., for IT governance [46], specificity [6], and IT knowledge in business units [3]). For others, the items built on the precursory case research findings (i.e., for decision authority, task responsibility, initiative, scope of use, and business knowledge in IT unit). For appropriate semantic granularity, business/IT dimensions were operationalized on five-point scales (business, business with IT involvement, business and IT equally, IT with business involvement, central IT) [10], and high/low dimensions were scaled on seven points. In line with our theoretical model, the IT governance construct was operationalized by formative items, that is, the three distinct decision domains described earlier that mutually define an application-level mode of IT governance appropriate for this study. All the remaining constructs are operationalized by reflective items. Application governance, the second-order construct, is operationalized using a repeated indicator approach, that is, by combining the indicators of its two first-order constructs: decision authority and task responsibility.

Several steps were taken to refine these measures as well as to assure reliability and validity. During this procedure, each construct was reduced to two or three items (two for seven-point and three for five-point scaled items). Since high-ranking IT managers were the targeted respondents, limiting total questionnaire length and reducing redundancy was a major goal. In a first step, a category sorting was performed with eight fellow researchers, that is, the items were shuffled and had to be sorted according to the construct definitions. Second, an online pretest was conducted with a total of 29 IT professionals from industry and consulting as well as experienced researchers who were requested to complete relevant sections for a company and two applications (SaaS and on-premise) of their choice. The pretest yielded acceptable reliability values (alpha > 0.8) for all the items and constructs except one (specificity), for which one item was subsequently rephrased. The entire final questionnaire was pretested in paperbased form in think-aloud meetings with three different chief information officers and resulted in only minor revisions regarding understandability and layout.

## Data Acquisition and Sample Characteristics

The survey for this study was conducted in conjunction with the German BIT Survey 2011.<sup>4</sup> We retrieved the addresses of German-based firms with more that <sup>€</sup>50 million of revenues and more than 500 employees (excluding the public sector) from a commercial publisher of company information. The focus on large companies is motivated by the assumption that we would find a greater variance in business/IT governance arrangements—as well as a higher sensitivity for this topic—in large firms [46]. In April 2011, formal invitations were sent out to 2,886 companies, including a paper version of the questionnaire, a return envelope, and a cover letter that included references to the project Web site and to an online version of the same questionnaire. As an incentive, we offered a small gadget for the first 100 respondents as well as the chance to win a tablet computer for all the participants. After a six-week response period and combined telephone/e-mail reminders, we received feedback from a total of 534 companies, of which 220 provided usable responses (60 percent online, 40 percent paper-based). Nonusable responses resulted from respondents who did not fully complete the online questionnaire (195), who were unable to participate because of corporate policies (90), and from returned letters due to unknown recipients (29). We attribute this moderate overall response rate (7.6 percent) to the total questionnaire length and to an increasing exposure of senior IT decision makers to research inquiries, which was also stated in some of the reminder calls. A further 13 records were removed because of missing or inconsistent values in the questions measuring the constructs in our research model. Detailed response and sample characteristics are also reported elsewhere [49].

The respondents reported being top-level information officers (20 percent), senior IT managers (47 percent), IT managers (20 percent), IT staff (3 percent), as well as chief executive officers (4 percent), senior business managers (3 percent), and business managers (3 percent) with a median of 11.5 years of work experience. Textual job descriptions revealed that respondents in business roles were chief operating officers (COOs), business unit IT managers, or in similar positions with IT responsibility; we decided not to remove these records from the sample and also identified no statistically significant bias.<sup>5</sup> The industry distribution (based on the German industry classification code) did not reveal a significant bias compared to the distribution of invited companies. The size distribution (measured by the number of employees from the external database versus those reported by the respondents) indicates that larger firms responded overproportionally to our inquiry. The mean number of employees is 4,780 (quartiles: 700, 1,300, 2,500; skewness: 6.7) and mean number of IT employees is 130 (quartiles: 7, 15, 35; skewness: 11.2).

After completing the questionnaire section on the overall IT organization, the participants were asked to provide information about one SaaS or one on-premise application of their choice that is relevant to their firm and that they are knowledgeable about. For the purpose of distinction, SaaS was defined as “any enterprise software that is deployed over the internet by an external provider and is accessed by users at different companies through the Web browser,” while an on-premise application was defined as “any enterprise software that is installed in a traditional way and run on computers belonging to the ‘premises’ of your company, i.e., this installation is only used by your company.” Out of the 207 respondents, 131 provided information about an on-premise application and 76 regarding a SaaS. The distribution between the SaaS and on-premise applications by common application types is presented in Table 2.

We assess discriminant validity by evaluating cross-loadings as well as the Fornell– Larcker criterion [11, p. 321]. First, the mean of absolute item-to-construct crossloadings is 0.143 with maximum values at 0.633 between DecAuth and TaskResp, thus below the critical value of 0.7. Since both constructs (DecAuth and TaskResp) represent dimensions of application governance, this correlation is in line with our theoretical model. Second, the Fornell–Larcker criterion, stating that construct-toconstruct correlations should be below the square root of the AVE (represented as diagonal elements in the matrix), is as well fulfilled for all first-order constructs (see Table 3b).

As for all self-reported data, it is recommended to assess a potential common method bias (CMB) due to the subjects’ tendency to give socially desirable and cognitively consistent answers [31]. Although social desirability may play a minor role when the dependent variable is criterion-free (e.g., application governance), we assess potential CMB in two ways: Harman’s one-factor test [31] as well as a latent method factor approach [29]. The first factor from an exploratory factor analysis on all the variables of the theoretical model accounts for 0.27 of the total variance (loading distinguishably on the items of application governance). This provides a preliminary objection against the existence of a single dominant method factor that would explain the majority of variance in the sample [31]. We also follow the procedure described by Liang et al. [29] and include a latent method factor in the PLS model that comprises all the model indicators potentially influencing the construct’s principal indicators.<sup>7</sup> CMB can be assessed by comparing the variance of model indicators explained by substantive constructs with the variance resulting from the method factor. Regarding our model, the average substantively explained variance is 0.726 while the average variance from the method factor is 0.013 (ratio 1:56). In addition, after bootstrapping, all the substantive loadings remain significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , while most of the path coefficients from the method factor are not significant. Altogether, these results indicate that the presence of a CMB is not a serious concern in this study.

## Model and Subgroup Tests

To test our association hypotheses (H1–H6), we first assess the structural model for all 207 organization-application pairs. Statistical significance of the parameter estimates is assessed based on one-tailed tests with pseudo t-values from separate bootstrapping procedures each using 1,000 resamples.<sup>8</sup> Given the exploratory nature of this research, we consider our association hypotheses to be supported if the path coefficients for the full sample are significant at the 0.10 level.

To test our differential hypotheses (ΔH1–ΔH6), we assess the structural model separately for the 131 firms providing information about on-premise applications and the 76 organizations using SaaS, and compare the path coefficients for the on-premise and SaaS models by two kinds of tests: a t-test based on the standard errors as described by Keil et al. [27] and an adapted Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon (MWW) test based on the bootstrap samples, provided by Henseler et al. [25]. Both tests produce congruent results (see Table 4). We consider differential hypotheses as supported for p-values below the 0.10 level. However, associations that are not significant at the 0.10 level will be discussed as weakly supported if they nominally exhibit the expected subgroup differences and p-values close to the 0.10 level.

## Limitations

Before discussing the results, several limitations of this study merit consideration. First, due to questionnaire length concerns, all but one antecedent was operationalized with two items. Although the criteria for convergent and discriminant validity were fulfilled, a semantically broader operationalization may have resulted in a higher predictive validity for some of these antecedents [18]. In addition, our cross-sectional data only ascertain association, not the causal relationships inherent in our theoretical arguments. Furthermore, a bias may result from a sample in which the respondents were requested to choose the specific application on which to provide information; the choices are summarized in Table 2. As a robustness test, we repeated our analyses with a reduced sample of identical “baskets” of on-premise and SaaS applications $( n _ { \mathrm { _ 1 } } = n _ { _ 2 } = 6 8 )$ . These results are largely congruent with the tests of the full sample,<sup>9</sup> suggesting that application endogeneity is not a major concern for interpreting our findings for on-premise versus SaaS applications.

## Discussion

This st udy w as mot iv at ed b y t he need t o reexamine the earlier rationales for IT governance in light of emerging delivery models that defy the focal role of IT units for managing IT delivery. We took a multitheoretic approach to develop a theoretically grounded research model that built on a precursory qualitative multicase study of SaaS application governance and utilized a two-dimensional conceptualization of the horizontal allocation of IT decision rights at the application level to address our two research questions. Addressing RQ1, we found strong evidence that origin of initiative, scope of use, and business knowledge in IT units are significantly associated with application-level governance (H2, H4, H6). Regarding RQ2, we provide evidence that the type of delivery model moderates the association with scope of use (ΔH4). We also found weak evidence to support the hypothesized differential effects for the three other antecedents at the organization level, that is, IT governance (ΔH1), IT knowledge of business units (ΔH5), and business knowledge of IT units (ΔH6). Collectively, our findings complement the extant governance literature at the IT-function level (e.g., [10, 37, 46]) and contribute to the recent literature that takes a more modular view of governance in different subfunctions (e.g., [7, 43, 45, 51]). Moreover, we extend the SaaS literature (e.g., [5, 6, 12, 16, 41, 42]) by examining the internal governance of SaaS-based applications.

Drawing on agency theory, we hypothesized the influence of not only overall IT governance (H1) but also the self-interest of individual actors (H2). We found a much stronger association between application-level governance and the origin of initiative (H2). This finding strengthens the arguments of Xue et al. [51]: while overall decision rights can be allocated by an organization a priori, IT governance patterns that emerge are also contingent on other factors. However, while Xue et al. [51] focused on a preimplementation context, our model bridges the predecision phase (initiative) and postimplementation (application governance). These results therefore suggest a historicity of decisions where postimplementation application governance is strongly determined by the opportunism of individual actors. This provides support for the usefulness of an agency theory lens, which emphasizes the role of self-interest, for investigating IT governance phenomena. However, since this effect was significant (and practically equal in strength) for both SaaS and on-premise applications, our results suggest that IT and business unit self-interests come into play for both delivery models.

Drawing on the TCE perspective, we theorized that allocations of application governance also result from the need to economize on production costs as well as transaction costs relevant to the application characteristics specificity and scope of use. Here, on the one hand, our finding is that the scope of use variable has a significant effect on application governance (H4). Our theoretical rationale for why applications used by a smaller number of units are governed with greater business decision rights is con gruent with Xue et al.’s [51] findings for the influence of functional scope as an IT investment characteristic. Our additional contribution is that we show that the rationale of scope-based effects is even stronger in the presence of external delivery models, where the production cost advantages accrue on the vendor side (ΔH4). Accordingly, the imperative to leverage internal economies of scale becomes a weaker argument for centralizing governance to a corporate IT unit for SaaS applications. Thus, while TCE has frequently been used to explain outsourcing arrangements between the provider and client (e.g., [28]), our study demonstrates that TCE provides a valuable lens for explaining differences in internal IT governance arrangements. On the other hand, our operationalization of application specificity as the degree to which an application is customized to an organization and its context did not result in the hypothesized effects (H3). Assuming that applications (and application types) differ greatly in their degree of specificity [6] and that SaaS applications in general have a lower degree of specificity (as found in our sample shown in Table 4), this is a construct that requires additional research.

Drawing on the KBV perspective, we hypothesized that the extent of IT knowledge in business units and the extent of business knowledge in IT units are additional organization-level variables that influence the allocation of decision rights at the application level. However, we found strong evidence for the importance of business knowledge of IT units only (H6). In other words, greater business knowledge of IT employees is associated with greater IT unit application-level governance, and conversely, lower business knowledge is more likely to result in greater allocations of decision rights to business units. Although this relationship has been emphasized in the broader IS literature [2, 34], it has not been emphasized in the literature on the governance of the IT function (e.g., [10, 37]).

In turn, our results do not provide evidence that the IT knowledge in business units is associated with application-level governance (H5). However, we did find some preliminary support for our KBV arguments for both of our differential hypotheses—that is, the reduced need for IT knowledge in business units for SaaS application governance in business units and an increased need for business knowledge in IT units for SaaS application governance in IT units (ΔH5, ΔH6). Our findings therefore suggest that business competencies in IT units are even more important for IT units that seek to have decision rights for SaaS applications. This particular finding is consistent with the stream of IT outsourcing research that has stressed the need to build specific business competencies in IT units when managing IT outsourcing relationships, including business relationship building and business systems thinking skills [47].

## Theoretical Implications

This study has several theoretical implications for IT governance research. First, we demonstrate support for a conceptualization of application-level governance that comprises both decision control rights (decision authority) and decision management rights (task responsibility). While the IT governance literature at the IT function level has largely focused on decision control rights (e.g., [10, 37, 46]), this conceptual separation has also been applied for other subfunction governance contexts such as IT system development [43].

Second, this study employed a multitheoretical approach that applied three reference theories that have previously been used by management researchers to explain boundary choices. The empirical evidence for antecedents that are argued from the perspective of these reference theories (origin of initiative, scope of use, and business knowledge) provides support for this approach. The findings of this study corroborate that agency theory is a lens that explains not only organizational boundary issues for functional governance in general [14] but also for application-level governance. In addition, the TCE perspective helps to explain why an application characteristic such as scope of use can be influential for application-level governance choices and thus also correlates with the origin of initiative. Complementary to that, the KBV sheds light on the required capabilities of an IT unit or a business unit to govern at the application level. Nevertheless, we recognize that future research may also consider alternative theories to explain the application-level governance phenomenon.

Finally, our study contributes to a theoretically grounded IT governance theory at a more modular level and helps to explain application-specific arrangements that may deviate from overall IT governance schemes. Consequently, we seek to contribute to research that brings the notion of IT governance from a “monolithic” understanding at the IT function level [38, p. 108] toward a more modular view that explains appropriate arrangements for different IT activities and artifacts at a subfunction level.

## Managerial Implications

This study has implications for managers faced with establishing governance arrangements in diverse application contexts (e.g., SaaS and on-premise). Deviations from IT-centric overall governance schemes appear appropriate especially when an application’s scope of use is low and it is delivered via an SaaS model. For organizations that wish to prevent governance “drifts” (or possibly “shadow IT”), IT leaders should focus on improving the business competencies of their IT professionals and promote horizontal mechanisms that better integrate IT department decision makers with potential initiators and other stakeholders in the business units [9, 15]. Altogether, this study may therefore help practitioners to assess the potential effects that emerging delivery models such as SaaS may have on their existing IT governance arrangements.

## Implications for Future Research

We believe there are several promising ways that future research can build on the findings of this study. First, given the strong influence on application governance due to the origin of the application initiative, research is needed to shed more light on the relationships between this construct, a priori IT governance schemes, and postimplementation IT governance arrangements at the application level. Longitudinal and process-theoretic approaches appear to be especially appropriate for addressing these issues. Second, the lack of support for the application specificity antecedent deserves further investigation, given its potential theoretical importance. Especially fruitful here would be research designed to examine potential mediating characteristics of IT artifact properties that help to explain the influence of this construct on applicationlevel governance for different application types. Finally, our theoretical model did not include a criterion variable. Future research is therefore needed to develop normative models for application governance that build on the theoretical grounding and antecedent findings offered by this study and other related research.
