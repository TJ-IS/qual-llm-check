# The effect of social capital on community loyalty in a virtual community: Test of a tripartite-process model

Cheng-Chieh Hsiao <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Jyh-Shen Chiou

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Administration, National Chengchi University, No. 64, Sec. 2, Zhi-nan Road, Wenshan, Taipei 11605, Taiwan Department of International Business, National Chengchi University, No. 64, Sec. 2, Zhi-nan Road, Wenshan, Taipei 11605, Taiwan

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 8 April 2011 Received in revised form 10 July 2012 Accepted 5 September 2012 Available online 13 September 2012

Keywords: Social capital Online community Norm Trust Resource Utility Massive multiplayer online game

## a b s t r a c t

Drawing upon social capital theory and social exchange theory, this study attempts to elaborate the effect of social capital on community loyalty in a virtual community by proposing and assessing a tripartite-process model. Online gaming communities were selected as the research context for testing the model. After collecting 347 usable responses from a globally leading Massive Multiplayer Online Game (MMOG), this study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that a player's social capital in an online gaming community affects his or her community loyalty through normative, relational, and utilitarian processes. The results also show that resource accessibility has a positive impact on perceived game utility, which in turn affects community loyalty. Finally, we conclude with research limitations and theoretical and managerial implications.

## 1. Introduction

The Pew Internet and American Life Project reported that almost 84% of U.S. Internet users belong to certain online communities [65]. An online community is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an online group of people who interact with each other, share norms of behavior, and even act in a collective way [26,43,53]. Past research has indicated that online communities have impacts on Internet users' activities, such as social [36,31,85], knowledge [12,39,84,87], consumption [44], and gaming [20,21,33]. Recently, online service providers have created virtual communities to strengthen inter-customer exchange with the intent of increasing individual transactions [15,44]; customer loyalty toward the communities may affect the revenues of these providers.

Online communities create a new context for an individual to develop and accumulate social capital [65,86]. Although free riding appears to be a problem in virtual communities, online social capital serves as an intangible force that binds members together and increases their commitment to the community [51]. Social capital encompasses any aspect of a social structure that generates valuable consequences and facilitates an individual's actions within that social network [13]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, an actor's structural social capital is associated with either utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>t as resource accessibility [46,70] or relational affect as interpersonal trust [34,54]. To maintain social capital, an actor in a social network is likely to engage in resource and relational exchange with other members [1]. Relational exchange often contains both affective and normative aspects [13,54]. Such distinction is important to link social capital research to recent virtual community studies [7,19]. However, researchers have paid more attention to resource bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than relational outcomes [70,80]. In addition, past online community studies have examined the direct effects of social capital on users' behaviors, including knowledge sharing [12,84,87], the use of social networking websites [24,77], community participation [31], and Wikipedians' performance [59]. Relatively few studies have tested the relationships between online social capital and its consequences that lead to an individual's community loyalty. The distinction between the sources and consequences of social capital is consistent with recent advances in social capital research [1,54,70].

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to develop and examine a tripartite-process model of how an individual's social capital affects his or her community loyalty. Drawing upon social capital theory and social exchange theory, we differentiate the normative, relational, and utilitarian processes of social capital on community loyalty. We expect that these processes mediate the relationship between social capital and community loyalty. We investigate this with empirical data collected from online gaming communities. Online gaming communities are fundamental social units in the context of a Massive Multiplayer Online Game (MMOG). With the rapid growth of the MMOG market, the development of online gaming communities will strongly determine the success of an MMOG company.

## 2. Research setting

This study evaluates the proposed model in an emerging and dynamic virtual community context - that is, online gaming communities. In the world of MMOGs, an online gaming community is often called a “guild,” which refers to a social system in which a player's important relationships are formed and frame his or her social life [20,21,40,69]. Although individuals can play alone in an MMOG, a number of dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks and challenges are designed for team play [88]. Players tend to develop long-term guilds to assemble team members and undertake complex adventures ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. In addition, players can obtain other advantages from their guilds, such as meeting new friends, exchanging resources, and receiving social support. With these positive expectations, most MMOG players have a guild membership [20,90] and often engage in community activities.

MMOGs have three characteristics of an online gaming community. First, the character that a player assumes in an MMOG can belong to only one guild at one time. Membership exclusiveness allows players to interact and exchange more often with guildmates than with non-af<sup>fi</sup>liated players. Second, each guild has its own text-based chat channel in MMOGs. Collective norms and actions can be formed easily via the guild channel. Third, a player has his or her own guild rank. Guild rank, which represents the player's position in a guild, affects the power to allocate guild resources and recruit new members [55].

According to Nielson Entertainment's [56] report, active game players spend more than 5 hours a week playing games socially. As the social element of online gaming has become signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, the importance of online gaming communities has started to draw scholars' attention [20,21,33,49,69,88]. Guilds manifest the vitality of online social dynamics, as they help each player develop social capital and facilitate social exchange in MMOGs. Therefore, online gaming communities present an excellent context in which to study virtual community behavior.

## 3. Theoretical framework and hypotheses

Our conceptual framework describes the indicators and effects of social capital in an online gaming community. The model draws upon social capital theory [1,13,35,54] and social exchange theory [9,37,78]. As shown in Fig. 1, individual centrality and social interaction ties serve as two indicators of social capital in a guild. Social capital has positive impacts on a player's community loyalty (i.e., intention to stay in the guild) through perceived social norms, perceived community trust, and resource accessibility. Finally, since game utility is one of the major reasons that a player stays with an online game [38,45], the partial mediation role of perceived game utility of an MMOG is also examined in our research.

## 3.1. Social capital in a virtual community

Coleman [13] suggested that “social capital is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by its function. It is not a single entity but a variety of different entities, with two elements in common: they all consist of some aspect of social structure, and they facilitate certain actions of actors within the structure.” Nahapiet and Ghoshal [54] suggested that social capital exists within networks of relationships that are possessed by an individual or social unit

This study de<sup>fi</sup>nes social capital as a player's structural properties in a virtual community. We focus on structural social capital in a guild for two reasons. First, social capital theory is concentrated mainly on how the social structure of an entity serves as a productive resource [13,54]. Structural capital re<sup>fl</sup>ects the fundamental form of social capital. Second, Coleman [13] and Granovetter [34] suggested that structural embeddedness can generate other aspects of social capital. This study attempts to extend online social capital literature by examining the effect of structural properties on relational capital in online gaming communities.

This study conceptualizes social capital as a high-order representation of individual centrality and social interaction ties in a virtual community. Individual centrality refers to the extent to which a focal individual is connected to others in a network [2,73,84], whereas social interaction ties represent the frequency of participation in community activities, the amount of time spent, and the frequency of interaction with members [12]. These constructs have been used as structural social capital in past studies [12,42,73,75,79,80,84].

3.2. Integrating social capital into social exchange theory: toward a tripartite-process model

Furthermore, the concept of social capital covers not only its sources, but also its consequences that facilitate certain actions of an individual [1,51]. This study elaborates the impact of social capital on community loyalty by incorporating social capital theory into social exchange theory. Blau [9] de<sup>fi</sup>ned social exchange as a process of central signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in social life and underlying the relationships between individuals and groups. We argue that social capital theory and social exchange theory are not mutually exclusive, but can be integrated together. Social capital theory indicates the structural antecedents of individual actions [13], whereas social exchange theory represents a major perspective for explaining the mechanisms of social behavior [9,25,78]. The social exchange perspective has also been implied in social capital studies [11,13].

Social capital theory indicates that an actor's structural social capital may result in relational affects [34,54] and utilitarian bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [70]. Lin [46] echoes that an individual's structural advantage can result in the success of expressive and instrumental actions, which are similar to relational and utilitarian outcomes of social capital. In a similar vein, social exchange theory suggests that an actor's social life often involves relational and resource exchanges [16]. Furthermore, intense social exchange will not only create an actor's social capital in a social entity [34,80], but also lead to his or her compliance to norms that describe how the actor feels obligated to behave reciprocally [9,13]. Such normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence has been distinguished from the psychological affect in past studies of social capital [13,54] and virtual community [19]. Thus, this study regards the normative, relational, and utilitarian consequences as three theoretically distinct aspects that characterize one's social exchange in a virtual community adequately.

Drawing upon the literature related to social capital [1,13,70,80], social exchange [9,16,25,78], and virtual community [7,19], perceived social norms, perceived community trust, and resource accessibility are hypothesized to mediate the relationship between social capital and community loyalty. Three mediating relationships constitute the normative, relational, and utilitarian processes of social capital in our model. Since the intensity of social ties to others may change over time, an actor needs to maintain his or her social capital continuously in pursuit of desirable consequences [1], leading to the actor's retention in a community. Next, we develop hypotheses for testing of the tripartite-process model.

3.3. The normative process: Social capital → perceived social norms→ community loyalty

This study de<sup>fi</sup>nes perceived social norms as subjective perceptions of an online gaming community's preferences about one's engagement in a behavior [22,27,78]. Social capital derived from intense interaction among members fosters the development of community norms [37,67]. Social exchange theory also suggests that frequent social exchange leads to some norms that depict how an individual should act and be obligated to behave reciprocally [9,16]. Since an online community is often characterized by a socially expected mode of conduct [43], an individual with strong social embeddedness in an online community may tend to abide by community norms to meet the community's expectations.

An important part of community norms is normative community pressure [27,78], which refers to perceived extrinsic demands of a community on an individual to interact and cooperate within the community [3]. Community norms that result from close social interaction may have a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on collective performance and simultaneously restrain members' openness to behave in alternative ways [54]. In MMOGs, players with strong ties to guildmates are more likely than others to develop a habit of cooperation in a guild [50]. To maintain the magnitude of their social capital, they prefer to <sup>fi</sup>t in with the community and go online to participate in scheduled collective activities rather than doing other things [9]. Hence,

H1. Social capital in a guild relates positively to a player's perceived social norms.

As Postmes et al. [63] suggested, social norms have a strong in<sup>fl</sup>uence on online behaviors in computer-mediated communities. These norms often relate directly to members' behavioral intentions toward the community [22,27]. Social exchange theory indicates that members who abide by social norms are likely to receive social rewards and avoid undesirable punishment from the social entity [9,37]. Those members who are rewarded socially are more likely to stay in the community. As a result, we suggest that conforming to community norms enhances a player's attainment of favorable outcomes from his or her af<sup>fi</sup>liation, leading to positive intention to remain in the guild. Thus,

H2. Perceived social norms relate positively to a player's intention to stay in the guild.

3.4. The relational process: Social capital→perceived community trust→community loyalty

Rousseau et al. [68] reviewed different conceptualizations and proposed a consensus de<sup>fi</sup>nition of trust as “a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations of the intentions or behaviors of another.” Singh and Sirdeshmukh [72] indicated that the expectation conceptualization of trust focuses on an individual's beliefs that the exchange partner would behave in a manner that is responsible, exhibits integrity, and is not potentially hazardous. Social exchange theory identi<sup>fi</sup>es trust as an essential affect of stable and favorable social exchange [9]. Because there is no guarantee to assure an appropriate return for a favor, social exchange requires mutual trust that each party will ful<sup>fi</sup>ll unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed obligations and not act opportunistically [9].

Social capital theory suggests that an actor's community trust comes from intense network closure [34,54,64]. However, trust must be cultivated steadily because it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to establish but easy to destroy. Thus, an actor needs to maintain his or her social capital by frequently interacting and cooperating with other members of an online community. The mutual trusting relationship becomes more solid when two members interact over time in a network [30,80]. The actor with greater social capital is more likely to express trust toward others who will reciprocate in proper fashion. Therefore,

H3. Social capital in a guild relates positively to a player's perceived community trust.

Social exchange involves more or less uncertainty and risk [9]. It is especially obvious in MMOGs because in-game transactions involve the increase and decrease of a player's virtual assets, which sometimes relate to real-world economic values. Since players are often anonymous and self-interested, a player's transient exchange with strangers is often risky [48]. The player tends to exchange repeatedly with guildmates as a means of reducing risk [14]. The intensity of within-community relationships can generate a player's feelings of trust, which lowers the fear of uncertainty that guild members may act opportunistically [10,34]. Therefore, community trust can enhance an individual's intention to stay in a community for future interaction with other members [9,84]. Past research has also recognized trust as an important determinant of user activities in virtual communities [12,39,66]. Hence,

H4. Perceived community trust relates positively to a player's inten tion to stay in the guild.

3.5. The utilitarian process: social capital→ resource accessibility → community loyalty

Lin et al. [47] noted that the nature of resources is embedded within a network. Social capital theory suggests that an actor's network closure within a social system can create greater access to resources for the actor [13,54,70,73,80]. Previous research supports that network resource exchange is affected by individual centrality [73]. On the other hand, resource exchange is also regarded as a part of social exchange [9,16]. Since resources are obtained mostly through interpersonal encounters [28], an actor with strong ties to other members tends to attain more resources in a social network [13,54]. In an MMOG, a player acquires many skills, capabilities, and objects in his or her journey in game adventures. A guild can foster the accumulation and exchange of tangible and intangible resources that each member owns. A central member with strong bonds to guildmates is more likely to allocate and use these resources in the guild. Thus,

H5. Social capital in a guild relates positively to a player's resource accessibility.

Social exchange theory suggests that an individual engages in social interaction due to the expectations of social reputation [9]. Resource accessibility enables the actor to increase his or her reputation in an organization [41,81]. Therefore, it is expected that a player who has greater access to virtual game resources is more likely to receive a higher degree of social power and sense of achievement in his or her community [91]. To maintain these desirable feelings, the player's intention to stay in the community should be positive. Therefore,

H6. Resource accessibility relates positively to a player's intention to stay in the guild.

## 3.6. Other variable: Perceived game utility

A major determinant of behavior is subjective expected utility [23,27], which refers to the degree to which favorable or unfavorable outcomes are expected from a behavior [8]. In this study, subjective expected utility is manifested as the construct of perceived game utility of an MMOG. A player's perceived game utility may depend primarily on whether the MMOG can help the player pursue entertainment goals ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and quickly [38]. Social capital theory suggests that an actor's resource accessibility in<sup>fl</sup>uences his or her success in a career [47,70]. Since most bene<sup>fi</sup>cial resources are related to the capabilities of the role, a player's resource accessibility ensures his or her self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in conquering high-level challenges [32]. Thus, greater resource accessibility from a guild helps a player succeed in these challenges, maximizing the utility of playing an MMOG for fun and entertainment. Hence,

H7. Resource accessibility in a guild relates positively to a player's perceived game utility.

Perceived game utility is similar to perceived usefulness of MMOG services from the perspective of the technology acceptance model [17,83]. Perceived usefulness refers to the extent to which one believes that the use of a system will improve one's performance [17,83]. Prior studies have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the positive relationship between perceived usefulness and behavioral intention to play online games [38,45]. As playing an MMOG can help a member achieve the goal of fun and enjoyment, the player is more likely to stay in the guild to play the MMOG with other members. Thus,

H8. Perceived game utility relates positively to a player's intention to stay in the guild.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data collection

The research setting in this study is online gaming communities located in MMOGs. The MMOG market is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant importance. Wu [89] indicated that the global MMOG market generated 6 billion US dollars in 2010, and market revenues are expected to reach 8 billion US dollars in 2014. Asian MMOG companies are considered a strong and emerging force in the online gaming industry [89]. In 2010, an important MMOG market in Southeast Asia is Taiwan [57], where MMOGs also represent the largest segment of the online game market [60]. Thus, we targeted the most popular MMOG in Taiwan, World of Warcraft (WOW), which has more than 300,000 players.

We posted a recruitment statement on three WOW-related online forums to invite voluntary participants. Online recruitment is effective because WOW players often visit online game forums to search and share game information. Players who had frequent playing experience with a game role in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c guild during the most recent two months were invited to participate in the survey via a link to our survey website. At the website, participants were asked to identify the titles of the role and its guild and then to <sup>fi</sup>ll out the electronic questionnaire accordingly. The database saved participants' responses automatically when their questionnaires were completed.

More than 1,000 visitors came to our website, and 392 respondents tried to answer the questionnaire. We dropped 45 invalid questionnaires and obtained 347 usable responses in total. All of these respondents quali<sup>fi</sup>ed for data analysis after veri<sup>fi</sup>cation of their information. We further recorded the actual community size for each respondent from the WOW system. Our respondents' communities varied in size from 10 members to 1,145 members. The average guild had 232.61 members (s.d.=159.30).

## 4.2. Measurement

For most of the constructs considered, established scales were used and modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to accommodate the research context. All items measured are shown in the Appendix A. In this study, guild social capital was represented as a high-order construct of individual centrality [2,84] and social interaction ties [12,80]. First, we conducted in-depth interviews with <sup>fi</sup>ve WOW players to develop a four-item scale for individual centrality. We used 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) to measure the responses. The preliminary scale was pretested with 36 players, and it achieved excellent internal consistency (alpha=.92). For social interaction ties, three items were adapted from Tsai and Ghoshal [80] and Chiu et al. [12]. The 7-point scales were anchored by 1 (extremely low) and 7 (extremely high).

Furthermore, objective data of guild rank were collected from each respondent to cross validate the self-reported scale of guild social capital. As we mentioned previously, guild rank refers to one's position in his or her guild (see http://www.wowtaiwan.tw). In our target MMOG, a player's guild rank ranged from 1 to 10, where a smaller rank number implies a more central position in the guild. If there were a high and negative correlation between the subjective and objective data, it would indicate that our self-reported scale appears to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent of a player's social capital in a guild. The analysis showed that the Pearson's correlation between social capital and guild rank was moderately negative (r=−.48, pb.001). Thus, our self-reported measurement was adequate to capture the concept of social capital in a guild.

With respect to other measures, three items for measuring perceived social norms were taken from Algesheimer et al. [3] and Shen and Chiou [71]. Three items for resource accessibility were adapted from Spreitzer [74], whereas perceived community trust was assessed with items modi<sup>fi</sup>ed from Tsai and Ghoshal [80] and Chiu et al. [12]. Items for measuring perceived game utility were based on Babin et al. [6] and Hsu and Lu [38]. The scale of community loyalty was adapted from Fishbein and Ajzen [27] and Shen and Chiou [71] to assess a player's intention to stay in the guild. All scales were anchored on 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

## 4.3. Data analysis

The two-step analysis procedure recommended by Anderson and Gerbing [4] was used in this study. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) was <sup>fi</sup>rst performed to assess the goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t, composite reliability, and construct validity in the measurement model. Then, the full structural equation model was evaluated to test our hypotheses. Each model was estimated by using Amos 5.0 [5].

## 6. Discussion and implications

## 6.1. Summary of this study

The current study provides strong support for our tripartiteprocess model of social capital in a sample of MMOG players. Social capital in an online gaming community has indirect impacts on community loyalty through the normative, relational, and utilitarian processes. In the normative process, the results showed that social capital in a virtual community affects one's perceived social norms, which increases one's intention to stay in the community. This is consistent with previous virtual community research [19]. However, one brand community study found that the normative in<sup>fl</sup>uence has no signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant impact on member continuance intention [3]. Our study differs from this previous research. In an MMOG environment, some dif<sup>fi</sup>cult challenges may carry a limitation in the number of available team members. To complete these challenges, a player may have no choice but to join and behave as the community expects, even though he or she is sometimes unwilling to participate in collective activities.

In the relational process, this study supported the notion that an actor's strong embeddedness within a virtual network creates a sense of social trust and con<sup>fi</sup>dence that enhances his or her social behaviors in the community. Community trust can effectively reduce the fear that members will engage in opportunistic behavior [9]. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in line with prior studies that have ascertained a positive relationship between member's trust and loyalty activities, such as knowledge sharing, in a virtual community [12,39,66]. Additional analysis showed that the indirect effect of social capital on community loyalty through perceived community trust is higher than that through perceived social norms and resource accessibility.

The relational process of social capital appears to be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential in online gaming communities.

In the utilitarian process, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that social capital in a virtual community has a positive impact on one's resource accessibility, which in turn improves one's loyalty toward the community. In an MMOG, online gaming communities act as repositories of resources that are available to each member, including virtual money, valuable objects, member support, and services. The more social capital an actor develops in a community, the more resources the actor can reach and use, and the longer the actor intends to stay in the community. Furthermore, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the partially mediating role of perceived game utility between resource accessibility and community loyalty. Similar to social capital research [47,70], we suggest that resource accessibility helps a player succeed in dif<sup>fi</sup>cult tasks. It generates a positive perception of game utility, affecting his or her retention in a guild.

## 6.2. Theoretical implications

Our tripartite-process model provides a holistic framework for understanding and explaining the indirect linkage of online social capital and community loyalty. This study contributes to social capital and virtual community research in several ways. First, our results showed that individual centrality and social interaction ties are well developed and validated to capture our conceptualization of social capital. For some virtual communities, user information is largely protected by online service providers. Collecting objective data that represent social capital in an online community appears to be methodologically dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Our subjective approach may help future researchers probe the effects of social capital on member activities in other online communities.

Next, previous research has argued that there might be a relationship between structural and relational dimensions of social capital [35,59,86]; however, relatively few studies have examined such relationship, especially in the context of online communities. This study con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that a player's structural social capital positively affects his or her perceived social norms and community trust, leading to community loyalty. The results also provided support for the mediating roles of these normative and relational elements pertaining to online social capital.

Third, we extend social capital literature by distinguishing the normative and relational in<sup>fl</sup>uences based on recent developments in online community research [19]. There might be different structural determinants for community norms and relational affect. Thus, our distinction appears to be contributory for future research to identify and examine other types of sociological antecedents that relate to social capital.

Finally, our results indicated that the effective integration of social capital theory and social exchange theory provides a structural view to explain a player's social exchange in an online gaming community. As social aspects of the virtual world become important, we recommend that future research incorporate social capital into other theories to probe more sociological processes of online community loyalty.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

The results of our study provide strong reasons for operators of MMOGs to promote their members to accumulate social capital in a virtual community. Online social capital can create high degrees of perceived social norms, perceived community trust, and resource accessibility that will in turn secure members' loyalty toward the virtual community. To effectively activate the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of social capital, detailed operation of the factors in the three processes should be continuously examined by the operator.

First, perceived social norms develop primarily through members strong connections to others in a virtual community. MMOG companies need to encourage members' social interactions with each other. We suggest that social networking communities may be created outside the game for each community. The out-of-game communities are helpful for members to strengthen guild-based social capital and their social relationships, as well as to deepen collective consensus and sense of community. This implication is consistent with recent developments in social games that attempt to connect player interactions with social networking communities (e.g., Facebook) out of the games.

Second, this study recommends practices that should be implemented to increase an actor's expectations and perceptions that other guild members will not act opportunistically and create potential hazards. Since perceived trust is a type of psychological and intangible property, virtual community managers can establish a trust-rating system. An actor's credibility, reliability, and trustworthiness could be rated or provided by other members who frequently exchange with the focal actor. These ratings could be displayed and reviewed publically in an online community. When an actor observes that members' ratings of trust in a community are high, the actor's perceived community trust will improve. The actor is more likely to interact with these members and stay in the community.

Third, resource accessibility from an online community has positive impacts on a member's perceived utility and community loyalty. In general, an individual's motivation to share and exchange valuable resources appears to be weak. There is a need for managers to establish adequate mechanisms to activate resource exchanges within online communities. Since incentive and reward are considered an extrinsic motivation to share knowledge in virtual communities [84], we suggest that offering community incentives and rewards may be a helpful way to increase a player's willingness to share and exchange his or her resources with guildmates. Another suggestion for MMOG companies is that in-game tasks of resource exchange be designed for a player to complete in his or her guild. These tasks would lead to a higher level of game utility, positively affecting the player's retention in the community.

## 6.4. Limitations

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings should be interpreted against several limitations of this study. First, social capital was measured subjectively by players in this study. Although this approach allowed us to evaluate construct reliability and validity, future research could collect objective data from online gaming communities to capture a player's structural properties of social capital. A second limitation is that this study was based on cross-sectional data. Since a virtual community is often dynamic and evolving, the ongoing relationships between social capital and its outcomes may change over time. A longitudinal design is recommended to test the evolution of social capital in a virtual community. A third limitation is that WOW was selected as the target MMOG due to its representativeness in Taiwan. However, the generalization of our results may be limited. Future research could collect data from different types of MMOGs to assess the external validity of our tripartite-process model.
