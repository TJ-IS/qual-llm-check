# From ideals towards practice: paradigmatic mismatches and drifts in method deployment

Tero Päivärinta,\* Maung K. Sein\* & Tuomo Peltola<sup>†</sup>

\*Department of Information Systems, Agder University College, Kristiansand, Norway, email: tero.paivarinta@hia.no, maung.k.sein@hia.no, and <sup>†</sup>SysOpen Digia Plc, Jyväskylä, Finland, email: tuomo.peltola@sysopendigia.com

Abstract. There is considerable debate in the information systems literature on how systems development methods (SDMs) are used in practice. On one side are those who take the position that these methods are not used at all. The other side posits that SDMs are used but not in the manner intended by the method devel opers. In practice, SDMs are adapted and modified to meet project exigencies, which results in unique methods-in-use in each project. We subscribe to the latter view and extend the argument by proposing that SDM modifications happen due to mismatches between the paradigmatic values inherent in the SDM, the method users and the organizational context. Further, planned modifications themselves result in shifts of paradigmatic values inherent in the SDM. To examine our contention, we conduct a case study in which we trace an SDM from its develop ment to its deployment and use in an organization. We show where the mismatches occurred and provide explanations for the mismatches. Our results indicate that paradigm differences explain most of the mismatches, and different factors contribute to the paradigm drifts simultaneously, even towards diverging orientations. We conclude that the true value of an SDM, in addition to its tool and technique use, is a basis for examining and self-reflecting about paradigmatic values. This is an essential part of learning to develop systems.

Keywords: information systems development, methodology, practice, method deployment, method use, ISD paradigm

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The use of systems development methods (SDMs) in practice has remained a much debated issue in the information systems (IS) literature. On the one hand, there are those who argue that methodologies should be rigorously defined, applied to practice as intended by the method developers, and systematically improved (Humphrey et al., 1991; Jarke et al., 1994). Whether this happens in practice is questionable. The body of empirical research that has studied systems development in practice tends to indicate that method use is far from normative. For a variety of reasons, systems developers either change aspects of the SDM, or pretend to use it (Lee & Truex, 1997; Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003). The role of SDMs is thus relegated to a means of ‘social defence’ (Truex, 1999) or a way to teach students (Mathiassen et al., 1996) or as a framework for use of tools and techniques (Fitzgerald, 1998). In fact, Avison & Fitzgerald (2003) believe we are in a ‘post-methodology era’.

The other side of the debate posits that SDMs are not used at all. Russo et al. (cited in Fitzgerald, 1998) found that only 6% of developers followed SDMs rigorously. This finding was also observed by Avison & Fitzgerald (2003). Other scholars take an even stronger position; they see SDMs as actually hampering the systems development process. They argue that SDMs are inflexible and hinder creativity (Veldwijk et al., 1993; Introna & Whitley, 1997; Whitley, 1998). Some have gone to the extent of advocating a more holistic and idiographic form of systems development that completely ignores SDMs – the so-called ‘amethodical systems development (Truex et al., 2000). This approach is ‘nonlinear, opportunistic, creative and pragmatic’ (Lee & Truex, 1997), and is in contrast to formal methods that have prescribed sequences of steps, sets of rules, techniques, tools and procedures and are well documented (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003).

We prescribe to a third perspective that falls between the two extremes. Our position is that SDMs are used in practice but not necessarily as intended by the method developers. These methods undergo a great extent of adaption as they are used (Stolterman, 1992; Fitzgerald, 1998). This position is not new. Empirical research has shown repeatedly that changes in SDMs emerge during use for pragmatic reasons and to meet project exigencies. No SDM can meet the contingencies of every project (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003). Such contingencies can be stakeholders’ contextual preferences and skills on method use, previous skills and IS development (ISD) approach held by the professional developers, industry standards on particular domains, and historical reasons in the organization where the system is being developed (Fitzgerald, 1997; Fitzgerald et al., 2003; Goulielmos, 2004). Proponents of situation-specific method engineering (Kumar & Welke, 1992; Brinkkemper et al., 1996; Tolvanen, 1998) recognize this pragmatism and propose systematic ways of utilizing and improving SDMs in context with method engineering tools.

We argue that it is not enough to merely recognize that SDMs undergo changes in use. Echoing Ørvik et al. (1999), we take the position that in addition to planned changes in SDMs done through method engineering, these methods also undergo changes due to mismatches of deep-rooted paradigmatic values between and within the various actors involved in systems development (SDM developers, systems developers and users) and the context (organization). Such paradigmatic mismatches affect the SDM adaption in two ways. First, any planned and conscious SDM adaption is influenced by the paradigmatic values of the entities involved. Second, any adaption itself results in shifts of paradigmatic values inherent in the SDM.

To study these issues, we conducted a case study of the deployment of a method called genre-based method (GBM) (Karjalainen et al., 2000; Päivärinta et al., 2001) by an organization, which provided consultancy and implementation services for developing document management systems together with their customers. We chose GBM because the method developers had actually described their original paradigmatic standpoints in the literature, which is rarely performed (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003). In addition, the method had been adopted into practice independent of the original method developers. We thus traced the change in the SDM as it moved from the development of the SDM in an academic research project to its actual use and evolution in a consulting organization. The model developed by Ørvik et al. (1999) was used to demonstrate that the changes that occur due to various reasons, such as change in organizational objectives, responding to external changes, or introduction of new equipment, all result in paradigmatic mismatches and lead to a paradigm shift.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we present the adaption model developed by Ørvik et al. (1999). In section 3, we describe the case study. In section 4, we present our findings. We discuss them in section 5 and suggest implications for practice and research. We conclude the paper in section 6 with a reflection on the role of SDM in today’s context.

## 2. SDM PARADIGMS AND PARADIGMATIC MISMATCHES

The SDM versions occur because of mismatches (Ørvik et al., 1999). These are the paradigmatic incompatibilities between and within the three main entities. Ørvik et al. (1999) term the differences between the manifest paradigmatic assumptions in the different versions of the SDM as premise mismatch. Mismatches 1–4 (Figure 2) fall into this category. Mismatch 1 occurs in the construction process if an SDM constructor explicitly uses an underlying SDM paradigm. Specific steps and procedures of the described SDM should then be designed according to the underlying paradigm and to support its core concepts. However, the procedures stated in the SDM may not reflect the paradigmatic assumptions, or two or more conflicting paradigms may be manifest based on paradoxical procedures. Mismatch 2 occurs between described SDM and understood SDM during the interpretation process. Mismatch 3 occurs between understood SDM and adapted SDM, while mismatch 4 occurs between the adapted SDM and SDM in use.

Ørvik et al. (1999) term the mismatch between paradigmatic assumptions inherent in an SDM and the paradigmatic beliefs and assumptions of the actors and that of the organization as context mismatch. These are mismatches 5 through 13 (Figure 2). Mismatches 5 and 6 occur between the SDM and the adapter/user. This happens during the interpretation process if the SDM adapter does not prescribe to the paradigmatic assumptions manifest in the described SDM or as he/she understands them to be. Mismatch 7 occurs during the adaption process, either consciously or unconsciously, on the part of the SDM adapter. The adaption process may have resulted in the adapted SDM being radically different in its underlying assumptions from the understood SDM. The same argument applies to mismatch 8, which occurs during the use process.

Mismatches 9–12 are mismatches between the paradigms manifest in the different versions of SDM and the paradigmatic values and beliefs prevalent in the organizational setting where the SDM is deployed. For example, an organization can be very mechanistic and bureaucratic in nature in contrast to an SDM which might focus on socio-technical innovations. Mismatch 13 reflects the mismatch between the paradigmatic values of the SDM adapter/user and those prevailing in the organizational setting. This is independent of the SDM deployment process and thus can occur at any stage. Table 1 summarizes the mismatches.

We used this model to understand how an SDM changed in our case organization. In doing so, we were aware of the caveat of Ørvik et al. (1999) that the two types of mismatches identified above can influence each other; that is, context mismatches may result in premise mismatches.

To analyse the paradigmatic mismatches, we need to be able to contrast different paradigmatic standpoints in a meaningful way. For this purpose, we use the framework proposed by Hirschheim & Klein (1989) that builds upon Burrell & Morgan’s (1979) work on the role of sociological paradigms in organizational analysis. Four different paradigmatic categories of ISD approaches and methodologies can be identified, namely, the functionalist, social relativist, radical structuralist and neo-humanist paradigms (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). Some researchers in the field of organization theory consider paradigmatic assumptions ‘incommensurable’, i.e. not comparable, according to any meaningful common measures (Jackson & Carter, 1991). However, we adopt the stance that we actually can discuss about relationships among the perspectives represented by different paradigms in a meaningful way (Weaver & Gioia, 1994). Moreover, as our study will illustrate, this standpoint includes that people can simultaneously carry differing, even contradictory, paradigmatic ideas while acting in the same organizational setting.

We do not claim any normative superiority of any identified paradigm over another, neither universally nor in the context of particular application processes of SDMs. Such a judgement is not necessary for our analysis. Rather, our case represents a descriptive study, which highlights intellectual challenges of creating and transferring philosophical or value-based method knowledge (Dahlbom & Mathiassen, 1992) from one intellectual context to another.

## 3. CASE STUDY: DEPLOYMENT OF THE GBM

We studied the deployment of a GBM of requirements analysis for document management systems in a consulting organization which we call DOC. Before we proceed with the description of the case, we briefly state our backgrounds and our role in the narrative in order to provide a context for the reader to make his/her interpretations. One of the authors is the original developer of the method (hereafter referred to as the developer/author), and another author was the team manager of DOC (hereafter referred to as the DOC/author). Both of them are from Finland and were educated in a Finnish university in an information system department. The interviews were conducted in Finnish. The third author, who is educated in a North American business school, is an ‘outside’ researcher not involved with any aspect of the deployment (hereafter referred to as the outsider/author). As we explain later, each author had a specific role in the case analysis.

## 3.1 Background

DOC’s document management consultancy was a small part of the mother corporation, which provided a comprehensive repertoire of information technology (IT) solutions and services.

The organization has conducted document management consultancy and system implemen tations in several business fields. Their document management business focused on analysing the customer’s document management environment and implementing Lotus Domino.Doc™ (IBM. 2004) systems and other Domino™-based solutions for the customer's informatior management. Briefly stated, Domino.Doc, currently known as Domino Document Manager, provides a software platform, on which a company can configure their document and records management systems. In 2004, the product was owned and marketed by Lotus/IBM, which also maintained comprehensive product documentation for developers, administrators and users (IBM, 2004). Lotus Finland once gave ‘the Domino.Doc partner of the year award’ to DOC. This indicates that the organization was highly regarded for the quality of their business, at least as seen from the viewpoint of a major global software vendor.

GBM was developed through research-oriented efforts in 1997–99, which took place mainly in an action research project in collaboration between the University of Jyväskylä and a few Finnish partner organizations. The contemporary issues and challenges of document management in the partner organizations functioned as a test bed for new development ideas and knowledge exchange about document management. After a few rounds of method develop ment in the project, the contemporary version of the method was adopted and elaborated further by a team of consultants in DOC. DOC had participated in the research project as a ‘hanger-on member’, with a motivation to adopt potentially useful ideas resulting from the project. The team manager of document management consulting (the DOC/author) had been employed by the university during the initial round of the method development in 1997. In that capacity, he contributed to the initial versions of the method ideas before he moved to DOC. Hence, he had personal motivation and ownership to try out the method in ‘real life’ beyond its academic birthplace. However, the more detailed description and analyses of the paradigmatic stance, method process and techniques took place mainly after the DOC/author had already left academia.

In addition to this individual motivation, DOC had also organizational motivations to adopt the method:

• the scope and size of enterprise document management projects was increasing by the end of the 1990s, and a method was considered to help cope with the management of projec complexity;

the business of document management consultancy was increasing in general, and hence there was a need to increase the size of the consultant team; and

• use of a method was regarded as beneficial for the image of the company: ‘it looked good in the brochures that we were using a methodology’ (Team manager, DOC).

However, there was no rational method evaluation procedure to compare GBM with any alternatives. As the team manager put it, ‘The method was available to adopt at the time, so we adopted’.

The document management consulting team in DOC formed a unique research setting, as it had used and elaborated the method during 2 years (1999–2000) for their business pur poses, independent of the academic method engineers. At the same time, the ideals and paradigm behind the method creation had been explicitly clarified by the method developers in the IS literature (Päivärinta, 2001a; Päivärinta et al., 2001). The document management consulting business, including the DOC team, was sold later to another company, although the previous mother organization of DOC still exists independently. Hence, to avoid any confusion about any currently existing company, we chose to use an artificial acronym, i.e. DOC, to refer to this team and their business at that time. The fact that the team in question does not exis any more in the form described here gives us greater ethical freedom for our interpretations: even the most critical of our views would not possibly harm an existing business.

## 3.2 GBM for requirements determination

In this section, we briefly describe GBM, focusing on its basic concepts and techniques, and paradigmatic assumptions while listing its steps. A more detailed description of the method is available elsewhere (Karjalainen et al., 2000; Päivärinta et al., 2001).

## 3.2.1 Basic concepts and techniques

A number of stakeholders have interests in the development of document management. A genre of organizational communication refers to a recurrent communicative action in an organization, characterized by its communicative purpose and, to some extent, by its form (which comprises communication media, structural elements and the symbol system used for representing information) (Yates & Orlikowski, 1992). For example, ‘Requirements specification’, ‘Lutheran marriage ceremony’ or ‘Finnish State Budget’ are identifiable genres in certain communities. A document genre, e.g. a ‘Journal article’, ‘Project plan’ or ‘Receipt’, instantiates as a document. Organizational document resources can be discerned, structured and analysed according to the identified document genres, existing or novel.

PUI (Producer or User of Information) entities produce and receive communicative actions and information in the organization, more or less according to the explicitly identified genres. PUI entities can include organizational processes, roles, units, teams, and even individua persons – depending upon how the stakeholders want to structure the organizational domain under analysis. PUI entities and genres are identified in collaborative sessions by using the diagonal-matrix technique (Figure 3). The resulting model is read clockwise: an instance of a genre is produced by a PUI entity which is horizontally at the same row as the genre, and is used by a PUI entity that is vertically in the same column as the genre.

Properties of the identified genres provide topics of interest, under which metadata can be gathered for the requirements analysis for document management; for instance, current desired applications to produce/disseminate/utilize information, archiving time, use frequency, and problems or development ideas. For this, the genre list technique (Figure 4) is used for gathering and analysing metadata about the genres.

## 3.2.2 Paradigmatic values

The paradigmatic value behind GBM was neo-humanism based on critical social theory (Habermas, 1984; 1987). The basic assumption and value orientation was that the method should simultaneously promote communicative, emancipatory and formal rationalities in devel opment (Päivärinta, 2001a,b). Communicative rationality assumes that the genre-based analysis should promote mutual understanding and informed argumentation among IT personnel, end-users, management and consultants. Especially, technological issues are submitted to the guidance of socio-organizational needs identified by these stakeholders. Emancipatory rationality assumes that the GBM should support equal and democratic communication during the development process, decrease dominance of typically dominating stakeholders such as the management and IT personnel, and produce ethically sound document management systems with regard to everyday communication conducted through them. The developer plays the role of a social therapist or emancipator, aiming purposefully to reveal and heal possible hin drances to communication, or unwarranted natural and social constraints that might hinder the stakeholders from receiving an emancipative and mutual understanding of the future system (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). The developer does not take sides among the stakeholders Formal rationality assumes that the GBM should produce the desired document management system with reasonable labour and efficient development efforts and methods, after the mutua understanding has been reached. The neo-humanist paradigm requires that these three rationalities should occur simultaneously. ## 3.4 Data analysis

We took every precaution to get unbiased interpretations of the data as possible. As mentioned earlier, each of the authors had a different role in the research process. The developer/author primarily examined the possible mismatches between the developed method and the adapted and used versions of the method. The outsider/author conducted the analysis of mismatch 1 – the mismatches between underlying paradigm and the developed method, and independently examined the developer/author’s interpretation. We adopted this arrangement to avoid the situation where the developer/author would possibly miss his own paradigmatic mismatches potentially inherent in his work, because such internal inconsistencies in ISD methodologies have been reported (Beath & Orlikowski, 1994). The DOC/author took the role of a discussant about the researchers’ interpretations of the data, and provided clarification and brought in possible new viewpoints to the interpretation. With this division of roles and duties in the case analysis, we tried to ensure both in-depth ‘closeness’ to the actual case to ensure the quality of interpretations from data (by the developer/author and the DOC/author), and appropriate ‘distance’ to the case (by the outsider/author) to bring in unbiased theoretica viewpoints.

The developer/author conducted the initial analysis of the data from the Finnish text and translated large parts of the data to English to enable the outsider/author to interpret further. The DOC/author provided the narrative of the adapted method in relation to developed method, his personal interview and the narrative of the subsequent method evolution before knowing the actual research goals of this paper. We are therefore confident that our previous personal agendas have not substantively biased either the data collection or the analysis.

## 4. FINDINGS: MISMATCHES

## 4.1 Premise mismatches: between paradigms of SDM versions

## 4.1.1 Underlying paradigm vs. developed method

The mismatch we examined here occurs in the construction phase of the SDM. GBM is somewhat unusual in that the underlying paradigm is explicitly stated by the method developer. The paradigm is neo-humanist and based on critical social theory. In general, the described paradigm in GBM adheres to these paradigmatic values. The role of the developer is appropriately stated as ‘emancipator’ and ‘social therapist’. The steps, especially the generation of diagonal matrix to identify genres, also foster communication. However, it is doubtful that socia constrictness and natural action can possibly be avoided if managers and shopfloor employees are present at the same sessions. The neo-humanist ontology is of a world order in conflict. If so, the conviction that a genuinely open discussion can take place between the managers and the managed is a paradox. The method does not specifically cater to ensure equal participation, let alone egalitarianism, unless all participants are committed to this worldview. This mismatch also manifests later in the process in the form of a contextual mismatch between GBM and the organization or site of its use. The method was developed in Finland, where the openness of participation is a widely shared value. In a more-hierarchical context, this assumption is unlikely to be valid. GBM also emphasizes on the identification of existing genres. This promotes thinking in prevailing realities and is contrary to the emancipation rationality of being unhindered from existing norms. We can see a move away from the espoused neo-humanist paradigm towards one more reflective of both social relativist and radical structuralist premises.

## 4.1.2 Developed method vs. understood method

The main mechanism used in DOC to understand the original GBM was person–level interaction between the developer/author and the team manager of DOC (the DOC/author), as he had closely followed the development of GBM. Hence, in addition to the documented method knowledge, his understanding of the method was based on discussions with the method developers during the development period (1997–99). Some ideas behind particular method components, such as the wall-chart technique (Benson et al., 1988), including the generic idea of the diagonal-matrix technique, were also already familiar to the two other consultants in the document management team, based on their educational and work background. The team manager was the major champion of the method in DOC, maintaining documentation about the method and promoting its further development within the team. The other consultants in the team learned the method through a learning-by-doing process: a new method user participated in one or two sessions with an experienced method user, after which he used it independently.

In his later reflection on how the method has been adapted and elaborated, the team manager of DOC stated:

In practice – if reflected now – we did not perhaps understand the whole method fully, and applied only some selected parts of that.

Given that the team leader had several discussions with the method developers over a 3-year period, his understanding of the method was not based solely on the published and documented sources of the method. These comments then highlight the challenges of making method knowledge explicit.

In line with Ørvik et al. (1999), who point out the difficulty of unearthing how a developed method has been actually understood by its adapters, our data include too few mentions to reflect on this issue further. On the other hand, we believe that understanding of a method actually materializes in the locally adapted method itself, and that it would be more interesting to look at the possible mismatches related to the adapted method, as it will in the end show the actual ‘paradigm’ of the method adapter. Henceforth we refer to the next version as developed understood method.

## 4.1.3 Developed/understood method vs. adapted method

At first, DOC had practiced general-level requirements analysis for document management with their customers without being tied to any particular software vendor. This was in line with the original method rationale of GBM (Karjalainen et al., 2000). The method was thus used for the first few customer cases rather straightforwardly in the form it was originally developed. However, the business focus of document management consulting had changed drastically over time from the general-level requirements analysis to selling the implementation work of Domino.Doc-based document management systems to the customers. Subsequently, the adapted method had continuously changed over time.

. . . the income from Domino.Doc licenses goes to Lotus, which produces actually very little profit for us. But our work . . . gives us much more profits . . . of course the actual implementation project . . . means work for us . . . A part of the projects are general requirements analysis and feasibility studies . . . and the customer then chooses whatever software. That was our starting point earlier that we did not start according to any specific software. Now, it has been for 1.5 years that we have aimed at . . . that if Domino.Doc would be a sensible option for the customer . . . then that is OK. (Team manager, DOC)

Table 4 illustrates the changes between the described GBM and the adapted version, of which the team manager also maintained a modest documentation. These changes which were based on experiences from consulting projects were:

A new step was established for setting the scope of development.

• Three steps within the new development objective of document management systems implementation were clearly modified from the original method steps (identify PUI entities; identify genres → technical document types; and identify technical document types and metadata).

• Two new steps emerged for the implementation of Domino.Doc (user group definition and document hierarchy definition), which, in connection to prototyping the implementation with the users, finished the system implementation.

• Two new techniques (Mind Map and Document-metadata matrix) with new notations were elaborated for the purpose of defining requirements for Domino.Doc implementations and were added to the method.

To demarcate the scope of development, the original method had leaned plainly on PU entities and genres. A new step for setting the scope of development emerged, because sometimes customers were uncertain about what should be actually developed in the beginning (including other initiatives related to document management, such as intranets, webservices, etc.). Several conceptual viewpoints for this were used. At this time, a colleague of the DOC team (who was not part of the team) introduced another method to elicit and record requirements. Called the Mind Map technique, it had been developed into software called Mind Manager™, and the consultants incorporated it in GBM (see Figure 5).

The adapted method variant for Domino.Doc-implementations did not include explicitly traceable continuity of transforming reguirements to implementation throughout the whole process, but required ‘creativity’ and ‘improvisation’ on the way. However, the steps adapted from the original GBM were still regarded as necessary. Those steps provided tacit knowledge of the organization and its documents, which implicitly assisted the consultants in the steps that turned the requirements to implementation. PUI entities and technical document types helped define user groups and document hierarchy. The final tailoring of the Domino.Doc-based solution was then prototyped in collaboration with the customer to prevent unwanted surprises and to capture late-emerging ideas. With Document-metadata matrices, these steps altogether iteratively complete the technological implementation.

One more [thing to consider] is the bridge to implementation . . . Another thing is whether it [making the method more explicit] would be worth the effort . . . There is a kind of improvisational gap . . . if we go towards the implementation, we do some class diagrams or some kind of use cases or some ER [Entity-Relationship] diagrams. The method is not complete. It does not say what should happen after genre analysis on the way towards technical specification . . . how to get the technical document types and user rights definitions in the method. That would be really useful! (Team manager, DOC, in a written comment on the developed method)

As stated earlier, there was a change in the business focus of the company from general level requirements analysis to selling the Domino.Doc-based document management systems. This change shifted the paradigm of the adapted method from the original neo-humanist ideals towards business-flavoured functionalism, in which the efficiency aspects and technologically constrained assumptions steered by the vendor-based product had taken over the original idea of emancipatory rationality for discussing about the problems without basing the solutions on any particular technology.

We . . . haven’t sold this general development thing very actively lately. (Consultant, DOC)

. . . Actually, when we start from the system viewpoint [Domino.Doc] then we do not have much problem analysis, whereas in rather many cases [of the general development] we used to link problems and development ideas to the use of the method, which brings more depth and general development viewpoint to the picture . . . (Team manager, DOC)

. . . if in our sessions the customers start to discuss about details or some other problems of their own there, we try to interrupt them that sorry this goes outside the topic of this session, we are busy, and we have a lot of things to clarify . . . (Novice consultant, DOC)

The tool support aspect of the adaption also resulted in paradigmatic mismatches. Instead of paper-based diagonal matrices on the wall, a spreadsheet-based application with a portable computer and projector was used to show and document the diagonal matrix and genre list (as well as the new techniques included in the ‘toolbox’, for the other purposes) immediately in the electronic form. A consultant fills in the models with his laptop according to comments expressed by the participants of the session(s). The consultants were aware that this may decrease the participation aspect of the sessions, thus contradicting the emancipatory ideal of the neo-humanist paradigm.

The electronic tool is a bit no good . . . Then those people who dominate more are able to speak louder and get more contribution to the models. In the traditional manual way, the quiet people can contribute more directly to the models. (Consultant, DOC)

In summary, our data indicate that the adapted method had changed over time according to the change in business strategy and goals of DOC as a consulting company, resulting in two observable main method variants. First, whereas the developed method originally denoted communicative and emancipatory rationales, the adapted main variant for the development of Domino.Doc implementations was affected by the strategic and functional rationality orientations of the consulting team, originating in new business opportunities in the field and cooperation with a particular software vendor. Second, the use of the Mind Map-based technique, perhaps ironically, improved communicative rationality in the adapted method. The number o general-level requirements analyses and identification of problems other than implementationrelated ones had been decreased, based at least partially on conscious selling strategy and subsequent agenda of consulting actions from the viewpoint of DOC.

## 4.1.4 Adapted method vs. method-in-use

The methods-in-use in particular development projects varied continuously from the documented versions of the adapted method, as each consultant had rather independent responsibilities for particular customers and projects. However, the consultants frequently discussed their experiences in the cases among themselves, which, in turn, affected the adapted method.

Although the team leader had documentation about the adapted method, he recognized that such documented method knowledge had stayed at a rather general level. This, together with the independence of individual consultants, caused varying practice of method use throughout the projects.

In an engineering firm, we developed document management for their projects. There we started sessions . . . wait a minute . . . now I started to think, whether we used wall charts at all there or not . . . we used only Mind Map in the two first sessions. We mapped first development ideas, what they have, and then we gathered more in-depth views with interviewing, and just wrote a report . . . [Q: Did you gather any information objects?] Yes . . . wait a minute . . . we gathered them with Mind Map . . . the general-level document types . . . In fact [the team manager] was doing the project there as well with me. (Consult ant, DOC)

For example, the issue of communicative rationality concerning the number of participants allowed in sessions, could vary case-by-case, and the method-in-use was tailored according to the context and scope of the customer project.

We have had the rule-of-thumb that the number of participants in the group sessions would be less than 10, no more [to make participative discussion effective]. [Later on the consultant described particular projects further] . . . and there we had participants from 5 firms [concerning document management for an organization network] and there were about 25 people sitting around one big wall-chart. Ummm . . . the result remained at a rather coarse level, that chart, but we had a lot of good discussion and they moved boxes from one place to another and debated what the process actually would be as each had a bit different idea about that . . . and they yelled to each other that it is not like that . . . and it was in that sense interesting from the viewpoint of general development, but it did not end with any complete description . . . if we compare to that which we did later on with each of the participant organization individually. (Consultant, DOC)

The method-in-use had also become, over time and case-by-case, more and more ‘technology oriented’, changing the paradigmatic focus from emancipation and communication towards the functionalistic design of Domino.Doc solutions in parallel with the change of business orientation of the company. This in turn had impact on the generic understanding of the ‘adapted method’ and the documented general-level guidelines for its use within the team. Project size, scope and existing technological platform for implementing document management solutions were mentioned as factors affecting such method-in-use, in addition to the experiences and preferences of the consultants. Hence, our data indicate that ‘mismatches’ were experienced contextually in the projects in which the method was going to be used and in the general-level business goals of the consulting organization. Over time, such experienced mismatches thus caused a ‘paradigm shift’ in the adapted method, as the team was more or less freguently discussing about the further elaboration of the method based or their experience and views of the business, and consequently changing the adapted method accordingly.

## 4.2 Context mismatches: paradigms in SDM versions vs. paradigms of adapters

## 4.2.1 Paradigm of adapters vs. developed method

In DOC, the team manager and two other senior consultants shared the concerns of emancipation and communicative rationality in the form of wide participation. This was obvious, for example, in their comments on what they considered to be ‘good’ in the GBM in general, as well as in their particular comments on the question of whether the method facilitates participation of all stakeholders.

The clear strengths of the method are: the viewpoint of collaboration . . . the customer can understand it easily and participate, and it takes different stakeholders into account. (Team manager, DOC, memo commenting the method)

. . . it is simple enough, this diagonal matrix, so that people can participate and are able to describe the document types in terms of their own work . . . We have tried to involve always as representative ensemble of stakeholders as possible. (Consultant, DOC).

Interestingly, the novice consultant, who had joined the organization after the change in the business scope, seemed to have much clearer value orientation towards efficiency of the process in the adapted method and expertise of the consultant as the dominating issues tha shaped the development work in general.

[Q: What is the greatest benefit of the method?] Uh, in the commercial sense . . . we get the system [Domino.Doc] hierarchy out from that . . . and the customer’s eyes are opened in many ways, as document management issues are not typically familiar to them . . . It is rather quick way to map . . . during one day we get a lot of things clear, and it is really effective in that the customer’s eyes are opened that this is what we actually have . . . (Novice consultant, DOC)

Moreover, when the novice consultant was asked whether the method in itself would increase or decrease the dominance and participation of any particular stakeholder, he answered:

Hhhhm . . . I guess this starts to relate a bit to sociology or something [the tone of the voice sounded like he considered the issue irrelevant] . . . eh, I don’t . . . I don’t think so.

To summarize, it appears that the senior consultants seemed to still share the ideals of emancipatory participation, covering inclusion, and communication among all stakeholders, whereas the novice consultant seemed to have much clearer functionalist and even strategic value orientation from the viewpoint of the changed business focus.

## 4.2.2 Paradigm of adapters vs. understood/adapted method

As we stated earlier, the three senior consultants in DOC seemed to have communicative, even emancipatory, ideals and attitudes, in line with the original method, which were not full present in the currently adapted method variant for Domino.Doc implementation business. In fact, a few comments directly indicated slight mismatch, i.e. a controversy between the consultants’ own values and the values partially indicated by the latest version of the adapted method, which highlights the business opportunities of the system implementation work, instead of general-level consulting.

[Q: Is it your first priority to sell Domino.Doc or general-level consulting?]

. . . let’s say . . . Do these tapes stay between us? . . . The fact that we sell our work [fo Domino.Doc implementations] brings more money for us. (Team manager, DOC)

. . . if we start to do that more general analysis . . . it has been actually a pity that we do so few such projects, as we now focus nowadays purely on the system viewpoint [Domino.Doc]. (Consultant, DOC)

However, we did not find any such controversial mismatch between the novice consultant’s values and the adapted method. One explanation for this might be that a newcomer does not question the business background of the organization so easily, especially when he does not share the same history of different business orientation that was experienced and shared by the senior consultants.

## 4.2.3 Paradigm of adapters vs. method-in-use

As the adapted method had been elaborated continuously by the consulting team, from case-by-case tailored methods used in actual projects, the same trends that we identify above apply. Our data did not reveal many new insights. The consultants were in charge of the methods to be used in the projects, while the customers had only minor interest in affecting the methodology used. Rather, the customers seemed to be just happy that the consultants were using any method at all in the first place.

By the way, one significant, perhaps a bit surprising, benefit of the method was the image, even marketing benefit, which it brought for us. In the marketing and sales situation we could state that we use a methodical approach here and that our company is actively developing such a methodology further. (Team manager, DOC, reflections of the method use 2004)

As consultants had a great deal of freedom to act in the projects under their own respon sibility, it is rather likely that no new mismatches existed in the actual methods used in the projects over those identified in relation to the adapted method.

## 4.3 Context mismatches: paradigms in SDM versions vs. paradigm of organization

As we did not interview any member of the customer organizations where GBM was used to model the target document management systems, we can only infer the organizational values from our interviews with the consultants.

## 4.3.1 Paradigm of customer organizations vs. developed method

Three kinds of potential mismatches between the different stakeholders of the customer organizations and the values promoted by the developed method were indicated by the consultants’ comments. First, a few managers in some organizations seemed to have a dominating worldview which did not encourage all stakeholders to participate.

. . . in one case we had a good dialogue among the participants, people commented and gave truly opposite viewpoints. Then a manager walked in, who had the style just to ‘announce things’, then the nature of the dialogue changed totally . . . Some groups can build the worldview through dialogue and discussion, but if there is one strong person then he will surely start to build that kind of worldview. (Consultant, DOC)

Second, employees were sometimes unmotivated to participate in development initiatives, which touched general-level issues beyond their particular work tasks, or they could speak o the ‘espoused’ reality in the presence of the management.

. . . sometimes . . . the presence of management may hinder the process, as people do no necessarily tell openly how the organization actually works, but they will tell how it is expected to work – and this is always the problem, people tend to smoothen the reality and their own daily actions . . . (Consultant, DOC)

. . . , if we have participants who are in too much grassroot level in the organization. It is difficult to get these people to get interested about this thing in general, if they are just thinking too much of their own jobs. . . . people just think that it is not my business to think about things beyond ‘my box’. (Consultant, DOC)

Third, it was indicated that some IT experts in the customer organizations might have change-resisting or prejudiced viewpoints on what the development of document managemen would be, in contrast to the spirit of the original method.

IT [information technology] people need to be involved, because they do not necessarily know readily about how the organization works and what their real needs are. They might think they know, but they do not have that holistic view. Another thing is that if someone from the organization suggests that we need this kind of system, and the IT persons have not participated and thus do not share the reasoning, they might even resist such initiatives . . . So it is better that IT people are involved from the start, because it is then easier to state the requirements and acquisition decisions to the IT department and they share the idea of the need. So there would not be so much resistance, as people are often sensitive to reject the worldviews created by other people in relation to the one of their own . . . If they do not participate, then the resistance might emerge quickly and good ideas would be buried before they are taken into production. (Consultant, DOC)

It appears that the consultants considered that a communicative, even emancipatory, facilitator approach would be a means for preventing problems, which might emerge from the political agendas of different stakeholders or from the general-level lack of motivation to participate.

An additional mismatch between the original GBM and the customers could be the ‘aca demic language’ of the method as reported for the academic publications, which could be seen as a kind of mismatch between the developed method and the customer’s reality.

We do not speak of genres or metadata [with the customer] . . . we do not even speak of document types in the beginning . . . In practice, and this is practical, we speak simply of information and producers of information and users. Everyone understands that . . . (Team manager, DOC)

## 4.3.2 Paradigm of customer organizations vs. adapted method

The mismatches between the customers and the developed method, mentioned above, can also apply to the paradigmatic issues of the first version of the adapted method, which was aimed towards general-level requirements analysis. After the adapted method had been elaborated towards efficient definition of technical requirements for Domino.Doc implementations, the data indicate that there might have been occasional cases in which some custome stakeholders would have still preferred more communicative general-level discussions about the document management implementations.

. . . if in our sessions the customers start to discuss about details or some other problems of their own there, we try to interrupt them that sorry this goes outside the topic of this session, we are busy, and we have a lot of things to clarify . . . (Novice consultant, DOC)

The consultants also indicated that the general-level language of the adapted method used within the consulting team did not always match with the ‘customer language’, which required special awareness inside the team.

We always try to speak of the ‘language of the project’. Language of the customer . . . that is, if they speak of processes so we speak of processes, if they speak of services, we speak of services . . . [Q: How is it among you in the team, is it your language or the customer language?]

Customer language . . . We can speak also with the customer’s terms among us . . . Of course, if someone joins who has not been earlier part of a particular project, we need to understand that they speak of functions or departments, but so that we can connect these logically, we know that we speak of information producers and users anyhow. (Team manager, DOC)

[In one case] they spoke about processes themselves, but in my opinion they were actually close to functions . . . they were somehow between processes and functions, or it varied a bit. So that sometimes it was a clear function and sometimes a clear process. . . . To understand, if it would be a process or function, it is not always very clear. (Consultant, DOC)

## 4.3.3 Paradigm of customer organizations vs. method-in-use

The same mismatches that we identify above also surface in the method-use situations. The presence of dominating managers or passive end-users needed special attention from the facilitators to get the method-in-use to work, just to get the technical requirements, let alone the early cases of general-level development discussions. On the other hand, the novice consultant had surely experienced such a situation, in which the customers had started to discuss about ‘irrelevant things’ from the viewpoint of Domino.Doc implementation, in a rea case of method use. Such individual cases thus had their impact on the later versions of the adapted method, and in most cases it is difficult to differentiate between these two categories of paradigmatic mismatches (i.e. the customers vs. adapted method and customers vs. method-in-use) from our data.

As stated earlier, the customers did not seem to care much about the method-in-use as long as a method was used. However, some customers had their own preferences for the development work. These did not appear to be ‘rational’ from the consultants’ point of view. One anecdote can shed some light. A customer’s (negative) experiences of the general-level idea of wall charts and diagonal matrices, which had been in use since 1970s in Finland, had caused change in one case to the suggested method-in-use, and actually promoted the use of the new electronic tools and the Mind Map technique.

It can happen that the customer says: ‘no, no’, . . . when we speak of wall charts, that ‘we had really bad experiences in 1974 of using those, we will not do that now’ . . . there was one such case. We just did the trick with Mind Map and projected the same issues onto the wal with Mind Manager and . . . that was then something <sup>☺</sup> . . . The same thing, but differen technique, and we did not call it the ‘diagonal matrix’. (Team manager, DOC)

## 4.4 Context mismatch: paradigm of method adapters vs. customer organizations

We have discussed at length the paradigmatic values held by the consultants and the customer organizations. The senior consultants in DOC were more sensitive to the communicative, even emancipatory, values in addition to making implementation-oriented business efficient. By contrast, the novice consultant seemed to have a rather plainly functionalist view of the development process, and even a slight leaning towards the strategic viewpoint of his company. In the customer organizations, many development processes led by the senior consultants had been communicative and participative. However, cases of dominating managers, end-users with passive or unrealistic attitudes to development discussions, and IT departments with unconstructive attitudes on development (if they were not in the processes from the beginning) emerged.

Here, the most-potential mismatch could perhaps emerge between a functionally/ strategically oriented consultant and a customer team wishing a great deal of communication and scrutiny of general-level problems in the beginning of the business case. On the other hand, the novice consultant in this case most probably would not have had the experience to deflate the influence of the dominating managers or other stakeholders, which would help those customer stakeholders who would not get their voices heard. Moreover, a functionalist could perhaps believe more easily the ‘rosy viewpoints’ of those stakeholders who would like to express their espoused view of the organization instead of describing the real situation.

In case the consultants have the communicative and emancipatory orientations in their mental toolbox, as the senior consultants seemed to have, one could expect that they would be in a better position to bring ‘therapeutic discussions’ to projects in which technically oriented requirements analyses would be preferred over general-level debate on the ‘better future’.

## 5. DISCUSSION

## 5.1 Paradigm drifts

Table 5 presents a summary of the paradigmatic mismatches observed in our case. Each observed mismatch caused a ‘paradigm drift’ between two or more paradigmatic standpoints. The arrows indicate the drift from one paradigm to another (marked with X) due to an observed mismatch.

To describe and explain such a paradigm drift, we used the four paradigms proposed by Hirschheim & Klein (1989), namely, functionalist, radical structuralist, social relativist and neo-humanist. In order to do so, we need to distinguish between the neo-humanist and the other three paradigms. As we stated earlier, it is important to realize that simultaneous occurrence of three rationalities, communicative, emancipatory and formal (Klein & Hirschheim, 1991), is required to fulfil the preconditions of the neo-humanist paradigm (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; 1994). The technical knowledge interest (Habermas, 1972), which forms the basis for the formal rationality orientation, is prevailing in the functionalist paradigm. In this case, the ‘analyst’, in the role of an expert, guides the analysis process, by finding out the system requirements from the other stakeholders and then developing a system based on those. In this paradigm, the communicative and emancipatory orientations thus do not steer the development process, insofar the analyst feels that he/she understands the ‘users’, ‘managers’ and others with whom to validate the requirements and to overcome the possible resistance. Communication is thus used ‘as a means to an end’ (Hirschheim & Klein, 1994), instead of an unquestionable value orientation in itself. A functionalist analyst plays the role of expert, mastering the development process and relevant issues to be discussed. If we leave out emancipatory rationality (with its focus on radical change) and focus on the communicative rationality and mutual understanding alone, we are close to the social relativist paradigm. Now, our main goal would be to integrate the previously diverse perspectives of different social actors in the domain to reach a common interpretation about the situation, and the system at hand. Here, the systems developer acts as a plain facilitator of ‘sensemaking’ and is satisfied after a common interpretation of the system at hand is created (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989).

Ideally, the neo-humanist orientation should not include plain strategic/dominating orientation to communication from the viewpoint of just one particular stakeholder (Habermas, 1984; Hirschheim et al., 1996), such as the management, employees, or – in this case – consulting company. In case a particular SDM or approach considers such strategic orientations to shape the truth in the world of conflicting interests, such a paradigm can be labelled as the radical structuralist (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). In the radical structuralist paradigm, the systems developer chooses his/her side, functioning as a political change agent for the good of tha stakeholder. The GBM tries explicitly to avoid such paradigmatic orientations, by trying to bring such strategic orientations to surface to be discussed and questioned (Päivärinta, 2001a).

During our data analysis, we tried to categorize seemingly interrelating mismatches together to form wider ‘stories’ in order to crystallize our interpretations of the phenomena and to form a basis for discussion. We found five such stories, which are also shown in Table 5:

-1: Converting paradigmatic ideals into concrete techniques offer method engineering challenges.

-2: Preferred tools simultaneously enable and constrain the ideals behind the SDMs.

-3: Stakeholder strategies may contradict noble ideals.

-4: Individuals improvise according to their own ideals.

-5: The paradigmatic standpoints and ideals vary among the stakeholders.

Below, we discuss these stories in detail.

## 5.1.1 Story 1: converting paradigmatic ideals into concrete techniques offer method engineering challenges

In line with Beath & Orlikowski (1994), who studied internal mismatches in the information engineering methodology, our study revealed that being paradigmatically consistent in the role of a method developer is an intellectually challenging task. In the initial version of the GBM, the way genres were identified was not fully in line with the ideal of radical change orientation of the neo-humanist paradigm, being more in line with the social relativist paradigm. Further, neo-humanist ideals, such as highlighting the ideal speech situation (Habermas, 1984) among all stakeholders, might include a paradox when we come to practical implementations of such situations. To overcome communication hindrances, such strategically oriented manipulation by dominating managers in group sessions, a method engineer may need more individually oriented tools instead of only pursuing group-communication techniques. Such tools may include confidential person-to-person interviews.

Similar internally mismatching paradoxes have been observed concerning the functionalist methodologies and techniques as well. For example, as early as in the 1980s, the area of information systems planning had seen a plethora of methodologies that pursued formal and rational development of information system portfolio in the enterprise. All of these were reported to suffer from serious deficiencies when judged according to the very same functionalist ideals of efficiency and effectiveness of the process and the usability of the outcomes (Lederer & Mendelow, 1986; Lederer & Sethi, 1988; 1992).

However, no paradigmatic inconsistencies between the method developer’s own values and the realized methods can be observed unless an explicit method has been suggested and defined. Explicit identification of such internal mismatches could actually serve as an exercise to enlighten the method developer further. At least in this case. he/she became more aware of two current mismatches between the realized method and the stated neo-humanist values – and such understanding could well be used for the future elaboration of the methods in question towards the explicated ideals.

## 5.1.2 Story 2: preferred tools simultaneously enable and constrain the ideals behind SDMs

The second story also appears somewhat paradoxical. The adoption of computer-aided tool instead of paper-based wall-chart technique to capture and record requirements had two consequences. First, it caused a paradigm drift from the emancipatory stance of democratic participation towards functionalism, preferring analyst efficiency in documentation for require ments definition instead of simultaneous contribution directly to the model and communication by the group. On the other hand, the utilization of a new Mind Manager tool was fitting to the pattern of using the laptop and projector for communicating more about the actual scopes of development projects. This paradoxically enhances emancipatory and communicative rationalities. Hence, the technological basis used to facilitate the systems development process clearly both constrained and enabled the ideals behind the initial method. Technology, fo example computer support for systems development process, is not necessarily good or bad per se in relation to particular ideals behind systems development – even when adopted due to purposes diverging from the viewpoint of particular ideals.

However, our study highlights the importance of explicit consideration of group dynamics and the role of technology and understanding of particular notations/language in the techniques-in-use. For example, method engineering should not be regarded only as the field that limits itself to implementation of notations, tools and process support for SDMs in the computer (Brinkkemper et al., 1996). Rather, our observations suggest that method engineers need also to be more specific about the particular methods beyond their implementations and formalizations in the computer. For example, in our case, the technology used to support particular notations and techniques had paradigmatic impacts on the ways people interacted with the models and with each other during the systems development process. Research on method engineering seldom, if ever, is specific on the paradigmatic standpoints of particular techniques implemented in the metamodelling tools beyond functionalist ideals for ‘tool support’. Explicit identification of paradigmatic values, e.g. concerning communicative, emancipatory, and even strategic rationales, might truly open new avenues for method engineers. For example, widely used computer-aided tools supporting ‘Rational Unified Process’ or ‘Unified Modelling Language’ (Booch et al., 1998), such as Rational Rose, were unlikely to have been designed from the viewpoint of emancipatory or communicative rationality orientations, relating to their use in participative development processes. However, such standpoints, with later links to analyst-driven implementation tools, could well provide innovative appliances to the intellectual repertoire of modern systems developers. Systems development technology is not just physical or digital artefacts. e.g. CASE (computer-aidec software engineering) tools. It is also socially constructed practices about interacting with the tools and with the people involved in the development process.

## 5.1.3 Story 3: stakeholder strategies may contradict noble ideals

As Table 5 shows, various mismatches caused the underlying paradigm of GBM more or less to drift from neo-humanism to the other three paradigms. The most obvious reason behind a few paradigmatic mismatches was the change in business strategy of DOC. The change from general-level consulting to promotion of a particular product further affected the sales and other interactions with the customers. The new system implementation-oriented method variant, which was much more analyst-driven and efficiency-oriented within the frame of Domino.Doc implementations, was clearly elaborated due to the new business opportunities. The novice consultant had either quickly learned the new strategy, or was already content with the functionalist. even strategic. orientation from before. and was rather confident about working according to it. However, in some cases, the customer stakeholders still showed initiatives to discuss about ‘irrelevant’ things from the viewpoint of the Domino.Doc-based system implementation process. This would indicate that all stakeholders did not share the ideal of functionalism leading towards effective and efficient implementation, wishing to spend more time on other issues.

All in all, our findings highlight the need for method developers, adapters and systems developers to be aware of the ideals they want to reach in relation to the possibly diverging ideals emerging in the different contexts of method deployment and use. On the other hand, our findings also indicate that use of a particular method by itself does not guarantee the conformance to the values behind it. Despite noble intentions, regardless of the paradigm, unforeseen opposite forces causing ‘paradigm drifts’ may well occur in different contexts. Again, it might be of help if the values guiding the development processes would have been explicitly discussed beforehand. Otherwise, it would be difficult to pursue pure efficiency and effectiveness of the functionalist paradigm, mutual understanding according to social relativism, emancipatory ideals of neo-humanism, or even ‘the most manipulative developmen strategy’ from the viewpoint of radical structuralism.

## 5.1.4 Story 4: individuals improvise according to their own ideals

In our case, the senior consultants who once adopted ways of thinking in line with the neo-humanist paradigm, later on either moved away from this worldview, or began feeling slight internal discomfort in relating themselves to the new strategic business orientation tha had caused a mismatch. However, this was not at all a problem for the novice consultant, who seemed to have a kind of functionalist, analyst-centred attitude, under the new commercia (radical structuralist) strategy for consulting by the time he had started to work in the company. Perhaps as a consequence, the senior consultants sometimes improvised against the previously adapted methodological guidelines, based on their experience or ideas of how to act in a particular development situation, and at least partially reverted back to the ideals of neo-humanism.

This observation has clear consequences for method engineers and local method adapters. Paradigmatic mismatches often accumulate. Later method adapters adapt and use methods that bear the imprint of the personal paradigmatic values of earlier adapters. In case the method developer or adapter would like to promote explicitly a particular paradigmatic standpoint, he/she needs to ‘know his/her people’. A functionalistic method engineer might not be particularly devoted to work as a labour partisan or social therapist, while a social relativist may find it difficult to make an ‘optimal requirements analysis’ quickly.

## 5.1.5 Story 5: the paradigmatic standpoints and ideals vary among the stakeholders

Table 5 shows that several forces caused by different mismatches simultaneously caused drifts towards diverging paradigmatic standpoints during the method deployment and use. Especially, from the viewpoint of DOC, the stakeholders in the customer organizations were not seen as a homogeneous group with regard to their value orientations, preferences and actions during the systems development process. While some managers could be democratic and participative, other managers could really freeze the emancipatory atmosphere. In cases where shopfloor employees were unmotivated, the development process inevitably became more analyst-oriented.

From the viewpoint of method deployment, these observations highlight even more the need to be aware of one’s values and fundamental assumptions, especially concerning method use. Implicit differences in such fundamental standpoints and assumptions can explain some of the stress, complexity, and even failures that have been experienced in numerous systems development projects throughout the last few decades. If the systems development process does not readily fit the paradigmatic orientations of major stakeholders, or the system analyst cannot see such fundamental nuances during his/her work, it is not difficult to imagine the challenges that might occur. For example, techniques such as diagonal-matrix generation that are premised upon open participation may not embody emancipatory principles in a rigid hierarchical context.

## 5.2 Explaining the findings in context

Our findings need to be seen in the context of the specific case that we studied. First, we were fortunate that our case represented a rare case (Yin, 1989). The paradigmatic assumptions of the method developers were well documented in research literature, which is not often the case (Avison & Fitzgerald, 2003). We also had access to the data of consultants’ experiences of the use of the methods and to actual documentation from a few cases.

Second, the adapting organization was a consulting company. The systems adaptors were consultants, and thus the method adaption happened outside the organization. The cultural paradigm of the adaptors’ organization could be different from that of the organization for which the actual systems were being built. This nuance is not captured in the Ørvik et al. (1999) model. Even if that is not the case, that is, even when an SDM is deployed internally in an organization’s IT department, there is a need to distinguish between the two entities. A cultural difference may exist between the IT function and the rest of the organization, or more specifically between the IT function and the end-user unit.

Third, the method in our case, GBM, was based on the neo-humanist paradigm, where the emancipation of the users was a prime objective. The prime catalyst was supposed to be discussion and reflection. Our data indicate that while there was a great deal of reflection and self-critique, most of it was confined to the consultants! We ponder whether this is attributable to the method. Would an SDM based on the functionalist paradigm have elicited the same reflection among the method adapters? We do not have reasons to believe so. Therefore, we propose that effects of paradigmatic goals of SDMs and the systems should also encompass the systems developers. We also speculate whether the mismatches we observed can be attributed to the paradigm on which it is based – neo-humanism is an ideal and ideals are never fully attained.

## 5.3 Implications for research

Our study has its limitations. The findings are based on perceived paradigmatic mismatches. There were likely non-perceived mismatches. Whether the drift would have followed a similar pattern had such mismatches been identified, is questionable. We leave it to future research to address this issue.

The findings are in accordance with those in related areas, such as strategy implementation, that indicate that strategies undergo reformulations as they move through the organization (Linesaker, 2002). Our contribution to the existing debate on method use is twofold. First, our findings do not indicate ‘amethodical’ development; rather we find support for local method engineering (Tolvanen, 1998) albeit in a more informal and unstructured way. Research on SDM use has shown that not only are SDMs modified in practice, but that a unique version of the method is created each time it is used (Fitzgerald. 1998). Several reasons have been identified, including contingencies. By examining the paradigmatic values of the methods, the method adapters and the organizational context, we have added another dimension into the understanding lens. Second, our findings show that when SDMs are adapted, perhaps in a planned and ‘conscious’ manner, there is often a shift in the paradigm of the SDM. This is caused by both the contingencies of the context and, more interestingly, by the deep-rooted paradigmatic values of the actors. Thus, SDM adaption is influenced by paradigmatic values, as well as result in paradigm shifts. This simultaneous structuration of the SDM paradigm needs to be investigated further.

Our study can also be seen against the wider background of SDM use and evaluation. The contingency-based approach argues that proper evaluation of SDMs can result in the selection of a method appropriate for a project (Siau & Rossi, 1998). Based on our findings, we argue that even when an appropriate method is selected, paradigmatic drifts occur unless a ‘paradigmatic’ evaluation is part of the evaluation process. It is, however, possible that methods were not understood as intended and hence adaption took place. Methods can be difficult to learn (Siau & Loo, 2002). Based on our findings, we cannot totally rule out this explanation. However, we do have evidence that the deployment process is influenced by paradigmatic assumptions. The interaction of human cognitive limitation and of conflicting paradigmatic values is an interesting avenue for future research of method evaluation and use.

Other research directions are generated by our study. For example, Ørvik et al. (1999) argue that context mismatches need to be examined during the deployment process. The question remains what causes context mismatches. We have shied away from commenting on the desirability of a particular paradigm. We also did not examine the final effect of the adaption process. The question of whether and how the developed system ultimately reflects goals of the underlying paradigm remained unanswered. This requires studies on the effects of deploying particular system developed with particular methodologies.

Our findings indicate a deployment process less sequential than in the Ørvik et al. (1999) model. While the use of the method necessarily follows the method adaption, it happens only in the initial deployment. Later deployment results in more circular processes (Mathiassen et al., 1996). Methods-in-use result in reflection on the part of the method adaptors which influence the next process of adaption – even within one project. This process is much more complex and simultaneous than the sequential model indicates.

## 5.4 Implications for practice

Our findings have implications for two separate, but intertwined, aspects of practice: teaching of systems development and actual development of systems. We echo Ørvik et al. (1999) that it is important for those involved in the process of systems development to be aware of the possible mismatches and the underlying causes that contribute to these mismatches. Clearly, we need to teach students about the paradigmatic assumptions. The question is whether we should explicitly teach them or should they learn through socialization. Our findings indicate that SDM adaption and values are learned by doing and through socializa tion. Yet, educational interventions may be useful, such as teaching the probable influence of the method engineering philosophy in general provided through the background institution of the consultants. This is especially so, because according to some IS scholars, SDMs are mainly useful for teaching (Mathiassen et al., 1996). We lend our voice to the proposal by Dahlbom & Mathiassen (1992) that ‘systems development philosophy’ should be an essential component of the education of systems developers, as well as of the education of future method engineers.

Paradigmatic analysis should also be an essential activity in the actual SDM deployment and use process. Our findings indicate that SDM adapters do go through self-reflection and examine their paradigmatic values. That paradigm drifts occur is not a problem per se. As Hirschheim & Klein (1989) suggest, such changes resulting in mixed paradigms often foster creative problem solving. Paradigmatic analysis is vital when we have a situation such as our case where the method adapter is from an organization different from the one where the system was being built. Two organizations may often have different goals, values and cultures This is the case in market-based development processes (Sawyer, 2001), where the companies for which systems are developed seldom have any particular interest in the SDM as such. Finally, there is a need to make developers aware that factors such as change in business environment lead to business opportunities and that may result in paradigm shift.

## 6. CONCLUSIONS

We set out to examine the greater issue of how SDMs are actually used in practice. Our findings tend to indicate that rather than an ‘amethodical’ approach, SDMs, indeed, can be used in practice, although not necessarily as intended by the SDM constructor. They undergo a series of changes, in response to a variety of reasons, some as mundane as pragmatics of a meeting and some as response to changing environments. However, these often lead to a departure from the paradigmatic values of the method developer. The question then can be raised: Whether such wholesale changes do not actually indicate a holistic and thus ‘amethodical’ approach to systems development? Further, why have an SDM at all?

Our answer is that we should view SDMs as emergent, ever-changing organic sociotechnical artefacts. Methods are not only ‘notations’ and ‘models’ inside the computer, they also impact how one acts in the physical space. Moreover, they embody what real values assumptions one has when acting with and interacting through a particular SDM in a particular context. Apart from their use in pedagogy, and as a framework for use of tools and techniques, SDMs are also vehicles for reflection. By setting our experiences and history of method use against the paradigms and values of the various versions of SDMs, we clarify our own values and thus ultimately affect the way we design systems.
