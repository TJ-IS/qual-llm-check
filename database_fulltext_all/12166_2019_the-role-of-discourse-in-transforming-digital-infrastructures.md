# The role of discourse in transforming digital infrastructures

Egil Øvrelid and Bendik Bygstad

Journal of Information Technology 1–22 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621983199DOI: 10.1177/0268396219831994 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf SAGE

## Abstract

Radical shifts in large information technology programmes or digital infrastructures are unusual, but they do occur, usually as a consequence of problems or misalignment. What we know less about is the role of discourse in these shifts. Our interest in this article is to investigate the role of discourse when digitalisation programmes encounter problems. Building on Foucault’s theory of discourse, our research question is: what is the role of discourse in the transformation of digital infrastructures? Our research approach is a critical realist case study, discussing three cases from eHealth innovation. We use Foucault’s archaeological methodology to identify the emerging discursive formations when a programme encounters difficulties. This enables us to analyse the causal relationship between discursive formations and other mechanisms in the infrastructure. We offer two contributions: first, we outline a framework to understand the role of discursive formations in digital transformation; second, we propose a set of configurations to explain how contextual factors and causal mechanisms contingently lead to the transformation of a digital infrastructure.

## Keywords

Discourse, discursive formation, digital infrastructures, digital innovation, digital transformation

## Introduction

In this article, we investigate how discourse influences shifts in large digitalisation programmes. Such shifts are unusual, but they do occur. Examples include the termination of the British eHealth mega-programme in 2011 (Greenhalgh et al., 2010) and the demise of the German e-prescription initiative (Klein and Schellhammer, 2017). In the private sector, there is an increasing number of disruptive digitalisation initiatives, such as the case of the DBS Bank (Sia et al., 2016).

From a practical perspective, the trajectories of these shifts vary. Some of them occur with a sense of crisis, involving a large number of stakeholders and the consequences may be serious for the owners of the initial initiative (Sauer and Willcocks, 2007). Others are less dramatic, and are characterised by a gradual change process, but with far-reaching implications (Aanestad and Jensen, 2011). As shown in project escalation theory (Keil et al., 1994), programmes often continue in spite of problems, because of prestige aspects and sunk costs. As alternative approaches emerge, shifts take place, and the ‘creative destruction’ of old information technology (IT) solutions makes way for the next generation of solutions.

Theoretically, these issues have been dealt with in vari ous ways. One perspective is presented in the strategic management information systems (IS) literature (Peppard and Ward, 2016), where new solutions are chosen after strategic management analysis. Another perspective is the punctuated socio-technical information change theory (Lyytinen and Newman, 2008), describing the episodic character of IS evolution – it alternates between periods of incremental and dramatic change. In digital infrastructure research, which this study belongs to, the focal object is the installed base, or, alternatively, in the research on path creation. The research on the installed base emphasises the continuity of large structures, focusing on cultivation rather than planning (Ciborra et al., 2000), on path dependency, and lock-in of the installed base (Aanestad et al., 2017). Seen from this perspective, transformations are unlikely and unwanted. The path creation research focuses, in contrast, on agency and innovation, arguing that the seemingly sticky structures may also serve as contexts for action (Garud et al., 2009). Disruptive shifts in large infrastructures have equally been conceptualised as path creation or as digital innovation (Henfridsson et al., 2009) and are characterised by shifts to new configurations of actors and technology. At the bottom of these perspectives lies the question, what does it take to change the course of a large programme?

In his work Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes (1970), the philosopher Imre Lakatos argued, with the aim of identifying a middle ground between Popper and Kuhn, that scientific programmes tend to continue until a viable alternative is presented. ‘The history of science refutes both Popper and Kuhn: on close inspection both Popperian crucial experiments and Kuhnian revolutions turn out to be myths: what normally happens is that progressive research programmes replace degenerating ones’ (Lakatos, 1978: 6). ‘There is no falsification before the emergence of a better theory’ (Lakatos, 1970: 119). Although Lakatos’ context is different than ours, we take the insight to be true also for large IT initiatives; that a programme will normally continue until a better alternative is available. However, how does a new alternative emerge in this context? We believe that the role of discourse in these transformations is under-researched in the literature. Or posed differently, how do new ideas lead to digital transformation?

In order to make sense of these quite complex processes, we build on the digital infrastructure literature (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010; Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013) and suggest a simple conceptual framework, which is illustrated in Figure 1.

First, the digital infrastructure consists of the interconnected systems and users that produce the services. Most of these structures have grown since the 1990s, or before, and include a complex mix of silo systems, networks, middleware, cloud services and user interfaces. Recently, they also include access for citizens through various apps and are growing into some of the largest digital infrastructures we know. Second, while impressive, the solutions are often not satisfying the needs of users and citizens, and politicians are under heavy pressure to improve them. The key approach is programmes, that is, large initiatives to offer new services to the citizens, integrate IT silos and increase efficiency, usually with a number of projects and co-operation with vendors. Third, many of these programmes have encountered problems that have triggered engaged discourses at various levels, both in the public and professional communities. Sauer and Willcocks (2007) described these discourses, in somewhat gloomy terms, as Greek choruses, where different stakeholder groups play blame games against each other.

The evolution of the three processes is highly interrelated, but they develop at different speeds. The digital infrastructure develops relatively slowly, as the large installed base of technology and organisational use is difficult to change (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010). This is countered by new programmes that aim to change them, usually lasting 5–10 years, before they are replaced by newer programmes with a different framing (Øvrelid et al., 2017). Sometimes, a competing initiative with a better solution (and discourse) overtakes a large programme (Aanestad and Jensen, 2011). In parallel, a public and professional discourse aiming to influence the programmes goes on in the media and in the professional arenas. It is fueled by the rich ecology of the digital world, with new technologies from vendors, new frameworks from consultancies and new insights from research.

Programmes, then, are often high-level umbrella designations established in order to concretise strategic goals. These strategic goals are conditioned by discourse. The goals of these programmes are often digital transformation, that is, a substantial change in both technology and processes (Fichman et al., 2014; Hess et al., 2016). Projects are more structured devices created to put programmes into practice. Projects are important for discourse – which is fast and volatile – to make an impact on the digital infrastructure. In a way then, something that ends up as transformation began as discourse.

There are several unresolved questions in this landscape, such as what does it take to change a large programme? how does a discourse become a force to change the digital infrastructure? how does public and professional discourse influence decision makers? and why do some, apparently reasonable and justified discourses, make no impact? We subsume these questions in our research question: what is the role of discourse in the transformation of digital infrastructures?

We proceed by reviewing the digital infrastructure research and the IS discourse literature. To develop our argument, we introduce a framework building on Foucault’s work; in particular, his concept of ‘discursive formations’. As our research context, we chose eHealth and the professional discourse<sup>1</sup> on eHealth infrastructures. We chose a large digitalisation programme in Norway and investigated how three different initiatives aimed at changing the course of the programme. In order to analyse the interplay between discourse, programmes and infrastructure, we build on critical realism, that is, we identify causal mechanisms. We offer two contributions: first, we outline a framework to understand the role of discursive formations in transforming digital infrastructures and second, we propose a set of configurations to explain how contextual factors and mechanisms contingently lead to digital transformation.

## Related research

## Evolution and transformation of digital infrastructures

The notion of information infrastructures emerged in the 1990s in order to describe and understand the tendency of increasing interconnectedness of IS operating in networks rather than in isolation. The understanding of IT as information infrastructures created a richer and broader understanding of the interaction between technology and organisation in the development of complex infrastructures. First, it changed the perspective from single organisations to organisational networks and from systems to infrastructures (Ciborra et al., 2000; Hanseth, 2002; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010). This also allowed a global and emergent perspective on IS (Ciborra et al., 2000). Second, it allowed research on different settings like health (Aanestad et al., 2017; Aanestad and Jensen, 2011; Braa et al., 2007; Hanseth and Aanestad, 2003), telecom (Nielsen and Aanestad, 2006), government and manufacturing (Ciborra, 2005; Henningsson and Hanseth, 2011) and on different levels like practices (Aanestad and Hanseth, 2000), organisation (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013), industry and society (Gal et al., 2008) and technologies, standards and Internet (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010; Hanseth and Monteiro, 1997) to be performed under the general notion of information infrastructures (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013).

The ‘classic’ studies on information infrastructures (Ciborra et al., 2000; Hanseth and Aanestad, 2003; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010) emphasised how infrastructures often deviate from their planned purpose and that the corporate context should be looked at as a runaway learning organisation (Ciborra et al., 2000). Because it is ineffective to use classic control efforts to plan the evolution of these heterogeneous networks, they should rather be looked at as organisms to be cultivated. A central premise for this way of framing infrastructural growth is the infrastructures’ inherent pathdependent embeddedness (Aanestad et al., 2017; Monteiro, 1998). Path dependency means that innovation initiatives have to relate to the existing installed base, and only be changed step-by-step through bootstrapping (Aanestad and

Jensen, 2011; Grisot et al., 2014; Hanseth and Aanestad, 2003). Although infrastructures are dynamic, they resist innovation initiatives and strategic plans that come from top management (Ciborra et al., 2000). As a consequence, the infrastructural evolution is unpredictable.

The literature on digital infrastructures (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013; Henfridsson et al., 2014; Tilson et al., 2010) differs slightly from information infrastructures in focusing more on the specific features of digital technology. This literature introduces more market-oriented concepts like ‘path-changing innovation’, ‘future path of human activity’, ‘innovation paths’ (Henfridsson and Yoo, 2014) and ‘multiple value paths’ (Henfridsson et al., 2018) as well as ‘innovation capability’ (Svahn et al., 2017) and draws attention to the ‘digital infrastructure as a category of IT artefacts’ (Tilson et al., 2010: 748). This orientation embeds a view of digital innovation as ‘the carrying out of new combinations of digital and physical components to produce novel products’ (Yoo et al., 2010: 725). According to Yoo et al. (2010), ‘digital innovation . . . requires a firm to revisit its organizing logic and its use of corporate IT infrastructures’ (p. 725). The main motivation behind this orientation is to break away from established innovation paths (Svahn et al., 2017) in order to enable rapid scaling (Huang et al., 2017).

The first stream of literature focuses on infrastructures evolution as incremental and gradual, while the second sees infrastructural change as more sudden, as disruptive and transformative. The first sees cultivation as a central concept for understanding information infrastructure maintenance and growth. Metaphors like organic growth and bootstrapping are aligned with the ‘runaway image’ of information infrastructures. The other sees innovation (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013), path creation (Henfridsson et al., 2014) and transformation<sup>2</sup> (Tilson et al., 2010) as more adequate ways of understanding infrastructure evolution – and disruption.<sup>3</sup>

In our research, we are interested in the role of discourse in transforming digital infrastructures. This entails understanding the explicit concretisation needed for discourse (fast and volatile) to change and transform a digital infrastructure (slow and resistant) through programmes and projects. Within the infrastructure literature, discourse is an implicit power operating within the theory, but its particular role in transformation is not clear. This stream of literature is abundant with metaphors and concepts that implicitly use discourse to explain how they evolve, but the explicit role of discourse in providing causal links between ideas, visions, strategies and infrastructure have not been investigated in a detailed way. The independent role of discourse apart from programmes, projects and infrastructure is not explicitly addressed.

## The role of discourse in IS research

In the broader field of IS, however, the role of discourse has been brought to attention in several ways. While some studies have been concentrating on the use of narratives and buzzwords in management practices (Baskerville and Myers, 2009; Swanson, 2002), others have looked at the relationship between discourse and practice during the implementation of technology or technological routines in organisations (Ellingsen and Monteiro, 2008; Gidlund, 2015). For instance, Bissett (2002) discusses the role of discourse in hiding the consequences of war technology. These studies, often critical, inspect the difference between discourse and reality, such as the role of discourse as an element in concealing management’s actual intentions, or as an ‘existential’ aid for the individual to handle change (Edenius, 2002; Wastell, 2002).

The research on organising vision (Ellingsen and Monteiro, 2008; Swanson and Ramiller, 1997) or policy discourses (Klecun, 2016) gives insight into the role of discourse in technological strategies and implementation. The success of organising visions depends on the flexibility and legitimation enabled through the discourse, and reciprocal transformations over time maintain the vision among the stakeholders. The distinct roles of discourse and materiality respectively, however, is not clear. Ellingsen and Monteiro’s empirical case from the Norwegian health sector is a striking example of the use of technological vision in project implementation. In this study, however, the discursive strategies operate at two different levels, where one of them – operating in the ‘hidden’ – wins. These studies do not explicitly address the causal relationship between discourse and infrastructure.

In the relatively recent research stream within IS on socio-materiality (Nyberg, 2009; Scott and Orlikowski, 2013, drawing on Barad, 2007), a key issue is the entanglement between the social and the technical, that is, the inseparability between meaning and matter (Nyberg, 2009; Scott and Orlikowski, 2013). In his paper on the role of discourse in an National Health Service (NHS) project, Constantinidis (2013) investigated how communicative action mediates and is mediated by IT in strategic projects. By observing strategic meetings and analysing strategic documents, his main contribution is the insight that discourse and technology work in constitutive entanglements, that is, that narrative brings ‘the agency and power of material objects’ (p. 216) to the forefront in specific organisational contexts.

In our work, in line with the critical realist perspective, we see activities in socio-technical organisations as consisting of both social and material objects that may or may not be drawn upon in particular situations, but which also exist apart from this interaction. This means that rather than looking at the relationship between discourse and infrastructure as constitutively entangled, we investigate them separately to see how they may change each other over time. Overall, we are interested in how discourse participates in transforming the digital infrastructure in situations where the existing arrangements are seen as inadequate to handle the emergent requirements.

In IS research, two main theories on discourse are dominant: Habermas’ theory on communication and Foucault’s theory on the role of discourse. While Habermas is looking for the ideal speech situation, which refers to the ‘conditions under which rational agents would be able to find a consensus by using the exchange of arguments’ (quoted by Stahl (2004: 4331)), Foucault (1972) is more occupied with reality as-is. However, in his book on archaeology, he does in fact provide a framework for analysing the discursive elements and consequently a way of understanding the desirability and legitimacy of discourse. In the next section, we outline this framework.

## Theoretical framework: discourses and discursive formations

The core investigations of French philosopher and historian of ideas Michel Foucault are concerned with the modern epistemes that emerged in the early modern age, their patterns of growth and their content. Foucault’s work from the 1950s until his death in 1984 consists of an impressive amount of themes from medicine, psychology, philosophy, economy and art, as well as political science and criminal law, always with a historical twist. Willcocks (2004) categorises Foucault’s authorship in three periods. In the first period (1950s–1969), he was mainly occupied with archaeology, discourse and knowledge. He then (1969–1976) moved through Nietzsche towards genealogy, power relations and power/knowledge. A new crisis emerged after the first volume of ‘History of Sexuality, and in his last years (1976–1984), he primarily occupied himself with questions surrounding ethics and care of the self. The use of Foucault within the field of IS has mainly been inspired by his second (genealogical) period, with his focus on the exercise of power, the way technology and technological knowledge embodies disciplinarian tendencies and strengthens power relations (see Willcocks, 2004, 2006, for a broad review). Our main use of Foucault stems from the first of these periods, with his occupation with understanding scientific and technological discourse and his framework of discursive formations to analyse the content of discourses. His book The Archaeology of Knowledge from 1972 outlines his method for performing these investigations.

## Statements, discourses and discursive formations

Foucault’s archaeology is primarily grounded in historical practice more than philosophical theory (Gutting, 1989). It is consequently not a theory of language, or of ideal philosophical conceptions of truth. Rather, Foucault’s archaeology establishes techniques for revealing how a discipline develops validity and objectivity (Gutting, 1989). The archaeological method is, consequently, not about identifying singular statements and how they transcend and change knowledge areas. Foucault’s method is about seeing discourse as practice, as a way of attracting other elements in a network. The decisive factor is not the underlying intention, but the way the discursive actions enter into specific knowledge formations (Schaanning, 2000). This means that discourse depends on a reference field in order to evolve and become powerful.

Three important entities in Foucault’s archaeological framework are statements, discourses and discursive formations. Statements are ‘the atom of discourse’ and defined as ‘a function that cuts across a domain of structures and possible unities, and which reveals them, with concrete contents, in time and space’ (Foucault, 1972: 98). This helps us to see statements as a contingent linguistic element (a word, a concept, a claim) that has a function across a domain and which is necessary in order to enable discourse. Discourse is ‘constituted by a group of . . . statements . . . assigned to particular modalities of existence’ (Foucault, 1972: 121). Stuart Hall builds on this to define discourse as ‘a group of statements which provide a language for talking about – i.e. representing – a particular kind of knowledge about a topic’ (Hall and Gieben, 1992: 291). Since discursive formations are defined as ‘the enunciative system that governs a group of verbal performances’ (Foucault, 1972: 130), we can understand discourse as a verbal performance consisting of several statements seeking or belonging to an associative field.

The statement leading to a discourse has to attach itself to a surrounding field – or a network, a field of association – in order to become discursive formations. First, this network includes means or techniques used in knowledge creation. Second, it contains practices, the organisational or cultural environments where such means are used systematically. Third, strategic considerations are connected to the use of means in the performance of practices. This relates to strategies, tactics or ways of thinking. The fourth constitutive element is institutions. An institution in this context is an apparatus that enables performance of specific discourses, strategies, practices and aids (Schaanning, 2000). The fifth element is discourse and regards the speech and writing associated with the production of a particular knowledge. Consequently, discourse is not enough per se. It has to be concretised through programmes and projects in order to have real impact.

A central core of discourse is, as we have seen, the statement, and the statements in reference to a subject, a field and materiality. According to Foucault (1972), a ‘statement must have a substance, a support, a place, and a date’ (p. 113), that is, it must be ‘anchored’ to something material (Schaanning, 2000). Foucault, then, is occupied with the field into which discourse enters, and the need to analyse the whole network to understand the content and the implications of the discourse. Even though this enables us to see possible connections between discourse and other formative elements, it remains to describe how discourse becomes discursive formations.

## The anatomy of discursive formations

In Foucault’s (1972) own definition, a discursive formation ‘is the general enunciative system that governs a group of verbal performances’ (p. 130). The term is, however, also used in particular discourses governed by this principle, discourses where concerns, perspectives, concepts and themes are shared. This embeds the idea that verbal performances need to contain some sort of shared ‘truth’ in order to be adopted and governed within the enunciative system.

In our context, we see discursive formations as systematic accumulations of discourse within particular knowledge areas and in particular situations that provide a way of addressing and potentially solving the particular challenges within a programme. This also indicates that a discursive formation is an accumulation of singular statements, which through time may turn into discourses when sufficient amounts of actors find the statements reasonable. These actors belong to certain institutional arrangements that may or may not accept the discourse.

Foucault proposes four analytical techniques to identify discursive formations (Table 1). First, a discursive formation can be identified by its area of interest; what it is about, the object of its occupation. In Foucault’s own work, these entities are Madness, Sexuality or The Clinic, but the main point is that these objects are constituted by the virtue of the different discourses on the object. By asking a question like ‘what is the key challenge that is addressed?’ or ‘what is discussed within this area of challenge?’ the researcher may gradually understand the role of discourse. The identification of the object is done through investigating the areas (surface of emergence and surface of appearances), where these challenges are talked about and identify how and from whom the forming of the object is done (authorities of delimitation). This can be done by analysing and relating the different ways actors talk about the object to one another and what particular grid of specification the actors are building their knowledge on. Examples from Foucault’s studies are psychiatrists studying the soul, clinicians studying the body or the history of disease within the patient’s family (Schaanning, 2000). The object consists of different logics of classification from different fields.

Second, the identification of enunciative modalities is about analysing the action of the different actors participating within or close to the network. Who is speaking, with what right and qualification? From what institutional site are they speaking and with relation to which possible networks? The actor’s utterance is of special importance if we understand the actor’s enunciative modality, that is, the mode of the actor, where, when and why the utterance is made. Foucault is particularly interested in the relationship between the speaker’s correspondence to the actual debate.

The third way of identifying and defining discursive formations could be to inspect how concepts are established and used. This can be done by investigating the relationship between concepts (types of dependence); by investigating their coexistence, or by looking at the procedures of intervention between concepts. Coexistence entails looking at how discourses travel between disciplines or by how old discourse is given new life. Intervention refers to how concepts from other fields are brought in and radically change the understanding of the object.

Finally, the fourth way, the formation of strategies, is occupied with different social aspects: how the discourse is ‘spread’ between fields, why something lives on and something disappears. Decimation deals with who decides which strategies are the most significant, why and the result of these decisions.

## The transformative power of discourse

According to Elder-Vass (2010), Foucault ‘tends to downplay the causal nature of his argument’ even if his project requires that discursive formations have causal power (p. 150). What is it that makes Foucault’s archaeological approach an investigation of the causal power of discourse?

In this article, three insights from Foucault’s archaeological method are of particular importance in framing discourse as causality: technological discourse is attractive, it is sticky and it may participate in transformation.

First, discourse – in our case technological discourse – is framed as attractive, important, solution-oriented and ‘rich’. A key element within discursive formations is the object. The legitimisation of discourse depends on the object being spoken of in a way deemed as qualified. A qualified discourse on the object may potentially trigger stakeholders, that is, the discourse depends on various institutional and structural elements that give the object power, and increases the knowledge of the object.

We see that programmes in difficulties will visit and investigate arenas where technological discourse is performed in order to be informed, to identify possible solutions and to extend the level of competency. Technological discourse can be constructive, creative and inspiring. According to Gutting (1989), ‘the savoir of a discursive formation provides the objects, and themes (theoretical strategies) that are necessary for a body of scientific connaissance’ (p. 251). According to Elder-Vass (2010), however, the principle that regulates the discourse and makes it more or less attractive and eligible is unclear. We use the opportunity to investigate the causal power of discourse in order to understand the causes that regulate the interaction between discourse and infrastructure.

Second, the archaeological method is also about identifying action-oriented relationships. The interaction between discourse and programmes enables a concretisation of discourse; it associates with and may attach itself to a surrounding network of statements, utilities, practices and institutions. Through this attachment, discourses may gradually turn into something more concrete and thus emerge into discursive formations. In order to come within the reach of the digital infrastructure, discursive formation needs projects.

This is our third insight from Foucault’s archaeological method. We are interested in how these discursive formations, through attachment, participate in transforming digi tal infrastructures. Discursive formations have, according to Foucault, transformative power, not only through discourse but also through their relationships and regularities in accordance with existing statements, utilities, practices and institutions (Foucault, 1972; Schaanning, 2000). Foucault’s (1972) archaeology is a systematic way of approaching ‘the analysis of transformations’ (p. 190). A transformation of discourse does not mean, however, that all the elements are altered, but that statements are governed by or included in new rules of formation (Foucault, 1972). This means that discursive formations, in order to participate in transformation, embed some sort of causal significance. They enable a connection between words and things, between subject and object and between agency and structure which possibly transforms the area of which they are a part. They also have the power to connect to material ity and, through this relationship, transform entities.

## Method

To investigate these complex interactions, we chose to conduct a comparative case study in the health care sector, enabling us to analyse, compare and contrast cases in their context (George and Bennett, 2005).

## Research approach and selection of cases

As stated in the introduction, we are interested in technological discourse and its implication on particular programmes, projects and the digital infrastructure. While the public discourse is certainly important, the professional discourse is more informed, more finely granulated and more responsible for finding solutions. Our point of departure was the difficulties experienced by a large digitalisation initiative in Norway: the Digital Renewal programme in Health South-East region. Health South-East serves a population of around 2.8 million, with more than 40 hospitals and 80,000 employees. The centralised IT service company Hospital Partner has around 1300 employees. Health South-East and Hospital partner govern a portfolio of around 4000 clinical systems, such as electronic patient record (EPR), lab systems, medical charts, chemotherapy and maternity records. Both in budget terms (6 billion Norwegian Krone (NOK)) and in digital transformation ambitions, digital renewal was one of the largest IT programmes in Norwegian history and was established in 2012 with two key aims: to standardise both work processes and technology across the region.

Although powerful, with highly centralised structure and control, the programme experienced a number of implementation challenges at various hospitals. In addition, the central IT-unit Hospital Partner had difficulties in serving the local hospital sufficiently, and local needs had low priority. Consequently, a rising number of clinicians complained about lack of local autonomy and innovation. This started a broader discourse, including clinicians, IT experts, vendors and the IT press, on alternative approaches. Some of these discourses emerged gradually as discursive formations, that is, as more focused initiatives emerged, aiming at initiating new initiatives. Some of these were able to attract support and money.

To investigate this development, we conducted an indepth case study (Wynn and Williams, 2012) of three such initiatives. Our selection criteria were that (1) the case should include an innovation initiative within an existing large infrastructure, (2) there was an identifiable discourse and (3) there should be a clear outcome of the initiative. We investigated three cases that satisfied these criteria, which are shown in Table 2.

The MediCloud project was a small innovation initiative that emerged when shortcomings in the huge eHealth programme Digital Renewal were acknowledged. The aim of MediCloud was to enable app developers to access the central clinical systems. From 2013–2016, MediCloud was subject to increasing interest, prompting the arrangement of the popular ‘innovathon’ track at the national eHealth conference in 2015, as well as several well-visited workshops in the autumn and winter of 2015 and 2016. However, the initiative ran out of steam in 2017 and eventually led to no specific results.

The SamKAD project at Aker was a successful innovation initiative at an emergency unit in Oslo, with the aim of improving patient flow internally, and with the city districts. However, from 2014 to 2016, Aker met significant resistance from the existing governance regimes and had to apply for funding from councils and similar in order to initiate and complete the milestones. The funding enabled Aker to implement a lightweight solution in order to improve patient-flow processes and later interaction with city districts.

The Kalnes project was performed at a new large hospital in Østfold County (Figure 2), which adopted the full regional infrastructure, but – based on extensive process innovation – introduced a new layer of lightweight IT<sup>4</sup> solutions for logistics and patient communication. The collaboration between all levels of the organisation through an innovative development strategy was relatively successful, and Kalnes was consequently outlined as a possible model for regional strategies.

## Data collection

Data collection (Table 3) was conducted during the period 2013–2018 and included 54 interviews, around 60h of observation, 13 seminars and workshops and analyses of a large number of documents. We interviewed managers, clinicians and IT personnel and focused on how key actors in the projects initiated the discourse, how arguments were built and alliances with decision-makers and vendors were built.

In each of the cases, our data collection started with interviews and discussions, where management and project leaders presented the main goals as well as the organisation of the IT-oriented innovation initiative. We then conducted observations; in the case of MediCloud, among project participants, and in the case of Aker and Kalnes, within the emergency unit and the wards, where challenges related to process flow were substantial. We identified core actors, their requirements and views on the solution and fresh ideas related to shortcomings and challenges. In addition, we analysed a broad range of organisational and technical documents in order to identify key challenges and possible solutions.

## Data analysis

Our data analysis strategy builds on combining Foucault’s (1972) archaeological framework with critical realism (Bhaskar, 1998), enabling a deep investigation of emerging discourses, their content and their consequent power to change system trajectories. To identify causal mechanisms and configurations, we drew on the technique of retroduction (Wynn and Williams, 2012).

Foucault understood that a speaker’s statement will always end up in a field where predefined rules and regulations exist and that to have power, the ability to change or reform a field or a programme, discursive formations have to relate to this pre-existing field (Hardy, 2010). We used this as an argument to align discursive formations with the critical realist philosophy<sup>5</sup> and to integrate discursive formations as a constitutive part of the realist vocabulary. Seeing discursive formation as a causal mechanism, operating externally to or internally within digitalisation programmes enabled us to inspect the interrelation between discursive formations and other mechanisms.

In critical realism, a central insight is the contingency of causality (Bhaskar, 1998; Sayer, 1992). This means that mechanisms that are triggered in one context may be left untouched in other contexts. This also indicates the existence of multiple causal paths that may or may not be triggered leading to a certain outcome. A methodological challenge is consequently to investigate how different mechanisms are configured and triggered to produce successful outcomes; or rather, which mechanisms influence the particular outcome of a certain programme within a particular digital infrastructure.

As there may be many causal paths, we need a way to structure our approach. The configurational approach (El Sawy et al., 2010) acknowledges the centrality of digital infrastructures in both obtaining strategic advantage and in gaining an improved understanding of the complexity associated with multiple causal paths. A configurational approach enables analysis of possible configurations by focusing on mechanisms and relevant context variation (the Context-Mechanisms-Outcome (CMO) scheme) to explain a particular outcome (Pawson and Tilley, 1997). This particular configuration consists of three central elements. The ‘context’ is the social or cultural conditions within which groups or programmes exist. The ‘mechanisms’ are the causal structures that may or may not be activated in order to produce certain ‘outcomes’.

Figure 3 demonstrates our configurational approach. We see discursive formations as a mechanism and investigate the causal relationship between discursive formations and other mechanisms in producing a certain outcome. In critical realism, this is called retroduction where plausible mechanisms that may have generated the empirical events are suggested, identified or eliminated (Sayer, 1992).

In the literature, we identified three contextual factors relevant to transformation of digital infrastructures. The two first factors are based on Lakatos’ (1970) claim that transformative changes occur through acknowledging the existence of perceived problems (PP in Figure 3) and through the introduction of a clear alternative (availability of a technological solution, AT). The PPs open up for external discourses which then relates itself to other mechanisms in the infrastructure. The third contextual factor is organisational anchoring (OA). It is well documented in IS research that many innovation initiatives fail because of insufficient social, managerial or technical links to the main organisation (Böhl et al., 2016; Cash et al., 2008). We understand OA as the degree to which a transformation initiative is supported by key actors in the related organisation or infrastructure. As shown in Figure 3, the three key factors are interrelated, but they are basically independent of each other. To summarise our configurational approach, we propose that radical change (‘Result’) in digital infrastructures is caused by the interaction between a discursive formation and other infrastructural mechanisms, conditioned by three contingent factors; PPs, ATs and OA.

Finally, Kalnes was a modern hospital where process innovation strategies addressed challenges related to waiting time, patient flow and interaction between the emergency unit and health wards.

We compared the three cases and found different levels of credibility/causality that made an impact on the cases’ degree of success, and on whether the discourse was translated into something actual in the digital infrastructure. ## Discourse, programmes and infrastructure

The framework established in the introduction of this article outlines our general analytical distinction between discourse, programmes and projects and digital infrastructure. Figure 4 takes this further and defines how we relate to these distinctions in our case study.

We define discourse as a way of constituting knowledge together with social practices. The general technological discourse concerning eHealth in Norway was re-oriented in 2014 towards innovation (Øvrelid et al., 2017). Based on this major discourse on innovation, several discursive formations emerged. We see discursive formations as systematic accumulations of discourse within particular knowledge areas and in particular situations that provide a way of addressing and potentially solving the particular challenges within a programme. The identified discursive formations particularly related to the Digital Renewal programme were as follows: the bimodal organisation, digital coordination and interaction and the digital hospital (left column in Table 5). While programmes are large-scale initiatives that aim at digital transformation, projects are concretised arrangements organised in order to address distinct parts of the challenges within the programme. Three concrete projects were established and benefitted from these discursive formations: MediCloud, Aker and Kalnes. These projects enabled interventions into the digital infrastructure and were given the opportunity to change it according to the particular goals.

## Case 1. MediCloud: the bimodal organisation

Discursive formation. MediCloud was established in order to speed up innovation within Health South-East. Since Medi-Cloud addressed innovation through open interfaces and cloud-based solutions, they eventually emerged as a possible part of a bimodal strategy. This possibility emerged also because managers in Health South-East were inspired by Gartner discourse on bimodality as a way of combining stability and innovation. The external discourse was very important throughout the project, and MediCloud lived on also because of good timing. Gradually, MediCloud’s existence depended on their status as a bimodal alibi. At a workshop in 2015, the MediCloud manager stated that Health South-East needed a bimodal initiative since ‘it is very difficult to stand with one foot in both camps’. A Digital Renewal programme manager said that the regional management needed support:

we cannot own everything, manage everything and control everything. We have a lot of clean-up to do, but not much time, and we experience a very high pace on the technological development. We need to rethink our governance strategies. Medicloud can be an alternative within this strategy. (Health South-East manager)

MediCloud used this strategic position when continuing their project.

Context. MediCloud addressed a significant shortcoming in the Digital Renewal programme in Health South-East; the lack of innovation and of local autonomy. The MediCloud initiative did not have many resources and was only supported by the management to a certain degree, but nevertheless had the ability to attract a range of technological and organisational participants to attend their workshops and conferences on digital innovation.

Object. MediCloud appeared as a fresh entrepreneurialminded initiative early on and labelled themselves both as an innovation arena and a provider of a technological platform for innovation. The proponents claimed they could provide an access point where app developers, in close collaboration with clinicians, could access health data. Consequently, they positioned MediCloud as a ‘broker’ between app-entrepreneurs and Digital Renewal. From early on, it was unclear whether MediCloud was an innovation arena providing access to health organisations or a platform providing access to health information. As one app developer said, ‘We hope that Medicloud can provide an interface towards health information, but they are not there yet’. The MediCloud managers thrived on the ambivalence by saying that they were ‘both an arena for digital innovation’ and ‘and a provider of an interface towards clinical information’.

Enunciative modalities. MediCloud’s role as a boundary resource for innovation attracted many stakeholders both internally – Health South-East managers, Hospital Partner employees and managers – as well as externally, where professional IT organisations like IBM, Cerner, Apple and Microsoft participated with inspiration together with many start-up firms. They all saw MediCloud as a way of gaining access to precious core health information. The ability to attract these stakeholders strengthened the discursive formation decisively but contained at least to challenging requirements. First, large IT corporations presupposed that their rules were followed, that is, renegotiating contract regulations on data access from thirdparty actors. Second, it required that MediCloud became part of the heavyweight governance regime anchored in the existing organisation.

Concepts. The discourse on MediCloud was conditioned by ambitious goals and big concepts. At the well-visited Innovathon track on the HelsIT conference in Trondheim in 2015, it was claimed that MediCloud was the ‘future innovation arena within national ICT’. MediCloud appeared as an ‘umbrella’ for small innovative health IT initiatives, stating, ‘We will create a playground where health professionals as well as innovators and entrepreneurs can experiment on health data’. The playground was supposed to be both educational and innovative.

Strategies. MediCloud positioned themselves early on as deliverer of ‘cloud-based architecture’. This technological orientation – never put into production – was, however, often relegated to a subordinate position on behalf of radical discourse. MediCloud claimed that they would radically change the health IT governance by removing old systems and start everything from scratch. During 2016 and 2017, the ambivalent discourse of MediCloud continued, but the MediCloud initiative had lost some of its confidence and power, causing Health South-East management to look elsewhere for innovation initiatives usable as a bimodal initiative in the Digital Renewal programme.

Mechanisms. In the MediCloud case, we identify one activated mechanism: the discursive formations. The discursive formation of the bimodal organisation was centred on MediCloud’s ability to establish an innovative mind-set within Health South-East, promising to provide a boundary resource for innovation. Since health information is increasingly valuable for a broad range of actors both from the public and private sectors, the provision of this boundary resource was potentially very important. MediCloud claimed to be a ‘future innovation arena within national ICT’ as well and positioned themselves as a radical opposition to with existing regimes. The discursive formation was through the programme and project concretised from being a bimodal organisation, to something more specific (innovation arena and technological boundary resource) that came within reach of the digital infrastructure.

Outcome. Gradually, it became clear that MediCloud was not able to provide a technological platform where the dis course could be turned into infrastructural action. As time went by and no platform was made – they were not able to trigger mechanisms that could connect discourse and infrastructure – discourse gradually evaporated.

## Case 2. AKER: digital coordination and interaction

Discursive formation. In the government coordination reform (issued in 2012), a political direction was given to use technology to address the challenge of poorly coordinated services and develop and improve municipality services closer to where patients live (Ausen et al., 2017; Norwegian Government, 2008). Some years later, several reports pointed to the lack of efficient interaction (Office of the Auditor General of Norway, 2016), such as the fact that patients were repeatedly diagnosed when moving between the primary and the secondary sectors (Norwegian Board of Health Supervision, 2016; Romøren et al., 2017). Increased digital ised interaction was viewed as central in reaching the goals of this strategy, and Aker took advantage of this discursive formation in establishing services that developed the digital interaction both in terms of clinical operation, increased quality in patient treatment, improved user experience and improved resource utilisation (Ausen et al., 2017).

Context. The SamKAD project was established because the existing infrastructure did not solve challenges related to internal logistics and external coordination between Aker and city districts. The initiatives to improve the infrastructure met some resistance. The EPR providers refused to participate, Oslo Municipality and management at Aker collaborated only to a limited extent, leaving it to the project to establish financial means to plan and carry out the project. An enthusiastic project manager together with a doctor and the manager of the emergency unit co-operated in three ways. First, they identified councils and similar to apply for funding. Then, because of the unwillingness from the EPR providers to participate, the project members identified needed technology for improving internal communi cation when receiving, treating and discharging patients. Finally, they engaged an expert organisation on organisational processes to help them identify improvement areas. Although the project did not have profound management support, the clinical actors, their executives and staff all participated in the project. As the project manager said: ‘We have a high degree of employee involvement. This is tremendously challenging, but it increases the quality of our services’. The planning and implementation enabled the different actors to customise the processes gradually.

Object. At Aker, the main object of the change processes was capacity utilisation, to improve internal logistics in order to address the peak of tasks which piled up during the day. The peak was a congestion of patient admissions, patient visits performed by the doctors and patient discharges. Manual routines dominated the registering of incoming patients and patient discharges, and Aker struggled to get an overview of internal resources. ‘The old systems do not harmonise with the dynamics of three-day hospitalisation; it was made in a doctor’s office many years ago’, said a project manager.

Capacity utilisation also requires improving the coordination between Aker and city districts. Aker and the districts interact through exchanging care-related messages, but these messages include a lot of information, and it is often difficult to determine which information is most recent and most vital. Consequently, the districts often called back to Aker asking about the patient condition, despite having received the care-related messages. Aker wanted to improve communications using a more transparent standard called ‘Activities in daily life’ (ADL), which covered most of the information the city districts needed and made use of visual qualities, making it easier for doctors to identify particularly important treatment areas.

Enunciative modalities. The discourse around the project grew gradually. Initially, actors like Oslo Municipality and existing EPR suppliers provided a lot of resistance partly in order to maintain rules and regulations protecting the existing infrastructure, but through a vital project manager, SamKAD was able to attract a range of stakeholders. Sam-KAD applied for research council funding and identified the relevant IT-system to solve the challenges related to capacity utilisation and coordination. The interest in the project grew both from organisations that wanted to cooperate with SamKAD and from other healthcare units that wanted to learn from the project.

Concepts. SamKAD addressed two important shortcomings in the existing infrastructure: inadequate organisation of internal logistics and resource- and energy-intensive interaction with city districts. The concepts of improved logistics and improved coordination and interaction was aligned with strategy documents from health ministries and audit authorities, which claimed that lack of coordination leads to a high amount of unnecessary resource use and insufficient quality of health services.

Strategies. SamKAD took the task seriously, hired an expert organisation to identify and describe organisational processes and analysed all their internal processes. The resulting 24 h at KAD model was used as a central map and ‘cognitive tool’ to enable the organisational actors to participate in the process innovation. Their plans to include city districts reinforced the reputation of SamKAD as an innovative project, and their confidence and ability to act impressed managers internally and externally.

Mechanisms. In the AKER case, we identified two active mechanisms: discursive formations and connection. Aker had a more concrete and less ambitious goal than Medi-Cloud. Aker concentrated on defining concrete goals related to improved logistics within the local emergency unit and then gradually improve the interaction with city districts. The connection mechanism is based on the fact that Aker was able to connect discourse and infrastructure by implementing a technological system – electronic whiteboards and mobile technology – which enabled materialisation of discourse.

Outcome. At Aker, the implementation of a lightweight infrastructure (Bygstad, 2016) only integrated to a small extent with the underlying EPR-infrastructure, which enabled them to come forward as an inspiration for other health units. The Aker project was visited and inspected by several national and international agencies. This inspirational role strengthened the discursive formation, in which a technological programme attached to discourse made the local and strategic credibility and usability of the constellation stronger. The project evaluation showed that Aker both saved money through more efficient use of resources, and reduced the level of stress, through more structured work activities. According to a nurse, ‘the patients are more satisfied now, as it is calmer here and they get more attention. They never said this earlier’. SamKAD was gradually turned into a complex project, where AKER co-operated with 4 hospitals, 60 nursing homes in 15 city districts and in total 660 general practitioners.

## Case 3. Kalnes: the digital hospital

Discursive formation. The Kalnes case is an example of how management-driven discourse from the outside can inspire internal and collective participation. Kalnes travelled well under the general discourse of digital innovation and more concretely under a discursive formation that can be labelled the digital hospital. In 2017, Kalnes was certified at Healthcare information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Level 6<sup>6</sup> as the first hospital in Norway, something that gave the Kalnes management a lot of integrity. Kalnes was mentioned in political speeches as a success case and from Health South-East strategists as a proud example of a mature digital hospital.

Context. A distinguishing aspect of the Kalnes project was its overarching focus on process innovation. An important actor incarnating the innovative initiative was the CEO. In 2011, he hired an energetic CIO and a process director as well as establishing a top management team built on process thinking and innovative technology. This also meant that the project both had the regional endorsement and local autonomy with the existence of predictable budgets, active and inspired management and participation from all levels of the organisation.

## Identified mechanisms

In this section, we have identified three mechanisms: discursive formation, connection and transformation, as well as a possible fourth, scaling (Table 6).

We were particularly occupied with the specific role of technology in mechanisms (Bygstad et al., 2016). According to Foucault (1972), the archaeological method ‘distinguishes several possible levels of events within the very density of discourse’ (p. 189). This also means that discourse goes through several concretising levels before it attaches itself to the surrounding network. In our case, discursive formations started as semantically perceivable entities, which, through programmes and projects, change to become a causal force that enables transformation of a digital infrastructure, given certain contextual factors. Through the pro cesses of concretisation, discursive formation becomes a mechanism with distinct abilities to first, identify areas where struggling programmes exists and second, to estab lish strategies to enter into arenas where debates regarding the struggling programme are performed. Third, discursive formations propose solutions to the acknowledged shortcomings in the programme, and fourth, they may (or may not) demonstrate that among its content is a technological solution. Discursive formations, then, is a system of dispersion that denotes the object, enunciative modalities, concepts and strategies included in the external discourse that competes on changing an existing infrastructure. A discursive formation that claims rather than demonstrates its connection to a technological solution may experience low confidence and gradually evaporate and consequently being rejected by the respective infrastructure.

The ‘Connection’ mechanism changes the infrastructure by introducing a new form of materiality that connects discourse and infrastructure. The mechanism is triggered when discursive formation becomes a part of the infrastructure, that is, it attaches itself to the existing infrastructure. The discursive formation relates itself to what Foucault calls its associated domain. The connection between discursive formation and infrastructure gives us a way of understanding materiality; it describes how the discursive formation gains strength by putting infrastructure into production.

The transformation mechanism enables an organisation to obtain specific changes in accordance with acknowledged shortcomings. It can be defined as the result of the connection between discourse and infrastructure when a large part of the infrastructure is changed through this interaction. Transformation has a greater impact than connection and may become the dominating form of production within the organisation.

Scaling denotes ‘a self-reinforcing process by which an infrastructure expands its reach as it attracts new partners by creating incentives for collaboration’ (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013: 918). In our case, this implies that the infrastructural solution is applied to a larger regional or national setting, that is, that it enters into and improves a larger eHealth programme by providing solutions that address acknowledged shortcomings. This mechanism is identified but not activated.

## Discussion

We return to our research question:

RQ 1. What is the role of discourse in the transformation of digital infrastructures?

In answering this question, we offer two contributions. First, we outline a framework to understand the role of discursive formations in digital transformation. Second, we propose a set of configurations to explain how contextual factors and mechanisms contingently lead to digital transformation.

## Transformation of digital infrastructures

Analysing our three cases, we find that two forces are important for a potential transformation of an established digital infrastructure to happen. First, in line with Lakatos (1970), we find that the degree of acknowledgement of the problem is important; it makes management more vulnerable for critique. Second, as we show in the cases, the strength of the discursive formation is critical for the external discourse to disrupt the existing order of things. Discursive formations are, however, in need of programmes and projects in order to arrive within reach of the digital infrastructure.

In our case, the general discourse on digital innovation in the health region enabled three discursive formations to emerge: the bimodal organisation, digital coordination and interaction and the digital hospital (Figure 4 and Table 5). This type of discursive formations made it easier for emergent projects to address shortcomings in the big Digital Renewal programme.

The MediCloud project addressed the lack of innovation within the big Digital Renewal programme and was able to formulate a strategy that set out to solve these shortcomings. Gradually, MediCloud stood out as a bimodal alibi for Digital Renewal. However, the lack of a working prototype gradually reduced the strength of the discursive formation, and MediCloud never became more than a vision.

In the case of Aker, the problem of poor logistics and exhaustive interaction was emphasised. The discursive formation of digital coordination and interaction enabled Aker to establish a project (SamKAD) and to acquire and establish a lightweight infrastructure that made the formation strong enough to disrupt the local patient flow solution. However, the project lacked support from the top managers and was not able to disrupt the lower levels of the infrastructure.

The Kalnes case was different. Based on the discursive formation of the digital hospital, the top management was able to clearly voice the problem, and the discursive formation aligned with a project that gradually became strong enough for a transformation.

At a more general level, we suggest a framework to aid in understanding these processes, illustrated in Figure 5, with a time axis.

Time 1 shows a digital infrastructure in a normal state. Changes are taken care of by cultivation of the installed base and by adaptation to changes in the environment (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010). The discourse is an internal part of the infrastructural arrangements and reproduces the infrastructure.

Time 2 illustrates the case when the digital infrastructure is experiencing a crisis, with external pressures and managers searching for remedies to their problems. In this situation, externally driven and often competing discourses may enter the scene, claiming to have solutions to the programme challenges. Sometimes, managers will deal with the situation, ensuring the setbacks are temporal and continue the programme as planned. Nevertheless, sometimes discourses grow into a discursive formation, with the potential to trigger a larger shift.

Time 3 illustrates the discursive formation and the transformation. This will be a turbulent period. When a distinctive discursive formation emerges, several things may happen. We focus on three factors: first, the discursive formation may be more or less left on the outside of the infrastructure and gradually evaporate; second, the discursive formation may lead to a minor shift in the existing infrastructure, maybe as a separate digital arrangement as a possible candidate solution; and third, the discursive formation may be powerful enough to trigger a real transformation of the digital infrastructure. We elaborate on these alternative trajectories in section ‘Connecting discourse and infrastructure’.

Time 4 illustrates that after the shift, the new internal discourse becomes aligned with the new digital infrastructure, returning gradually to a normal state, where the discourse and infrastructure are well aligned and support each other. We call this the masterialisation of discourse.

The framework deals, at a high level, with the interplay of discourse and infrastructure, and at a more concrete level on how discourse is translated into discursive formations used by programmes and projects in order to change or transform digital infrastructures. Earlier studies on the use of discourse in IS looks at the difference between discourse and reality. This is done by either describing the role of discourse in imagining what technology can achieve (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997), the role of discourse as an element in hiding management’s actual intentions (Ellingsen and Monteiro, 2008) or as an ‘existential’ aid for the individual to handle change (Edenius, 2002; Wastell, 2002). Within the literature on information infrastructures, discourse is treated as an implicit part of the infrastructures dynamics. Growth (Edwards et al., 2009; Star and Ruhleder, 1996), cultivation (Ciborra et al., 2000; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010), bootstrapping (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010) and generativity (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013) are all seen as fundamental although implicit providers of infrastructural evolution. In our framework, we contribute by describing the distinct role of external discourse when eHealth programmes encounter crisis and the process of internalising this discourse into the programme.

The digital infrastructure literature has offered various perspectives on transformation, with different emphasis on stability and cultivation (Aanestad et al., 2017; Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010) and path creation and change (Henfridsson et al., 2014). Our work is congruent with Lyytinen and Newman’s (2008) work of punctuated socio-technical information change theory, which describes the episodic character of IS evolution: it alternates between periods of incremental and dramatic change. Our detailed analysis of the interplay between discourse and infrastructure (illustrated in Figure 5), building on a critical realist approach (Elder-Vass, 2010; Pawson and Tilley, 1997), which proposes that transformations may be explained by various configurations of a set of mechanisms and three conditional factors. Doing this, we produce a more finely granulated analysis of the content of discursive formations, and of the relationship between discourse and other mechanisms lead ing to a certain outcome.

What the framework does not explain is how the discourse actually intervenes and changes the infrastructure, that is, the causal mechanisms that are activated. How this process unfolds will be discussed in section ‘Connecting discourse and infrastructure’.

## Connecting discourse and infrastructure

In section ‘Findings and analyses’, we analysed three cases using Foucault’s (1972) archaeological framework for discourse analysis and the CMO scheme by Pawson and Tilley (1997) for investigating the relationship between context, mechanisms and outcome. Inspired by Elder-Vass (2010), who raised the question: how can discursive formations have causal power, we regard discursive formations as mechanisms that need interaction with other mechanisms in order to persist. Earlier empirical research on digital infrastructures indicates that more than one mechanism is needed to obtain a shift (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013), and we propose that a discursive formation only matters when other mechanisms are triggered.

Building on our configuration framework (Figure 3) and our case analysis, we are now in a position to specify our configurations. In our configuration framework, building on Lakatos (1970) and inspired by El Sawy et al. (2010), we identified three core contingencies: PPs, AT, and OA. PPs relates to the shortcomings of the existing programme or situation. ATs refer to the actual solution that may change the physical part of the infrastructure. OA denotes the degree to which a transformation initiative is supported by key actors in the related organisation or infrastructure.

The case analysis revealed three mechanisms: connection, transformation, and possibly scaling, as well as connecting the fourth, discursive formation, to the infrastructure. The three mechanisms may be seen as sequential, with an increasing impact on the infrastructure. Our resulting set of configurations (Figure 6) can briefly be summarised as follows: the discursive formation may interact with the three mechanisms in the infrastructure, resulting in an outcome. Whether the mechanisms are activated is contingent on the three contextual factors, leading to different configurations.

We have identified three such configurations (Figure 6): evaporation, inspiration and organisational transformation (the first three are illustrated in the figure below, while the fourth configuration, large-scale transformation, is described in text). They reflect how a discursive formation affects the digital infrastructure, that is, the degree of strategic change: the first configuration leads to nothing, while the third leads to transformation of the digital infrastructure. In more theoretic terms, the configurations show how contextual factors and mechanisms are conditioning the outcome of the programme.

Evaporation. This configuration is characterised by an established discursive formation but with little or no impact on the digital infrastructure. It was exemplified by the MediCloud case, where the project was not able to trigger the necessary mechanisms for infrastructure change, and the project thus gradually halted. The reason for this outcome was first that only one of the three contextual prerequisites was met. Although a project distinctly aims to solve the PPs based on deep insight into strategic terrain inside the eHealth environment, a trustworthy technological solution must be available, and the initiative has to have some OA. In addition, the change strategy has to be aligned with or integrated into existing organisational routines, and the technological portfolio has to be extended to cover the new challenges. Discursive formations will encounter difficulties when they mainly rely on the power of the discourse itself.

The evaporation configuration is congruent with Ellingsen and Monteiro’s (2008) point that the flexibility in discourse around organising visions of technology can conceal the fact that discursive and actual strategies are operating at two different levels, and where one of them – operating in the ‘hidden’ – wins, while the other has no real impact in the end.

Inspiration. In this configuration, the discursive formation connects to the infrastructure through the connection mechanism. The fact that discursive formation connects to the infrastructure enables a deeper organisational impact, in which it also addresses two contextual factors: PPs and AT. When these two contextual factors are met, new mechanisms may be triggered.

Our example is the SamKAD project at Aker, where the main goal was to improve the interaction between emergency units and the city districts. Despite several challenges, they implemented a lightweight infrastructure separate from the existing infrastructure, and this enabled them to implement mobile technology and electronic whiteboards in three KAD units. Through this solution, they were able to stand forward as an inspiration for other hospitals dealing with similar challenges; Aker was visited and inspected by several national and international agencies. However, a challenge for the Aker initiative was that while core knowledge personnel participated, management remained partly uninvolved and the existing techno-structure was not willing to co-operate. This lack of profound anchoring affected the project in which budgets and economical predictability were absent, and the project management had to apply for external funding for each project. This threatened the project’s continuity and may also have led to the exhaustion of core personnel including project managers.

The inspiration configuration is similar to Swanson and Ramiller’s (1997) organising vision, in which technological development rests on the collective ability of the community to establish, maintain and negotiate discourse. Although the authors claim that ‘the product of talk or discourse, is effectively linked to material processes’ (Swanson and Ramiller’s, 1997: 468), it is not clear how this takes place. In our framing, the projects proceed or gain extended organisational impact when discourse and materiality are connected.

Organisational transformation. This configuration is characterised by a transformation, that is, the main components and relationships of the digital infrastructure are changed. As illustrated in Figure 6, it is caused by a strong discursive formation and triggered by the three contextual factors. The outcome of the interconnectedness between discursive formations addressing a PP, aligned with a relatively homogeneous organisational approach and with a technological strategy to implement and support the organisational strategies, is a transformed organisation.

Our example is the Kalnes case, where the digital infrastructure was changed by a completely new layer of light weight technology which was interacting systematically with the heavyweight core. While both the MediCloud and the Aker cases lacked or had limited support to put discursive formations into action, the Kalnes case had managerial support from early on. The transformation mechanism was partly triggered using a technological strategy that enables interaction between process innovation initiative and existing infrastructure, and partly by the fact that all levels of the organisation participated, including active and inspired management that provided predictable budgets. A third aspect of this transformation mechanism is that Kalnes, using innovative technology on top of a robust information infrastructure, aligned themselves with modern market economic strategies and fore-fronted themselves as a possible ‘innovation model’ for the bigger regional programme.

Figure 6. Our configuration: CMO.

Yoo et al. (2010) outline the potentially transformative power of IT when software products are organised into modules. We concur, but IT-enabled organisational transformation has at least two other aspects that have to be taken into account: the existing system portfolio and the existing organisational practices. We see the inclusion of these factors into programmes and projects as OA, which is a premise to obtain organisational transformation. The findings indicate that to obtain transformation, the whole organisation has to be involved in the change strategy. This is maybe not surprising, given that earlier studies have demonstrated the involvement of executives in IT management (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1991) and that the entire organisation must be involved in order to cultivate change (Sia et al., 2016). In the health sector, this picture is a bit more challenging, and later studies have pointed to the difficulties of transformation with the use of IT within the health sector (Agarwal et al., 2010). Transformation, we suggest, is obtained when innovative leadership is combined with OA materialised through budgets, plans and collective participation at all levels. In the same vein, the problems encountered by early business process re-engineering initiatives (Hammer, 1990; Hammer and Champy, 1993) can be explained by their very limited focus on OA.

Large-scale transformation. Our data indicate the potential presence of a fourth mechanism leading to a fourth configuration, labelled large-scale transformation. This configu ration is not part of Figure 6. The key mechanism in the configuration is scaling. Henfridsson and Bygstad (2013) defined scaling as a ‘self-reinforcing process by which an infrastructure expands its reach as it attracts new partners by creating incentives for collaboration’ (p. 918). We deviate to some extent from this in order to shed light on particular challenges when moving the focus from the private market to the public health sector. Scaling is consequently defined as ‘a self-reinforcing process by which an infrastructure expands by proposing a configured solution to common challenges’. In our framing, this means the ability of a project to expand the scope of its transformation and become part of a regional or national digital infrastructure.

The example is again epitomised in the Kalnes case. As noted above, the competitiveness of the Kalnes approach may bring them forward as a solution to the innovation requirements within eHealth programmes. If so, the scaling effect will be that the Kalnes model may be used in other regional hospitals as an integrated part of the Digital Renewal programme. Another aspect of the scaling mechanism is its ability to attract users and new partners. This may also include enabling access for external collaborators like other hospitals, health units and general practitioners, but also patients and relatives through a platform strategy or similar.

Summing up, we demonstrate empirically and theoretically that Foucault’s (1972) concept of discursive formation allows us to separate the concerns of discourse and digital infrastructure and also that it helps us to identify and analyse the role of technological discourse within digital innovation and transformation of digital infrastructures. The concept allowed us to perform a detailed analysis of the relationship between discourse and digital infrastructure, which was mainly lacking in previous infrastructure research (Hanseth and Lyytinen, 2010) and in the socio-materiality research (Scott and Orlikowski, 2013). We explain how a discursive formation disperses into an infrastructure and possibly changes it. It also extends the contribution of Constantinidis (2013) in showing that although discourse and technology are entangled, a careful analysis of the interplay between discourse and infrastructure over time (Figure 5) reveals that they can be analytically ‘untangled’.

In addition, our methodological approach demonstrates that bridging of Foucault’s concept of discursive formations with critical realism (initially suggested by Elder-Vass, 2010) is a promising avenue for research in the digital infrastructure field, in which discourse is a prominent phenomenon.

## Limitations

The paper is experimental in several ways. First, it aims to disentangle discourse from infrastructure and thematise their relationship differently from what has been done in earlier infrastructure and IS literature. Second, its use of critical realism, a method for identifying causality, influences both the theoretical and the empirical findings. The use of Foucault framed within the philosophical field of critical realism might challenge the general understanding of how Foucault could be used within the field of IS, particularly because some of the powerful social aspects of Foucault’s discourse analysis may be left unused.<sup>7</sup>

We also acknowledge that data analysis occupied with identifying causal mechanisms as explanations for sociotechnical change may miss some of the rich texture of organisational and social reality. On a more concrete level, we realise that the configurations are not exhaustive and that there might be other configurations. Even though we have performed an internal validation of the mechanisms and the configurations, we have not assessed them with regard to other cases.

## Conclusion

In this article, we propose a way of investigating the role of discourse in the transformation of digital infrastructures. Inspired by Lakatos (1970), we are particularly interested in the discourse that emerges outside a troubled eHealth programme. Using Foucault’s archaeological method, we identify the discourses’ content and motivation and how they structurally and discursively propose a solution to the acknowledged shortcomings of the existing eHealth programme. After having identified what we call discursive formations – a particular accumulation of objects, actors, concepts and strategies – and framed them as a mechanism, we use the CMO configuration from Pawson and Tilley to analyse the relationship between discursive formations and other mechanisms in the infrastructure.

The result of this combination is two contributions to the field of information infrastructures. First, in section ‘Transformation of digital infrastructures’ we outline and explain a way of understanding the gradual internalisation of discourse through three steps. Then in section ‘Connecting discourse and infrastructure’, we propose a set of configurations to explain how contextual factors and mechanisms contingently lead to transformation. The four configurations are conditioned by the actualisation or non-actualisation of contingent mechanisms. While ‘evaporation’ is the result when only one mechanism – discursive formations – is trigged, ‘scaling’ may be the result if all the four mechanisms are trigged. Consequently, our findings also shed light on the mechanisms involved in obtaining organisational change and how they are triggered in sequence.
