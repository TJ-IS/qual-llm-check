# JUST ENOUGH INFORMATION? THE CONTINGENT CURVILINEAR EFFECT OF INFORMATION VOLUME ON DECISION PERFORMANCE IN IS-ENABLED TEAMS<sup>1</sup>

Jeffrey K. Mullins and Rajiv Sabherwal Information Systems Department, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas Fayetteville, AR U.S.A. {jmullins@walton.uark.edu} {rsabherwal@walton.uark.edu}

Information, which is the critical resource provided by information systems (IS), is available in increasing volumes due to rapid technological progress. This causes a tension between the cognitive limitations of individuals and their access to large volumes of information. This tension is important not only for individuals but also for teams, which are increasingly making important organizational decisions. Recognizing this tension, this paper investigates the relationship between information volume and decision performance in IS-enabled teams. We use information processing and schema theories to develop a contextualized theoretical model, wherein information volume has a curvilinear (inverted U-shaped) effect on decision performance, and this effect is moderated by three context-relevant attributes of the team members: computer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, and learning goal orientation. We conducted two complementary studies, with similar experimental contexts and procedures but different types of participants and different tasks. In these studies, 236 individuals in 118 dyads (Study 1) and 200 individuals in 60 small teams (Study 2) made decisions over time using an enterprise resource planning system for a competitive virtual firm in a business simulation. We used objective data on system use and decision outcomes, along with survey data, to test the theoretical model. The two complementary studies find consistent results supporting the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance and find that this relationship is flattened by high computer self-efficacy. Study 1 also supports the hypothesized steepening effects of high computer anxiety and learning goal orientation on the curvilinear relationship. Study 2 does not support these effects of computer anxiety and learning goal orientation but highlights some boundary conditions for them. Together, the studies provide insights into how information volume affects decision performance in IS-enabled teams, and how context-relevant team member attributes moderate this relationship and themselves affect decision performance.

Keywords: Information overload, cognitive load, schema, computer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, learning goal orientation, enterprise resource planning system, curvilinear relationship, team composition, small teams

## Introduction

Vast amounts of information circulate through organizational information systems (IS) (Günther et al., 2017), social media (Chen & Wei, 2019), and communication technologies in general (Saunders et al., 2017). Information is crucial in organizational decision-making (Hoefer & Green, 2016; Sabherwal & King, 1995), but decision makers face challenges from not having enough of the right information (e.g., Wang et al., 2021) to being overloaded by choices (Zhang & Xu, 2021). Thus, they must first decide how much, and what, information to access. This paper’s focus is on information volume, or the amount of information processed (the number of cues accessed) to make decisions (Paul & Nazareth, 2010).

Increasingly, organizations are relying on teams to make decisions (De Dreu et al., 2008; van Knippenberg et al., 2015). Following Kozlowski and Ilgen (2006, p. 79), we view teams as: “(a) two or more individuals who (b) socially interact …; (c) possess one or more common goals; (d) are brought together to perform organizationally relevant tasks; (e) exhibit interdependence …; (f) have different roles and responsibilities; and (g) are together embedded in an encompassing organizational system.” Teams must learn and rely upon complex IS to gather and process relevant information (Ke et al., 2021). Prior research has examined how individuals use varying amounts of information in making decisions (Roetzel, 2019) but without focusing on decision-making by teams. While teams often make better decisions than individuals (e.g., Kocher et al., 2006; Maciejovsky et al., 2013), collaboration in teams can sometimes impair performance (e.g., Kocher & Sutter, 2005; Woolley et al., 2007) and individuals comprising them may experience information overload (Grisé & Gallupe, 1999). However, little research has considered how the volume of information affects performance in team decision-making. Recognizing this gap, the role of IS in enabling team decisions (e.g., Seeber et al., 2017), the increasingly collaborative nature of organizational decision-making, and the possibility that IS may provide too much information (Tarafdar et al., 2015), this paper addresses the question: How does the information volume in a team affect the team’s decision performance?

Some teams handle larger volumes of information or better utilize IS than others to obtain the information needed for superior performance. Extensive prior literature has suggested that team composition—in terms of team members’ knowledge, skills, abilities, and other attributes—plays a key role in team processes and outcomes (e.g., Bell et al., 2018; Mathieu et al., 2014). Specifically, individual cognitions (Heninger et al., 2006; Minas et al., 2014) and attributes (Bell, 2007) affect information processing in teams. Thus, teams of individuals with certain attributes might process information more effectively than other teams. Although prior literature has examined how individuals’ attributes (e.g., experience, knowledge) affect their ability to use information to make decisions, it has not explored how team composition affects teams’ ability to do so. How team composition moderates the effects of other predictors of team performance also remains an open question (Mathieu et al., 2014). Given our premise above that information volume affects a team’s effectiveness in decision-making, our second research question is: How does a team’s composition moderate the effects of its information volume on its decision performance?

In addressing the above research questions, we develop a contextualized theory (Hong et al., 2014) to illustrate (1) a curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance in IS-enabled teams, and (2) how relevant team composition attributes moderate this relationship. We integrate information processing theory (IPT; Simon, 1978) with schema theory (Axelrod, 1973; Bartlett, 1932) and its learning-focused derivative, cognitive load theory (CLT; Sweller, 1988; Sweller et al., 1998) to explain how team composition affects cognitive load in team decision-making. Given the increasing volume of information available through modern IS, the integrated nature of business and resultant need for collaborative decision-making, and the need to adapt to constantly evolving technologies, we develop our theory in the omnibus context of IS-enabled decisionmaking by teams. Accordingly, in viewing team composition, we select three attributes that are particularly salient in this context—computer self-efficacy (CSE), computer anxiety (CANX), and learning goal orientation (LGO)—as discussed further below.

We conduct two studies of team decision-making enabled by an enterprise resource planning (ERP) system. ERP systems deliver extensive information to decision makers (Tan et al., 2020) in a holistic and loosely structured decision context (Browne et al., 2007), and thus represent an appropriate context for examining the effects of information volume. We use survey data to measure team members’ attributes, which are relevant in ERP research (Chou & Chen, 2009; Guo et al., 2019; Hwang et al., 2016) but have not been studied in teams or as influencing information processing. The two studies— of dyads and small teams—involve the use of an ERP system to make decisions in a competitive business simulation.

## Theoretical Foundations

Broad theories often struggle to adequately explain the complexities of modern work practices (Rousseau & Fried, 2001) such as IS-enabled decision-making by teams. By accounting for context, theories can maximize their explanatory potential (Johns, 2006) and identify new factors that can help to extend the original theory (Whetten, 2009). We adopt a contextualized approach to IS theory development and follow the guidelines suggested by Hong et al. (2014). This involves grounding the work in general theory, contextualizing that theory to a specific research domain, identifying and modeling context-specific factors, examining the interplay between those factors and the IS, and considering alternative contextualized models.

Our premise is that: (1) a team’s decision performance is a function of information volume, and (2) this function is influenced by the composition of members’ attributes that are salient to the decision context. We build on this premise in the omnibus context of IS-enabled decision-making by teams. We first discuss theories of cognition under the broad umbrella of information processing in decision-making. We contextualize our theoretical approach by considering how teams process information, and how team composition affects information processing in teams. Appendix A provides additional details about how this paper follows the Hong et al. (2014) guidelines.

## Information Processing: From Problem Recognition to Cognitive Load

Based on IPT, decision-making begins by recognizing and accepting a task to achieve or a problem to solve (Simon, 1978). The decision makers must then determine what is available to them via existing knowledge or schemas stored in long-term memory. Based on schema theory, which originated with Bartlett (1932) and was then introduced to meaningful learning (Ausubel, 1962), computer programming (Mayer, 1981), and information systems (e.g., Galletta, 1985), schemas are mental organizing structures that emerge from sociocultural and environmental interactions (Axelrod, 1973; McVee et al., 2005). They can help decision makers make sense of their task environment e.g., how to navigate it, what new information is needed for a decision, and where to find it. The decision maker must then gather new information needed for the task. This process of foraging for new information (Pirolli & Card, 1999) involves scent (environmental signals<sup>2</sup> leading to desired information), patches (spaces containing information), and diet (information deemed optimal for the task). Schemas help decision makers identify information scent, navigate to information patches, and determine the quantity and type of information diet. As with food diets, volume is critical to success, and information volume depends on the decision maker’s chosen information diet, i.e., the accessed information or cues.

The decision maker’s ability to process greater information has been studied over the past several decades (Schroder et al., 1967; Simon, 1978; van Knippenberg et al., 2015). Decisionmaking involves information processing to (1) achieve a task or solve a problem (i.e., perform), and (2) construct or update schemas in long-term memory to use in future decisions (i.e., learn). We next consider the mechanisms involved in information processing based on findings from cognitive neuroscience and return to a complementary psychological theory of how decision makers allocate and process information in working memory.

Central to IPT are the limitations of working memory, which provides temporary storage and manipulation of the information needed for tasks that require bridging across perception and behavior (Baddeley, 1992; Draschkow et al., 2021). Working memory helps to control attention and action (Oberauer, 2019) and is subject to interference from external and internal sources (Lorenc et al., 2021). Cognitive neuroscience research views short-term retention of information (working memory) and control of that information (executive function) to guide behavior as distinct processes. It also indicates that working memory is not a dedicated brain subsystem but a process in which the brain allocates attention by activating and deactivating mental representations across multiple subsystems (D’Esposito & Postle, 2015). Recent work has highlighted the interactions of brain subsystems and factors that impede or promote decisionmaking in IS-related contexts such as internet gaming (Wei et al., 2017), conceptual modeling (Bera et al., 2019), and gamification (Mullins & Sabherwal, 2020).

Consistent with the concept of mental representations in cognitive neuroscience (D’Esposito & Postle, 2015), schemas are activated and organized in working memory in decision-making or learning, and periodically revised or refined based on new observations. While drawing upon existing schemas can help reduce information processing requirements (Sweller et al., 1998), learning requires that a portion of cognitive resources are allocated to creating and automating schemas. CLT builds upon schema theory by proposing that available working memory is allocated across three distinct, exhaustive, and additive types of cognitive load—intrinsic, extraneous, and germane—affecting how schemas are developed and decisions are made.

Intrinsic load reflects the task’s inherent difficulty and depends on the number of elements that must be processed, the extent of interactivity between them, and the subject’s expertise. Sweller et al. (1998) suggest that humans can probably only concurrently deal with two or three items of information when needing to process rather than only hold information. Interactivity among elements thus imposes a greater working memory load for problem solving, leaving little room for schema construction or additional information processing. We adapt CLT to focus on decision-making, instead of learning (e.g., Schöbel et al., 2019) and thus view intrinsic load to be jointly determined by the nature of the information used and the decision makers’ expertise.

Extraneous load was originally described in the domain of instructional design as reflecting distractions from the environment, such as activities required of students or how the material is presented. We view it more generally as any use of working memory that is neither intrinsic to the task nor beneficial in constructing (i.e., creating or revising) schemas.

Germane load refers to working memory that is “directed toward processes that are relevant to learning and, in particular, toward the construction and mindful abstraction of schemas” (Sweller et al., 1998, p. 264). If the sum of intrinsic and extraneous cognitive load is below the limits of working memory, available attention may be redirected toward understanding the interactivity of elements by combining them into a higher-order schema to be stored in long-term memory and later recalled into working memory as a single element (Sweller et al., 1998).

Our above broad theoretical lens synthesizes theories of information processing. We next contextualize our theory to the domain of IS-enabled decision-making by teams. To do this, we first integrate the literature on cognition in teams to explain the mechanisms underlying the relation between information volume and performance in team decisionmaking (RQ1), and then discuss how three context-relevant team composition attributes affect this relationship (RQ2).

## Research Hypotheses

## Effects of Information Volume on Decision Performance

Focusing primarily on task-relevant information (i.e., intrinsic load), the IPT literature has established a curvilinear “inverted U-shaped” relationship between information volume and performance (Roetzel, 2019; Schroder et al., 1967), such that additional information improves decision-making up to a certain point, but thereafter produces information overload, a state in which information processing requirements exceed capacity, resulting in reduced performance (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Information overload has been well-researched at the individual level (Roetzel, 2019). Although some research at the individual level has found a linear relationship, it relies on subjective appraisals (e.g., Laato et al., 2020; LePine et al., 2016) rather than objective data.

Impacts on cognitive load: Combining CLT with prior work on information overload, we recognize that IPT focuses on the volume and diversity of task-relevant (intrinsic) information being processed and does not consider other constraints on working memory, such as those imposed by germane or extraneous load. To highlight this distinction, we refer to cognitive overload as the condition in which cognitive processing requirements, including intrinsic, germane, and extraneous loads, exceed available cognitive processing capacity.

Additional information improves decision-making up to a certain point, reaching a peak when the most relevant information (representing the intrinsic load for the decision) occupies the limited working memory. As the accessed information goes beyond the working memory capacity, a part of that memory is redirected toward prioritizing and filtering information, leading to confusion and difficulty in recalling prior information (Schick et al., 1990). This redirection of working memory raises extraneous load, increasing the likelihood that relevant information is replaced by less relevant information, producing cognitive overload.

Cognitive load in teams: Recent advances in CLT extend it to collaborative teams (Kirschner et al., 2018) through the concept of collective working memory (Kirschner et al., 2009). Consistent with the findings of cognitive science (e.g., Maertens & Pollmann, 2005) on which CLT builds, cognitive capacity can be increased by dividing task processing between two brains, but this increases coordination and recombination costs (increasing extraneous load). Parallel processing and accessing redundant information incur additional costs in ISenabled team decision-making (e.g., Miranda & Saunders, 2003). These costs reduce the performance of simpler tasks, but are justified for complex tasks (Kirschner et al., 2011). Similarly, schemas in long-term memory may be distributed between two brains, but involve transaction costs of cognitive exchange via borrowing, linking, and reorganizing (Kirschner et al., 2018). As a result, cognitive capacity does not improve as much as expected in teams (Jones et al., 2004). Thus, individuals’ schemas comprise a distributed long-term memory, and can be shared, linked, and reorganized but with transaction costs similar to shared working memory.

Information volume at the team level: Information volume in IS-enabled teams has primarily been studied in the group support systems (GSS) literature, which situates GSS, and IS in general, as paradoxically contributing to both the problem and solution of overload (Dennis et al., 2001; Schultz & Vandenbosch, 1998). Overload can arise from increased volume and complexity of information in GSS (Fjermestad & Hiltz, 1998; Heninger et al., 2006) but can be mitigated by process and system design (Ellwart et al., 2015; Kolfschoten & de Vreede, 2009), enabling GSS-supported teams to outperform traditional teams (e.g., Lam & Schaubroeck, 2000). The GSS literature includes studies of informationoverload coping strategies in groups (Jones et al., 2004; Whelan & Teigland, 2013) and the effects of low and high levels of individual-level information volume on individuals’ outputs when using a GSS (Grisé & Gallupe, 1999).

The information overload phenomenon has been discussed to some extent at the team level, but these studies either did not empirically study overload (Seeber et al., 2017) or used perceptual measures of overload (Magni & Maruping, 2013;

Mitchell et al., 2016). Thus, little work has considered the impact of changing levels of objective information volume on decision-making in teams, especially when measuring the constructs at the team level. One exception is an examination of information volume’s curvilinear effect on decision time and information flow (Paul & Nazareth, 2010). Moreover, prior studies have found a curvilinear relationship between email communication frequency and team performance (Patrashkova-Volzdoska et al., 2003) and found that excessive information in a team reduces its ability to improvise (Magni & Maruping, 2013).

As discussed above, the prior literature includes some discussion and results suggesting cognitive overload in teams, theoretical arguments for cognitive overload based on IPT and CLT, and individual-level studies finding information overload. In light of these consistent streams and our above arguments, we propose that as the volume of information used in team decision-making increases, the decision outcome improves up to a certain point and decreases thereafter. More formally, we posit:

H1: Information volume in team decision-making exhibits a curvilinear (inverted U-shaped) relationship with decision performance such that a higher information volume improves decision performance to a certain point but reduces it thereafter.

The dotted line represents the level of cognitive capacity, at which point additional cognitive load of any type will reduce performance.

## Moderating Effects of Team Composition

Team member knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics represent individual differences that are relatively stable and have intra-psychological origins, which comprise the microfoundations of human capital resources (Ployhart et al., 2014). These attributes are an important and understudied aspect of team composition in decision-making (Doblinger, 2022), especially for newly formed teams (Bell et al., 2018). Over time, team-level abilities also emerge, but the attributes of a team’s members continue to play an important role in team performance (Mathieu et al., 2014). But how do these attributes combine within teams? As with collective working memory and collaborative CLT, we propose that individuals’ attributes combine as compositional attributes of a team when roles are negotiable and tasks are substitutable.

When considering the effects of team composition, we adopt a traditional personnel composition model in which attributes of the individuals combine to support team performance (LePine et al., 1997; Mathieu et al., 2014). Thus, individuals attributes are resources that help team performance, and the task’s demands determine the importance and amount of each needed resource (Roetzel, 2019; Steiner, 1972).<sup>3</sup> The resource requirements for different types of tasks may necessitate three different types of aggregation: in additive tasks, each member’s ability can be used and members can compensate for one another’s attributes; in conjunctive tasks, attributes are not substitutable resources and performance therefore depends on the weakest member; in disjunctive tasks, performance depends on the strongest member, who would take over responsibilities and communicate frequently with the other members (LePine, 2003; Steiner, 1972).<sup>4</sup>

Members of self-managed teams may divide tasks based on their abilities (Doblinger, 2022). Thus, less challenging tasks could be handled by team members who are less confident, more anxious, or still learning while more challenging tasks could be undertaken by the team member with the most suitable attributes. We conceptualize each attribute as additive in IS-enabled decision-making due to the substitutability of tasks, but also control for attribute diversity in the team profile (Chan, 1998; Mathieu et al., 2014) and conduct robustness tests using conjunctive and disjunctive aggregation. We identify four criteria for selecting a set of team composition attributes. First, we seek a parsimonious subset of context-relevant attributes from a vast landscape of potentially desirable team member attributes (Ryan & Ployhart, 2014). Our next two criteria, based on prior literature on teams, focus on attributes that (1) have intra-psychological origins, and (2) are relatively stable (Bell et al., 2018; Ployhart et al., 2014). Finally, we consider attributes that are likely to affect information processing in IS-enabled decision-making. More specifically, recognizing that individuals’ attributes influence information processing capacity (Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Saunders et al., 2017) and based on CLT (Janssen & Kirschner, 2020; Sweller et al., 1998), we consider factors that can affect extraneous or germane load, thereby influencing the team’s ability to process intrinsic load and hence affecting decision performance.

## Selection of Team Composition Attributes

We consider three attributes. Each is a dispositional attribute that is especially salient in the omnibus context of IS-enabled decision-making by teams. First, we consider the two most widely studied individual attributes regarding IS (Attig et al., 2017): CSE and CANX. These attributes are also malleable, as they can be influenced over time through experience and are thus of more practical relevance than relatively fixed traits such as computer playfulness and innovativeness (Thatcher & Perrewé, 2002).<sup>5</sup> Second, we consider the individual attribute of a “growth mindset,” LGO, given its relevance to learning a new complex IS (Dweck, 2016).<sup>6</sup> Also, these attributes affect different types of nonintrinsic cognitive load: germane (LGO), extraneous (CANX), or both (CSE). Taken together, these three attributes represent social, cognitive, and affective differences that can influence working memory allocation during decision-making.

The next three sub-sections examine how team composition affects working memory use through effects on cognitive load in IS-enabled decision-making. Prior literature implicitly assumes attributes at the population mean when considering information volume’s (i.e., intrinsic load’s) effect on performance. Accordingly, we suggest that teams with moderate levels of attributes will conform to the expected curvilinear relationship between information volume and performance. Similarly, we suggest that a moderate level of information volume will involve “enough” information for decision-making while accounting for team composition effects, but deviations from a moderate level will affect performance differently for low and high levels of each attribute. Thus, we focus on high and low levels of each attribute and consider their effects as information volume deviates from moderate levels to higher or lower levels to describe the flattening and steepening effects on the curvilinear relationship between information volume and performance (i.e., changing the shape of the curve based on deviations from a moderate level of each attribute). ## Computer Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy, defined as the belief in one’s ability to accomplish a task, is considered a stronger predictor of behaviors when it is measured more locally (i.e., closer to the predicted behavior) (Bandura, 1977). CSE is the primary localized self-efficacy belief in IS literature and is defined as one’s self-perceived ability to accomplish a task using a computer (Compeau & Higgins, 1995).

We suggest that, in IS-enabled decision-making, higher CSE leads team members to feel confident in their existing schemas and thereby enables them to use a greater amount of decisionrelevant (intrinsic) information before reaching a state of cognitive overload.

Impacts on cognitive load: Individuals with higher CSE focus better on the task and use analytic strategies, thereby attaining better performance (Wood & Bandura, 1989). Task focus reduces extraneous load via reduced attention to extraneous factors, and analytic strategies draw upon existing schemas to efficiently utilize task-relevant information (intrinsic load). We suggest the opposite is the case for low CSE via greater attention to extraneous factors such as selfdoubt and the overestimation of environmental demands (Bandura, 1993; Wood & Bandura, 1989).

We also expect a lower allocation of cognitive resources to schema development (germane load) in decision makers with high CSE because they believe existing schemas are sufficient for using the IS, and higher in those with low CSE because they will need to allocate more resources to learning how to use the IS (germane load) and more effort in navigating the IS (extraneous load).

Effects at the team level: CSE has been viewed and assessed at both individual (e.g., Compeau & Higgins, 1995) and group (e.g., Gallivan et al., 2005) levels. Consistent with prior IS literature (e.g., Venkatesh & Windeler, 2012) and our focus on team composition, we treat CSE as an additive resource that is substitutable across team members. For example, team members with higher CSE could perform tasks involving more complex use of the IS (e.g., a multistep process with an intricate interface), while team members with lower CSE could perform simpler tasks (e.g., monitoring a basic report).

Impacts at different information volume levels: Teams using moderate levels of information volume in decisionmaking will achieve satisfactory performance. As information volume decreases, teams with high CSE are likely to achieve better decision performance due to their ability to deploy more efficient analytic strategies. With efficient analytic strategies, teams are more likely to select information that is more relevant to the decision and less redundant with known information. Teams with low CSE, by contrast, may devote greater attention to aspects of the system rather than to task-relevant information. This results in a lower likelihood that accessed information will actually be used for the decision, which exacerbates the negative impact of low information volume to further decrease decision performance.

As information volume increases, teams with high CSE can process more task-relevant (i.e., intrinsic) information because task focus and analytic strategies enable lower extraneous load. Teams with high CSE can also rely on prior schemas, reducing the need to construct new schemas while more efficiently navigating the IS and thus having a lower germane load. Via strategic use and focused effort, selfefficacy improves problem-solving efficiency when demands on working memory increase (Hoffman & Schraw, 2009). Conversely, when teams with low CSE face high information volume, they process less task-relevant information before reaching cognitive overload because selfdoubt and focus on navigating the IS increase the extraneous load. They will also have a greater germane load due to the perceived need to develop new schemas.

Based on the above discussion, we expect an increase in CSE to improve decision performance at both low and high levels of information volume. Hence, we posit:

H2: CSE flattens the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance in teams such that an increase in CSE reduces the negative effects of both high and low levels of information volume on decision performance.

## Computer Anxiety

Anxiety is a cognitive-affective structure of the human defensive motivational system, marked by a sense of uncontrollability and a focus on potential negative events (Barlow, 2000). It is studied as both a dispositional trait and a temporary state (e.g., Spielberger & Reheiser, 2009). Like self-efficacy, anxiety has been localized to domains with varying levels of specificity, such as social anxiety (Leary & Kowalski, 1997) and test anxiety (Wine, 1971). We view CANX as a localized form of trait anxiety that denotes the fear of making mistakes or accidentally causing harm when using computers (Heinssen et al., 1987). Anxiety can negatively affect the performance of computer-based tasks (e.g., J. Wang et al., 2017). We propose that CANX becomes salient when IS-related schemas are used (Nishida, 1999) and influences performance through its effects on working memory during IS-enabled decision-making.

Impacts on cognitive load: Individuals with higher CANX are less likely to select and use relevant information due to inattention to the task (Leon & Revelle, 1985; Miu et al., 2008). The cues they select are more likely to contain extraneous information, thereby inhibiting the use of cues with relevant information (intrinsic load). By contrast, individuals with lower CANX are less likely to select irrelevant cues, thereby reducing extraneous cognitive load.

Also, individuals with higher CANX would have greater ontask and off-task thoughts through a phenomenon called cognitive interference (Glass & Knight, 1988). Increased ontask thoughts would cause them to “overthink” a problem, wasting time and working memory. Increased off-task thoughts (extraneous load) would shift their attention to an internal state of worry and consequent spontaneous selfrelevant thoughts (Forster et al., 2015). By contrast, individuals with lower CANX are less prone to overthink the task or worry about making a mistake, making greater working memory available for intrinsic load.

Effects at the team level: Consistent with our conceptualization of CSE, we view CANX as additive and substitutable across team members. For example, team members with lower CANX could perform tasks that require greater use of the IS (e.g., accessing multiple reports simultaneously), while team members with higher CANX could focus on tasks requiring lower use of the IS (e.g., refining strategy based on a simpler aggregated report). Additionally, CANX manifests in lower use of an IS through psychological distancing, but also in higher use through seeking social support (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010). To the extent that team members with higher CANX need social support for increased use of the IS, the working memory capacity of team members with lower CANX is reduced accordingly, further suggesting a substitutive effect.

Impacts at different information volumes: Teams using moderate levels of information volume in decision-making will achieve satisfactory performance. As information volume decreases, teams with high CANX are less likely to select relevant information due to inattention to the task. For relevant cues, anxiety-induced overthinking may also lead to poorer decisions. This exacerbates the negative effect of low levels of information volume and further reduces decision performance. By contrast, teams with low CANX that access lower information volumes are more likely to select relevant information and avoid overthinking.

As information volume increases, teams with high CANX face cognitive interference, which limits the amount of decision-relevant information that can be processed effectively. As the amount of relevant information increases, the extraneous load from overthinking and self-focused worry will lead to a state of cognitive overload, thereby diminishing decision performance. Prior work has shown that other types of anxiety limit the functional capacity of the working memory available for information intrinsic to the task (Eysenck & Byrne, 1992; Gage et al., 2003), supporting the argument that working memory is partially used by the extraneous cognitive load constraints imposed by greater CANX. By contrast, teams with low CANX are not constrained by the effects of cognitive interference and worry on extraneous load.

Thus, we expect an increase in CANX to reduce decision performance of teams at both low and high information volumes. Hence, we posit:

H3: CANX steepens the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance in teams such that an increase in CANX increases the negative effect of both high and low levels of information volume on decision performance.

## Learning Goal Orientation

Goal-orientation theory (Dweck, 2016; Dweck & Leggett, 1988) proposes a social-cognitive view of achievement motivation and suggests that individuals are oriented toward different types of goals. We specifically consider LGO, the core construct in the “growth mindset,” which reflects the tendency to behave in ways that develop new skills, as an attribute that is likely to influence germane load in decisionmaking. Prior research has revealed various effects of LGO on outcomes, including positive linear relationships with learning enjoyment (Cheng, 2011) and creativity (Hirst et al., 2009), an inverted U-shaped effect on team efficiency (Bunderson & Sutcliffe, 2003), and a long-term positive effect on sales performance (VandeWalle et al., 1999).

Impacts on cognitive load: Individuals with higher LGO are more likely to optimize their cue selection for learning, allocating more working memory to germane load to support schema development. When decision performance is the primary goal, schema development may become secondary, and any resources allocated to it may hurt task performance (Sweller, 1988). Accordingly, decision makers with lower LGO are more likely to use accessed information to make decisions rather than to develop schemas. The tension between cognitive load (via system and task complexity) and learning (via exploration of an IS) (Liang et al., 2015) suggests that differences in allocating cognitive resources can affect both learning and performance outcomes.

The influence of LGO on working memory utilization can also be viewed in terms of a prior classification of exploratory learning into two main processes: transformative and regulative (Njoo & de Jong, 1993). These two processes differ in their use of feedback to assess the quality of prior decisions, with cue selection and behavior optimized for schema construction (germane load) rather than to conduct the task itself (intrinsic load). Individuals with higher LGO are likely to use these feedback-seeking processes for learning (VandeWalle & Cummings, 1997), which reflects a greater germane load during decision-making. Less working memory capacity is thus available for problem solving, reducing decision performance. By contrast, individuals with lower LGO are more likely to use accessed information to improve decision performance.

Effects at the team level: Consistent with our treatment of CSE and CANX, we propose that LGO is additive and substitutable across team members. To the extent that decision-making is negatively affected by team members with high LGO expending cognitive resources for schema construction, team members with low LGO can offset it by focusing on “getting things done.” But, if multiple members focus on schema construction, this effect would be additive and hurt decision performance. We also expect LGO to be substitutable based on social learning. A team member with a high LGO may ask questions or provide the team with new knowledge that is not directly related to performance, thereby increasing the team’s germane load.

Impacts on different information volume levels: Teams using moderate levels of information volume in decisionmaking will achieve satisfactory performance. As information volume decreases, high LGO may reflect the use of transformative processes, which include scientific inquiry through analysis, hypothesis generation/testing, and evaluation. The pursuit of transformative learning processes by teams with high LGO involves schema construction through “if-then” and similar experimental approaches, resulting in behavior that inherently depends on a lower amount of relevant information for each decision. Thereby, the effects of decisions can be better isolated and understood for schema construction. Transformative processes are dominant when learners try to explain to others how something works (Eysink et al., 2009). The use of lower information volume for learning, rather than decision-making, exacerbates the negative effect of lower levels of information volume and further reduces decision performance. By contrast, teams with low LGO are more likely to use the accessed information for decision-making rather than learning, resulting in relatively better performance.

As information volume increases, high LGO may reflect the use of regulative processes, which focus on planning and monitoring the environment. Regulative learning processes involve schema construction through extensive monitoring, resulting in behavior that depends on higher amounts of information. Regulative processes are more prevalent when learners want to investigate how something works (Eysink et al., 2009), as in teams with high LGO. Such teams would use a large amount of the information accessed in such processes for schema construction (high germane load), increasing the likelihood of cognitive overload, and the use of information for learning rather than decision-making, which further reduces decision performance. By contrast, teams with low LGO are more likely to use the greater amounts of accessed information for decision-making rather than learning, resulting in relatively better performance.

Based on the above discussion, we expect an increase in LGO to reduce decision performance at both low and high levels of information volume. Hence, we posit:

H4: LGO steepens the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance in teams such that an increase in LGO increases the negative effects of both high and low levels of information volume on decision performance.

Overall, the proposed research model (Figure 2) includes the curvilinear effect of information volume on decision performance (H1), and the moderating effects of three dispositional factors—CSE (H2), CANX (H3), and LGO (H4)—in IS-enabled decision-making by teams through effects of extraneous load, germane load, or both.

## The Empirical Studies

We conducted two studies to test the proposed model. We began by studying the smallest possible teams (i.e., dyads) as a bridge from the well-studied individual level to demonstrate how collective working memory is also subject to the effects of too little or too much information, and how this relationship is conditioned on team composition. This initial test used student subjects in a controlled classroom environment. We undertook the second study using a more complex experimental context and ascended the ladder from dyads to small teams (3 or 4) of working professionals to further test the model and identify potential boundary conditions.

Study 1 was conducted using a competitive accelerated realtime business simulation game (ERPsim) in which small teams of students used a real-world SAP system to manage firms engaged in the sale and distribution of bottled water in Germany (Léger et al., 2007). Designed as a tool to teach business process integration and ERP, ERPsim is used by numerous universities worldwide. Given the semi-controlled nature of its environment and the ability to capture system use details and performance outcomes, ERPsim is increasingly being used in empirical research (e.g., Kwak et al., 2019; Super et al., 2020). Its participants manage their virtual firms using SAP to execute strategies and monitor and control their firms’ operations by accessing current data and implementing decisions. An external system simulates the market environment, accessing data in SAP and generating customer decisions based on a market model that incorporates the availability, pricing, and marketing of products across simulated firms.

The simulation (distribution game) in Study 1 was conducted over three rounds of 20 virtual days (each day lasting about 60 seconds) each. Each game spanned three hours, two of which were used for instruction and preparation. The simulated market generates orders, deliveries, and payments at the start of each virtual day so that the participants could consider new information and make decisions to adjust product pricing, regional marketing, inventory replenishment, and forecasting. Participants in this simulation managed the distribution of six different products (identical across teams): cases of standard water, spritz water, and lemon spritz water, each in half-liter and one-liter sizes. Each team of two to three participants began the game with 1,000 cases of each product in stock with the objective to maximize profit through the sale of these products to customers in three geographic regions of Germany. Only sales of the initial inventory were permitted in the first round. Replenishment and forecasting processes were integrated into the second and third rounds. Participants could monitor sales, inventory, procurement, and financial reports for their firms, and a periodic market report of the total sales volume and average price by product and region.

Possible decisions included forecasting and pricing at the product level, marketing at the product and geographic region level, and planning and replenishment at the virtual-day level. Decisions on one virtual day were accounted for in the market behavior on the next virtual day. All reports accessed and decisions implemented by participants were recorded in system transaction logs and were used to operationalize information volume.

Study 2 used a more advanced version of ERPsim set in a cereal manufacturing context. In this version of the simulation (manufacturing game), teams managed product design, production forecasting and control, and financial investments in addition to the activities described in the distribution game. Consequently, this game involved teams of three or four participants who received additional instruction and practice prior to playing the full game. Each game involved eight hours of instruction and practice, followed by a four-hour final game involving 120 simulated days, divided into four or six rounds, with time allotted to prepare prior to each round.<sup>7</sup>

## Participants and Data Collection

For Study 1, we collected data over two semesters in seven sections of an introductory IS course. The students earned course credit for participation. Before the simulation, participants completed an online survey on moderating and control variables. We sent the survey to 312 students and received 276 usable responses (88.5% response). Students in each class were randomly assigned to teams, producing 150 teams, most with two participants. We retained 118 teams of two participants (i.e., dyads), where both members participated in the simulation and completed the pre-survey. The 236 participants in the final sample had an average age of 22.4 years (SD = 4.3) and 32% were women. We used this sample for construct validation and standardized the scores before aggregating to the dyad level for subsequent analyses.

For Study 2, we collected data from 11 games involving 200 individuals across 60 teams. Four games involved students in a professional IS graduate program. They participated in the study as part of a project assignment in an introductory ERP course (the final performance in the game counted for less than 2% of the course grade). Seven games were part of an IS new-hire training program for two large energy firms. Both subsamples received similar instruction and practice time with the simulator prior to the final game, which was used in the analyses. Before the simulation, participants completed an online survey to collect moderating and control variables. We sent the survey to 200 participants and received 197 usable responses (98.5% response). We randomly assigned participants to teams, producing 60 teams of three or four members each, all of which were used in the analyses. Of the 200 participants in the final sample, 29% were women, with a mean age of 27.5 years (SD = 8.2). We used this sample for construct validation and standardized the scores prior to aggregating them to the team level for subsequent analyses.<sup>8</sup>

## Measures

The study context offers a valuable analog to organizational performance. Given the nature of the business simulation and the stated goal to maximize profit, each virtual firm’s (i.e., team’s) profit per round is an appropriate measure of the dependent variable, decision performance. The market environment can scale from one to 26 teams, with a total market size expanding linearly with the number of teams (standardizing results across games). In Study 1, product costs were fixed per product, and the only other costs firms incurred were for regional marketing. Products in inventory were valued at cost, so no profit or loss was incurred on inventory holding. Sales were the only source of firm revenue, and profit was computed as sales minus marketing and inventory costs. In Study 2, firms incurred both fixed and variable costs that had to be monitored to ensure profitable operations. Profit in Study 2 was computed as sales minus fixed and variable costs, and also accounted for loan interest and asset depreciation. To account for different market environments in each version of the game, we standardized round profit separately for each game type (distribution game, four-round manufacturing game, six-round manufacturing game).

We measured the primary independent variable, information volume, using data from system use logs, and computed it for each round as the ratio of the amount of information accessed to the number of distinct decisions by the team. Since each virtual day brought potential new information through the system, a key component of the numerator in the information volume calculation was the number of distinct reports accessed by the team. Reports varied in complexity, and so each report was weighted using its number of columns, which ranged from three to 11. The denominator in the information volume computation is the number of distinct decisions (e.g., to change a product price or forecast) made by the team. We aggregated by round because team performance was reported at the end of each round.<sup>9</sup> This measure is consistent with prior studies of group-level information processing and overload, which measure the number of cues accessed over a decision period (Paul & Nazareth, 2010) and the number of decisions made in a time-constrained period (Paul et al., 2013). Our measure combines these into a ratio variable and incorporates a weighted numerator to represent the team’s volume of information and its use. Appendix B provides the details on the procedure for determining information volume.

We measured moderators at the individual level using established scales. Appendix C provides the items. We measured attributes before randomly assigning teams and thus assumed these attributes to be independent of group influence (Klein et al., 1994) and not subject to threats related to the nonindependence of observations (Kenny et al., 2006). Consistent with our conceptualization of the attributes as additive, we aggregated individual-level measures to the team level and specified a composition model for aggregating attributes as a combination of the ideal types of additive and dispersion models (Chan, 1998).<sup>10</sup> Additive aggregation is appropriate when team members can compensate for one another’s strengths or weaknesses when completing a task (LePine, 2003). Recognizing that deep-level composition variables (i.e., psychological characteristics of team members) can be important predictors of team performance and accounting for the potential effects of intra-team differences (Bell, 2007; Klein & Kozlowski, 2000), we used measures of dispersion as controls and tested alternate variable configurations for robustness.

Specifically, to control for intra-team variation in these constructs, we included the range (maximum minus minimum) of member values in the primary analyses. The use of aggregated measures for analyzing group-level outcomes is consistent with prior research in both IS (e.g., Kang et al., 2017; Venkatesh & Windeler, 2012) and organizational psychology (e.g., Gonzalez-Mulé et al., 2020; Ilies et al., 2007; Ostrowski et al., 2022; Z. Wang et al., 2017), and this paper improves on prior measures by incorporating the dispersion of team member attributes.

We included control variables that may influence team performance in all analyses. We controlled for time effects by including the proportion of the game completed at each observation. We also controlled for work experience, as those with more experience were more likely to know the business concepts embedded in the simulation. We also controlled for SAP experience, measured as the average number of months of self-reported experience for each team. Finally, we used two measures from the post-simulation survey as controls variables to account for the members’ prior knowledge of each other (in months for Study 1; on a scale of 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “very well” for Study 2), and additional planning, or the extent to which they discussed the simulation between class sessions (on a scale of 0 = “not at all” to 7 = “very much”). We used a combination of exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to establish convergent and discriminant validity. The measurement models are valid for both EFA and CFA, with all primary factor loadings over 0.50. The average variance extracted (AVE) for all constructs exceeds 0.50, and the square root of the AVE for each construct exceeds correlations with other constructs, indicating adequate convergent and discriminant validity, respectively. Appendix D presents correlations, descriptive statistics, and reliabilities for both studies.

## Estimation

We used Stata 16.1 for all analyses. Prior to computing interactions, we mean-centered all variables to reduce multicollinearity and simplify the interpretation of the results (Hayes, 2017). Due to the panel nature of the data, we determined a random effects model to be appropriate based on nonsignificant Hausman (1978) tests with nonrobust standard errors and confirmed this finding using robust standard errors (based on Wooldridge, 2010). We find panellevel heteroskedasticity based on a likelihood ratio test (S1: χ<sup>2</sup> (117) = 488.72, p < 0.001; S2: χ<sup>2</sup>(59) = 129.74, p < 0.001) and serial autocorrelation based on a Wooldridge (2010) test (S1: F(1, 117) = 7.16, p < 0.01; S2: F(1, 59) = 5.12, p < 0.05). Finally, because autocorrelation may differ in magnitude across teams, we tested for panel-specific firstorder autocorrelation (PSAR1). A Hausman test showed better fit for the PSAR1 model in S1 (p < 0.001) but not S2 (p = 0.52). For consistency across the studies, we adopted a conservative approach and corrected for PSAR1 in both studies. Thus, we used a feasible generalized least squares (GLS) regression, removing team-specific time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity and correcting for panel-specific heteroskedasticity and PSAR1.

## Results

Tables 2 and 3 present the main analyses, including results for controls only, main effects, and the full model. In Study 1, information volume has a positive and significant effect, and its quadratic term has a negative and significant effect, supporting H1 with the proposed inverted U-shaped curve. Study 2 replicates this finding, further supporting H1. Figure 3 depicts the plot for the main effect between information volume and decision performance in the top row followed by the plots for the interactions of CSE, CANX, and LGO with information volume. All plots show the predicted relationships using margin estimates based on the fitted panel models. For example, the plots for the curvilinear relationships (Figure 3, top row) are consistent with those expected theoretically (Figure 1). Interaction plots include curves showing predicted values at three levels of each moderator: one SD below the mean, at the mean, and one SD above the mean.<sup>11</sup> These plots are discussed in the results below.

In all the plots, we find patterns similar to the expected curvilinear effect in H1, with decision performance reducing as information volume either increases or decreases from a moderate level. H2 proposes a flattening effect of CSE on this curvilinear relationship, i.e., CSE is expected to reduce the negative impact of change in information volume from moderate levels in either direction. The positive and significant quadratic interaction for CSE supports H2. In Figure 3, the curves for high CSE, despite having lower intercepts, are flatter than those for low CSE, suggesting that higher CSE reduces constraints on limited working memory when using a complex IS such as an ERP system. In both CSE plots, the high CSE curve appears to approach its apex at the maximum depicted level of information volume (3 SDs above the mean) but does not curve back down, suggesting that teams with high CSE did not reach cognitive overload. Conversely, teams with low CSE do exhibit negative performance impacts of cognitive overload.

H3 proposes a steepening effect of CANX on the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance, i.e., CANX is expected to amplify the negative impact of high and low levels of information volume. In Study 1, we observe a negative and significant quadratic interaction term for CANX, supporting H3. In Figure 3, the Study 1 plot for CANX shows a clear difference between the positive near-linear relationship for teams with low CANX and the inverted-U relationship for teams with high CANX. This suggests that similar to high CSE, low CANX allows more efficient processing of a higher information volume without reaching cognitive overload. Surprisingly, we find the opposite effect in Study 2, based on a positive and significant quadratic interaction term for CANX. While the difference between the curves in Figure 3 for Study 2 is less pronounced, teams with high CANX appear to perform better at both lower and higher information volumes. A possible explanation for this finding is a pronounced range restriction in the CANX variable in Study 2, which we further discuss below.

H4 proposes LGO as steepening the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance, i.e., it is expected to amplify the negative impact of high and low levels of information volume. In Study 1, we find a negative and significant quadratic interaction term for LGO, supporting H4. In Figure 3, the Study 1 plot for LGO shows a clear difference between the U-shaped relationship for teams with low LGO and the inverted-U relationship for teams with high LGO. This suggests that teams focused on learning (high LGO) via high levels of either decisions (low information volume) or information (high volume) may suffer performance issues. Low LGO, by contrast, does not seem to induce cognitive overload when information volume would otherwise be high enough to hurt performance. In Study 2, we find the linear, but not the quadratic, interaction term to be negative and significant (p < 0.05). The pitch (i.e., linear slope) of the curve is steeper for teams with low LGO, resulting in performance being worse at low information volume, but better at high volume, than teams with high LGO.

The support for H1 in both studies shows that while increased information improves decision-making, having too much information may result in a state of cognitive overload in which decision performance suffers. Study 1 indicates that cognitive overload is more likely to occur when team composition (i.e., low level of CSE, high level of CANX, and high level of LGO, consistent with H2, H3, and H4, respectively) leads to greater commitment of working memory resources to extraneous and germane cognitive load, thus reducing the working memory available to process taskrelevant information. Study 2 suggests that the main cognitive overload effect extends from dyads to teams (H1), and supports the flattening effect of CSE when using a complex IS (H2). The unexpected flattening effect of CANX (H3) and nonsignificant effect of LGO (H4) suggest potential boundary conditions for a theory of cognitive overload.

## Robustness Tests

We conducted several robustness tests. Appendix E provides the results for robustness tests for Studies 1 and 2, all of which support the main results.<sup>12</sup>

To test alternatives to our additive conceptualization of the moderating variables, with their ranges as controls, we conducted three robustness tests in Study 1 and two in Study 2. Multiplicative measures may be appropriate for tasks that are more sensitive to limitations than to strengths (Einhorn, 1971; Wright, 1974). For both studies, we used the geometric mean (a multiplicative measure) in place of the additive and range measures to account for both the mutuality and magnitude of variables (Polites et al., 2012; Smith & Barclay, 1997), such that intra-team divergence will result in lower dispositional attribute scores. In Study 1, we tested two alternate conceptualizations (i.e., disjunctive and conjunctive) using the maximum and minimum values of these constructs for each dyad (while controlling for the minimum and maximum, respectively), thus representing all individual measures within each regression. In Study 2, we tested an alternative model using each variable’s standard deviation instead of range.

An additional robustness test for each study used a model that only corrects for the first-order autocorrelation (AR1) without assuming it to be panel-specific. We included this because a Hausman test indicated PSAR1 in Study 1 but not Study 2, and, as noted earlier, our main test was based on a conservative approach with correction for PSAR1 autocorrelation in both studies.

In Study 1, the dyads in three of the class sections were physically separated during rounds two and three and used a simple online chat tool (Google Chat) for communication. In complex decision-making, dual-task interference caused by using a separate communication tool may hinder performance (Heninger et al., 2006). Thus, we included online as a binary control (0 for face-to-face and 1 for online communication for any given dyad and round) in a robustness test.

No robustness tests such as the above were needed for Study 2, where the interaction mode remained the same throughout the process for all teams. However, unlike Study 1 (where all teams were dyads), the team size varied between three and four in Study 2. Therefore, we conducted a robustness test in Study 2 including team size as an additional control variable. For Study 2, we also tested the model with variables mean-centered by game type (four- or six-round), based on a t-test indicating differences in information volume between the two groups (p < 0.05).

## Endogeneity Tests

Because the main independent variable, information volume, could have been influenced by performance, we conducted a regression-based Hausman test (Hill et al., 2018) to assess potential endogeneity. This test involves the selection of an instrumental variable (IV) that is correlated with the suspected endogenous variable but not with the outcome.

We selected general system navigation as an instrumental variable, using the natural log of visits to the main system menu and other navigation activities (based on system logs) that were not directly associated with operating the simulated firm. We confirmed via regression analyses that: (1) the IV predicts information volume, and (2) does not predict decision performance. We then used the estimated residuals from (1) above alongside information volume to predict decision performance and account for heteroskedasticity using robust standard errors. Nonsignificant coefficients for the IV in both studies (S1: p = 0.51; S2: p = 0.19) indicate no threat of endogeneity.

We repeated this analysis with an alternate IV specification tailored to each study. In S1, the simpler setting required operational efficiency, but success did not depend on deeper market analysis. Thus, we developed a measure of market intensity by multiplying the number of each team’s market-focused decisions made by the number of fields in reports relevant to that decision. By contrast, the more complex setting of S2 assumed operational efficiency and required deeper market analysis to succeed. We developed a measure of operational intensity by multiplying the number of each team’s operations-focused decisions by the number of fields in reports relevant to that decision. We repeated the three-step Hausman procedure for S1 using market intensity and S2 using operational intensity as IVs. Both IVs predicted information volume but not decision performance and the estimated residuals were not significant in the final step (S1: p = 0.75; S2: p = 0.94), further indicating no threat of endogeneity.

## Discussion

Building on IPT and schema-based theories, we have argued that having “just enough” information is desirable for decision-making, and that having too little or too much information is undesirable. Cognitive load includes more than just the accessed information relevant to the decision; it also includes nonintrinsic information in working memory, such as IS navigation requirements, external stimuli, thoughts of apprehension or worry, and otherwise productive cognitive needs such as learning. Even when accessing a similar volume of information, different decision makers vary in their ability to effectively retain and utilize that information, especially in the case of having too little or too much information. Using these arguments to contextualize our theory to IS-enabled decision-making by teams, we developed a theoretical model including a curvilinear (inverted U-shaped) relationship between information volume and decision performance, and the moderating effects of three team composition attributes that affect decision makers’ cognitive load.

Testing the theoretical model, through two studies of decisionmaking by dyads and small teams, provided compelling evidence that cognitive overload ascends levels of analysis, from the well-established effects at the individual level to dyads (Study 1) and teams (Study 2) via shared working memory. Appendix D summarizes the differences between the teams in the two studies in terms of demographic and moderating variables, with t-tests indicating Study 1 dyads as having lower prior experience, lower CSE, higher CANX, and lower LGO than Study 2 teams. We draw upon these differences in the following discussion of the results.

Study 1 fully supports the proposed model, including the curvilinear relationship between information volume and performance, as well as the moderating effects of all three team composition attributes. Thus, although it is not possible to directly measure the three types of load in working memory, the empirical results support the proposed hypotheses concerning their effects.

Study 2 is consistent with Study 1 in supporting the curvilinear relationship between information volume and decision performance (H1). Also, consistent with H2, the results of both studies suggest that teams with higher CSE are less likely to experience the negative effects of cognitive overload when using a complex IS to complete a task. Thus, teams with higher CSE reach cognitive overload at higher levels of information volume, as can be seen in interaction plots for CSE and information volume for both studies (Figure 3). However, the increase in CSE in Study 1 (but not Study 2) implies a downward shift in the curve for the relationship between information volume and decision performance, which is consistent with the negative main effect of CSE on performance, in both the main effects model and the full model (Table 2). A possible explanation for the negative main effect of CSE in Study 1 relates to the Study 1 dyads having low CSE—and lower than the Study 2 teams—which is consistent with their having minimal business experience. Amid others with even lower CSE, those with moderate CSE might get overconfident and underestimate potential problems, and thereby perform worse than those with lower CSE, who might feel more challenged and put in greater effort. Further research is needed to understand the effect of CSE relative to the others with whom a team or individual competes.

Although both studies support H1 and H2, their results differ regarding the effects of CANX (H3) and LGO (H4). This may be due to the two studies having different profiles of participants, despite being in similar simulated business environments. The main effect of CANX is nonsignificant in both studies. The scores for CANX for Study 1 dyads range from 1 to 5.5 on a 7-point scale but the range is much more restricted (1 to 3) in Study 2 teams, which is consistent with the Study 2 participants being IS professionals. The unexpected positive and significant quadratic effect of CANX in Study 2 might be due to the observed range restriction. Teams with higher CANX in Study 2 are closer to the midpoint of the scale, and so may have performed better than teams with lower CANX, who exhibit such low anxiety that they might have taken the task to be too easy. Like any human emotion, anxiety is adaptive at moderate levels but may be maladaptive at high or low levels (e.g., Cardinale et al., 2020). Some level of anxiety can be helpful, and “can serve a motivating function,” as noted by Cheng and McCarthy (2018, p. 538).

LGO had a positive direct effect on performance in Study 1. High LGO thus helped the participants in Study 1, who were undergraduate students, in learning the new system. However, along with the positive main effect, and consistent with our expectations (H4), higher LGO also led to cognitive overload being reached earlier in Study 1 and to performance being lower at both low and high levels of information volume. In contrast to Study 1, the main effect of LGO in Study 2 is not significant, but LGO does detract from performance as indicated by the significant negative effect of its linear interaction with information volume. These teams had greater prior SAP experience, and so did not need to learn SAP, but had greater LGO than Study 1 dyads. However, due to the greater relevant knowledge, LGO did not seem to affect when the team reaches cognitive overload (as indicated by the nonsignificant quadratic interaction term). Moreover, we found a negative effect of LGO dispersion in both studies. Greater LGO dispersion may imply that members of the team focus on different outcomes when making decisions (e.g., one individual focusing on acquiring information to learn and thereby perform better and the other focusing more directly on better performance without seeking to learn). These overlapping but somewhat different results in the two studies point to the effect of LGO being fairly nuanced, contingent upon the participants’ experience and knowledge, and needing further investigation.

We also found significant effects of two control variables in Study 1 and three controls in Study 2 in the full models. The effect of game completion control (i.e., proportion of the game completed so far) is negative in Study 1 but positive in Study 2.<sup>13</sup> We also found work experience to have a positive effect in Study 1 (but not Study 2), suggesting that having at least some business experience provides an advantage in a novel business simulation. Additional planning had a positive effect on performance in Study 2, as teams that spent more time planning and preparing for the simulation outperformed others. Surprisingly, in Study 2 we found SAP experience to negatively affect performance, suggesting that the more experienced teams might have believed that their SAP experience would provide them with an advantage and thus did not prepare well.

In interpreting the effects of LGO above, we noted different levels of SAP experience in the two studies as a potential explanation. In light of this, and the unexpected negative effect of prior SAP experience in Study 2, we conducted post hoc analyses to explore its moderating effects on the curvilinear relationship.<sup>14</sup> Study 1’s results indicate significant negative linear and positive quadratic interaction effects, such that higher SAP experience flattens the curve, but Study 2’s results indicate significant positive linear and negative quadratic interaction effects, such that higher SAP experience steepens the curve. The contrasting results across the studies when considering SAP experience only as a control (Tables 2, 3), when seen in the light of lower prior SAP experience of Study 1 teams (mean/maximum = 0.5/9 months) versus Study 2 teams (mean/maximum = 3.7/21 months), raise the possibility that similar to the unexpected findings for CANX in Study 2, having some SAP experience is helpful in using the system for decision-making, but having experience beyond a certain point could harm decision-making. One possible explanation for this, other than the reduced preparation mentioned above, may be that teams with moderate SAP experience may have more experience in other areas that would help with the decisions, which teams with high SAP experience (e.g., specialists) may lack. We could not test this possible explanation due to lack of information on the other experiences of the team members, but it parallels conclusions from prior literature. Narayanan et al. (2009, pp. 1862-1863) note: “individual productivity is maximized when there is a good balance between specialization and exposure to variety … neither a narrow and deep specialization nor a scattered range of diverse experiences are most conducive to learning and productivity.”

## Limitations and Future Research

The findings of this paper should be viewed in light of its limitations. First, our context and operationalization of information volume emphasize ecological validity over experimental control. Our goal was to study decision makers in an environment that is a reasonably accurate simulation of reality. The setting involves sufficient complexity and interactivity such that participants can choose the amount of information to access (rather than being assigned to fixed high, medium, or low load conditions) for each decision, and their decisions are subject to the dynamics of a competitive ecosystem. Inherent to this complexity is the possibility that alternate explanations for the observed phenomena may be advanced. Two alternate explanations involve the effects of cognitive biases, which can negatively impact an individual’s decision quality in technology-mediated group discussions (Minas et al., 2014), and the effects of factors that were not controlled or measured. We tried to reduce the effect of cognitive biases by providing objective data to teams, but unobserved factors (such as the attention each team gave to potential demands beyond those related to the decision, learning, and anxiety) could have affected the results. We attempted to statistically control for alternate explanations, but additional research is needed to further study the effects we have found. Further, while we believe that capturing a natural range of information volume (while assessing potential endogeneity) affords richer investigation of the curvilinear relationship, we acknowledge that random assignment to varying load conditions in future experiments could help to reaffirm the proposed causal mechanisms.

Second, the paper is affected by the broader limitations of studies using experiments with participants working on simulated tasks. As mentioned above, we tried to address such limitations by providing a realistic experimental setting, allowing teams freedom to make their own decisions, using process experiments, and conducting two studies with similar experimental contexts and procedures, but different sets of participants. Rather than manipulating information volume, teams had a fixed amount of available information at any given time (which was well beyond their processing capacity and varied between games and, in Study 1, between rounds), but had to decide how much information to use. Despite these efforts, the studies are of simulations. Further research using longitudinal field experiments is needed to address this limitation.

Third, to keep the theoretical and empirical work focused, we examined only three attributes, selected based on theoretical and empirical reasons. Other attributes may influence working memory use in different contexts, including mental ability, which may impact the baseline working memory capacity; personality factors such as neuroticism, which may impact extraneous load; and factors such as trust or empathy (e.g., Kellett et al., 2006), which may fluctuate over time and impact how teams handle load. Importantly, we measured attributes at the individual level in these studies and conceptualized them as additive and substitutable resources to be used during team decision-making, controlled for their dispersion, and illustrated the robustness of our findings using alternate configurations (i.e., multiplicative, conjunctive, disjunctive). While this approach is consistent with prior organizational behavior literature on teams, we caution future research to carefully consider the nature and relevance of individual-level attributes in team-level processes. We suggest that this approach could help to strengthen the bridge from individualto team-level decision-making and propose that future studies on information processing in teams should also consider latent team-level attributes (e.g., collective efficacy).

One additional limitation relates to the measure of information volume. Consistent with the information processing literature, we measured information usage during decision-making. Our weighted measure exhibits good ecological validity based on the curves in Figure 3, with dyads experiencing overload when using more than 6 to 10 cues per decision, and small teams experiencing overload when using more than 12 to 18 cues per decision, consistent with the classic assertion (Sweller et al., 1998) and recent empirical neuroscientific evidence in a natural immersive environment (Draschkow et al., 2021) that individual decision makers can only process about three interactive cues in working memory. The aggregate measure provides a good estimate of the volume of information accessed for each decision and was necessitated here by the complexity of the context of the studies; however, future research could unpack decisions at a more granular level and examine the cognitive load implications of different types of information.

## Theoretical Contributions

Decision makers collectively shape organizational outcomes. We answer calls to consider attributes of decision makers in studies of cognitive overload (Rutkowski & Saunders, 2018) while recognizing that organizational performance depends on the interdependence of multiple decision makers. By examining information volume at the team level and explicating the impact of team composition attributes in the context of competitive virtual organizations, this paper moves from individual- to team-level research on information processing and decision-making. Prior studies have discussed the curvilinear effect of information volume on performance at the individual level but little research has examined the effect at a team level. For example, Grisé and Gallupe studied overload at the individual level in the team context but “…with the implied intent that their categorizations would be combined at the next stage” (p. 172). By examining the contingent effects of team composition on the curvilinear effect of information volume on performance, our theory helps to explain and predict (Gregor, 2006) how teams process information when making decisions. We offer initial insights into these mechanisms in Study 1 by considering the simplest teams (i.e., dyads) and their members’ attributes in predicting simulated organizational performance. We take a “bottom up” approach, both conceptually and empirically, by accounting for the individual attributes of dyad members. In Study 2, we ascend to the next level of analysis by considering small teams (i.e., 3 or 4 members) and their members’ attributes in a similar setting and take a similar approach to aggregating individual attributes while controlling for dispersion and testing alternate aggregations.

This paper contributes to the literature by examining how CLT, which has been used at the individual level, can be contextualized to dyads and small teams. It contributes to CLT by articulating the mechanisms contributing to team-level cognitive load and providing a method to assess CLT in teams. By using CLT to consider multiple types of information stored in working memory, it extends prior views of information overload that only account for task-relevant (intrinsic) information. By considering extraneous and germane demands on working memory in addition to intrinsic information, the paper offers a more nuanced explanation of the curvilinear effects previously found in studies of information overload. This nuanced view incorporates decision makers’ attributes, which affect extraneous and germane demands on working memory.

We contribute to the broader IS literature by answering calls to theorize about information, the persistent and everincreasing resource central to the field, and its relationship with systems and people (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Petter et al., 2018). In studying attributes of decision makers, this paper advances our understanding by contextualizing theoretically at the team level, integrating the simultaneous and interacting influences of information volume and team composition, which have hitherto been studied separately, to provide richer insights into their cumulative impacts on decision performance. More specifically, the paper provides new insights into the influence of IS-relevant attributes such as CSE and CANX. These two attributes influence how working memory is used when interacting with an IS, above and beyond the demands of a task that requires IS use. This paper is among the first to consider the mechanisms through which these attributes influence the use of information in decisionmaking. We found that another attribute, LGO, has a steepening influence on the cognitive overload curve during early interactions with a new IS, highlighting a potential “dark side” to an attribute that is regarded as desirable. The results suggest that under heavy information volume, LGO may limit decision makers’ effectiveness in proximal performance outcomes. Study 2 suggests that this dark side may be less salient when the IS is more familiar, but further research is needed to examine the effect of LGO in decision-making in the longer term, such as how the mental schemas developed under such conditions might help future decision-making.

Finally, this paper contributes to the literature on decisionmaking and cognitive overload by advancing a novel measure of information volume. By leveraging IS use and performance logs, we formulate a fairly precise measure of the amount of information accessed for decision-making. By accounting for the specific decisions made, and the relative complexity of information accessed, we develop a measure of information volume based on IS use that is consistent with the goals of overload research. This measure of information volume, focused on the use of an IS to gather information and enable decisions, exhibits the expected curvilinear relationship with decision performance in both dyads and small teams using a real ERP system.

## Implications for Practice

Managers know that having enough information is vital for effective decision-making, but also feel the effects of cognitive overload when trying to use more information than they can process. Human information processing capacity remains limited; thus, organizations increasingly rely on teams to make decisions in information-rich and technologically complex environments. By studying teams of decision makers using a real ERP system, we find support for the belief that teams, and organizations, have more information than they can effectively process.

All teams are not equal in their ability to process information, and we highlight three salient and malleable attributes of a team’s members that can influence how the teams process and use information in decision-making. When creating and developing teams, managers should consider how these attributes of team members might affect a team’s ability to effectively use complex IS for decision-making. Specifically, if the team needs to process information more efficiently, or under time pressure, our findings suggest that members with higher CSE and lower CANX may spend less cognitive effort on the IS itself and give greater attention to useful information. Thus, for teams using IS to make decisions based on high volume or velocity of information, developing team members with greater CSE and lower CANX seems useful, as does designing systems to reduce the stress of navigating the IS to find information, especially under time pressure (e.g., Moody & Galletta, 2015). However, consistent with prior literature on anxiety (e.g., Cheng & McCarthy, 2018), we find that very low levels of CANX should be avoided, as a small amount of CANX is beneficial. This could be achieved, for example, by including some individuals with high CANX in the same teams as individuals with low CANX.

While having a growth mindset (LGO) benefits individuals, teams, and organizations in numerous ways, we also find that IS-enabled teams with high LGO may struggle at first to perform while they focus on learning. We suggest that such teams may be better suited for decisions for which more time is available so that they can benefit from additional time to become familiar with a new or dynamic environment, and

LGO would therefore ultimately prove beneficial (Dweck, 2016). Conversely, more urgent decisions could be assigned to IS-enabled teams with low LGO. Based on our findings, we also caution that conflicting mindsets (i.e., greater differences in LGO within a team) can be detrimental to performance, at least in the short term, as some members focus on getting things done while others explore and learn.

## Conclusion

This paper proposes and tests a model of cognitive overload when teams enabled by IS make decisions, thereby extending prior work on information overload at the individual level. It highlights the nuanced interactions between teams and IS by examining detailed system use behaviors along with team composition, while studying dyads and teams of decision makers using a contemporary ERP system to manage simulated organizations. As decision makers struggle to process the growing volume of available information, the adverse impacts of overload on their decisions become increasingly salient. The initial insights obtained in this paper can hopefully motivate further inquiry into team cognition and decision-making.

## Measurement of Information Volume

The following procedure is used to operationalize information volume, measured as the ratio of information cues accessed (via reports) during a round divided by the number of decisions enacted during that round. We use this procedure for both studies, but we illustrate the procedure based on the simpler Study 1 scenario (distribution game). Six reports are available in the distribution game, varying in complexity, and containing three to 11 columns. Information in each report is updated for each simulated day. Thus, the highest number of information cues that can be accessed from a report in one round is a product of the number of days in the round (twenty) and the number of cues in that report. The actual number of information cues accessed by a team from one report in a single round is based on the number of days on which the report was accessed. Table B1 lists the reports, the number of columns or “cues” it contains, the maximum number of possible cues per round, and the specification of variables a-f which are used in the calculation of the numerator.
