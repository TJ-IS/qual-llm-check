The aim of this article was to explore the relationship between human actors and technology in the context of a social media platform, developed by a leading Italian <sup>fi</sup>rm in the food industry, and created to keep in contact with its customers, while gathering innovative ideas from them. In order to address these issues, we adopt a theoretical approach that is deeply rooted in Pickering's “mangle” theory, and Jones' subsequent metaphor of “double dance of agency” i.e. human and material agencies. A longitudinal case study has been developed with two rounds of interviews with marketing and R&D managers. A total of 8 recorded interviews, for 22 h overall. We examined conditions in the ongoing functioning of the platform, and considered how the website's dynamic materiality con<sup>fi</sup>gures and recon<sup>fi</sup>gures the practices and possibilities of different modes of engagement by multiple users (i.e. <sup>fi</sup>rm employees and customers) and vice versa. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, dealing with the case of a social media platform developed by a <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned organizational ends, the entanglement is considered to be double. One entanglement is between the human agency of customers and the material agency of the social media platform. The other is between the human agency of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the material agency of the social media platform, including all actions that human agents (i.e. <sup>fi</sup>rm employees) undertake in seeking to channel material agency and shape actions of other human agents, i.e. customers.

## 1. Introduction

Common understanding of the innovation process today builds on the observation that <sup>fi</sup>rms rarely innovate alone and that the innovation process can be seen as an interactive relationship among producers, users and many other actors (Laursen & Salter, 2006; Piller, Ihl, & Vossen, 2010; Sofka & Grimpe, 2009). Based on the idea that one of the fundamental sources of knowledge for innovation is the customer (Prahalad & Krishnan, 2008; Seybold, 2006), coupled with drastic reductions in communication costs through the use of web technologies, some <sup>fi</sup>rms have begun systematically involving users in their innovation processes by leveraging social media tools (Bughin, Hung Byers, & Chui, 2011). Also referred to as Web 2.0, these tools rely on active content creation by users or members as a central distinguishing feature. In most cases, companies use social media to do what traditional advertising does: persuade consumers to buy a company's product or service. Nevertheless, some companies have recently become aware that social media is not simply another channel for distributing corporate information or an add-on to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's promotional media mix (Aula, 2010; Nair, 2011). These companies are experimenting with more advanced uses of social media, i.e. involving consumers through invitations to participate in various activities, such as product development (including idea generation, design and engineering, testing and launching), customer service feedback and so on (Füller & Matzler, 2007). The debate is open on the ability of consumers to develop ideas that can inspire product or brand managers to veer hugely from a chosen course of action, i.e. to develop radically new ideas (Urbik, 2012).

Firms' use of social media to involve customers in the innovation process is a relatively recent issue, which has been dealt with in the literature using several approaches (see e.g. Piller, Vossen, & Ihl, 2012). Here our focus is on the use of social media to gather innovative ideas from an online community. Other study perspectives have included incentives to contribution (Piller & Walcher, 2006) and methods for idea screening and evaluation (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008; Toubia, 2005).

The use by a <sup>fi</sup>rm of social media based tools, which engage a crowd of external actors to pursue speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovation goals raises several issues relevant to the relationship between human actors and technology. Broadly speaking, these issues concern the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of conceptualizing the roles of the different actors involved, their capability to act, the impossibility to anticipate or control the consequences of their actions, and the inextricable intertwining of their acting, among others. In order to address these issues, we adopt a theoretical approach that is deeply rooted in Pickering's “mangle<sup>1</sup>” theory, and Jones subsequent metaphor of “double dance of agency” i.e. human and material agencies. Human agency is typically de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ability to form and realize one's goals (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Giddens, 1984). Material agency is defined as the capacity for nonhuman entities to act on their own. apart from human intervention (Leonardi, 2011). Material agency is temporally emergent in practice and symmetric to the concept of human agency, a part of intentionality (Pickering, 1993, 1995). According to this theoretical approach, the process of information systems development and use is understood as an emergent process in which human and material agencies are inextricably intertwined. We adopt this approach in the context of the empirical investigation of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c social media platform for innovation — in the Mill I Wish (MIW). MIW was developed by a leading Italian <sup>fi</sup>rm in the food industry to maintain contact with and gather innovative ideas from its customers. This case is particularly relevant because companies in the food sector are just beginning to experiment with social media (Engagement DB report, 2011).

We examine the ongoing functioning of MIW, and consider how the platform's material agency<sup>2</sup> con<sup>fi</sup>gures and recon<sup>fi</sup>gures the practices and possibilities of different modes of engagement by multiple users (i.e. <sup>fi</sup>rm employees and customers) and vice versa. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, dealing with the case of a social media platform developed by a <sup>fi</sup>rm to gain clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned organizational ends, the entanglement is in fact considered to be double. One entanglement is between the human agency of customers and the material agency of the social media platform. The other is between the human agency of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the material agency of the social media platform, including all actions that human agents (i.e. <sup>fi</sup>rm employees) undertake in seeking to channel material agency and shape actions of other human agents, i.e. customers.

We draw on the mangle conceptual model in our consideration of the inception and evolution of a social media platform. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the aim of this article is to trace the mutual adaptation of material and human agency in practice and identify the dialectics of resistance and accommodation, i.e. the tuning process through which the technological and the social achieve an interactive stability (Pickering, 1995).

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section we present the theoretical perspective informing the study, followed by methodology. The case setting makes up Section 4, which is followed by the empirical evidence, both in terms of description and discussion, in Section 5. The paper concludes with implications for researchers and practitioners.

## 2. The mangle model and the double dance of agency

Proceeding from the assumption that “social media is about sociology, not technology” as sociologist Brian Solis (2007) claims, we argue that the study of a social media platform could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a broader base of sociological in<sup>fl</sup>uences and a consideration of the role of material and human agents in its practice. Below we introduce the model of human and material agency offered by science sociologist Andrew Pickering. Pickering's approach has been successfully applied to the study of information systems implementation in organizations; therefore, it may offer a productive conceptual approach to the study of a social media platform in order to foster innovation in an organizational context.

Drawing on actor network theory, Pickering suggests that technological systems need to be understood in terms of interaction between human and material agency and the two can be seen as constitutively intertwined. He describes the process of mutual adjustment by which human and material agency are interactively stabilized over time as a temporally emergent dialectic of resistance and accommodation represented by the mangle of practice. Resistance can be offered by any entity, including material objects and it can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the occurrence of a block on the path to some goal (Pickering, 1993); accommodations are made by the user to overcome or avoid resistances, and Pickering calls the interplay of human and material agency, as they interactively stabilize each other, the “dance of agency” (Pickering, 1995). Thus, when encountering resistance in achieving an intended capture of material agency, human actors adopt strategies of accommodation, such as revising goals, intentions, or practices, or adjusting of technological parameters. Pickering discusses the mangle of practice mainly in relation to scientists' attempts to construct devices that capture particular material phenomena, which can be seen as direct attempts to marshal material agency. When the capturing of material phenomena is not the primary objective, but rather their marshaling towards the achievements of organizational goals, as in the case under exam, or broader social ends, we can see the mangle model as a form of “double mangle” (Jones, 1999), in which both material and human agency are mutually and emergently transformed. Jones introduced the concept of a temporally emergent double mangling where human agents seek to channel material agency to shape the actions of other human agents in a sort of “ongoing double dance of agency” (Jones, 1999). This model considers different kinds of actors who, through a sort of implicit hierarchy, in<sup>fl</sup>uence one another in an ongoing dialectic of resistance and accommodation. According to its author, this model is useful in studying the particularly complex interactions related to information technologies with their intangible products and extensive involvement in a diverse range of organizational work practices.

The mangle theory and its subsequent developments have been applied to examine how people enact their human agency in response to technology's material agency in several organizational contexts and referring to several kind of technologies (see e.g. Rose et al., 2005; Svahn et al., 2009; Wagner et al., 2010). Recently this approach has been applied also to the case of a computer simulation technology for automotive design (Leonardi. 2011) and to examine the temporally emergent process that entangled the material agency of a dispensing robot with the everyday practices of hospital pharmacy work (Barrett et al., 2005). To the authors' knowledge, there are not many contributions regarding the use of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c concept of mangling in social media studies. Among them, we <sup>fi</sup>nd Steinkuehler (2006), Olohan (2011) and Kuk and Davies (2011). Steinkuehler (2006) applies the concept of mangling to multiplayer online gaming in order to demonstrate that the game actually played by participants is not the game designers originally had in mind but rather one that is the outcome of an interactively stabilized mangle of practice of designers, players and broader social norms. Olohan (2011) makes a “mangle-inspired” case about translators' contributions to an online technical forum. Kuk and Davies (2011) apply mangle theory to explicate the distinction between the conditions for initial participation, and the conditions for sustained participation in service innovation with open data provided by governments.

## 3. Methodology

In order to reach this paper's goal, a single longitudinal case study was developed. The company selected for the case study – Barilla – is highly innovative and represents one of the most engaged companies experimenting with social media in Italy today.

Data was collected primarily by means of semi-structured interviews inside the company and by observations of the social media under investigation.

Interviews ranged between 40 min and 2 h and involved several key informants covering different roles in the company (see Table 1). Interviews were conducted between September 2009 and the end of 2010. All interviews were carried out by the research team and detailed notes were taken. Subsequently, full write-ups were done. In this phase, interviewees were given the opportunity to make comments on drafts in order to support the credibility of the analysis. Secondary data were collected in accordance with the literature on social science research methods, advocating the use of different sources of data and methods to validate one another (Jick, 1979). In particular, data from several secondary sources (such as annual reports, newspapers, magazines, articles, master's theses, blogs, blog comments, white papers and research reports, as well as industry presentations and public commentary made by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's social media team) were used to enrich the portrayal of the phenomenon under study.

Observations were completed between September 2009 and September 2011 and they include all of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's public social media outlets, including MIW, Facebook, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's Twitter accounts. The authors monitored these services weekly via website visits as well as through Twitter search. Monitoring included ongoing ranking of ideas, introduction of new ideas, comments posted on MIW blog, updating about realized projects, and so forth.

Regarding data analysis (see, for example, Sahay & Robey, 1996), the method for analyzing the interview data was intended as a process of progressive re<sup>fi</sup>nement, moving from raw transcribed interview text toward more general theoretical inferences. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions performed on data for each round of interviews included coding and splitting. For the <sup>fi</sup>rst round, the authors began with a simple coding scheme that included broad categories describing initiation, implementation and ongoing outcomes of the platform. Within these broad categories, more detailed coding schemes were identified to include more speci<sup>fi</sup>c positions expressed by respondents, e.g. each platform functionality or speci<sup>fi</sup>c expected outcomes. The initial coding scheme was re<sup>fi</sup>ned through an iterative process whereby authors independently read sample transcripts, coded text into preliminary categories, discussed and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the scheme. According to this scheme, all interviews were split into coded segments. Through data examination of second round interviews, the authors enriched the scheme with new categories describing platform operation, assessment of outcomes, possible unintended outcomes, solutions adopted, newly emerged goals and so on. This scheme was also re<sup>fi</sup>ned and the interview data were coded accordingly.

Secondary data, such as press releases, blogger comments, detailed reports on observation of all the <sup>fi</sup>rm's public social media outlets, <sup>fi</sup>rst of all MIW, were analyzed and coded in broad categories according to consumer attitudes expressed towards the platform over time. Within these broad categories, more detailed coding schemes were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed to include more speci<sup>fi</sup>c interpretations expressed by the platform's users/observers. From this analysis, the online community's main conservative vs. innovative position emerged.

The <sup>fi</sup>nal step in data analysis involved reading empirical data through the lens of the double mangle theory. First of all. collected data allowed the distinction and examination of two main developmental stages in MIW's life. The <sup>fi</sup>rst started in March 2009, with MIW's <sup>fi</sup>rst release, and ended in September 2010, when signi<sup>fi</sup>cant software changes were made, resulting in a new release of the social platform. The second started at MIW's second release and ended in September 2011, when observation concluded. The <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage closed because its natural end corresponded with the termination of MIW's <sup>fi</sup>rst release; the second stage was still open, however the authors elected to end their observation in September 2011. The aim of this was to have two developmental stages of a comparable length of time, i.e. both 12 months.

In each stage of the platform's evolution over time, frames of meaning for the different actors were derived from the analysis of interview data related the <sup>fi</sup>rm and of secondary data related to consumers/ users — mainly activity on the platform and on social media. Such frames of meaning were compared and confronted with the main elements of the mangle. The <sup>fi</sup>rm's and the users' agency were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as human agency that intertwines, in what we de<sup>fi</sup>ned outer and inner entanglements respectively, with the non-human, i.e. material, agency of the platform.

Whenever the intertwining between the human agency of customers and the material agency of the platform resulted in a block of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's goals, the instance was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as resistance. On the contrary, any output of the entanglement between the human agency of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the material agency of the platform directed at bypassing or contrasting resistance, whether involving modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the platform features or not, was classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as accommodation. Thus the evolution of the platform over time is read as a continuous dialectic of resistance and accommodation.

## 3.1. Case setting

Originally established in 1877 as a bread and pasta shop in Parma, Italy, Barilla is now one of the top Italian food producers and a leader in the pasta business worldwide, in the pasta sauces business in continental Europe, in the bakery products business (which constitutes the focus of the present paper) in Italy and in the crisp bread business in Scandinavia.

The company employs more than 13,000 people, exports to more than 100 countries and in 2011 had net sales of €3,916 million, EBTDA (Earnings Before Taxes, Depreciations and Amortizations) of €476 million and EBIT (Earnings Before Interests and Taxes) of €296 million, net pro<sup>fi</sup>t €76 million (available at www.barillagroup.it). The company has been managed for over 130 years by the same family and is now run by the fourth generation.

Mulino Bianco (White Mill) brand was created in 1975 to launch a new line of oven-baked products. The concepts of wellness and healthy eating were captured by associations with nature and Old World traditions. The word “Mulino” (mill) stood for a simple and ancient way of making wheat products; “Bianco” (white) was attributed the psychological perception of purity, force and brightness. Although White Mill's (WM) marketing strategy is imbued with the values of the “good old days". Barilla is a modern global company; it is highly innovative and has a special focus on social, environmental and economic sustainability. The company's innovation portfolio – which is constantly revised on the basis of de<sup>fi</sup>ned priorities – is made up of approximately 150 projects at different stages of advancement to be <sup>fi</sup>nalized within two to three years. In 2011 Barilla invested €36 million in R&D and employed about 250 people dedicated to research, technological and product innovation and quality control (source http://barillagroup.com).

This article sheds light on the characteristics of the social media platform for innovation – MIW – which the <sup>fi</sup>rm implemented in 2009. Through this platform (http://www.nelmulinochevorrei.it/), customers can share ideas and suggestions about company's products, promotions, initiatives, social/environmental commitment and other aspects. The platform's main feature is an application allowing customers to submit new ideas to the company, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the WM business area, and comment on, vote on and revise others' ideas.

Ideas are collected in a structured manner, according to areas (products, promotions, packaging, environment and social issues and other) and thematic <sup>fi</sup>elds (e.g. new ingredients, new recipes and new products, regarding the Products area). Ideas are then voted on by the community and those ranked the highest (top ten) are considered for implementation by WM's marketing department.

The MIW homepage (see Fig. 1) illustrates the “idea space” in a sidebar on the left side of the page by enumerating how many ideas are available for voting in each thematic <sup>fi</sup>eld. Users can explore the idea space in two main ways: by navigating through areas and thematic <sup>fi</sup>elds or selecting one of the following options: Most voted ideas, Recent ideas, and Not to forget ideas. Concerning the <sup>fi</sup>rst way, if a user chooses to navigate via areas and thematic <sup>fi</sup>elds, they are presented with a list of ideas in those categories. Concerning the second way, when users click “Most voted ideas”, the Top 10 ideas are displayed, sorted by number of votes received. Similarly, when users click “Recent ideas” a collection of the most recently posted 14 ideas is displayed. When users click “Not to forget ideas” (a feature added in a second release of the platform), ideas that garnered few community votes but that are deemed interesting by WM staff are displayed. They are listed in 4 categories: funny ideas, useful ideas, nostalgic ideas and “Yummy ideas”

## 4. Empirical evidence: presentation and discussion

Fig. 2 represents MIW's two main developmental stages described in Methodology (Section 3).

As is clear from Fig. 2, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two entanglements for each developmental stage. One, called outer entanglement. happens between the firm and the platform. while the other. called inner entanglement, is between the customers and the platform. Therefore, there are two kinds of human actors that intertwine with the same platform. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which has speci<sup>fi</sup>c goals to be reached through the platform, intertwines with it to reach them and, last but not least, is responsible of the platform's initial design and subsequent modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. The second is the customer, the <sup>fi</sup>nal target of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's actions, whose behavior is entangled with the platform but that cannot directly modify its features. Moreover it is worth noting that the two entanglements are themselves deeply intertwined in that outcomes of the <sup>fi</sup>rst, i.e. the outer one, result in changes in the second and vice versa, as described in the following.

The presentation of the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage starts with a description of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's purposes that guided the platform's design and development. These purposes, which become embedded in the platform's features, can be seen as the beginning of the <sup>fi</sup>rst outer entanglement between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the platform. With its implemented functionalities, the platform frames social activity, enabling and constraining but not dictating what is possible. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>rm designs and implements the platform's functionalities and, in this way, tries to shape the customers' behavior in their entanglement with the platform.

Secondly, the <sup>fi</sup>rst inner entanglement between the platform and the customers will be described. The interaction emerging from practice depends in part on the intentions and adaptive abilities of the people involved; therefore, its outcome is largely unpredictable and constantly emergent.

Thirdly, this outcome is compared to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's original purposes, highlighting the match, gaps and unintended results. The mismatching and unintended outcomes can be read as resistance in the dialectic of resistance and accommodation described by Pickering to explain the process through which humans seek to shape material agency toward particular goals. The assessment of resistance and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to face it structurally through signi<sup>fi</sup>cant platform changes features as accommodation, and thus, de facto, closes MIW's <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage. Indeed, as the following will make clear, the dynamics of resistance and accommodation characterize the entire <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage of the platform. The accommodation strategies chosen by the <sup>fi</sup>rm from time to time to face resistance in the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage do not imply changes in platform features, i.e. software modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. On the contrary, they involve changes in the behavior of the <sup>fi</sup>rm which re-interprets or even breaks some rules of the “game” to try and redirect outcomes of the interaction between customers and the platform (i.e. the inner entanglement) towards the <sup>fi</sup>rm's purposes. These in-between accommodations can be seen as part of a trial-and-error process culminating in the decision to make signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes to the platform software as a structural solution to the main instances of resistance occurring in the period. Moreover, in the light of outcomes in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, the <sup>fi</sup>rm revised and extended its initial goals and, consequently, decided to make some further changes to the platform. Thus, similarly to what was done for the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage, our presentation of the second developmental stage starts with a description of changes made to the platform compared to the <sup>fi</sup>rst version. Then, the second inner entanglement between the platform and the customers is described. The outcome deriving from such an entanglement is compared with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's new and revised purposes, highlighting their matching/un-matching and unintended results. Further accommodation strategies, which are described in the following, did not involve any relevant software modi<sup>fi</sup>cations as of September 2011, when our period of observation concluded. They involved point changes in the platform, as well as in the behavior of the <sup>fi</sup>rm which tried to redirect the outcomes of the interaction between customers and the platform (i.e. the inner entanglement) towards the <sup>fi</sup>rm's purposes.

## 4.1. The 1st developmental stage

Fig. 3 represents the two entanglements – outer and inner – we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage. The outer entanglement includes: the <sup>fi</sup>rm's initial purposes and relevant features implemented on the platform; ongoing soft accommodations (i.e. without software modi<sup>fi</sup>cations) provided by the <sup>fi</sup>rm to emerging resistance from the inner entanglement; <sup>fi</sup>nal assessment of resistance and the <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to provide structural accommodations to them (i.e. making software modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the platform). The inner entanglement describes the intertwining between the human agency of customers and the material agency of the platform and how this may end, from time to time, into a resistance for the <sup>fi</sup>rm.

At launch time in March 2009, WM had the following main objectives for its digital strategy:

1. To gather all brand lovers into an organized online community leveraging several examples of spontaneous online fan-clubs that arose for speci<sup>fi</sup>c WM products, such as “Pan di Stelle” biscuits or cult-like snack “Soldino”, which is now out of production. In 2009, the FB page entitled “Give us back Soldino” had about 10,000 subscribers, while there were about 700,000 Pan di Stelle fans.

2. To obtain feedback on WM initiatives and gain new insight from the online community. DCM spoke of a sort of “recruitment” of people to engage in a new relationship with the brand. As regards the type of insight the company expected from MIW, in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of interviews, DCM admitted:

“We don't expect breakthrough ideas from our customers online. MIW represents more an innovation in communication than a tool for involving consumers in innovation. We receive precious insight by listening to our customers, but this insight is necessarily incremental in nature. We pursue radical innovation by activating completely different research practices, by going far from our business and from what we know. Crowd-sourcing is not useful when you do not have a clear idea of what you are looking for and when you have scarce information.”

To reach the <sup>fi</sup>rst objective, the <sup>fi</sup>rm decided to go “where the clients are”. In the words of DCM:

“This choice reflects the viewpoint of customers who, first of all, hang around in their digital world; second, they draw on digital resources that are able to give answers, services, or opportunities. Third, people surf the Net according to what attracts their interest and they finally arrive at what intrigues them or brings back fond memories.”

Following this principle, the <sup>fi</sup>rm decided to monitor its fans meeting places both directly and indirectly, assigning each channel a speci<sup>fi</sup>c task and maintaining a balanced relationship among them. Moreover, through its digital strategy, the <sup>fi</sup>rm wanted to have people experience its world by engaging them directly. MIW is part of WM's overall digital strategy, with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c role of “engaging people and giving them a voice”. MIW takes the concept of crowd-sourcing and opens it up to any customer upon registration. MIW integrates and cross-promotes WM's web presence; MIW is bi-directionally linked to the of<sup>fi</sup>cial company web page and is also available as an FB application through which users can vote and comment on others' ideas. Users can also connect their MIW account with FB. In this way, any action they do on the MIW site or on MIW's FB application is visible on their FB bulletin boards.

To reach the second objective, the main tools that WM activated were a blog and, above all, an application where people can put forward ideas and vote/comment/work on others' ideas.

MIW's <sup>fi</sup>rst year of activity recorded about 4,120 ideas; six of these were selected for implementation. MIW users are mainly WM customers and brand lovers, even though participation is open to anyone. From participation data after the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of activity, users were 27% men and 73% women (98.6% from Italy); the average age was 35. Women mainly using the platform have no children, are college educated and browse from home (these site demographics have been provided by Alexa.com at http://www.alexa.com/ siteinfo/nelmulinochevorrei.it#). The community seems to be driven by people that strive to re-create the past, perhaps connected to their childhood, by proposing the re-edition of old products, old packaging, old gadgets, etc. Several WM products have been on the market since the mid-seventies, so the community was formed prevalently by those who were children at that time. The mechanism of having the same users vote for their favorite ideas seemed to have a conservative effect that cut off the most potentially innovative ideas. Thus, in this <sup>fi</sup>rst phase, MIW acted mainly as a generator of incrementally new ideas, as DCM had foreseen. In spite of the online community's generally conservative nature, a couple of disruptive radically new ideas were proposed. They were not the most voted, but nevertheless the marketing unit selected them for implementation. In spring 2009, two users proposed that WM support protected WWF (World Wide Fund for Nature) sites in Italy. MIW answered: “All of the WM team liked this idea so much that we decided to implement it, even though it is not one of the 10 most voted ideas. We contacted representatives of the WWF organization to understand how we could work together to support WWF sites in Italy”. From this idea, three successful FB projects were started. Through one of them, the “count-a-tree” project, WM committed to planting one tree in a WWF site for each ten users that voted for their favorite WM product in the “Fruit histories” (milkshakes and fruit-cups) line, engaging in a sort of FB competition. Only one week from the application's launch date, 2500 users had voted and 250 trees had been planted. After 20 days, the <sup>fi</sup>nal target of a planting whole orchard had been reached and currently the competition continues on through new initiatives.

Through the mangle conceptual model, the story of MIW's <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage can be read as a continuous dynamic of resistance and accommodation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the online community's conservative nature can be read as resistance, while the marketing unit's direct intervention to select the most innovative ideas for implementation, independently from their results in the community voting process, can be read as accommodation enacted by the company in response to the previously mentioned resistance.

Although users' motivations and goals are important elements of human agency, we did not investigate these elements as they were not the objective of this paper. Several studies handle this issue considering both extrinsic (money, recognition, and reputation) and intrinsic (social status, task ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, and altruism) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (see e.g. Boudreau et al., 2011; Harhoff et al., 2003; Jeppesen & Lakhani, 2010; Terwiesch & Xu, 2008; von Hippel & von Krogh, 2003, 2006). At the same time, the material agency of the platform, which is entangled in the everyday practices of users, concretely participates in the production of output. As Scott and Orlikowski (2009: 19) af<sup>fi</sup>rm, “social media are not neutral pipes through which knowledge is delivered but integrally and materially part of knowledge production”. For example, in the application to submit/vote/comment on ideas, simply presenting ideas sorted from most to least recent/voted/ commented on/consulted might in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' behavior. Deciding whether this mechanism (e.g. most voted ideas will receive more votes while least voted ones will remain least voted) is self-sustaining and self-reinforcing is not the aim of this paper. Nevertheless, we can say that, in line with the extant literature rooted on mangle theory, the output of what we call inner entanglement is the unpredictable and emergent result of the mangle of practice between users and the platform. Such a result can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, from time to time, with the list of the 10 most voted ideas that WM takes into consideration for realization. This is a simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation, in that the result of entanglement between users and the platform is more rich and complex, involving, for example, the creation of a community that shares opinions and takes part in other company initiatives through their blog. We have narrowed the focus on the application to the gathering of ideas in order to exemplify our argument. The discussion on mangling could readily be extended to include all its features.

As we have already stated, the inner entanglement results in resistance whenever it is an obstacle on the path to the <sup>fi</sup>rm's goals. For example, the users' conservative attitude and their continuous desire to recreate the past can be read as resistance towards the <sup>fi</sup>rm's purpose of gaining new insight from the online community. In this case, the choice to produce limited editions of old products can be seen as accommodation by the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The winning idea was to start up production of a biscuit that was popular in the seventies. The <sup>fi</sup>rm carried out the idea of re-producing the biscuits and offered them as a free sample for customers who bought two packages of biscuits or snacks. In this way, the <sup>fi</sup>rm satis<sup>fi</sup>ed its most devoted customers, the “former children” community, without really returning to the past. In the same vein and in response to continuous calls for old surprises,<sup>3</sup> the <sup>fi</sup>rm activated a blog entirely dedicated to them and run by the person who has been managing WM surprises and promotions since 1978. As previously noted, another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant instance of resistance against the <sup>fi</sup>rm's purpose of gathering new ideas from customers was inherent in the mechanism of idea voting. The online community was conservative; therefore the most innovative ideas risked being excluded. The accommodation chosen by the <sup>fi</sup>rm was to break the “rules of the game” by directly selecting the most promising ideas – according to the marketing department's evaluation – even though they were not the most voted by the community. At the end of the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage, the <sup>fi</sup>rm had identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the main forms of resistance to its original purposes, and once it had experienced the platform's potential, it identi<sup>fi</sup>ed new goals to be reached. The main forms of resistance involved both contents and methods and referred to the community's conservative tendency. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, resistance can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the tendency to suggest ideas repeatedly on the same issues, ideas frequently connected to a return to the past and the tendency to exclude the most radically new ideas by simply not voting them. Another instance of resistance against the <sup>fi</sup>rm's dream to have a creative community working on innovative ideas for WM was that what the <sup>fi</sup>rm had was, at best, a community of “creatives”. In fact, the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of MIW revealed that people tended to prefer suggesting their “original” ideas instead of working on someone else's ideas. The result was that suggested ideas were frequently replicated or were very similar to others, whereas promising ideas, which would have needed only slight adjustment to be considered for realization, remained untouched. The <sup>fi</sup>rm decided to accommodate these instances of resistance through modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the platform's features. At the same time, these modi<sup>fi</sup>cations would also facilitate the achievement of the new goals the <sup>fi</sup>rm identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the platform.

## 4.2. The 2nd developmental stage

Fig. 4 represents the two entanglements – inner and outer – that we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the second developmental stage.

The second developmental stage starts with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to make signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes to platform features in order to provide a structural solution to the main resistance/unintended outcomes encountered in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, as well as to reach the company's new/revised goals. In particular, after the successful WWF initiative and receiving some unexpected, highly innovative ideas, the company interpreted these facts as signals of MIW's potential to gather not only incrementally new ideas, but also radically new ideas — ones that had to be of<sup>fi</sup>cially recognized by new and more challenging goals, as well as empowered by new procedures and new professional <sup>fi</sup>gures. This bias of a scarce innovative potential on the part of customers was also shared by DCM who declared at the launch of MIW that the <sup>fi</sup>rm's expectations were limited to incrementa ideas, as reported in the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage. Now, as emerged from the second round of interviews at the beginning of the second developmental stage, DCM radically changed his mind. In fact he claims:

![](/api/attachments/5TZV79JZ/fulltext/images/b74277e8f68d3de69de57bdee8f0ef8e9504b6d262ab70e0b3100bb8e96e959e.jpg)  
Fig. 4. The second developmental stage.

“How would you define the idea of a WM theme park? I don't know if it will be realized, but it is certainly a mold-breaking idea! We can definitely expect breakthrough ideas from MIW!”

As a result, in the second developmental stage, the company decided to pursue customer involvement in the innovation process with greater emphasis by fostering the proposal of radically new ideas and by improving the quality of proposals received. These new goals implied the implementation of new platform functionalities, such as:

– a procedure called search–sift through–<sup>fi</sup>nd

– a tutor

– polls on speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues.

The search–sift through–<sup>fi</sup>nd procedure was introduced both to limit the conservative attitude of the online community and to foster more radically innovative ideas, therefore it represents both an accommodation to users' resistance and a mean to pursue the company's new innovation goals. This procedure allows marketing staff and brand managers to choose an idea, independently from the success it garnered in the community (number of votes). By means of this new procedure several really innovative ideas can be directly selected and developed. Moreover, a set of ideas deemed interesting by WM staff but with few community votes are resubmitted to the community for reconsideration through the “Not to forget ideas” link. This new procedure impacts both the outer and inner entanglement. On one hand, it facilitates managers in identifying ideas with the most potential by means of a more advanced search and presentation system; on the other hand, it encourages users to go beyond easy suggestions while stimulating more challenging proposals. It is worth noting that, due to their abstract nature, ideas have to be analyzed by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's employees, which limits the applicability of automated information retrieval approaches. In this case, the platform mainly helps by providing an overview of large amounts of data, so that the marketing staff can gain insight from this information. Once an idea has been selected by means of this new procedure and has been developed, it appears on the list of developed ideas. It is clearly stated that the idea did not receive the highest ranking from the community, but that it was chosen directly by the company's management, thus contributing to a further reason for a sense of satisfaction by the contributor.

The tutor was introduced to provide users with help in order to improve the quality of the proposals received, thereby reducing idea repetition and increasing recombination. Therefore, it represents an accommodation to previously detected user resistance. The tutor provides suggestions on how to formulate ideas in order to make them clearer and have greater chances of being chosen by the community. However, the tutor does not provide any judgment on the quality of ideas. He/she also encourages users to check if their idea already exists in the community, avoiding resubmission of a previously existing idea. Repetition causes the idea to split votes, resulting in a low correlation between the quality of an idea and the number of votes it receives. In the case of great similarity with an existing idea, the tutor may encourage users to post their idea description as a comment instead of a new stand-alone idea. The tutor can also open new discussion threads and delete inappropriate posts to safeguard the company's reputation. Through a tutor, the company aims to be present, not to show off and to remain as neutral as possible.

Polls on speci<sup>fi</sup>c issues of particular interest to the company were introduced to pursue the objective of getting new ideas from the community more effectively. It represents an accommodation to the users' resistance of proposing mainly conservative ideas, aimed at re-launching discontinued products. Each brand manager can launch a quantitative/qualitative poll and then the Marketing Research Unit identi<sup>fi</sup>es users to be involved in the poll in order to have a strati<sup>fi</sup>ed sampling of the population under investigation. This procedure rewards customers with indirect <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives (free products) on the basis of the intensity of their participation in the polls.

In the second developmental stage, there were changes in the professional <sup>fi</sup>gures involved in MIW. As a consequence of the new procedures, a more systematic involvement of managers in different units was enacted. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst developmental stage, DCM acted as a sort of “idea-router”, involving the relevant brand manager, the marketing unit or the platform innovation manager from time to time. In the second developmental stage, with the new “Search–sift through–<sup>fi</sup>nd” procedure and the launch of speci<sup>fi</sup>c polls, brand managers and the marketing unit were stimulated to interact directly with the tool and use it to look for new ideas on speci<sup>fi</sup>c themes of interest. WM realized that only selected managers and experts in speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>elds can usefully interact with customers and – through this interaction – identify radical innovative ideas. So, on the one hand, new procedures were introduced in order to enlarge the commitment of the whole company and, on the other, a carefully selected group of employees was chosen to directly interact with customers. The new professional <sup>fi</sup>gures involved are themselves a form of accommodation to the resistance that emerged both in the inner and outer entanglements. In the inner entanglement, the resistance addressed is represented by the above-mentioned conservative customer behavior. In fact, merely knowing that there were marketing and innovation experts behind the scenes with the task of observing and scouting “highly innovative" ideas had the effect of stimulating the most innovative wing of the online community. In the outer entanglement, the resistance addressed is represented by the risk of bias connected to the involvement of only one person (i.e. DCM), or one department (i.e. the digital communication department) in the initial screening of ideas.

Moreover, in order to guarantee company engagement in MIW at all levels, three further types of procedures were added during the second developmental stage: a weekly review meeting involving Directors, the Business Development and Innovation Unit (BD&I) and the Digital Communication Unit; a monthly newsletter sent to BD&I, MKT, the Marketing Research Unit and Trade; and periodical review meetings involving the whole company.

In the following, some of the resistance-accommodation dynamics subsequent to the start of the second developmental stage – indicated in Fig. 4 as “Firm's ongoing accommodations to resistance” – will be described.

A <sup>fi</sup>rst example of resistance regards a slowing down of participation in the online community and the subsequent accommodation of the company. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst months after launching MIW, the number of new ideas as well as comments on others' ideas grew at a fast pace. Then, in the second developmental stage, this pace started to decrease and, for a certain time, the number of ideas remained almost unchanged. The company interpreted this resistance in MIW's evolution path as a result of the fact that, up to that moment, WM had not yet launched any product based on ideas collected from MIW. This lack of action most likely provoked dissatisfaction among contributors. The <sup>fi</sup>rst accommodation introduced by the company was to provide users more information about work in progress by means of the “Under realization ideas” link. Afterwards, a re-design of the evaluation process was undertaken in order to shorten the time-to-market factor. Small and easy to implement projects were given priority in order to show the community that the company was true to its word. Handling the process in a timely manner was an illustration that the company was not using MIW as a mere communication channel, but that it seriously considered MIW as a precious source of new ideas. For customers, it is highly important that their suggestions are actually used by the company to innovate, as attested to by comments posted on the MIW blog:

“I like the idea that one day someone in the supermarket could say DO YOU SEE THIS BIODEGRADABLE PACKAGE? IT WAS AN IDEA OF MINE!”

According to the interviews, this accommodation seemed to produce the desired result and the participation rate on MIW began to increase again.

The second example of resistance-accommodation dynamics that we will describe regards the role of the tutor, introduced to help customers in formulating new ideas. These were the words used to introduce this new role:

“Hello, my name is Daniele. I am at your disposal to help you on MIW. I have been involved in communication and social networking for many years. I love to read and listen to music and I observe what happens on the Net very carefully. I surf blogs and sites to find the most interesting trends.”

However, interviews revealed that the use of a tutor turned out to be initially below company expectations. The community did not seem to appreciate this new feature and scarcely used it. This customer behavior can be read as resistance towards the <sup>fi</sup>rm's aim of gathering better formulated ideas from the online community. A few months later, the following comment was posted on the blog of a popular social media expert:

“Personally I would prefer a woman as a tutor. Not too young, and who doesn't talk about social networking or blogs. Maybe a WM employee, let's say Emma, who has worked in the company for many years. She is there not only to provide technical assistance but also to listen. I am sorry for Daniele but a man who seems to be a professional Web consultant is not the same thing!”

Other comments in the same vein were also later posted. Therefore the company decided to change the way the tutor was introduced to the community. The words used to introduce his/her role became:

“Hello, I am at your disposal to help you on MIW. You can contact me via Skype or MSN messenger to answer any questions or doubts about the use of MIW in real time. I'm waiting for you!”

After this company accommodation, the message became more generic and better suited to MIW users who are not young Net surfers – as described above – and who, in fact, started to rely on the tutor more often and more readily. Again, this is an example of how social media are not “neutral pipes” and a few words to introduce a new actor into a community can strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence users' behaviors.

It is worth noting that it is never known in advance which accommodations will be successful nor it is known whether accommodations will themselves lead to the emergence of new resistance. In fact, after this accommodation, a new post, this time on the MIW blog, stated:

“Could we know please who is talking to us in such a kind way as a tutor? Some may not like such an impersonal approach…”

Broadly speaking, in the second developmental stage, the <sup>fi</sup>ne tuning of wording used to communicate with the community seemed to be the main kind of accommodation undertaken by the company. The language turned from a sort of of<sup>fi</sup>cial and political jargon – as noted by some users – to a more emotional and easy tone.

As illustration that the resistance-accommodation dynamics are a never ending story, interviewees told us of a plan to start a third developmental stage introducing some structural changes in the platform's features. Among others, the changes will include features to help users explore the idea space by linking similar ideas more clearly on the site, therefore reducing the repetition of similar ideas while increasing recombination with other existing ideas.

## 5. Conclusions

The scope of this article includes exploring the mangling of an information system and its users in the context of a social media platform. The lack of evidence in mangling research related to social media (with very few exceptions mentioned in the theoretical section) was addressed through the empirical case study of MIW, a social media platform developed by a leading Italian <sup>fi</sup>rm in the food industry, which was created to maintain contact with its customers and gather innovative ideas.

This paper aims at providing theoretical and practical contributions. On the theoretical side, it provides three main contributions.

1) It provides detailed attention to the co-evolution over time of human-material mangling, an aspect that papers in this area often omit.

2) It aims to provide a clear picture of a series of inter-related emergent phenomena, entangling managers, users, and the social media platform. Among information systems, social media are characterized by high marshaling potentialities, real time modi<sup>fi</sup>cation opportunities as well as real time cause–effect detection. It can be said that social media are a sort of modeling clay which allow human agents to con<sup>fi</sup>gure and recon<sup>fi</sup>gure its shape in a way precluded to other kinds of information systems. Social media based systems are places where each event, no matter how minor, assumes relevance and is literally broadcasted in real time. This also renders the dynamics of resistance and accommodation occurring on or ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed by social media particularly evident and easily interpretable. For example, in the case under examination, only some forms of accommodation to users' instances of resistance involve modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the platform, such as new procedures or new features. Other forms of accommodation to resistance manifested online happen in the real world, such as marketing initiatives or involving new professional <sup>fi</sup>gures in idea selection. Nevertheless, they are immediately broadcasted on the platform, where each fact is explained and contextualized. Thus, the social media platform contributes to enriching reality with new meaning, giving a sense to some of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's choices and involving the customer in them. This propensity of social media towards externalization and communication makes this media an ideal context to study the dynamics of resistance and accommodation and thus apply the double mangle theoretical construct.

3) It introduces further dimensions in mangling. Indeed, we observe that the double mangle can only partly explain MIW's evolution over time; whereas other mangles, which occur not only in reality – and are broadcast on the platform – but also in the whole social media ecosystem, should be considered. In fact, as is well known in the literature (Hanna et al., 2011), once a <sup>fi</sup>rm decides to develop social media strategies, the whole ecosystem of online social media, made of related elements of both digital and traditional media, is activated in some way. An in<sup>fl</sup>uential blogger may use words of appreciation or critique for some aspects of MIW, receive numerous comments by his or her followers and then WM may shortly “accommodate” the problem. Thus, besides the double mangle <sup>fi</sup>rmplatform-users, which evidence indicates as closely interconnected, other mangles on different, loosely interconnected platforms (i.e. FB, Twitter, blogs, consumer forums, etc.) may have considerable impact on its evolution over time.

On the practical side, the mangle perspective on MIW's evolution also offers useful insight for practitioners. Analysis of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's initial objectives for MIW and description of a tortuous path made of resistance and accommodation clearly indicate that the output of social media is unpredictable, as it depends on several factors and is temporally emergent. Firms that decide to use social media for any reason, including gathering innovative ideas from customers, should clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne their objectives, constantly monitor the outcome, and be prepared to adjust their objectives over time. Sailing is a relevant metaphor for this process: to go from one point to another, you must consider wind, sea, direction, tail wind, changes of course and, lastly, consider that along the way, you may even decide a slight change in your destination. Differently from a motorboat, on a sailboat you are constantly in touch with the surrounding environment and the results you obtain depend on several concurrent forces that contribute actively to those results

Future studies should seek to extend our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally exploring social media platforms for innovation in different organizational settings and industries. In fact, we note that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are limited to the extent that we only examined the adoption of one speci<sup>fi</sup>c digital innovation in a particular organizational context, but we believe our insights are valuable and generative. Future research is now needed to verify and elaborate on them, as well as to explore the possibility to apply the mangle theory to the entire social media ecosystem that is, in fact, deeply interconnected.
