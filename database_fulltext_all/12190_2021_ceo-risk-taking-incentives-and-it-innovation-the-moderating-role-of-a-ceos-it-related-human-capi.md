# CEO RISK-TAKING INCENTIVES AND IT INNOVATION: THE MODERATING ROLE OF A CEO’S IT-RELATED HUMAN CAPITAL<sup>1</sup>

Inmyung Choi Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, 703 Flint Avenue, Lubbock, TX 79409 U.S.A. {inmyung.choi@ttu.edu}

Sunghun Chung Leavey School of Business, Santa Clara University, 500 El Camino Real, Santa Clara, CA 95053 U.S.A. {shchung@scu.edu}

Kunsoo Han Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke St. W., Montreal, QC H3A 1G5 CANADA {kunsoo.han@mcgill.ca}

Alain Pinsonneault Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, 1001 Sherbrooke St. W., Montreal, QC H3A 1G5 CANADA {alain.pinsonneault@mcgill.ca}

Despite the importance of information technology (IT) innovation in today’s digitalized world, little research attention has been paid to examining how firms can incentivize IT innovation. To fill this gap, the current study investigates the impact of managerial incentives provided to chief executive officers (CEOs) on IT innovation, measured by the number of IT patents. In particular, we examine the role of risk-taking incentives provided to CEOs, captured by the sensitivity of CEO wealth to stock return volatility (i.e., Vega). Vega can motivate CEOs to engage in risky IT innovation projects by aligning their wealth with firm-specific risk. In so doing, we focus on how CEOs’ IT-related human capital (i.e., IT education and IT experience) moderates the relationship between Vega and IT innovation. Our empirical analyses reveal that a higher Vega encourages CEOs to support more IT innovation; more importantly, the impact of Vega on the amount of IT patents is stronger for firms with CEOs who have higher levels of IT education and IT experience. Our study contributes to research and practice by conceptualizing a CEO’s IT-related human capital and validating its moderating role in the relationship between risk-taking incentives provided to the CEO and the amount of IT innovation.

Keywords: IT innovation, IT patents, agency theory, managerial incentives, risk-taking incentives, Vega, IT human capital, IT education, IT experience

## Introduction

A newcompensation plan, announced in 2018, for the founder and chief executive officer (CEO) of Tesla, Elon Musk, has received a great deal of attention because of its bold pay package.<sup>2</sup> He will be paid by stock options only when the market value of Tesla reaches \$650 billion within ten years, which is more than ten times the current value of the company as of January 2018. Most of his compensation consists of stocks and stock options; he currently draws no salary, although he owns approximately 20% of the company. Despite many questions regarding the effectiveness of such a bold compensation package, Tesla has unceasingly spurred the development of new technology—for example, it recently filed innovative patents for a new technology promising a 1 million-mile battery<sup>3</sup> and the deep learning processing of selfdriving data.<sup>4</sup> This begs the question as to whether risky compensation plans such as the one implemented at Tesla matter for firm innovation.

Motivated by this anecdote, the current paper examines how CEO compensation packages can affect information technology (IT) innovation in firms. Given the importance of IT innovations in firms’ survival and prosperity (Han et al. 2012; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Trantopoulos et al. 2017),<sup>5</sup> it is not surprising that firms try to stimulate such innovations through various mechanisms, one of which includes compensation packages offered to CEOs (i.e., offering stocks or stock options). Particular attention is given to IT innovations because they differ from other types of innovations. Specifically, prior studies suggest that IT innovation is more abstract and intangible in nature (software innovation in particular), compared to other types of innovations that are tied to tangible objects (e.g., chemicals, drugs, etc.) (Bessen 2013; Chung et al. 2019; Venkatesan 2015; Vo 2012). Further, IT innovation typically embeds broad classes of technologies (e.g., software, hardware, operating systems, modules, etc.) that interact together in a more or less integrated way (Chung et al. 2019; Venkatesan 2015), which makes IT innovations more complex than other types of innovations. For instance, a given IT innovation can contain multiple IT applications designed to solve different problems. The abstract and complex nature of IT innovations and the fuzziness of their boundaries make their development highly uncertain and make them riskier compared to other types of IT innovations (Chung et al. 2019). In fact, this is illustrated by the fact that there have been a large number of litigations related to IT innovations (Bessen 2013; Vo 2012).

Therefore, CEO compensation packages that provide risktaking incentives play a particularly important role in IT innovation. CEOs are important for IT innovations because they establish and oversee the business policies and strategies of their firms (Banker, Hu, Pavlou, and Luftman 2011; Masli et al. 2016). Further, they also determine the level of funds that firms invest in innovations in general, and in IT innovation in particular (Barker and Mueller 2002). Compensation packages are important: by managing a CEO’s compensation scheme, firms can encourage him/her to make strategic decisions that will increase shareholder value. In particular, companies can design and manage their CEOs’ compensation scheme in such a way that it provides risk-taking incentives. Companies do so by configuring the volume of stock options and the exercise price of the stock options in order to nurture ambitious and risky behaviors (Larcker and Tayan 2012).<sup>6</sup>

While IS researchers agree on the importance of IT innovation, they have examined the impacts of IT innovation (e.g., Barrett et al. 2012; Boudreau 2012; Huang et al. 2017; Lee and Berente 2012; Svahn et al. 2017) without paying attention to how CEO compensation schemes can affect IT innovations. Although a handful of IS studies have examined the role of managerial compensation and incentives, they focused on the impacts of managerial compensation on either firm performance (Anderson et al. 2000) or IT investments (Masli et al. 2014; Rojas et al. 2016; Xue et al. 2017), but not on the impacts on IT innovation. We therefore have a limited understanding of how different compensation packages can affect innovations in firms. As a result, there is little guidance offered to firms regarding the role of CEOs’ compensation schemes in nurturing IT innovation and under what conditions a certain compensation scheme is more (or less) effective in stimulating IT innovation. We aim to address this gap in the literature by investigating the impact of CEO incentives on IT innovation, with a focus on the moderating role of the CEO’s IT-related human capital.

We first examine the effects of risk-taking incentives given to a CEO (i.e., a CEO’s wealth sensitivity to stock return volatility, a.k.a. Vega) (Armstrong and Vashishtha 2012; Sunder et al. 2017) on IT innovation, measured by the number of IT patents. A higher Vega means that a CEO’s option value increases when stock return volatility increases; therefore, a CEO with a higher Vega is more likely to endorse risky projects, as doing so would increase the stock return volatility.<sup>7</sup> The number of patents has been widely used as a reliable measure of innovation because it is based on objective and external evaluation processes by a governmental agency (e.g., Saldanha et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2020). Since the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) evaluates patents using specific criteria such as novelty, usefulness, and nonobviousness (Hall 2003), and since these criteria have been consistently used over time, we can compare the number of patents across firms and over time (Wu et al. 2020). We identify IT patents based on the patent classes corresponding to hardware and software patents, as defined by the USPTO.<sup>8</sup>

While the effects of Vega on top management behaviors and initiatives have been studied in the finance literature, they have received little attention in the information systems (IS) field,<sup>9</sup> especially in the context of IT innovation. By drawing on agency theory (Jensen and Meckling 1976), we examine how risk-taking incentives provided to CEOs influence the variation in the amount of IT patents.

More importantly, in order to understand the particular conditions under which CEO incentives are more effective in stimulating IT innovation,<sup>10</sup> we examine the moderating role of CEOs’ IT-related human capital, which we argue can influence CEOs’ motivation and self-confidence related to investments in IT innovation. The agency theory literature suggests that agents’ (i.e., CEOs) economic behaviors are not determined exclusively by monetary incentives (i.e., Vega), but are also influenced by agents’ human capital (Becker 1962; Leibenstein 1966; Pepper and Gore 2015).<sup>11</sup> Drawing on this literature, we posit the following: given the same compensation scheme, various CEOs may respond differently (in terms of how much they invest in IT innovation) because their propensity to promote and support IT innovation can vary, based on their IT-related human capital. Although compensation is an important motivational factor that induces CEOs to perform to the best of their abilities, CEOs’ motivation and self-confidence toward IT innovation can be influenced by their relevant abilities and experiences (Wowak and Hambrick 2010), thereby affecting how responsive they are to incentives. More precisely, we suggest that CEOs’ IT-related human capital (specifically, IT education and IT experience) can moderate the relationship between Vega and IT innovation because CEOs’ IT-related human capital can affect their perceived risk toward IT innovation, their ability to stimulate and guide IT innovations, and their expectations regarding the outcome of IT innovation (Carpenter et al. 2003; Park and Tzabbar 2016).

Based on a novel dataset consisting of 353 firms over the period 2002–2012, we find that the impact of Vega on IT innovation (measured by the number IT patents) is positive and significant. We find that, on average, a one-percent increase in Vega (approximately \$2,000 sensitivity to stock return volatility) corresponds to a 0.08% increase in IT patents (approximately 0.22 IT patents). More importantly, our findings indicate that a CEO’s IT-related human capital, measured by the IT-related degrees that the CEO holds and the number of years that the CEO spent in the IT industry, strengthens the relationship between Vega and the number of IT patents. Our results are robust to various alternative specifications and econometric concerns such as endogeneity and reverse causality.

This study makes several important contributions. First, our study measures the IT-related human capital of CEOs and examines its role in the relationship between risk-taking incentives and IT innovation. By doing so, we extend prior studies that have examined the impact of risk-taking incentives on IT investments (Xue et al. 2017) and R&D investments (Coles et al. 2006) but have not considered the role of managerial human capital. Second, our study extends the stream of research that theoretically argues that executives’ personal characteristics interact with incentives to influence their behaviors and organizational outcomes (e.g., Pepper and Gore 2015; Wowak and Hambrick 2010); it does so by empirically showing that CEOs’ domain-specific education and work experiences are important personal characteristics that can interact with risk-taking incentives to affect risk-taking behaviors in a given domain (IT innovation in our case). Finally, we make a novel contribution to the IT innovation literature by identifying CEOs’ risk-taking incentives as an important antecedent of IT patents, and by providing a comprehensive understanding in regard to the role of CEO ITrelated human capital in the incentive–innovation relationship.

## Theoretical Background

## Risk-Taking Incentives: The Sensitivity of CEO Wealth to Stock Return Volatility

Agency theory (Jensen and Meckling 1976) posits that there is a conflict of interest between agents (i.e., CEOs) and shareholders in maximizing the market value of firms. This conflict of interest also occurs when CEOs make managerial decisions on high-risk investments or projects (Armstrong and Vashishtha 2012; Benaroch and Chernobai 2017). Conventionally, providing equity-based managerial incentives (i.e., in the form of stocks and options) to CEOs has been suggested as a way of mitigating the conflict of interest between CEOs and shareholders. Equity-based managerial incentives align the interests of CEOs with those of shareholders by incentivizing CEOs to maximize the market value of firms. This scheme motivates CEOs to increase their wealth by boosting their stock and option values.

More precisely, we can distinguish between two types of equity-based managerial incentives (Coles et al. 2006; Core and Guay 2002; Xue et al. 2017). The sensitivity of CEO wealth to stock prices (a.k.a. Delta) captures the extent to which managerial incentives are aligned with the long-term performance of firms captured by stock prices (Coles et al. 2006). A higher Delta means that CEOs dedicate their efforts to increasing the market value of their firms and minimizing their firms’ losses. For instance, a CEO may commit himself/ herself to increasing the market value of his/her company because he/she will be paid by stock options whose value will increase by the long-term performance of the firm. However, he/she may not endorse risky projects: although the projects are expected to bring long-term benefits to the firm, the increased risk from these projects is not fully reflected in his/her compensation scheme. Thus, we need to consider the sensitivity of CEO wealth to stock return volatility (a.k.a. Vega), which captures the extent to which managerial incentives are designed to encourage CEOs to pursue risky projects and implement more aggressive policies that are typically associated with higher levels of stock return volatility. When the compensation packages of CEOs are weighted toward stock options (i.e., a higher Vega), their wealth increases as stock return volatility rises. If a CEO has a higher Vega, he/ she is more likely to support risky projects because increases in stock return volatility from risky projects will result in a higher value for his/her stock options.

## Agency Theory and the Role of CEOs’ IT-Related Human Capital

Recent studies in the agency theory literature (Pepper and Gore 2015; Wiseman and Gomez-Mejia 1998) argue that agents’ (i.e., CEOs) economic behaviors are not solely determined by monetary incentives, and recognize the importance of various behavioral factors including agents’ human capital in motivating their economic behaviors. An important assumption in agency theory is that agents are risk-averse, and their risk-taking preferences are fixed; as a result, individual differences across agents are irrelevant (Wiseman and Gomez-Mejia 1998). However, recent studies suggest that CEOs’ risk-taking preferences are not fixed (Gomez-Mejia et al. 2017); thus, the heterogeneity in risk-taking preferences across CEOs needs to be incorporated in terms of understanding the role of managerial incentives. Based on this notion, we argue that CEOs may have different risk perceptions about IT innovation projects, as well as different expectations of project outcomes, depending on their IT-related human capital (Leibenstein 1966; Pepper and Gore 2015).

While recent studies in the agency theory literature recognize the importance of agents’ human capital in motivating their behaviors, most of these studies have not focused on human capital’s role in the relationship between incentive compensation and agents’ behaviors and performance outcomes. A notable exception is Wowak and Hambrick’s (2010) conceptual paper, which proposes that incentive compensation and executives’ personal characteristics (e.g., experiences, abilities) have an interaction effect on strategic decisions and organizational performance. Specifically, they argue that executive characteristics such as personality, experiences, and abilities influence how executives respond to risk-inducing incentives by shaping their motives, cognitive frame, and selfconfidence. We build on and adapt Wowak and Hambrick’s model to theorize how a CEO’s IT-related human capital influences the link between risk-taking incentives (Vega) and IT innovation.

CEOs with substantial relevant expertise and experience in a given domain have a better and more holistic understanding of innovations in the domain; thus they are likely to be more confident in pursuing risky innovations, compared to CEOs with less expertise and experience (Park and Tzabbar 2016; Wowak and Hambrick 2010). Also, CEOs with relevant human capital (e.g., a higher level of IT education and more IT industry experience) are likely to have a more positive cognitive frame with respect to risky IT projects. Hence, they may be more responsive to risk-taking incentives (Wowak and Hambrick 2010), and thus may invest more in IT innovation at the same incentive level (Vega). In sum, we argue that the efficacy of risk-taking incentives (Vega) in mitigating the riskaverse tendency of CEOs and motivating them to support IT innovation critically depends on the human capital of CEOs, which influences their motivation, confidence, and cognitive frame toward IT innovation.

Human capital refers to the stock of knowledge, skills, and abilities that are accumulated through education and experience (Becker 1962; Hmieleski et al. 2015). Previous literature (Ang et al. 2002; Hillman and Dalziel 2003; Hmieleski et al. 2015) has primarily considered two dimensions of human capital: education and experience. Education is a general form of human capital that pertains to the level of formal education; additionally, experience is a specific form of human capital that is related to the amount of work experience in firms and industries (Hmieleski et al. 2015). In order to examine the moderating role of CEOs’ human capital relevant to IT innovation, we focus on the IT-related education and experience of CEOs.

## Hypotheses Development

In this section, we develop our hypotheses on how Vega affects IT innovation, and how the two types of CEOs’ ITrelated human capital moderate the relationship between Vega and IT patents. Our main argument is that CEO Vega can significantly affect the amount of a firm’s IT innovation. According to agency theory, CEOs take a risk-averse stance in making strategic decisions because the increased risks and potential benefits from strategic initiatives are not adequately compensated by conventional incentive mechanisms (Stein bach et al. 2017). In such situations, CEOs would attempt to secure or increase their current wealth (i.e., salary and bonus) by focusing on conservative projects rather than betting on promising, but risky projects. Therefore, CEOs who are compensated with conventional managerial incentive schemes (e.g., salary, bonus) would not be willing to dedicate a large amount of resources to IT innovation.

Companies can motivate CEOs to pursue and nurture IT innovation by increasing the risk-taking incentives (Vega) given to them (Larcker and Tayan 2012). A higher Vega means that the value of stock options given to CEOs increases proportionally when the volatility of stock prices increases. Under this compensation scheme, CEOs become more supportive of IT innovation because the increased firm risk from IT innovation will be reflected in the option value of the firm, which in turn will increase their wealth. Indeed, prior studies have shown that CEOs with a higher Vega invest more in R&D projects than in capital expenditures (Coles et al. 2006).<sup>12</sup> Moreover, they engage more in aggressive tax strategies, which are expected to generate greater net benefits for firms with increased risk (Rego and Wilson 2012). Also, senior managers with a higher Vega take a more proactive stance toward IT investment, which increases firm risk (Xue et al. 2017).<sup>13</sup> Applied to our context, this notion suggests that an increase in Vega will lead to more IT innovation by attenuating the risk-aversion tendencies of CEOs. Therefore, we posit the following:

H1: CEO risk-taking incentives (Vega) are positively associated with IT innovation (i.e., the number of IT patents).

Our second argument postulates that the IT-related educational background and IT-related career experiences of a CEO moderate the influence of Vega on the amount of IT innovation. CEOs have the organizational power to appoint top management team (TMT) members and influence the strategic decisions of top managers (Barker and Mueller 2002; Cummings and Knott 2018; Masli et al. 2016). However, the extent to which CEOs exert their power depends on how much they are motivated to do so. While CEOs are motivated by risk-taking incentives (i.e., Vega) to nurture IT innovation, their abilities and experiences, which shape their human capital (Pepper and Gore 2015), can help them achieve better results in terms of innovation.

In addition, the human capital of CEOs can influence how responsive they are to risk-taking incentives by affecting their expectations and risk preferences with respect to IT innovation (Bantel and Jackson 1989; Barker and Mueller 2002; Geletkanycz and Black 2001; Hoskisson et al. 2017). We argue that the IT-related educational backgrounds and career experiences of CEOs shape their risk-taking preferences toward IT innovation, which in turn influence the relationship between Vega and IT innovation.

First, the more educated a CEO is in an IT-related domain, the more confident and optimistic (and less risk-averse) he/she will be in his/her strategic decisions related to IT. Prior studies suggest that CEOs with higher self-confidence are likely to focus more on the success of risky projects rather than on the potential failure of such projects; therefore, they are more likely to pursue innovation (Galasso and Simcoe 2011) and invest in new product introductions (Simon and Houghton 2003), compared to those with lower selfconfidence. Also, CEOs with higher levels of IT-related education can have more positive expectations and higher risk tolerance toward IT innovation since they have a better understanding of both the outputs of IT innovation and its risks.

Moreover, the IT-related educational backgrounds of CEOs can influence their motivation toward IT innovation. CEOs with IT-related educational backgrounds are more likely to support IT innovation because they have well-honed IT knowledge (Tyler and Steensma 1998) and are more capable of assessing the potential value of IT projects (Banker, Feng, and Pavlou 2011). When CEOs have a lower level of IT knowledge or know-how, they are less confident and may have relatively low expectations with respect to the potential benefits of IT projects; therefore, they will try to minimize losses regarding these projects. In such a situation, the impact of Vega on IT innovation would be weakened because such CEOs have relatively low expectations and low risk tolerance related to IT innovation. To summarize, in a firm with a CEO who has a higher level of IT-related education, increasing CEO Vega will have a stronger effect on IT innovation, compared to a firm having a CEO with a lower level of IT-related education. Thus, we posit the following:

## H2: A CEO’s IT-related education positively moderates the relationship between CEO risk-taking incentives (Vega) and IT innovation (i.e., the number of IT patents).

Further, we argue that CEOs with more IT-related career experiences are likely to have higher risk tolerance and stronger motivation toward IT innovation at the same level of risk-taking incentives provided to them (Vega). Prior studies suggest that CEOs’ experiences can be a determinant of their confidence and their risk preference regarding strategic choices (Carpenter et al. 2003; Herrmann and Datta 2006; Wowak and Hambrick 2010). Indeed, CEOs’ perceptions of opportunities and threats, which are directly related to risktaking, depend on their domain- and industry-specific experiences (Hambrick and Mason 1984; Kor and Misangyi 2008). When CEOs are confident in their abilities to pursue different strategic choices, they are more prone and motivated to take risks (Wowak and Hambrick 2010).

Particularly, CEOs with a greater amount of IT-related experience are likely to be more supportive of IT innovation because it occurs in highly specialized areas, such as IT industries, where CEOs with an industry background have advantages in terms of scanning and identifying opportunities in the market (Custódio et al. 2019). IT-related career experience can help CEOs not only to figure out business problems and solutions associated with IT innovation but also to understand the nature of specific IT innovation because the successful exploration of new business opportunities requires industryspecific work experience (Colombo and Grilli 2005).<sup>14</sup> Since IT innovation follows a path-dependent pattern, prior experience in IT industries is useful in understanding the success factors of IT innovation and in making IT innovation projects successful. Similarly, previous experience in customer preference and competitors in IT industries is critical in terms of diffusing IT innovation to the market: the experience is beneficial with regard to positioning a new product or service from an IT innovation and managing the competitive dynamics around firms to successfully commercialize the IT innovation (Kor and Misangyi 2008). Moreover, CEOs with more ITrelated experience can develop greater self-confidence, which can lead to more innovative behaviors (Wowak et al. 2010). In this sense, CEOs with more IT-related experiences are more supportive of IT innovation, compared to those with less IT-related experience, due to their advantage with respect to IT-specific knowledge. Hence, we argue that CEOs who have more IT-related career experiences are likely to devote more resources to IT innovation in response to a higher Vega, due to their higher motivation and risk tolerance toward IT innovation. Therefore, we posit the following:

H3: A CEO’s IT-related experience positively moderates the relationship between CEO risk-taking incentives (Vega) and IT innovation (i.e., the number of IT patents).

## Empirical Analysis

## Sample and Data Sources

To construct our sample, we first identified Standard and Poor’s (S&P) 1,500 companies with non-missing CEO compensation data from ExecuComp and chose our sample period to be 2002–2012.<sup>15</sup> We obtained and quantified the managerial compensation data (i.e., Vega, Delta, salaries, and bonuses) of CEOs, along the lines of previous studies (e.g., Xue et al. 2017). Then, we quantified the educational backgrounds and career experiences of CEOs based on data from BoardEx (e.g., Xue et al. 2018) and multiple online sources, such as the company’s website, Bloomberg, and Hoover’s company profiles, among others. We further acquired data on the boards of directors from Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS), accounting/financial data from Compustat, and stock market data from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). We also obtained patent data from the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) and matched it with the financial data from Compustat.<sup>16</sup> As shown in Table 1, the firms in our sample are from a wide range of industries, and our final panel dataset contains 1,620 firm-year observations covering 353 unique firms in our observation window (2002–2012).

Table 3 presents the results of estimating the fixed-effects models.<sup>21</sup> Model 1 (2) includes ITED (ITEX) and its interaction term with Vega. The interaction terms between Vega and ITED, and Vega and ITEX are positive and significant (â = 0.027, p < 0.01 in Model 1; â = 0.006, p < 0.05 in Model 2), which means that the IT-related education and experience of a CEO amplifies the impact of Vega on IT innovation. ## Addressing Endogeneity

One empirical concern is that Vega can be endogenous if firms select their Vega based on unobservable factors. Such endogeneity happens when a firm’s decision to adjust a CEO’s risk-taking incentive (Vega) may be influenced by its expectations regarding the performance of IT innovation reflected in the IT patent stock. We conduct additional analyses to address these endogeneity concerns regarding Vega.

First, we employ the system Generalized Method of Moment (GMM) estimation (Arellano and Bover 1995; Blundell and Bond 1998). Previous studies suggest that the system GMM estimation not only addresses endogeneity, but also eliminates bias from unobserved heterogeneity (Bardhan et al. 2013; Menon and Kohli 2013; Pang et al. 2014; Tambe and Hitt

2012). We use the system GMM estimation, treating all of the variables as potentially endogenous (including Vega and IT-related human capital variables), except for year dummies. In order to construct the instruments for the system GMM, we use the lag(2) of the levels for the explanatory variables in the transformed equation and the lag(1) of the same variables in differences in the levels equation. We also employ the dynamic GMM by including the lagged dependent variable (IT patents) to control for the effect of the prior year’s IT patents. System GMM results were consistent with our fixedeffects estimation results.

Second, we also used two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimation (e.g., Kleis et al. 2012; Kohli et al. 2012). We employed novel instrumental variables that are valid and exogenous to a CEO’s Vega: CEO sudden death and buyerfirms’ Vega.<sup>23</sup> Model 1 of Table 4 shows that our instruments are valid, and the results are qualitatively similar to the main results.

Third, we used the control function (CF) approach with a continuous treatment developed by Garen (1984), as well as the extended Garen’s approach (Luan and Sudhir 2010) employed by previous IS studies (Khuntia et al. 2019; Saldanha et al. 2017). The results in Models 2 and 3 of Table 4 corroborate our main findings.

Finally, we conducted Granger causality tests (Granger 1969) to formally address the potential reverse causality issue. The results suggest that the CEO’s Vega Granger-causes IT patent stock, while IT patent stock does not Granger-cause the CEO’s Vega.<sup>24</sup> Taken together, these additional analyses suggest that endogeneity is not a serious concern in our study, which provides further credence to our findings

Also, while prior IS studies have examined various aspects of executive characteristics, such as CIOs’ technical background (Banker, Feng, and Pavlou 2011), IT executives’ power (Lim et al. 2013), and CEOs IT expertise (Haislip and Richardson 2018), we have had a fragmented view regarding the role of CEOs in IT initiatives. In this study, we proposed and empirically examined two dimensions (i.e., IT education and IT experience) of CEOs’ IT-related human capital, based on a seminal study in the human capital literature (Becker 1962). We believe that our conceptualization and measurement of IT-related human capital can inform future research.

Second, we also extend recent studies that emphasize the role of executives’ personal characteristics in how they react to incentives (e.g., Pepper and Gore 2015; Wowak and Hambrick 2010). Based on the long-held assumption of agents’ monotonic risk preferences, the traditional agency theory literature does not explain how different CEOs behave differently, given the same incentives. While a few studies theoretically argue that executives’ personal characteristics can interact with incentives to influence their behaviors and organizational outcomes (e.g., Pepper and Gore 2015; Wowak and Hambrick 2010), empirical evidence to date has been scarce (Wowak et al. 2017). Our research extends this stream of research by theorizing and empirically showing that CEOs domain-specific education and work experiences are important personal characteristics that can interact with risk-taking incentives to affect risk-taking behaviors in a given domain (IT innovation in our case).

Third, our study contributes to the IS literature by proposing an objective and reliable measure of IT innovation and identifying its antecedents. Prior studies have focused either on how IT contributes to general innovation (Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017) or on a specific aspect of IT innovation such as software patents (Branstetter et al. 2019; Chung et al. 2019) without directly examining IT innovation. A possible reason for this is that there has been no solid or reliable measure available to capture firms’ IT innovation. In this study, we suggest an objective way to measure IT innovation, based on IT patent data. Moreover, we identify CEO risktaking incentives and their interaction with his/her IT-related human capital as important antecedents of IT innovation.

Our research also has practical implications. First, our findings suggest that stockholders can incentivize CEOs to make risky investments related to IT innovation via designing and managing CEOs’ compensation schemes. If board members want their firms to engage in more IT innovation, they can compensate CEOs with a high Vega so that their CEOs will support IT innovation more strongly. Second, and more importantly, our results underscore CEOs’ IT-related educational background and IT-related experiences as important factors that determine the efficacy of Vega in fostering IT innovation. Our study emphasizes that firms need to scrutinize IT-related human capital when they hire a new CEO, as these factors will significantly influence the firms’ IT innovation activities in the future.

## Limitations and Future Research

This research is not without limitations, and we suggest several directions for future studies. First, while we examined the role of CEOs’ IT-related education and experiences in IT innovation, other individual characteristics of CEOs can also affect IT innovation. For instance, it would be interesting to examine how an MBA education moderates the relationship between risk-taking incentives and IT innovation. In addition, founder CEOs usually take more risks than other CEOs (Forbes 2005). Furthermore, CEOs’ personalities and values may influence how responsive they are to risk-taking incentives (Wowak and Hambrick 2010). Examining the role of other CEO characteristics in motivating CEOs to engage in IT innovation would be an interesting avenue for future research. Second, we measured IT innovation based on the number of software and hardware patents. While using the number of IT patents has the aforementioned merits, it may not fully reflect firms’ IT innovation activities. Thus, developing finergrained measures of IT innovation would be an interesting avenue for future research, although it will be challenging because one would need to access firms’ internal data related to their IT innovation activities. Third, while our study focused on examining the antecedents of IT innovation from the perspective of CEO compensation and IT-related human capital, future studies should also investigate how IT innovation increases firm performance, and how risk-taking incentives influence the performance effect of IT innovation. Specifically, IT patents may have a substantial impact on firms’ market performance, given that IT patents have a strong potential to change the current business processes and contribute to creating new businesses and markets.

## Conclusion

This study examined how the managerial compensation given to CEOs influences IT innovation with a focus on how CEOs’ IT-related human capital (i.e., IT education and IT experience) moderates the relationship between a CEO’s risk-taking incentives (Vega) and IT innovation. Our results reveal that increasing Vega is an effective risk-inducing compensation scheme for fostering IT innovation, and that this relationship is moderated by CEOs’ IT education and IT experiences. Thus, this study unravels the roles of CEOs’ compensation schemes and IT-human capital in the context of IT innovation. This study has important implications for both research and practice, and we hope that this study can inform future research efforts on IT innovation.
