# Knowledge transfer in virtual settings: the role of individual virtual competency

Yinglei Wang\* & Nicole Haggerty<sup>†</sup>

Information Systems, Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, ON, Canada, N6A 3K7, \*email: ywang@ivey.uwo.ca and <sup>†</sup>email: nhaggerty@ivey.uwo.ca

Abstract. Economic forces, competitive pressures and technological advances have created an environment within which firms have developed new ways of organizing (e.g. virtual work settings) and managing their resources (e.g. knowledge management) in order to maintain and improve firm performance. Extant research has highlighted the challenges associated with managing knowledge in virtual settings. However, researchers are still struggling to provide effective guid ance to practitioners in this field. We believe that a better understanding of individual virtual competency is a potential avenue for managing the complexity of knowledge transfer in virtual settings. In particular, we suggest that optimal knowledge transfers can be achieved by individuals armed with the right personal capabilities and skills for virtual work, particularly when those knowledge transfers are emergent, bottom-up and cannot be specified a priori. The virtual competency exhibited by individuals can be the key to overcoming the constraints of knowledge transfers with such characteristics because underlying competency can facilitate effective action in unfamiliar and novel situations. In this conceptual research, we develop a theoretical model of individual virtual competence and describe its role in the communication process, which underpins effective knowledge transfer in virtual settings. Additionally, we consider the antecedent role that prior experience in virtual activity plays in aiding workers to develop virtual competence, which in turn engenders effective knowledge transfer. We conclude with implications for future research and for practicing managers.

Keywords: virtual organization, computer-mediated communication, knowledge management, knowledge transfer, competence

## INTRODUCTION

Several environmental factors have driven firms to change the way they organize the work place (Igbaria, 1999; Saunders, 2000). Managing increasingly complex tasks, coordinating outsourcing arrangements, responding to globalization – these activities all require flexible and distributed intra-organizational and interorganizational work arrangements (Townsend et al., 1998; Lipnack & Stamps, 1999; Saunders, 2000). Fortunately, advances in information technology (IT) have made it possible for people to communicate and coordinate remotely at relatively low financial cost (Constant et al., 1996; Saunders, 2000). The combination of technology and necessity has helped create a new way of organizing work – the virtua organization (Hedberg et al., 1997; DeSanctis & Monge, 1999).

Virtual organization (virtual work) is the working environment where people work remotely across time and/or place and/or organizational boundaries (Townsend et al., 1998). It is different from traditional work because using a large variety of different technologies for communication and coordination of work is required, rather than just face-to-face communication (DeSanctis & Monge, 1999; Lipnack & Stamps, 1999). Communication technologies that are widely adopted in this environment include email, instant messaging, company intranets, software such as Lotus Notes (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Sharepoint (Microsoft, Seattle, WA, USA), video conference, knowledge management systems and sometimes even the telephone (Webster & Trevino, 1995; Saunders, 2000; Johnson & Lederer, 2005). Examples of virtual work include working from the office with others using email and other technologies (whether they are local or distant people), working from home or from satellite offices, working on the road and groups of professionals working together across the interne on common topics. tasks, proiects. etc (Chudoba et al.. 2005).

All of these increasingly common examples demonstrate certain levels of virtuality or virtualness. According to Chudoba and colleagues (2005), virtuality describes the degree of the discontinuity of collective work. When collective work becomes less continuous because of the difficulties caused by factors such as geographical dispersion (as an example), this work setting becomes more virtual. The major factors that contribute to virtuality include varying time zones, varying geography, differences across organizational boundaries and differences in work practices among group members (Chudoba et al., 2005; Gibson & Gibbs, 2006). The level of virtuality is a function of all these factors, a higher level suggesting more collaboration difficulties between individuals and, consequently, more coping and communication efforts.

Concurrent with the evolution of the virtual organizational form, academics and practitioners have come to emphasize the strategic role of knowledge and intellectual capital in firms. From Peter Drucker’s claims of knowledge as the new basis of competition (Drucker, 1999) to important books including The Knowledge Creating Company (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), Wellsprings of Knowledge (Leonard-Barton, 1995) and Working Knowledge (Davenport & Prusak, 1998), knowledge and the evolving intellectual capital within the firm have become essential considerations in management practices. The significance of managing knowledge within firms to be competitive, combined with the distributed or discontinuous essence of virtua organizational forms, places enormous challenges on today’s business leaders who are driven to seek optimal firm performance (Griffith et al., 2003).

If knowledge is indeed a source of competitive advantage, then, in particular, the capabilities to support knowledge transfer become critical in distributed or virtual settings. A major advantage of virtual organizations is that they help exploit the knowledge of distributed employees with diverse backgrounds and experiences (Townsend et al., 1998; Malhotra & Majchrzak,

2004). Consistently, prior research has suggested that people with different backgrounds are able to contribute to integrated knowledge repositories to support a firm’s knowledge management activities (Alavi & Tiwana, 2002). However, to create such a repository, knowledge has to be able to flow among members so that their interactions both transfer knowledge and help generate new knowledge (Nonaka, 1994). Thus, regardless of the form of knowledge (explicit, codifiable knowledge or tacit, observation- and interaction-based knowledge), effective knowledge transfer of diverse knowledge forms and sources within virtual organizations is a critical activity that ensures the success of knowledge integration and, consequently, the overall performance of virtual organizations (Rafaeli & Ravid, 2003; Sarker et al., 2005).

However, many challenges to effective sharing of knowledge remain in virtual settings (Furst et al., 1999; Mark, 2001; Vakola & Wilson, 2004). In particular, using IT to remotely commu nicate creates communication difficulties that are not apparent in traditional work arrange ments (Cramton, 2001; Powell et al., 2004). These difficulties jeopardize the transfer of knowledge in virtual organizations because communication plays such an important role in knowledge sharing activities and outcomes (Sarker et al., 2005; Hasty et al., 2006). Research has revealed that the effectiveness of knowledge transfer in virtual organizations is limited because people tend to artificially simplify knowledge when using technology as a communication medium, and communication messages can lose richness in these settings (Griffith et al., 2003). It has also been argued that difficulties arise because this way of working is a novelty – firms and especially individuals are still at the early stage of learning how to use this organizing form and the technologies and communication skills that support it (Kock, 2004).

Given this ongoing learning process accompanying virtual organizations, we approach knowledge transfer in virtual organizations from a social cognitive (learning) perspective (Bandura, 1986) at the levels of the individual worker. While other knowledge research has provided useful insights at the level of the group and firm (e.g. Szulanski, 1996; Argote & Ingram, 2000b; Tsai, 2001), our work emphasizes the notion that knowledge work is, at the end of the day, carried out by individuals who must deploy their own skills and abilities to accomplish their work effectively (Nonaka, 1994; Shariq, 1999). Thus, we focus on individual knowledge, skills and abilities (KSAs) rather than specific technical features and organizational factors, which have themselves received both theoretical and empirical research attention (see Powell et al., 2004). In this paper, we develop a concise theoretical model that explores how knowledge transfers are related to individuals’ demonstrated abilities in virtual organizations and how those individual abilities are characterized and developed.

Our goal in this conceptual work is to make theoretical contributions in the domains of knowledge transfer and virtual organizational forms. First, we introduce and define a key factor, virtual competence, and explore its role in knowledge transfer in virtual settings. Our research addresses a call for further exploration of the KSAs that members of virtual organizations reguire in order to undertake virtual work (Powell et al.. 2004). Researchers have posited that there are likely different KSAs that contribute to virtual work success than the KSAs required to be successful in traditional settings (Townsend et al., 1998; Furst et al., 1999; Shin, 2004). What they are and how they are developed are not yet clear (Powell et al., 2004). Synthesizing findings in existing research on virtual organizations and integrating a novel cognitive approach using a general framework of competence at work (Spencer & Spencer, 1993), we propose a set of individual capabilities required in virtual settings, labelled virtual competence. We theorize that individuals with higher virtual competence are able to accomplish knowledge transfer in virtual settings more effectively because they can cope better with the constraints caused by communication issues in such settings.

Second, we draw on social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), channel expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999) and media naturalness theory (Kock, 2004) and offer a theoretically based holistic view of how individuals can develop the capabilities to cope effectively with virtual organizational forms. We offer a set of propositions to advance both practices and research on the development of individual KSAs that link to the effectiveness of knowledge transfer and virtual work in virtual settings.

This paper is organized as follows: The following section establishes background on the phenomenon of interest – knowledge transfer practices in virtual settings. We explore the underpinning communication research perspectives that have informed research in that context. We follow with a proposed conceptualization of individual virtual competency. Our work is predicated on a foundation of specialized communications capabilities relevant to knowledge transfer in virtual settings. Then, we discuss how individual competency in virtua settings is developed. The paper concludes with suggestions for management practice and future research.

## KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER AND COMMUNICATION IN VIRTUAL ORGANIZATIONS

Knowledge transfer is critical in virtual organizations. Generally, researchers have argued that knowledge transfer is a major mechanism to share best practices and facilitate knowledge integration, in order to enhance both short-term performance and competitive advantage (Argote & Ingram, 2000b). However, knowledge transfer has a special meaning for virtua organizations. In these settings, organizational members are more likely to have diverse backgrounds than in traditional work environments (Townsend et al., 1998; Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2004). So, it is critical that individuals in virtual organizations transfer knowledge in order to build a common frame for collaborating effectively (Sarker et al., 2005). Working in a virtual organization is also a learning process (Robey et al., 2000). Virtual organizations usually have many temporary teams (Panteli & Duncan, 2004), so to some extent team members play the role of knowledge transfer agents – they are the seeds that carry and spread knowledge across the whole organization, a similar notion as ‘learning-by-hiring’ (Song et al., 2003) where individuals are hired from the outside to bring in new knowledge. These arguments verify that knowledge transfer plays a more important role in virtual settings than in traditional co-located settings.

In this study, we approach knowledge transfer from an individual perspective. From this perspective, knowledge transfer could be defined in one of two ways. Knowledge transfer within person is defined as knowledge acquired in one situation being applied to another (Singley & Anderson, 1989). Alternatively, knowledge transfer can be defined between people as transmitting knowledge to and absorbing knowledge from each other (Sarker et al., 2005). Given our focus on virtual settings that emphasize collective work, we focus on the second kind of transfer – knowledge transfer between individuals using some form of mediated communication. Therefore, in keeping with prior research, we define knowledge transfer as the process by which a certain ability is transferred from a source to a recipient (Darr & Kurtzberg, 2000; Ko et al., 2005), where the source or the recipient could be an individual or a group of individuals (e.g. Argote, 1999; Ko et al., 2005). From a cognitive perspective, this process can be described as a process of coding, transmitting and decoding knowledge artefacts (Garavell et al., 2002). In a virtual setting, at one end, the source(s) must codify knowledge into a certain type of knowledge artefacts (words, text, actions, etc.). The knowledge artefacts are transmit ted within the context of a virtual setting by using some form of technology. Then, at the other end, the recipient(s) interprets or decodes the artefacts received. While these knowledge transfers can certainly take place within the context of group and collective virtual settings, our research focuses on the resulting implications that virtual knowledge transfers have on cognitive elements of the phenomenon that necessarily are at the level of the individual.

This definition of knowledge transfer emphasizes the transfer of ability rather than just the knowledge artefact itself. For us, knowledge is defined as justified beliefs and helps individuals act effectively (Nonaka, 1994). Thus, when knowledge is effectively transferred, the recipient experiences an increment in ability, which results in enhanced individual work performance and collectively organizational performance of the destination (Szulanski. 1996: Argote 8 Ingram, 2000a). Although creating and transmitting knowledge artefacts are necessary steps to this goal, they do not guarantee the transfer of ability. The recipient has to cognitively make sense of the artefacts and develop ability based on the codified knowledge (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Garavelli et al., 2002; Hasty et al., 2006).

This view of knowledge transfer resembles the process of communication, which includes coding, transmitting and decoding signals (Shannon & Weaver, 1949). Both definitions highlight the handling of artefacts in order to generate meanings for participants. Regarding knowledge artefacts as kinds of signals, knowledge transfer can largely be viewed as a particularly specialized communication process between the source and recipient, through which knowledge is negotiated back and forth between the two and passed on in the form of changed ability in the recipient (Ko et al., 2005; Lin et al., 2005). This theorizing suggests an avenue to study knowledge transfer through a communication lens, which is known as ‘a communications metaphor’ of knowledge transfer (Szulanski, 1995, p. 437).

In fact, theories and findings of communication research have frequently been drawn upon by research on knowledge transfer and proven fruitful. For instance, a well-cited mode categorizes the factors that influence the effectiveness of knowledge transfer at various stages (initiation, implementation, ramp-up and integration) into four groups: the characteristics of knowledge sources, the characteristics of knowledge recipients, the context in which knowledge transfer happens and the knowledge transferred (Szulanski, 2000). Szulanski himself derived these factors from Shannon & Weaver’s (1949) classic in communications research that are still used today to study effective online communication (e.g. Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997;

Carlson & Zmud, 1999). This application of communication research directed our attention to individuals involved in the process, extended our understanding of challenges to the effectiveness of knowledge transfer at the level of the individual and provided a rich avenue for further investigation, which we take up here.

In addition, communication practices appear more salient than other factors that influence knowledge transfer in virtual settings (Hasty et al., 2006). The influence of specific communication practices on knowledge transfer in traditional settings has not been well researched. This may be because of the dominance of face-to-face modes of communication, which are viewed as a given in these settings and simply assumed to be effective enough to facilitate knowledge transfer. However, effective communication activities cannot be taken for granted in virtual settings (DeSanctis & Monge, 1999). The underpinning element of virtual organizations, computer-mediated communication (CMC), has been criticized for its limited capacity o transmitting rich information and consequent ineffectiveness for transferring non-codified knowledge (Griffith et al., 2003). Besides, virtual organizations are often made up of individuals with diverse cultural and language backgrounds (Robey et al., 2000; Gibson & Gibbs, 2006). This diversity can intensify the challenges of communicating effectively and impede knowledge transfer (Robey et al., 2000; Cramton, 2001). In sum, the communication mode (CMC) that virtual organizations use creates new knowledge transfer issues that are not yet well understood.

Therefore, we believe that communication theory provides a useful lens for understanding knowledge transfer in virtual organizations and brings with it particular perspectives that can help organizations improve knowledge transfer. Through this lens, many difficulties associated with knowledge transfer can be attributed to ineffective communication media or underdeveloped media-based communication skills in participating individuals. For instance, one of the most widely recognized problems in knowledge management is that tacit knowledge is hard to transfer (Argote, 1999; Alavi & Leidner, 2001). From a communication perspective, this can be explained by the stance in prior research that lean (non face-to-face) communication channels in virtual settings do not provide enough support (bandwidth) for the necessary transfer of rich information to assist the recipient to acquire tacit knowledge (Daft & Lengel, 1986). In other words, tacit knowledge is difficult to transfer because people are not able to communicate their thoughts effectively in a mediated setting. This implies a technology-based problem (which is the typical attribution made about tacit knowledge transfer problems) but it also, we believe, implies an individual communication skill problem (which has not been specifically investigated). Either way, knowledge transfer is likely to become more effective if there is a way to improve the effectiveness of communication in virtual settings (Ko et al., 2005).

Current research investigating the improvement of communication effectiveness in virtua organizations focuses on media usage issues. This approach is based on media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986) and emphasizes the complementariness of various communication channels to the needs of the message (rich channels for rich information etc.). Previous studies have shown that early face-to-face meetings and training can help overcome the limitations of lean media (and other social concerns) by increasing technology familiarity, assimilating individuals’ backgrounds and developing interpersonal relationships between team members (Warkentin & Beranek, 1999; Suchan & Hayzak, 2001). However, these solutions require people to be together, which can be problematic in virtual settings (Townsend et al., 1998; Mark, 2001); they do not solve the essential and ongoing difficulties of CMC in virtual organizations.

Our alternative approach is to look at the individuals instead of the media. We base our theorizing on social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), channel expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999) and media naturalness theory (Kock, 2004). We believe that individuals are inexperienced in and ineffective at communicating in virtual environments particularly when the goal is knowledge transfer leading to the development or acquisition of new skills in the recipient because it is such a new phenomenon (Kock, 2004). Therefore, we investigate knowledge transfer in virtual organizations by focusing on the development of individua capabilities which we cast as the underpinning of effective knowledge transfers. We propose the concept of individual virtual competence (IVC), which represents the key KSAs that enable individuals to undertake effective knowledge transfer in virtual environments – capabilities that overcome the technology-based limitations of such settings. Further, our model suggests that IVC is positively associated with prior virtual experiences from a variety of sources. In the next sections, we develop the theoretical justification for this model along with our propositions.

## A NEW CAPABILITY: IVC

Previous studies have investigated knowledge transfer from many perspectives (Argote, 1999). Some of them look at knowledge itself, some look at the parties involved, some look at the link between those parties and some look at the context (Szulanski, 2000). However, given that knowledge is a product of cognition, knowledge transfer is essentially a human process (Shariq, 1999). Therefore, in this paper, we emphasize individuals and focus on their KSAs, which facilitate knowledge transfers as either the source and/or recipient of knowledge. Ou purpose is to advance the understanding of the effects of individual factors as distinct from and in addition to contextual, relational and other factors previously identified.

We adopt a social learning perspective when investigating individuals’ KSAs. Virtual organizations have only become prevalent during the last decade or so with the increasing deployment of communication technology that is now common in many firms (Townsend et al., 1998). New deployments of collaborative communication technologies and the skills to use them effectively are still new to many people, making it difficult to cope with the new way of working (Kock, 2004). Many people in organizations are still learning how to use IT for purposes of communicating and collaborating to accomplish work virtually (Staples et al., 1999). Researchers generally agree that competence is an important outcome of learning and a major antecedent of performance (Kraiger et al., 1993). Therefore, we have adopted competence as a focal concept in our theorizing. The idea is that if individuals possess more of a certain type of competence, they will be able to achieve higher performance (Kraiger et al., 1993). If we can conceptualize the types of new competences emerging as a necessity in the face of changing technologies and organizational work practices, then, we believe, we can make major contributions to research and practice.

Competence has an important role in human activities, and various types of competence have been widely studied in information systems research (see Spencer & Spencer, 1993; Marcolin et al., 2000; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004; Lin et al., 2005). According to the Oxford English Dictionary (1989), competence is the state of having the necessary ability, authority, skill, knowledge, etc. Because what is ‘necessary’ depends on the context, the theorization of competence may vary in different contexts. In the field of information systems, different types of competence have been identified as playing important roles in influencing individual performance with technology and business/technical management performance. Examples include user competence in the IT adoption literature, IT competence and business competence in the IT personnel literature (Munro et al., 1997; Marcolin et al., 2000; Bassellier et al., 2001; 2003; Bassellier & Benbasat, 2004). Extant research offers rich insights into how individuals’ abilities influence their performance within and across various contexts – except in virtual settings.

In the context of virtual settings, we define virtual competence as the extent of an individual’s KSAs to work and communicate in virtual environments for the purposes of completing collaborative virtual work projects. This definition implies the context in which we study individual competence as well as the multidimensional essence of competence. Similar to the role competence plays in other settings (Spencer & Spencer, 1993), virtual competence reflects KSAs such that people who have higher virtual competence should perform activities (such as knowledge transfer) better than individuals who have lower virtual competence in virtual organizations.

Our literature review and synthesis of individual competence in the information systems (IS) literature provides guidelines to theorize the dimensions of virtual competence. First, we identified studies in the IS literature on competence by a search of ABI/Inform (Abstracted Business Information) with competence and IT (IS, communication) as the keywords. This keyword search gave us the broadest possible spectrum of available research across a wide variety of journals, rather than delimit our search to just specific journals. Secondly, we looked through the bibliographies of key articles to find other relevant articles. Appendix I is a summary of the key articles we found in the IS literature. This list is not an exhaustive but a representative one that covers key articles pertaining to individual competence in the IS literature. From the studies summarized in Appendix I, we can see that there is a consensus in the literature that competence is multidimensional. For instance, IT competence of managers has been proposed consisting of three components, explicit IT knowledge, experience with IT and cognition (vision) (Bassellier et al., 2003). Accordingly, empirical studies have used a variety of formative measures for competence (Marcolin et al., 2000). Among various components, several factors have been argued to be the building blocks of competence, and the IS literature most commonly mentions: context-specific knowledge, skill and self-efficacy (Marcolin et al., 2000; Bassellier et al., 2003). Based on these exemplars and the general framework of competence at work developed in the literature (Spencer & Spencer, 1993), we theorize that three essential dimensions form virtual competence in virtual organizations: virtual self-efficacy, virtual media skill and virtual social skill.

The first dimension of virtual competence is virtual self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is a futureorientated belief about one’s abilities to accomplish behaviours and tasks in a particular context (Bandura, 1986). This belief influences individuals’ choices about the effort and persistence exerted when undertaking behaviours. An individual with strong self-efficacy beliefs is more likely to master challenges in various situations. In the context of virtual settings, we define virtual self-efficacy as an individual’s belief about his/her abilities to use CMC and undertake work tasks remotely. Consistent with the role of self-efficacy in other human behaviours, we theorize that virtual self-efficacy towards CMC and towards performance in virtual settings has a major influence on an individual’s ability to cope with such settings, to persist through difficulties and to be prompted towards accomplishment (Staples et al., 1999). This dimension reflects personal self-concept manifested in virtual settings, a component of competence that motivates individuals’ behaviours at work (Spencer & Spencer, 1993).

Two components constitute virtual self-efficacy. The first component is computer selfefficacy (CSE). CSE is defined as an individual’s confidence in his/her ability to use computer technology (Compeau & Higgins, 1995). Because virtual organizations are built on a variety of computer technologies, individuals will not perform well if they do not have the confidence to work with these technologies at a general level. Therefore, CSE is necessary in virtual settings; it taps into the technical concerns of individuals in such settings. The second component is remote work self-efficacy (RWSE). RWSE describes an individual’s confidence in his/her ability to work with others in the remote mode (Staples et al., 1999). Not being face to face may cause significant changes in the way people work; individuals thus have to have the confidence to cope with these changes to accomplish tasks in virtual settings (Fuller et al., 2006). Therefore, RWSE is also critical in virtual settings. It corresponds to the non-technical concerns of individuals in virtual settings. Because performing tasks (e.g. knowledge transfer) in such settings requires both a capability to use collaborative communication technologies and an ability to be able to complete tasks without physical collocation (Staples et al., 1999), we have included both these types of self-efficacy into our theorizing of virtual self-efficacy.

The second proposed dimension of virtual competence is virtual media skill. Virtual organi zations heavily rely on CMC, so the ability of individuals to use this new type of media is critical to their performance in virtual organizations (Kock, 2004). Virtual media skill is thus defined as an individual’s skill at using features of the virtual information and communication technology (ICT) setting to its full potential in order to facilitate communication. Different from virtual self-efficacy, virtual media skill is a part of the skill component of individual competence in virtual settings. Skill is performative rather than belief orientated (as CSE is) and emphasizes the generalization of knowledge to novel situations (Kraiger et al., 1993). Virtual media skill goes beyond what CMC is and how to use it. Using CMC itself might be easy; however, understanding its potential in virtual settings requires more and is much more valuable (Carlson & Zmud, 1999). For instance, learning how to use email to send and receive messages is not a very difficult task for most people. For some people, it is just a text tool, while, for others, it is a completely different way of collaboration (Lee, 1994). It is the ability to apply the same technology to different extents in various scenarios that indicate the different skill levels.

The third dimension of our conceptualization of virtual competence is virtual social skill. The virtual organizing form has been criticized because it does not facilitate necessary and important social interactions (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999), even though these are critica factors for successful virtual operations as they can improve cohesiveness and facilitate collaboration (Pauleen & Yoong, 2001; Ahuja & Galvin, 2003; Oshri et al., 2007). Given the technological limitations of existing media in virtual settings, we focus on the individual to explore potential facilitators of social interactions. We define virtual social skills as an individual’s knowledge about and skill at building social relationships with others in virtual settings. Social skills are not new to individuals; people socialize all the time. Nevertheless, it is important to realize many conventional skills are not applicable in virtual settings (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). Virtual social skill recognizes the differences in social activities between virtual settings and traditional settings. It includes knowledge about how to deal with such differences. For example, in traditional settings, shouting is generally easy to recognize and interpret. In virtual settings where text-based communications play a role, CAPITALIZATION OF COMMUNICATIONS CAN BE INTERPRETED AS A FORM OF SHOUTING. Hence, virtual social skill reflects an understanding of the emerging accepted etiquette for online communications and the skill to cope with it. It pertains to both the knowledge and skil components of individual competence in virtual settings.

The three dimensions of virtual competence correspond to the perceptual, technical and social concerns in the virtual organization literature (see Martins et al., 2004; Powell et al., 2004). As a relatively new environment. working virtually may feel uncomfortable and sometimes be frustrating for the individuals involved (Kock, 2004). Virtual self-efficacy indicates an individual’s willingness to make efforts and to persist in coping with the new environment. It reflects the perceptual beliefs of individuals. However, willingness itself is not enough; individuals also need know-what and know-how to function in virtual settings. Virtual media skill indicates an individual’s capability to leverage CMC technologies. It is a basic yet critical skill in virtual settings. It has technical elements but is not only technical. Proficiency in virtual media skill helps individuals see the full potential of virtual environments and improvise according to different situations. Virtual social skill pertains to a different set of knowledge. Compared with virtual media skill, it is less technical. It reflects an individual’s know-what and know-how in coping with other people.

Our theorizing of virtual competence contextualizes Spencer & Spencer’s (1993) genera framework of competence at work in virtual settings. Spencer & Spencer (1993) have argued that individual competence in work settings consists of five dimensions, including motives, physical and psychological traits, self-concept, knowledge and skills. Nevertheless, from a social learning perspective, self-concept, knowledge and skill are the most important ones among these five components because they are prone to change and can be developed over time (Bandura, 1986; Sandberg, 2000). Given that virtual work is an emerging phenomenon and people are still adapting to it (Kock, 2004), we view virtual competence as an acquired capability (Kraiger et al., 1993) and believe these dimensions constitute virtual competence in virtual settings. With virtual self-efficacy representing self-concept and virtual media skill and virtual social skill covering specific knowledge and skills, respectively, these capacities are exercised concurrently within virtual settings. revealing the profile of a competent individual in virtual settings.

# VIRTUAL COMPETENCE AND KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER IN VIRTUAL SETTINGS

In this section, we discuss the mechanisms by which virtual competence influences individuals performance in knowledge transfer situations. From the communication perspective we have adopted, we theorize that virtual competence influences knowledge transfer through its impact on the development of effective communication practices.

As discussed previously, communication plays a crucial role in knowledge transfer (Alavi & Leidner, 2001; Hasty et al., 2006). Communication practices constitute the process by which knowledge artefacts are transferred (see Shariq, 1999; Garavelli et al., 2002). Besides the transmitting of knowledge artefacts and relevant information between the source and the recipient (Lin et al., 2005), the coding and decoding of knowledge artefacts both involve communication activities (Ko et al., 2005). For instance, individuals on the recipient side may rely on several communication exchanges with the source or others to make sense of the knowledge artefacts jointly (Brown & Duguid, 2001). Knowledge transfer is thus inseparable from communication. Without communication interchanges, knowledge can only be mastered by its host, and there is no way it can be understood and utilized by others.

In addition, communication effectiveness appears more critical in virtual settings. CMC is the very basis for other activities in virtual settings (Townsend et al., 1998). Unfortunately, it is very different from traditional communication and is still difficult to use effectively for individuals in many cases (Kock, 2004). Although other factors may also influence knowledge transfer in virtual settings, communication has become more salient because of its essential role in supporting virtual operations (DeSanctis & Monge, 1999; Powell et al., 2004; Hasty et al., 2006). Sometimes, ineffective communication appears to be the bottleneck that constrains the potential of virtual organizations (see Griffith et al., 2003). Therefore, we focus on this key element and argue that effective communication is the very basis of effective knowledge transfer in virtual environments.

Proposition 1: The effectiveness of knowledge transfer leading to the development and acquisition of new capabilities in the recipient in virtual settings relies on individual participants being capable of engaging in effective communication practices.

The effects of virtual competence on knowledge transfer happen through its influences on individuals’ communication activities. Our conceptualization implies that improving virtual com petence aids in the transfer of knowledge in virtual settings because our three dimensions provide a competence foundation on which effective communication actions and knowledge transfer are built.

First, individuals who have higher virtual self-efficacy are more likely to accomplish communication goals successfully via information communication technologies than individuals with low virtual self-efficacy. Computer self-efficacy has been found to be positively related to the outcomes of using information technologies (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Compeau et al., 1999). People will perform better if they have confidence in their abilities because this confidence motivates them to mobilize their resources and persist in order to succeed (Bandura, 1986). In this case, individuals who detect difficulties in using technology broadly for communications and knowledge exchange tasks, but those who have high computer selfefficacy will be more likely to seek multiple avenues to overcome potential difficulties and to persist through multiple attempts with information technologies and, thus, ultimately achieve better communication outcomes. In addition, the other aspect of virtual self-efficacy, remote work self-efficacy, also adds to individuals' mobilization of resources to undertake the work and their willingness to persist. There are issues other than technical ones in remote communication. For instance, individuals in different time zones might find it is hard to keep up with each other. Remote work self-efficacy motivates individuals to continue to make efforts to deal with these non-technical issues (Staples et al., 1999). Consequently, individuals who show higher virtual self-efficacy will achieve better communication outcomes because they are more likely to master the challenges of communication technologies as well as the challenges of the remote mode of working and collaborating.

Second, individuals who are more skilled in virtual media will use CMC in a more effective way. Different individuals, with different skills in using a certain medium, experience the capacity of the medium differently (Lee, 1994; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). People who have more experience with a medium and who are more skilful at using it will perceive more richness than others and. thus. are able to transmit richer information through the same channel (Carlson & Zmud, 1999). This capability is of great importance in virtual settings where usual rich media, for example, face-to-face, are largely absent (Townsend et al., 1998). Separated by geographical, organizational and professional boundaries (Chudoba et al., 2005), individuals in virtual settings have an increased need for communication in order to establish a mutual knowledge base and a social network for collaboration (DeSanctis & Monge, 1999; Cramton, 2001; Fiol & O’Connor, 2005). However, the relatively lean media adopted may not be able to support this need to the extent as rich media could (Johnson & Lederer, 2005). The skill to use lean media in rich ways may compensate for the limitations of lean media; skilled individuals are likely to achieve superior effectiveness in task orientated communication as well as general communication by being able to provide multiple cues and immediate feedback via CMC (Chidambaram & Jones, 1993; Dennis & Kinney, 1998; Kock et al., 2006).

Thirdly, individuals who have better virtual social skills will be more efficient in communication in virtual settings and better able to build trusting relationships. Individuals with good virtual social skills are usually good at building a common understanding with others. As discussed in previous sections, communication and knowledge transfer involve coding and decoding processes (Shannon & Weaver, 1949; Szulanski, 2000). Common understanding between the two ends of communication activity will enable communication to be efficient because it requires less effort in coding and decoding (Garavelli et al., 2002). In addition, establishing socia relationships may reduce the transaction costs associated with communication in virtua settings (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). For instance, it has been found that interpersonal trust among virtual team members makes them more open to each other and thus significantly improves team performance (Warkentin & Beranek, 1999; Paul & McDaniel, 2004). Overall, individuals with good virtual social skills are more likely to achieve satisfactory communication outcomes with less time because they are more capable of building mutual understanding and other important relationships in virtual settings.

In sum, all the dimensions of virtual competence pertain to the effectiveness of communi cation in virtual settings. The improvement on each dimension leads to better communication practices. Therefore, improving overall virtual competence facilitates communication and consequently improves knowledge transfer in virtual organizations. Thus, we propose:

Proposition 2: In virtual settings, effective communication practices between individuals depend on each individual’s virtual competency so that individuals who have higher virtual competence will be able to better contribute to effective knowledge transfer as a source or a recipient in knowledge transfer exchanges.

## THE DEVELOPMENT OF VIRTUAL COMPETENCE WITHIN VIRTUAL SETTINGS

Having discussed the importance of virtual competence in knowledge transfer in virtual settings, we complete our theorizing by considering how virtual competence develops within the course of everyday virtual activities. ICT has changed the way people work and live (Chidambaram & Zigurs. 2001). One important conseguence of this movement is that activities related to virtual settings are more and more pervasive in both daily life and workplaces (Igbaria, 1999; Chidambaram & Zigurs, 2001). As a result, individuals are becoming more and more experienced with ICTs and related activities (such as communication, information seeking, socialization, etc.).

From a social cognitive theory perspective, experiencing these activities unavoidably influ ences individuals’ skills and perceptions (Bandura, 1986). Along this line, we adopt social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1986), channel expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999) and media naturalness theory (Kock, 2004) to explore the potential effects of experiences with virtual settings and related activities on the three dimensions of virtual competence: virtual self-efficacy, virtual media skill and virtual social skill. Our research reveals that experiences with virtual settings and related activities help individuals build their competence in communicating virtually and working remotely by contributing to the development of each of the dimensions of virtual competence. These dimensions form the foundation of capabilities that enable individuals to communicate and collaborate in more complex, subtle ways.

In the context of knowledge transfer, we theorize that individuals’ development of virtual competence facilitates their ability to work in virtual organization (virtual work) and improves their ability to transfer knowledge in those contexts. On the right side of this figure, we conceptualize communication activities as the processes by which knowledge transfer in virtual settings is tied to the virtual competence of individuals. ## Developing virtual self-efficacy

According to self-efficacy theory, people develop confidence in technologies and behaviours through their experience of observing, using and evaluating them (Staples et al., 1999). In virtual settings, people expose themselves to these technologies, putting them to various uses such as communication and collaboration. In this way, they have opportunities to develop their self-efficacy towards remote communication via ICT and working without being collocated (Bandura, 1986). Therefore, experiences with virtual settings and related activities enhance both components of virtual self-efficacy: computer self-efficacy and remote work self-efficacy. With enhanced virtual self-efficacy, people feel more comfortable with and capable of using CMC and will sustain the motivational effort to persist in some of the more difficult communicative tasks associated with knowledge transfer types of communication. Therefore:

Proposition 3: Individuals who have previously engaged in more virtual work activities and related virtual communication activities will develop over time greater virtual self-efficacy, and this improves their virtual competence.

## Developing virtual media skill

According to channel expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999), the characteristics of individuals, their experience with a medium (learning effects) and the work context all influence perceptions of media richness. Among the four kinds of experience, experience with the channel directly pertains to the medium itself. When people use media in virtual settings, an accumulation of experience towards the media happens (Kock, 2004; Massey & Montoya-Weiss, 2006). During this accumulation, individuals develop knowledge bases that enable them to code and decode rich messages (Hasty et al., 2006). They also gain the understanding of the nuances in applying the media to various partners, topics and different contexts to create rich messages despite ‘lean’ media use (Warkentin & Beranek, 1999). Overall, increasing experiences enhance an individual’s capacity to use lean media in rich ways, which in turn improves communication in virtual organizations and is reflected in their enhanced virtua competence (Lee, 1994; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). Therefore, we propose:

Proposition 4: Individuals who have previously engaged in virtual work activities and related virtual communication activities will develop better skills in using remote communication technologies in virtual organization, and this improves their virtual competence.

## Developing virtual social skil

Individuals constantly conduct social interactions with others in virtual settings (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003). For instance, virtual settings (including virtual communities on the internet) have engendered shared cultures and norms across geographical and racial communities for individuals participating in various interactive activities (Wellman et al., 1996; Sarker et al., 2001). In addition, empirical studies have shown that people can develop close relationships through remote communication (Parks & Floyd, 1996; McKenna et al., 2002). These practices give individuals opportunities to improve their skills of being with others virtually. They develop the understanding of the differences in social activities between virtual settings and traditional settings and gain the knowledge about how to deal with these differences. With the increase of experience, individuals become more adept with social interactions in virtual settings (Bandura, 1986; Kock, 2004). Experience in creating and adopting shared norms and relationships can assist individuals to develop shared knowledge and desired interpersonal relationships (such as trust), which improve the effectiveness of communication and, thus, knowledge transfer (Szulanski, 2000; Levin & Cross, 2004). Therefore:

Proposition 5: Individuals who have engaged previously in virtual work activities and related virtual communication activities will develop better social skill when interacting with others in virtual organizations, and this improves their virtual competence.

In sum, experiences with virtual settings and related activities help people develop all three dimensions of virtual competence, and this in turn improves their communication in virtual organizations. Given that communication is the foundation of knowledge transfer, we have argued that developing IVC will improve knowledge transfer in virtual settings.

## CONCLUSION AND FUTURE RESEARCH

In this paper, we have studied knowledge transfer in virtual organizations and proposed individual competence rather than technological or social factors as a key contributor to effective knowledge transfer. Competence is a widely discussed concept in multiple disciplines. This concept provides a lens to conceptualize the role of the individual in various phenomena and suggests a practical mechanism to which managers can attend. When we understand the way in which competence drives performance in various areas, we can design training, professional development, feedback and reward mechanisms to foster that compe tence (Kraiger et al., 1993; Marcolin et al., 2000).

We define virtual competence as the KSAs that individuals develop to cope with virtual environments. We conceptualize IVC as comprised of three dimensions: virtual self-efficacy, virtual media skill and virtual social skill. We argue that virtual competence influences the effectiveness of knowledge transfers through its direct impact on the effectiveness of communication in virtual settings. Higher virtual self-efficacy indicates higher motivation to persist and a broader approach to selecting available resources when an individual faces difficulties in communication. Better virtual media skill means greater capacity in transmitting information via CMC and more skilful use of specific technologies and technology features. More adept virtual social skill implies higher efficiency in building a common knowledge base and other interpersonal relationships that are important to communication processes. Overall, high virtual competence is desirable and facilitates knowledge transfer in virtua organizations because it arms both source and recipient with the motivation, knowledge and skills to effectively create, transfer and decode knowledge artefacts using a variety of technologies.

We draw on self-efficacy theory (Compeau & Higgins, 1995; Staples et al., 1999), channe expansion theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999), media naturalness theory (Kock, 2004) and theories about social skills in virtual settings to present the mechanisms to develop virtual competence. Specifically, experiences with virtual settings and related activities will have a positive influence on the KSAs of individuals to communicate and work effectively in virtual organizations. Experience with CMC gives people more confidence in using it in a virtual work setting; it helps people perceive CMC as a richer medium and enables them to use it for rich, complex communications (Carlson & Zmud, 1999; Staples et al., 1999). In addition, prior experiences in developing social relationships in virtual settings enable people to develop skills that help build mutual understanding and important relationships (such as trust) with others (Bandura, 1986; Larsen & McInerney, 2002; Kock, 2004). In sum, experiencing virtual settings and related activities enhances all of the dimensions of virtual competence. In addition, because each o these dimensions directly impacts the effectiveness of communication, experiencing virtual settings and related activities has a positive effect on communications and consequently knowledge transfer in virtual organizations.

Our theorizing shifts research focus to individuals and emphasizes the fundamental role of individuals’ KSAs in knowledge transfer. Studies on knowledge transfer and virtual organizations have attributed emerging issues, such as ineffectiveness and poor performance, to communication failures and the ‘newness’ of remote working arrangements (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999; Mark, 2001; Martins et al., 2004). Taking direction from a recent literature review (Powell et al., 2004), our efforts are to explore a key individual level concept and thereby extend our understanding of its role in knowledge transfer. Our argument is that improving IVC can bring positive changes to the processes and outcomes of knowledge transfer beyond technological changes or other factors in virtual organizations. This conclusion has special meaning for transferring knowledge that is emergent, bottom-up and cannot be specified a priori. Technological mechanisms are difficult to design for this kind of knowledge transfer because it is so unpredictable. In this case, it is the capacities of individuals to cope that can assist in making the transfers effective. In other words, once we build the highway of competencies, all kinds of activities/traffic can flow over it even though we may not know what is coming.

## Future research

This paper develops a theory that integrates the literatures of knowledge transfer, communi cation theory, virtual organizations and individual competency. We conceptualize IVC as a new factor that facilities knowledge transfer in virtual settings through the communication perspective. Given that the competence–performance link is context specific, further conceptual work could theorize about work practices beyond knowledge transfer in virtual settings, which might be influenced by virtual competence. As well, empirical studies could be developed to validate our arguments and to further explore the role of virtual competence in knowledge transfers from other perspectives. In addition, individual-level factors are only part of knowledge transfer processes. Other factors, such as organizational factors and cultural factors, play important roles in determining the effectiveness of knowledge transfer in virtual settings as well (Furst et al., 1999; Jackson, 1999). In this paper, we solely focus on virtual competence and do not compare it with other factors that influence knowledge transfer. This focus is a result of the lack of research on individuals themselves in the current literature. However, research on potential interactions between virtual competence and other factors would surely enhance the understanding of knowledge transfer in virtual settings.

When discussing the development of virtual competence, this study only explores the potential influences of experiences with virtual settings and related activities, while detailed mechanisms about how to obtain these experiences are not discussed. Experiences with virtual settings can be obtained through a variety of activities. For example, experiences with virtual settings may be gained through both formal training and informal daily practices. Moreover, informal daily practice could happen in both work settings and individuals’ daily lives outside of work (Igbaria, 1999; Chidambaram & Zigurs, 2001). It is not clear yet what and how effective these mechanisms are. Studies are needed to explore a wide range of the mechanisms that provide the types of experiences that best develop virtual competence. The results of these studies will provide good references for organizations to enhance employees’ learning and development so that individuals are well armed for an increasing proportion of virtual work in their careers.

Furthermore, this study adopts an optimistic view of the impact of experiences with virtual settings and related activities. We acknowledge that there may be negative effects if the experience is unpleasant, unrewarded or underdeveloped. For instance, if an individual has experienced online fraud, it is reasonable to suppose that he/she will not find it easy to trust someone he/she knows only through remote communication. A negative experience may reduce an individual’s virtual self-efficacy, inhibit his/her communication performance and limit his/her ability to transfer knowledge effectively in virtual teams at work. Further exploration in this direction may be productive.

## Managerial implications

Understanding individual competencies and the development of them within virtual organiza tions has important implications for managers. First, the literature suggests that hiring people with more experience in virtual communication could improve the performance of virtual teams (Staples et al., 1999). In the same vein, individuals’ exposure to virtual settings and related activities may be an indicator of their ability to transfer knowledge in such settings and thus forms a potential selection criterion for both hiring and placement on virtual teams. For instance, in interviews, a few questions about previous experiences with online communities, e-commerce and instant messaging might help recruiting managers identify suitable candidates for virtual teams.

Second, because experiencing CMC can help people understand and appreciate this remote interaction mode, organizations may benefit from encouraging (and even technologically supporting) employees to use a broad array of online communication and informationsharing tools in both work and their daily life (e.g. participating in virtual communities associated with personal hobbies) because these activities also build virtual competence. This encouragement can take many forms including incentives, monitoring and evaluation and rewarding effective behaviours to provide positive experiences which will support future, ongoing development of virtual competence (Bandura, 1986).

Third, many studies have argued that socialization is an important part of virtual organizations (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003). Developing social connections is very difficult in work settings where people cannot meet in person (Oshri et al., 2007). One way for firms to address this problem is to develop and encourage team members to participate in relationship-building mechanisms such as online games or online social activities (Chidambaram & Zigurs, 2001). These mechanisms not only help virtual organization members to know each other but also enable them to gain more experience with remote communication and cooperation modes. Similar ideas are gaining currency in the education literature with research on how educating students in virtual environments equips them to work in a virtual society (Jones et al., 1998; Larsen & McInerney, 2002).

In sum, our theorizing identifies the KSAs needed in virtual settings and thus enables managers to consider these important KSAs as selection criteria, as important skills to be trained and finally as important skills and abilities that should be rewarded in evaluations and performance.

We have extended the orientation of recent research by introducing the concept of IVC and by considering the impact of experiences with virtual settings and related activities on knowledge transfer in virtual organizations. A number of fruitful research opportunities are available for researchers in various disciplines. Studies along this line may contribute to our understanding of knowledge transfer and virtual organizations and thus contribute to the whole body o knowledge regarding management practice.
