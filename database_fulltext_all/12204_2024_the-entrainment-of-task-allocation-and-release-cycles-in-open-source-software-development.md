# THE ENTRAINMENT OF TASK ALLOCATION AND RELEASE CYCLES IN OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT<sup>1</sup>

Aron Lindberg Department of Information Systems, School of Business, Stevens Institute of Technology, Hoboken, NJ, U.S.A. {alindber@stevens.edu}

Aaron Schecter Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A. {aschecter@uga.edu}

Nicholas Berente Department of IT, Analytics, and Operations, Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN, U.S.A. {nberente@nd.edu}

Phil Hennel Faculty of Business Studies and Economics, University of Bremen Bremen, Germany {hennel@uni-bremen.de}

Kalle Lyytinen Department of Design & Innovation, Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, OH, U.S.A. {kalle@case.edu}

In this study we identify a process of “entrainment” around open source software (OSS) development release cycles to capture patterns of self-organized task allocation among developers. We conducted an abductive, computationally intensive study of eight OSS projects, using relational event modeling to analyze 1,169,489 actions covering 93 major software releases. The process of entrainment that we identify involves three task allocation mechanisms: (1) developer-issue inertia, (2) developer contribution frequency, and (3) issue popularity. Our analysis demonstrates that these mechanisms and the phases of the release cycle entrain each other. Before a major release, developers engage in a concentrated mobilization phase, whereby they democratize development activity and increasingly allocate community contributions to the set of issues related to the release. After a major release, the extended cleanup phase garners a greater share of development work from recently highly active developers and dilutes the activity of these developers across a wider range of issues. Our theorizing suggests that major releases constitute important events around which OSS communities self-organize and we characterize how this occurs. Our research contributes to theorizing on organizing in OSS communities by explaining how selforganizing task allocation interacts with release cycles through the mechanism of entrainment. We also contribute to the literature on entrainment by showing how it may unfold in the context of online peer production communities such as OSS.

Keywords: Open source software, release cycles, self-organization, entrainment, task allocation

## Introduction

Open source software (OSS) involves distributed volunteerdevelopers performing tasks that aggregate into software artifacts (Raymond, 2001; Lindberg et al., 2016). Research on OSS development emphasizes how independent developers self-allocate their efforts across incremental contributions over time (Howison & Crowston, 2014). Consequently, the received view of OSS development is largely that it is self-organizing and driven by the individual agencies of developers. However, research also suggests that structural macrolevel factors, such as release cycles, impact the work of OSS developers (Düring, 2006; Teixeira, 2017).

Release cycles are milestones through which OSS communities periodically release new versions of the software. Each release embodies a specific temporal pattern of community action (Teixeira, 2017; Fitzgerald, 2006; Düring, 2006; Gary et al., 2009). Releases involve publicly signaled deadlines that punctuate OSS activity. Organizational research shows that people will allocate their contributions to tasks differently immediately before or after such “punctuated transitions”—the major events that break up the flow of activities—compared to the steady state of activities (Gersick, 1988, 1991). Hence, the way that selforganized OSS developers allocate their tasks across time is likely to vary according to different stages of release cycles. Existing research, however, does not provide an understanding of the relationship between release cycles and the task allocation of OSS developers. Hence, we ask the following research question: How do OSS release cycles relate to the self-organized allocation of developer tasks?

Understanding how release cycles relate to the way that developers allocate their contributions to tasks is important to both research and practice. First, identifying such process dynamics in OSS is important because OSS organizing is a novel organizational form and understanding how this form differs from others is relevant to organizational research (von Krogh & Von Hippel, 2006). Research is still uncovering the complex dynamics around OSS organizing and understanding the temporal dynamics of OSS communities is still in its infancy, yet it has been shown to be important for a variety of outcomes including project performance (Chełkowski et al., 2021; Setia et al., 2020). OSS activity is a relatively novel form of work, and understanding how this work interacts with release cycles can enable organizations involved with OSS projects to enhance this work (Germonprez et al., 2019). For example, some practitioners seek to measure the speed of innovation in OSS activities (CHAOSS, 2022), a metric related to release cycles. Further, key events, such as major releases, can have dramatic impacts on the health of OSS communities (Goggins et al., 2021). OSS governance consortia and corporations interested in influencing OSS projects would do well to understand how release cycles might interact with other project dynamics. For example, efforts to mitigate risks in OSS projects (Germonprez et al., 2021) need to be conscious of the different sorts of risks that are more likely in different phases of projects. In short, understanding the interactions that produce varying patterns of task allocation can help to shape the ongoing work of OSS communities.

The interaction of rhythmic behaviors such as release cycles and task allocation patterns can be understood using the concept of “entrainment” (Ancona & Chong, 1996, 1992)— the temporal synchronization of behavioral patterns with phenomena other than the behavioral patterns themselves. Existing research shows that self-organizing groups entrain themselves into patterns around punctuated transitions (Gersick, 1988, 1991). This maps well to OSS development, which is punctuated by regular releases, and suggests that through entrainment, the patterning of task allocation may be configured differently across a release cycle.

To understand the entrainment of release cycles and task allocation mechanisms, we conducted a data-driven, computationally intensive study (Berente et al., 2019; Lindberg, 2020; Miranda et al., 2022) using relational event modeling (REM, see Butts, 2008) and qualitative analysis. We found that patterns of task allocation vary in different phases of the release cycle, indicating support for entrainment as a mechanism connecting self-organizing task allocation and release cycles. The three phases we identified are: (1) pre-release, (2) post-release, and (3) steady-state. Compared to the steady-state phase, during the pre-release phase, task allocation tends to be more widely distributed across a broader range of developers and issues while being focused on issues that developers have already worked on in the past. During the post-release phase, task allocation is increasingly driven by developers who have contributed more frequently in the recent past, who now contribute to a variety of issues brought on by the recent release, as compared to the steady-state phase. Our research offers three main contributions to theory: (1) extending the prevailing view that emphasizes individual-level task allocation mechanisms to show how structural factors (i.e., release cycles) can interact with these mechanisms, (2) identifying entrainment as a mechanism through which the association between release cycles and task allocation is mediated, and (3) explaining how entrainment theory can be updated to account for the distributed nature of online communities.

## Conceptual Development

To make sense of how self-organized collaborative activity relates to temporal punctuation, organizational research has drawn upon the theoretical lens of entrainment (Bluedorn and Denhardt, 1988). Generally, entrainment involves the synchronization, or alignment, of an agent’s behavioral patterns with some observed activity patterns in the agent’s environment (Ancona & Chong, 1996, 1992). The concept originates from physics and was discovered in the 17th century by Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008). Subsequently, entrainment has been used in biology (e.g., Aschoff, 1979) to explain how behavioral patterns of organisms can be entrained to biological sources, such as the entrainment of the circadian rhythm to light in the external environment (Wright et al., 2013).

Entrainment has been shown to occur in a variety of organizational contexts (McGrath et al., 1984; Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008; Feldman, 2003). Self-organizing groups adapt to the pace set by external deadlines in a way that is fairly predictable over time (Gersick, 1988; Gersick & Hackman, 1990). Self-organizing group activities are markedly different when approaching a deadline, compared to after the deadline has passed. Gersick (1988) found that the patterning of teamwork shifts dramatically at the midpoint of a project, i.e., the “deadline effect” (Gersick, 1991: Roth et al., 1988; Zhu et al., 2019), suggesting that teams start to change their behavior as a deadline approaches. Entrainment in social contexts can move in both directions because temporal punctuations such as deadlines are not always strictly exogenous—a phenomenon described as “mutual entrainment” (McGarva & Warner, 2003). For example, a deadline may spur work, but work may also delay the deadline if it turns out that specific tasks will not be completed in time.

In OSS communities, one can expect the task allocation of developers to become entrained with the cyclical patterns of project release cycles. In traditional systems development, cyclical and episodic patterning of work is captured by the idea of phases of activities (Davis & Vick, 1977; Thummadi & Lyytinen, 2020). Cycles (i.e., sprints, phases, releases) involve “punctuated transitions” that structure and pattern work for software development teams. Developer contributions tend to vary in periods before and after such punctuated transitions (Düring, 2006; Teixeira, 2017). In OSS communities such structures typically crystallize over time as communities work through consecutive versions of software that grow in complexity (Mockus et al., 2002;

Winter et al., 2014). To effectively manage the evolution of the software and implement associated changes in a planned manner, OSS projects typically structure their code releases into chunks of varying sizes—major and minor releases (Michlmayr et al., 2007; Wright & Perry, 2009). A major release includes significantly novel features and may involve substantial changes to the organization, architecture, and scope of the overall codebase of the project. Major releases may involve more challenging and novel problems that demand deeper technical expertise and more extensive coordination among developers and other stakeholders. Such releases constitute punctuated transitions (Gersick, 1988) in OSS development which are likely to entrain developer activities in systematic ways. We distinguish between three phases of an OSS release cycle: pre-release, post-release, and steady-state (see Table 1).

Pre-release phase: Before an OSS community makes a major release, it needs to decide what new features are to be included and determine how to allocate the development work across a pool of tasks to complete the release. The period prior to a major new release (Michlmayr et al., 2007) therefore involves intense coordination and negotiation around what features should be included (Wright & Perry, 2009). Further, the period will involve cooperation to solve problems and debate feasible approaches to how the features need to be architected, designed, and implemented (Majchrzak et al., 2013; Tuertscher et al., 2014).

Post-release phase: After a major release the new features will be utilized by a pool of users in different settings or tried out as separate functions that can support the operations of external software, such as plugins. This exposes new residual interdependencies in the software (Lindberg et al., 2016) manifested in reported bugs (Glance, 2004; Rossi et al., 2009). As new bugs surface (Raymond, 2001) developers must work to change the code in their efforts to remove identified bugs (Crowston & Scozzi, 2004).

Steady-state phase: When a major release is not imminent, OSS development activity continues in a “business as usual” state where development activities unfold in a fluid manner (Faraj et al., 2011). During the steady-state phase, developers operate relatively independently (Raymond, 1999, 2001) and the results of their activities take the form of minor releases. Such work, for example, includes fixing still lingering bugs, as well as adding code to incrementally superposition additional (i.e., “nice to have”) features or to improve the efficiency or quality of current features (Howison & Crowston, 2014).

## Task Allocation in OSS Communities

In OSS task allocation is typically viewed as a selforganizing process (Lindberg et al., 2016). Developers autonomously identify which issues they select to work on based on their needs and priorities—allocation depends on the “itches” that they wish to scratch (Raymond, 1999). They engage in “open superpositioning” (Howison & Crowston, 2014) by developing functionally additive layers of code on top of each other to address salient issues. Howison and Crowston (2014) define a task as “a series of actions undertaken by participants” (p. 35) to produce a specific change in the software (or related documentation, etc.). As such, task allocation is concerned with both who will take action (developers) as well as what issues developers contribute to.

Therefore, task allocation connects developers and issues temporally into an episodic system that can be described as a bimodal network between developers and issues (Quintane et al., 2014). Such a bimodal network is a fundamental representation used in social network analysis (Borgatti & Everett, 1997) and has been used to capture how human actors and IS artifacts interact with each other (Contractor et al., 2011; Kane & Alavi, 2008).

Building on this conceptualization, we posit that task allocation may arise from the three components of the bimodal network: developers (who), issues (what), and actions developers contribute towards an issue (relation between developers and issues). First, there is a task allocation effect stemming from who has made contributions to an issue in the past. Prior contribution frequencies of developers may influence who will make contributions in the future—we refer to this as “developer contribution frequency.” Further, there is a task allocation effect stemming from what issue has been the focus of past contributions. This effect involves “issue popularity,” through which issues that already have received a lot of attention will tend to receive more future attention. Finally, developers tend to contribute to issues that interest them and inform their idiosyncratic needs, and thus there is a relation between developers and issues. These issues tend to be the same sorts of issues that developers have worked on in the past—what we refer to as “developer-issue inertia.” We describe each below (summarized in Table 2).

Developer contribution frequency: OSS communities consist of a range of developers—some of which are highly active and others who are only active sporadically. More active developers often drive feature development and attend to challenging development issues, while less active developers may report and fix minor bugs (Aberdour, 2007; Crowston & Howison, 2006; Dahlander & Frederiksen, 2011; Fitzgerald, 2006; Germonprez et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2019). Developers who contribute frequently are likely to tackle important issues and ratchet up their contributions toward difficult issues or engage in setting up and negotiating future directions of the software (Xiao et al., 2018). Indeed, past successful activities, such as submitting significant patches that were accepted, lead to a stronger future willingness and commitment to participate in the development process (Ho & Rai, 2017). Highly active developers are also experienced and know how to contribute (Maruping et al., 2019); they act as stewards of the community (Sijbrandij, 2016). Thus, previously frequently contributing developers are likely to also contribute to a larger number of issues in the future. Existing research suggests that, in aggregate terms, development activity becomes concentrated in a smaller number of high-activity developers (Winter et al., 2014). Hence, the distribution of task allocation associated with prior contribution frequency exhibits an increased concentration of activities to already highly active developers. Such increased concentration can have positive effects; for instance, concentration has been found to increase the health of OSS projects (Amrit & Van Hillegersberg, 2010).

Issue popularity: When a development issue receives a lot of attention, it is likely to continue to attract further attention in the near future (Barabási & Reka, 1999; Johnson et al., 2014). An issue may become popular because it represents a particularly severe bug or an attractive feature or because of strong interdependencies with them (Lindberg et al., 2016). Popularity may further strengthen because developers, like most humans, are social creatures and exhibit herding behaviors (Oh & Jeon, 2007). Agents tend to mimic the behaviors of their neighbors, thereby explaining why an already popular issue (tackled by other developers) will receive even further attention from the rest of the crowd. Through herding, developers can feel validated by the actions of others (Bateman et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2013) and become more interested in contributing to issues that have already received significant developer attention. Popularity effects lead to a skewed allocation of work such that development activity concentrates on a few popular issues (Madey et al., 2002). For example, some issues may emerge as highly contentious and attract extensive debate that serves as a magnet for developer contributions (Butler & Wang, 2012; Kim et al., 2018). When developers concentrate their work on these few popular issues, it is likely to encourage internal bonding among involved developers and thereby “improves the motivation to share knowledge to assist others” (Kim et al., 2018, p. 265).

Developer-issue inertia: Generally, OSS developers work independently and focus on their own topics of interest, reflecting their needs (Howison & Crowston, 2014). When left to their own devices, developers continue to pay attention to the issues that have captured their attention in the past (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2006). This may occur when developers have deep expertise in particular technical areas—for example, security issues (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016)—that only a few select developers have the competencies or communitysanctioned rights to contribute to. Such repeated actions contributed toward a particular issue can lead to inertia (Schecter et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2020), characterized by a developer concentrating their efforts on specific issues. When a developer contributes frequently to a particular issue, this is likely to be associated with high levels of focus and engagement that create momentum, or flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 2009), factors that may encourage further contributions to the same issue (Goh and Pentland, 2019, p. 1914). Such dynamics may be associated with the increasing compartmentalization of work within a community, sometimes referred to as “digital folds” (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016), within which specialized work may occur.

To summarize, we suggest that task allocation in OSS development is associated with three mechanisms reflecting a bimodal network of developers and issues (developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, developer-issue inertia) that are entrained with the timing of major releases throughout a release cycle.

## Method

To investigate how release cycles entrain with developer task allocation, we adopted a data-driven computationalintensive approach to theory development (Berente et al., 2019; Miranda et al., 2022). Figure 1 describes the process. We followed a multi-case sampling strategy (Miranda et al., 2022) and sampled eight typical, successful midsized OSS projects focused on web development tools and frameworks hosted on GitHub<sup>2</sup> (Angular, Bootstrap, Django, ember.js, Material UI, NodeJS, Rails, and Symfony). We downloaded publicly available data and bracketed the activity into specific periods around major and minor releases. We then identified and operationalized constructs related to task allocation and release phases. To detect and analyze activity patterns—reflected by the operationalized constructs—and their change over time in the sampled projects, we applied relational event modeling (REM) (Butts, 2008). We used REM to estimate whether individual-level allocation mechanisms vary in the strength and direction of their effects before, after, and between major OSS releases. We conducted qualitative analysis using interviews and archival data from a single exemplar project in the sample (Rails) to interpret and illustrate the REM models. We then abductively (Lindberg, 2020) iterated between statistical models, the literature on task allocation and entrainment, and qualitative analysis to theorize the model coefficients and the significant interactions identified in the REM models. Lastly, we conducted a series of robustness checks.

## Data Collection

We sampled the eight OSS projects based on similarity (Miranda et al., 2022)—they were similar in terms of size, goals, use of releases, platform, etc. While all eight projects were medium-sized web development frameworks, they were nevertheless among the larger projects housed on the

GitHub platform. All sampled projects enjoyed some level of corporate sponsorship, as is common among projects of this size. The projects also varied in terms of several dimensions commonly included in OSS analyses (such as the size of the code base), affording some variation in the sample for more robust results (see Table 3). To further increase heterogeneity in the sample we collected data both from “native” GitHub projects (i.e., those that always have existed on GitHub) and “non-native” projects (i.e., those that have also resided on a code repository other than GitHub). All projects except for Django and Rails were native. Finally, we sampled projects following a mix of alternative release strategies: whether different projects used a feature-based or time-based release strategy (Michlmayr et al., 2015; Michlmayr & Fitzgerald, 2012; Teixeira, 2017; Wright & Perry, 2009). This variety in our sampling, coupled with robustness checks, allowed us to generalize findings across midsized OSS projects on GitHub, even though they differ in several respects. During the analysis, we confirmed that none of the sampled cases followed a publicly listed prioritization or task list. To test whether the threat of such distortion was present, we checked the list of issues and pull requests for any “pinned” items (i.e., visually and distinctively placed at the top of the list) and checked GitHub homepages, ReadMe files, the project’s webpage, forums, or mailing lists for any reference to a prioritization approach, list, or other prioritization or process definitions for each project to ensure that no such threat was present.

We collected project data over a decade (2009 to early 2019). The data consisted of the creation and closing times (i.e., marked as resolved) of issues, as well as their authors. On GitHub, issues can be either bug reports without code or pull requests containing code. Contributions consist of adding either code or comments on these issues. For comments, we collected the creation time and the author. Pull requests are handled by the GitHub API as issues and are included in the data set. To tie actions to specific users we collected all usernames for each action (i.e., comments or commits). Finally, we collected the creation time and the author of commits to the source code repository as well as the number of lines of code attached to each commit. To identify the releases, we collected the tags of each tagged commit and, when applicable for the individual project, the releases via a designated API call. Lastly, we collected qualitative data from each project website covering their release management policies. To aid our interpretation of the REM analyses we conducted 13 in-depth interviews with contributors to a single exemplar project (Rails). Based on the interviews, we collected 164 archival data items such as newsletters, forum posts, conference presentations, etc.

## Relational Event Modeling

To capture patterns in the relations between developers and issues over time, we used relational event modeling (Butts, 2008). REM is a network analog to survival models (Cox, 1972) or duration models (see Wooldridge, 2010) in that they are used to determine how one or more covariates impact the time until an event occurs. In our study context, a relational event is operationalized as a timestamped link between a developer and an issue produced when a comment or commit is contributed to the issue. Because developer-issue events repeat themselves, REM is a type of recurrent survival model (Lin et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2001), which has a similar interpretation to non-recurrent survival models but incorporates information from the prior sequence. Because of this, REM accounts for the path-dependent nature of event data, while accounting for the non-independence of network ties. We analyzed sequences directly rather than collapsing the event data into cross-sectional measures of networks, because doing so would have led to significant validity threats (Howison et al., 2011; Quintane et al., 2014).

Like other statistical models of networks, REM emphasizes the influence of independent variables (influential patterns) over the dependent variable (event, probability of a link). REM is not meant to be predictive in the traditional sense. Rather, it assesses the “relative strengths of potential mechanisms” (Butts, 2008, p. 156) for explaining the event, which aligns with multitheoretical, multi-level views of social networks (Contractor et al., 2006). We applied REM to identify significant patterns of task allocation among developers. Specifically, we used REM to test the influence of each mechanism—such as developerissue inertia—on who (which developer) does what (interacts with an issue), and when (Leenders et al., 2016). We identified how the strength of such mechanisms fluctuated significantly across release cycle phases.

The key component of our REM analysis is the event rate. The event rate measures how frequently one would expect an event to occur. A larger rate corresponds to a more common event, and vice versa. Every possible event (each possible developer-issue pairing) has a rate, and the rates can be used to discriminate between what is likely to occur and what is not (Butts, 2008). The likelihood of an event occurring is modeled as proportional to the event’s rate. ## Dependent Variable

The dependent variable in our study is an action taken by a developer directed towards a particular issue; this is the focal relational event. Essentially, every observation in our dataset is an instance of a link between a developer and an issue. Collectively, links form a temporal sequence of relational events. We notate the dependent variable as $y _ { i j t }$ which takes a value of 1 if link $( i , j )$ occurs at time ?? and a value of 0 for all other links $( u , v )$ at time ??. This dependent variable can be linked to the previous regression model such that: $P r \left( y _ { i j k t } = \right.$ $\begin{array} { r } { 1 ) = \frac { \lambda _ { i j k } ( t ) } { \sum _ { u , v } \lambda _ { u v k } ( t ) } . } \end{array}$ . The REM procedure then estimates the rate values to maximize the observed likelihood.

## Independent Variables

We identify three variables that we expect will relate to the likelihood that events occur, given the prior sequence of events: developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, and developer-issue inertia. To ensure that these measures reflect the state of the project at the time of each observation, we used the recent event history for calculating statistics. Using the entire history would cause us to over or underrepresent the relative developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, and developer-issue inertia of certain developers and issues. Further, taking a cumulative approach would give equal weight to events generated by individuals or projects that are no longer active.<sup>4</sup> In other words, when aggregating data over long periods of time, we cannot distinguish between nodes that are active and those that are not. We set the time window for recent events to thirty days and calculate statistics using events from this window.

The first independent variable is developer contribution frequency. As noted, this is a measure related to the developer of an event (Figure 2). We calculate the contribution frequency of developer ?? by counting the number of events that originate from ?? within 30 days prior to the current time ??. The more times ?? has taken an action in the past, the greater their activity will be. A positive effect of developer contribution frequency in a REM model indicates that an event is more likely to originate from a developer who has contributed more frequently in the recent past.

The second independent variable, issue popularity, is a measure related to the issue of an event (Figure 3). We calculate the popularity of issue ?? by counting the number of events directed at issue ?? in the 30 days prior to the current time ??. The more attention issue ?? attracts, the greater its popularity. A positive effect of popularity in an REM model indicates that an event is more likely to be directed at an issue if it has been a frequent target in the past.

Our third independent variable, developer-issue inertia, is a measure related to the link between a developer and an issue (Figure 4). This measure captures the tendency for events to repeat themselves over time. We measure the inertia between developer ?? and issue ?? as the number of times ?? has directed an action at ?? within 30 days prior to the current time ??. A positive effect of developer-issue inertia in a REM model suggests that the next event to occur is more likely to be one that has happened frequently in the recent past. ## Control Variables

Control variables include the following. The tenure of the developer in the project was measured as the difference in time between the developer’s first observed activity and the present time ??. Whether the developer is a core contributor was measured by a binary dummy variable, based on the developer’s access privileges<sup>5</sup>. The age of an issue is defined as the difference between the time of the first observed contribution and the present time. Whether an issue is a pull request was measured by a binary dummy. Comments and commits were distinguished by including an event-type dummy. Comments are communications among developers, whereas commits are proposed changes to the codebase. We controlled for different project release strategies by including a dummy. These strategies may influence how developers act before and after a major release and they include feature-based strategies (i.e., a release occurs when a selected pool of new functions is ready), time-based strategies (i.e., releases are scheduled), or a mixture. Finally, we accounted for the tendencies of developers to work with those who are similar to them by calculating a homophily measure (Aral et al., 2009), defined as the average similarity between the developer and other developers connected to a particular issue. Two developers are considered similar if their patterns of editing and commenting over time are highly comparable.

Because our relational event statistics operate at a microlevel (developer-issue pairs), we needed to demonstrate that these microbehaviors coalesce into specific macrolevel properties, as suggested in column three of Table 1. By connecting our proposed variables to established macrolevel structures, we helped establish the nomological validity of the selected constructs. ## Delineating Phases

We earlier identified three phases in OSS development for each release cycle: pre-release, post-release, and steadystate. Because semantic versioning (e.g., https://semver.org) practices differ somewhat between projects, we relied upon the magnitude of change for each release to identify major releases. The degree of change was measured as the relative number of commits included in a release. The ratio of commits was calculated by counting the number of commits since the last release and dividing the number by the total number of commits for the project. We then classified major releases as any labeled release in which the magnitude of change was in the top 10% of all releases. This cutoff was varied during the robustness checks. For example, the project Symfony had 375 total releases; of these releases, we labeled 10 as major using the top 10% threshold. The number of major releases during the study period ranged between 7 and 15, the average number of major releases within the sample was 10.8, and the average release cycle was 11.1 months.

Once we identified the major releases for each project, we set the post-release phase as the 30 days following the release, and the pre-release phase as the 30 days leading up to the next release. If periods overlapped (i.e., less than 60 days between releases), we delineated the phases at the halfway point. All other periods are parts of the steady-state phase. In our analysis, we included dummies to represent each release cycle phase. For instance, the post-release dummy variable has a value of 1 if the observation occurred during the phase immediately following a release, and 0 otherwise. We also calculated the interactions of the dummies and each of the three task allocation mechanisms. The interactions represent the differences in the effects of the task allocation mechanisms across the three phases.

## Qualitative Data Analysis and Interpretation of Models

To aid in illustrating our quantitative findings and interpreting the coefficients of the REM analyses, we analyzed the interviews and archival items. During this process, we coded all of the data, generating 416 codes in the process. These codes were then streamlined and synthesized into a set of codes pertaining specifically to release cycles and task allocation. Simultaneously, we also iterated in an abductive fashion between data analysis and the literature on release cycles in OSS (Lindberg, 2020). Out of this process, major themes emerged, which captured the characteristics of how the pre- and post-release phases deviate from the steady-state phase. This helped to illustrate and interpret the coefficients from the REM analyses theoretically.

## Findings

We fit the REM model in several steps—beginning with control variables only, each task allocation mechanism one by one, and then models with interactions. All models consider the full set of 1,169,489 events across the eight projects. The standard errors are clustered on the project level to account for heterogeneity across projects. Table 6 provides the full results for all models.

Model 1 includes controls for developer tenure, issue age, whether a developer is core, average similarity, dummies for event type and parent type, and dummy variables for the phases (pre-release and post-release). Model 1 indicates that developers tend to contribute less frequently as they are active for longer and contribute more frequently to older projects. We also found that core members contribute more frequently and that developers exhibit homophily. Finally, we note that events occurred more frequently in the pre- and post-release phases of the projects (0.071, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ and 0.075, $p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1$ , respectively). To put these values in context, events occurred 7.36% faster<sup>8</sup> during the pre-release phase, compared to the steady-state phase.

Model 2 includes developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, and developer-issue inertia. In Models 3-5, we include them independently. We found that each had a positive and significant impact on the rate of events (0.339, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; 0 . 0 8 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ; 0 . 1 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , respectively). The result implies that if a developer’s recent contribution frequency is higher, they are more likely to initiate a relational event. Specifically, for a one-standard-deviation increase in frequency, there is a corresponding 40.35% increase in the likelihood that a developer will act next. Further, if an issue’s recent popularity is higher, it is more likely to attract relational events in the future. Precisely, a one-standard-deviation increase in issue popularity corresponds to a 9.20% increase in the likelihood of receiving an event. Finally, the more a developer has directed events towards a particular issue in the recent past, the more likely it is that they continue doing so; a one standard deviation difference in prior contribution to an issue is associated with an 11.96% increase in the likelihood of a repeated relational event. These results are consistent with Model 2 (including all three). In Models 6 and 7, we introduce the interactions between pre- and post-release and the three independent variables separately to ensure consistency with our main model, which we describe next.

In the full main model, Model 8, we include all variables for each of the three task allocation mechanisms, as well as their interactions with the pre-/post-release dummy variables. The model tests whether the influence of the task allocation mechanisms is significantly different in the pre- or post-release phases, relative to the steady-state phase. We find that developer contribution frequency has a significantly stronger effect post-release (0.058, p < 0.001) and a weaker effect prerelease (-0.051, p < 0.001), compared to the steady-state phase.

## Robustness Checks

First, because we sampled eight OSS projects in our analysis, we sought to determine whether one single project significantly influenced our findings. We reran the full REM models, but excluded each of the projects one by one. In each case, we either confirmed our findings or found neutral support (at most two interaction terms were nonsignificant). Similarly, we fit a model to each project data separately. We still found consistent results: for all six of the relevant interaction terms, the sign and significance were the same for the majority of projects in our dataset. Further, no project differed from the main result in more than two terms, which is a similar outcome to leaving out one project. Essentially, we found that while there is indeed some heterogeneity across projects, there are no systematic deviations from the overall results.

Second, we varied the duration of the pre-release and postrelease phases. We considered phase lengths of 14 and 60 days and reran the REM models using these phase lengths. In the new models, we only found one discrepancy in the postrelease phase inertia with a 14-day window. For the 60-day window, the model is consistent. This suggests that the patterns we found are robust over longer periods, though they may vary slightly in shorter time windows.

Third, we sought to determine whether our determination of a major release impacted our results. We tested two alternative thresholds, the 95th percentile and the 75th percentile. When we raised the threshold to the 95th percentile, we observed that the pre-release effect of frequency became positive. We attribute this to the fact that these are the largest releases in the entire project (there are generally only about three of these major releases) and, as such, there is a stronger effect of a dominant few working at a higher rate. For the lower threshold of the 75th percentile, we found that the post-release effects became highly like the pre-release effects. We conclude that by lowering the threshold, we greatly increased the number of major releases and that the pre- and post-release phases thus started to overlap. As such, we conclude that our chosen threshold of the 90th percentile is reasonable for examining the effects of releases on entrainment.

Fourth, we tested whether the task allocation mechanisms we measured varied based on link type (comment vs. commit). We fit a model using only comment actions; this reduced our dataset to 168,667 events. We found only one difference in the results; post-release popularity was positive and significant for comments but nonsignificant in the full model. We attribute this effect to the social nature of comments and the tendency for developers to join a conversation rather than resolving issues directly through code (Lindberg et al., 2016).

Fifth, we determined whether the patterns we observed are driven by core developers who work persistently on the project or whether peripheral developers exhibit similar patterns. We fit two models, one with only core members and one that excluded core members. These two models both included roughly 50% of the data. The model with core members only is fully consistent with the main results presented in the paper. For the model with peripheral members only, there are two discrepancies. In the post-release phase, non-core developers tend to exhibit a stronger popularity effect compared to the steady-state phase and a stronger inertia effect compared to the steady-state phase. Essentially, after a major release, non-core members tend to focus their efforts on issues that have previously received a lot of attention, whereas core members distribute their attention more evenly. The finding makes intuitive sense since peripheral members may be more interested in resolving problems for the small number of issues they are invested in, rather than exhibiting the general pattern of stewardship demonstrated by core developers.

Sixth, we considered the possibility that developers working on the project may be prioritizing select issues because of explicit assignment. We leveraged the assignment feature in GitHub to determine (1) whether an individual is assigned to a particular issue, (2) how many times an individual has been assigned any issue at all, and (3) how many assignments involve a specific issue. We calculated three variables: a binary variable indicating if developer ?? is assigned to work on issue ?? at time ??, a count of assignments to developer ?? up to time ??, and a count of assignments regarding issue ?? up to time ??. When we added these variables to the full model, we found consistent results with the original model. Alternatively, we fit REM models to two subsets of data: issues only, with and without assignments. Again, we found consistent results, with slightly stronger activity and inertia effects in the subset with assigned issues.

Finally, we tested the proportional hazards assumption that is implicit in any survival model. We applied the standard approach of finding the correlation between the scaled Schoenfeld residuals and time. We used the omnibus approach of rejecting the proportional hazards assumption if the correlation is significant for any predictor. We did indeed find that the assumption was violated. However, in practice, such violations are common, particularly with large datasets. To determine whether the violation invalidated our results, we fit two alternative models. One is a conditional logit model, which uses only order information. The second is a model with a time trend, where we allow the covariates to be functions of time. In both cases, the models are fully consistent with our main results, indicating that our findings are robust despite the violation of the proportional hazards assumption.

## Theory Development

Our findings highlight how self-organized OSS task allocation mechanisms (i.e., developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, and developer-issue inertia) shape development activities differently across the three phases of a release cycle (Table 8). While the mechanisms are emergent in that they are not directed by some higher authority, they interact with the release cycle phases and thus become partially entrained with them. Our theorizing is presented in the following manner: for each of the two phases that we compare to the steady-state phase, i.e., the pre-release and post-release phases, we offer explanations rooted in our statistical modeling, corroborated by qualitative evidence and triangulated with insights from the literature to describe the sort of work that is done during each phase. We provide an explanation for the patterns we identify. That is, we construct an interpretation of the estimated interaction coefficients (Miranda et al., 2022). To align with our statistical modeling, propositions are written in relation to the steady-state phase.

Our findings suggest that in the steady-state phase, each of the three self-organizing task allocation mechanisms is positively associated with subsequent task allocation. This suggests that each of these effects is evident throughout an OSS project. Hence, the ongoing process of open superpositioning (Howison & Crowston, 2014) is at least partially driven by developer contribution frequency, issue popularity, and developer-issue inertia. Steady-state activity is therefore largely self-organizing, based on developers’ past work on issues. Our analysis offers evidence for the open superpositioning process and elaborates on specific mechanisms through which it takes place.

## Pre-Release

Our results suggest that the pre-release phase is characterized by an increased pace of activities relative to the steady-state phase (0.073, p < 0.001). In particular, the upcoming major release is associated with increased developer-issue inertia (0.023, p < 0.001). That is, developers will work on select features repeatedly, possibly to finish them in time for the upcoming release, or to achieve consensus with others who also have shown an interest in the same issue (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016). A stronger developerissue inertia effect also suggests that developers show a decreased tendency to engage with new issues, instead focusing on a familiar subset of features—essentially doubling down on the issues that they are focusing on to see them to completion. In contrast, during this phase, the other two task allocation mechanisms, developer contribution frequency and issue popularity, became weaker (-0.051, p < 0.001; -0.017, p < 0.001), indicating a distribution of work that is less centralized—essentially, more democratized.

This suggests that the upcoming release, often through a beta version or a release candidate (Wright & Perry, 2009), correlates with widespread engagement with the codebase and centers attention on release-related issues rather than on specific issues that are generally popular within the community. Many issues important to the release may not be the traditionally popular issues, and developers may therefore not allocate their attention to these popular issues as much as they usually do (i.e., issue popularity decreases in strength). Similarly, pending major releases also seem to be associated with broader participation across the community (i.e., developer contribution frequency decreases in strength).

Before a major release, negotiations among developers regarding what features should be included in the upcoming release intensify (Rails developer Rollo: “I want this to go in. Somebody else doesn’t want it to go in.”). As a major release commonly contains novel features, there are lots of conflicts, debates, and discussions about which features should be included and how they should be implemented (Rails core developer Xantur: “Some people are very inclined to make strong points about one position or another position”). Often such discussions center on whether a certain use case should be supported or not. As Rails developer Rollo noted:

You kind of do have to fight for your features, but at the end of the day, somebody has to make a call in like a feature is in or out, a pull request is either in or out … I’ve had features that have just been merged in with no problem at all just from one of the core members who’s just like “Oh I love this,” and then like I’ve had features that have been merged in and later somebody’s like “Whoa, whoa, whoa, wait a second,” and then I’ve had to go back and make changes, and I’ve had features that have been I guess turned down initially and you have to come back with supporting evidence and really make it in and make a fight.

It is common to use a beta version or a release candidate to let people test out the new release. A release candidate refers to a version intended for developers to test. Other developers are invited to give the release candidate a try (Rails core developer Aragorn: “If you have an app that you are planning on upgrading to Rails 4, go test it with the release candidate.”). Release candidates are a way of signaling to the community that a new release is imminent, as indicated by Rails core developer Aragorn:

[regarding release candidates] I think the main factor is just advertisement—really. Like getting the word out—like tweets, mailing list, all that—getting the word out really has an impact on testing I think.

Concurrently with such signaling, a wider range of developers become involved, not with what is popular but with issues that strike them as important in relation to their own interests and needs (Raymond, 1999). Consequently, they are prone to work hard to get such contributions included in the impending release. This suggests that during the pre-release phase, contributions focus on the upcoming release and resolve known roadblocks. Usually, the core team provides a release candidate before a major release (Scacchi, 2007) so that active community participants can see what new features and bug fixes the new release contains and how this interacts with their various projects that use the software (in this case the Rails framework). This thus serves to communicate the urgency of the upcoming release to the broader community.

This observation may be due to the increased salience of unforeseen interdependencies (Lindberg et al., 2016) that are discovered as releases approach (Michlmayr et al., 2007). In such cases, work is less dominated by a few active developers and is less focused on a few highly popular issues, yet the active developers are intensely engaged with their chosen issues (Xiao et al., 2018). This pattern of activity describes a sort of self-organized collective action focused on the issues related to the release that invokes the metaphor of a “barn raising,”<sup>9</sup> where distributed developers focus more on the issues relevant to the release and less on the typically popular issues.<sup>10</sup> Essentially, release-related issues garner attention and crowd out some of the issues typically allocated to popular issues not directly related to the release (i.e., “crowding out”).<sup>11</sup> Those who typically work on these release-related issues allocate more effort to them (“doubling down”), and those who do not typically address these issues now allocate some attention (“democratizing”).<sup>12</sup>

In sum, our findings suggest that in self-organized communities such as OSS, during the pre-release phase, contributors exhibit a concentrated mobilization pattern of collective action in their task allocation. The associations between task allocation and past activity levels, or what others have focused on in the past, are weakened relative to the steady-state phase. Hence, we state the following proposition:

Proposition 1: Before a major release, developer task allocation is entrained to produce a concentrated mobilization pattern of collective action. This mobilization pattern involves three components:

a. Democratizing: More developers are involved compared to the steady-state phase.

b. Doubling-down: Developers who are focused on release-relevant issues during the steady-state phase will allocate more attention to these issues.

c. Crowding out: Developers reduce task allocation to typically popular issues not important to the release compared to the steady-state phase

This proposition highlights how self-organized OSS communities mobilize action when approaching a release. This, to an extent, reflects the deadline effect of increased activity leading up to transitions (Gersick, 1988; Larson et al., 2020). But this is notably different from traditional characterizations of the deadline effect in important ways. Traditional research deals with entrainment in teams that are relatively small and fixed in their membership (Gersick, 1988; Gersick & Hackman, 1990). Therefore, the mobilization of effort in terms of democratizing the process is less apparent. Certainly, in small teams, one might expect doubling-down and crowding-out effects, particularly due to limited team membership. But this is not necessarily the case across large communities where developers have a choice and are not required to work together to make the release successful. Indeed, although many sophisticated OSS communities implement release management practices, where release managers assign tasks to community members, the perennial challenge for these release managers is to coordinate volunteers when they have little to no power over those volunteers (Poo-Caamano et al., 2016). However, we provide evidence that issues relating to releases do indeed draw contributions from across the community. We point out how developers mobilize effort in the community and channel community attention. Our qualitative data show how certain developers are passionate about elements of the release and they are motivated to encourage others to contribute and to address reservations and objections that other developers may have. This concentrated mobilization implies that the frequency and timing of releases may be associated with the variety of issues that developers attend to and thus the potential innovativeness of development communities overall. It is likely the case that active community members such as release managers can indeed influence task allocation and direct contributions to support an upcoming release.

## Post-Release

In the post-release phase, much like the pre-release phase, the overall pace of activity quickens relative to the steadystate phase (0.034, p < 0.001). This may occur because bugs and problems identified from the release are only discovered as the major release goes live, and this creates a sense of urgency for fixing the problems. While the pre-release phase is associated with widespread participation by developers, as indicated by the weak developer contribution frequency effect, the post-release phase suggests the inverse of this (0.058, p < 0.001)—contributions are more centralized with the most active developers (Crowston & Howison, 2006; Xiao et al., 2018). Further, during the post-release phase, the issue popularity effect is insignificantly different from the steady-state phase (-0.003, ns). As such, developers neither pay more or less attention to popular issues during the postrelease phase, compared to the steady-state phase. Last, the effect of developer-issue inertia is weakened (-0.006, p < 0.001) compared to the steady-state phase. This suggests that developers do not focus on a few familiar issues, but rather spread their efforts across a broad range of issues. Such behavior is perhaps due to a major release generating a significant number of new bugs (Glance, 2004; Rossi et al., 2009), rather than due to the trends of the community overall. This necessitates “busywork” that is neither guided by issue popularity (Crowston & Scozzi, 2004) nor by the past contributions of a particular developer to a particular issue (developer-issue inertia). The demands of cleaning up bugs after a major release may decouple developers from prior, fixed patterns of behavior.

After a major release developers find that the software applications that they have developed using their chosen software framework have problems. Often, they discover that existing software functionality is not compatible with the new version of the software (Rails core developer Shagrat: “This is outrageous! Rails broke everything!”). Essentially, they uncover residual interdependencies (Lindberg et al., 2016) not addressed before the release. Further, as the new release becomes widely used within the community, additional bugs are uncovered and difficult edge cases arise in relation to using new features (Rails core developer Shagrat: “Ruby is so dynamic and applications do so many things that are kind of ridiculous, but you can’t guess every possibility of people would want to do and some things slip through.”). The difficulty of addressing some of these bugs means that already active developers increase their activity further to meet the technical demands. Additionally, these problems necessitate work on a broad range of issues brought up by the recent major release. Major releases often introduce major changes that require the attention of experienced developers. As Rails core developer Yavien put it:

Changes made in minor versions are small and should not require large-scale application changes, unless required for security fixes. Changes made in major releases often do require large-scale application changes.

Highly active developers also pointed to their role as a sort of “bug fixer of last resort” describing how they will have to eventually address the issues that others do not, as illustrated by Rails developer Jago:

“Yeah, that is a bug. We need to fix that,” and you know eventually someone will find it and it’ll be important enough to them that they go fix it, or if I get bored on a weekend or something.

This suggests that during the post-release phase, many issues are “cleaned up”<sup>13</sup> and the burden of doing so falls on already highly active developers (“developer concentration”).<sup>14</sup> As stewards of the project, core developers take it upon themselves to perform the complex fixes or those fixes that nobody else will address. Thus, those developers become more dominant in the community, thereby furthering the centralization of the community (Amrit & Van Hillegersberg, 2010). Because these highly active developers need to focus on issues that they otherwise may not have chosen to focus on, we see a broadening of task allocation among developers (“developer-issue dilution”). Developers often cannot exclusively focus on the issues that they normally focus on because there may be unresolved interdependencies (Lindberg et al., 2016) requiring them to allocate their effort to issues more evenly.

In sum, during the post-release phase, the association between task allocation and prior developer contribution frequencies is strengthened while the association between task allocation and developer-issue inertia is weakened. There is less consideration of what the developers or others have worked on in the recent past. This suggests that a centralized group of developers search widely across the codebase for a variety of burning issues to address—a form of extended cleanup. By “extended” we refer to the reliance on relatively fewer developers to handle a larger number of issues. Essentially, the contributions of these developers are spread out across the project. Hence, we state the following:

Proposition 2: After a major release, developer task allocation is entrained to produce the extended cleanup of issues remaining from the release. This cleanup pattern involves two components:

a. Developer concentration: Fewer developers focus on cleaning up issues compared to the steady-state phase.

b. Developer-issue dilution: Developers spread their effort more evenly across the issues they contribute to compared to the steady-state phase.

Although this observation may stand to reason—after a major release developers must clean up bugs (Glance, 2004; Rossi et al., 2009; Crowston & Scozzi, 2004)—our findings elaborate on this understanding in critical ways. First, it is noteworthy that in anticipation of a release more of the community participates, whereas the cleanup falls on the shoulders of those who already contribute heavily to the project. This is not an obvious result. It would make sense that the entire community, as they scratch their own itches (Howison & Crowston, 2014), may be more focused on fixing the problems in the wake of a release. Instead, we find that the burden rests on relatively fewer participants across the community. Explanations for this involve the complexity of dealing with unresolved interdependencies (Lindberg et al., 2016) that may require a broader perspective and greater expertise that highly active developers may have (Xiao et al., 2018). This, however, certainly implies a certain level of risk (Goggins et al., 2021) that OSS development entails relying on relatively few developers to clean up after releases and may result in a protracted cleanup of issues. OSS projects would do well to understand how to mitigate this risk (Germonprez et al., 2021).

Together our propositions suggest that OSS communities move across three states: open superpositioning in the steady-state phase, focused collective action that we refer to as concentrated mobilization prior to a major release, and then an extended cleanup after a major release. As such, we can think of the release cycle as an oscillating state machine. As a major release approaches, the concentration of contributions across both developers and issues decreases while compartmentalization due to inertia increases, only to be reversed again after the release. OSS release cycles are not only linear changes in task allocation but also rhythmic oscillations in how narrow contributions are concentrated across developers and issues (Figure 7).

## Discussion

This research has examined task allocation in OSS and found that three self-organizing task allocation mechanisms become entrained with release cycles according to different patterns. This shows that task allocation is different before and after major releases. Below we detail contributions for theory and practice, specify limitations, and suggest future research.

## Contributions to Theory

This research offers three main contributions to theory: (1) extending open superpositioning as the main lens through which to understand task allocation, (2) identifying entrainment as a mechanism through which the association between release cycles and task allocation is mediated, and (3) explaining how entrainment theory can be updated to account for the distributed nature of online communities.

First, we contribute to theory by showing how release cycles are associated with task allocation. The received view of selforganizing in OSS communities rests on the “open superposition” view of developer activity (Howison & Crowston, 2014). In this view, independent contributors focus on their own needs and defer difficult problems until they become tractable through the gradual accretion of smaller changes. The view locates the agency behind task allocation within developers themselves, suggesting that they selforganize. These effects can be assumed to hold at the steadystate phase of an OSS release cycle. Our research, however, suggests that task allocation of individual developers interacts with the rhythm of release cycles through entrainment (Ancona & Chong, 1992).

Therefore, our findings question some self-organization assumptions that undergird a good deal of research on OSS (Howison & Crowston, 2014; Lindberg et al., 2016; Medappa & Srivastava, 2019). While OSS developers, in principle, operate autonomously, our findings suggest that several structural factors interact with this task allocation. Hence, our study contributes to and extends past work, which has hinted at the presence of community-level factors that are associated with how individual developers makes decisions regarding their task allocation or other aspects of development (Ducheneaut, 2005; Qureshi & Fang, 2010). Our contribution, specifically, is to show how the individual agency of developers interacts with community-level factors, i.e., release cycles, to produce task allocation patterns.

Second, we contribute to theory by identifying entrainment as a mechanism that connects release cycles and task allocation. This offers a way of understanding task allocation that is neither “undersocialized” and overly self-organizing (Granovetter, 1985) nor overly reliant on structural explanations, such as social network studies emphasizing the effects of, for example, the degree to which developers are deeply embedded in a network, on project success (Grewal et al., 2006). Perspectives that excessively rely on either selforganizing or structural explanations render us unable to see the second-order effects of any one factor. That is, we cannot expect structural changes to have a linear effect on selforganizing task mechanisms, as they fundamentally interact with each other. Release cycles clearly influence activity, but they are endogenous—they are also a result of that activity.

Our research goes beyond purely individual-level explanations of OSS activity and also beyond top-down and structural explanations by identifying a mechanism through which individual developer behavior interacts with structural factors, i.e., through entrainment with release cycles. Release cycles are co-constituted with developer task allocation through the entrainment of rhythmic patterns over time. We thus contribute to the OSS literature by identifying entrainment as an important mechanism that connects the individual agency of developers with structural factors such as release cycles.

Entrainment not only explains the presence of indirect relationships between release cycles and developer’s task allocation patterns but also hints at the presence of other potentially entrained patterns within OSS and other online community activities. These may include innovativeness and new technology features (Xiao et al., 2018), risk mitigation in OSS (Goggins et al., 2021; Germonprez et al., 2021), socialization of new members (Qureshi & Fang, 2010), membership tenure patterns (Ren et al., 2015), overall life cycle patterns (Kane et al., 2014), and weekly and annual patterns entrained to school years or vacation rhythms, among many other conceivable examples.

Crucially, entrainment is a bidirectional process through which two (or more) rhythmic processes influence each other until they become synchronized. The literature refers to this as “mutual entrainment” (McGarva & Warner, 2003). Therefore, it may also be the case that task allocation mechanisms influence the timing of release cycles. We do not know, however, if the directionality of these effects is equally strong, or perhaps if it differs according to contextual conditions such as the relative distribution of the core and the periphery of a community. Regardless of the relative directionality and strength of these effects, our findings suggest that the relationships between release cycles and self-organizing task allocation mechanisms are both complex and bidirectional.

Third, we contribute to entrainment theory by explaining how it needs to be thought of differently within the context of distributed communities. Entrainment theory has usually been applied in the context of a classical organizational model (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008) to examine how internal processes of an organization become entrained to the environment or how specific cycles, such as internal product development and marketing cycles, become entrained to each other. Such cycles, when situated in traditional organizations, can be centrally controlled and coordinated (Shipp & Richardson, 2021). However, our research concerns distributed communities of volunteer-developers who cannot be centrally controlled, at least not entirely. Certainly, there is some release management in many OSS communities, and this also involves release managers, but they have little direct authority (Poo-Caamano et al., 2016). OSS communities have relatively weak management hierarchies, and formal roles, such as release managers, may influence work but rarely direct it; therefore, goals emerge and are negotiated among participants over time (Winter et al., 2014). This stands in stark contrast to the situation in traditional organizations where activities such as release management are enabled by hierarchical organizing mechanisms. In self-organizing contexts outside of any organizational “container” (Winter et al., 2014), our view of entrainment captures a process that not only entrains two easily identifiable processes but also suggests an ongoing synchronization of behaviors among large arrays of heterogeneous individuals as well as community-level processes.

Hence, we contribute to entrainment theory by explaining how it may be applied in distributed communities of volunteerdevelopers, such as OSS communities. Entrainment in distributed online communities is distributed across sets of actors who enact multiple diverse processes, all of which connect to each other in partially sparse and partially dense networks, resulting in effects that move in multiple directions with varying strength at the same time. This represents an extension to current applications of entrainment in the context of organizing and helps us think of entrainment as a distributed, complex, and multidirectional process.

## Contributions to Practice

The entrainment perspective on OSS communities may help to identify hitherto ignored sources of performance. Prior research suggests that entrainment increases performance, while the lack of entrainment pushes processes “out of synchronization,” which is detrimental to performance (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008). To increase performance, practitioners may want to encourage a concentrated mobilization pattern of collective action before a major release and extended cleanup of issues after a major release.

Further, our research indicates that a major release is a useful signaling mechanism to promote intensive work on select features by individuals who focus on the same issue repeatedly. This may be particularly helpful when specialized knowledge is necessary to move the project forward (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016) or when complex features need to be finished in a short period of time (Xiao et al., 2018). Release managers may therefore want to consider using major releases to spur accelerated development while being mindful of not exhausting the resources of the community. Inversely, a conservative community may want to use major releases sparingly, to maintain focus on incremental rather than radical innovation and feature development.

Finally, task allocation may also influence release cycles, suggesting that it is important for release managers to pay attention to how tasks are being allocated across the community before a particular release schedule is set. Setting release cycle schedules without considering task allocation patterns may cause the community to lose entrainment and fail to synchronize processes, which reduces productivity and performance (Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008). Hence, release managers need to examine task allocation patterns when planning a release to avoid the risk of losing synchronization. This may require the creation of specialized dashboards enabling release managers to monitor task allocation patterns.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are several limitations to our study that need to be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the theory that we have formulated is obviously limited by the context within which it was developed—OSS release cycles. This means that we should be careful to not overgeneralize to other contexts with different characteristics. OSS is characterized by fleeting membership, high turnover, low entry barriers, and knowledgeintensive work (Faraj et al., 2011; Howison & Crowston, 2014; Lindberg et al., 2016; Shaikh & Vaast, 2016). Therefore, we can expect that the degree to which our results can be generalized to other contexts is limited by the extent to which such contexts embody similar characteristics. Some insights may be generalizable to other peer production contexts, such as wikis, where external events such as national elections may serve as sources of entrainment (Arazy et al., 2020b). Further, other contexts, such as open hardware (Powell, 2012) or collaborative design contexts such as 3D printing (Kyriakou et al., 2017) may become entrained to external technology trends or releases of crucial software. Contexts where agile methodologies are used may exhibit similar patterns (Fowler & Highsmith, 2001;

Maruping et al., 2009) around major releases, customer-related deadlines, etc. Future research should examine whether entrainment to deadlines and other release cycle patterns is an important mechanism driving task allocation in such contexts.

Second, we did not examine the substantive content of developer activities, nor did we look at detailed changes in code. We only looked at task allocation, i.e., which developers contribute to which issues and the patterns that are formed as a result. Our analysis reveals little about the substance of development work. This opens interesting research vistas regarding the effects of the content of the work and related discussions across the OSS release cycle. Prior work (Arazy et al., 2020a; Lindberg et al., 2016) has looked at the sequencing of activities and how such patterns form emergent routines. While we looked at patterns of task allocation, we did not examine the sequencing of different development contributions. Therefore, changes in the sequencing of different types of tasks or contributions across release cycle phases represent an interesting future direction to extend this work (Lindberg et al., 2022).

Third, due to the conceptual nature of mutual entrainment (McGarva & Warner, 2003) we are not making any causal claims in this study. Entrainment is an interactive process through which multiple oscillators influence each other. However, such influence may not be uniform over time or equally strong in both directions. In our study, we did not analyze such dynamics.

Our research suggests several avenues for future research. Scholars may need to examine to what degree different factors emerge as “zeitgebers” (German for “time-giver,” see Pérez-Nordtvedt et al., 2008) during entrainment. That is, some cyclical processes grow so strong that they entrain other processes without in turn being entrained by them. Researchers may, for example, investigate whether release cycles act as zeitgebers for some subsets of tasks in OSS communities. It is reasonable to expect that they do so because of their prominent role in demarcating major changes in the software. To what extent and for what subsets of tasks this effect is the strongest is, however, still unknown.

As we have examined a limited, nonexhaustive set of rhythmic patterns in OSS development, outstanding questions include: Along what qualitative vectors do developer behaviors vary rhythmically beyond task allocation mechanisms and release cycles? Which of these vectors entrain each other? How do they entrain with important macrolevel cycles such as project life cycles, and how do they entrain with environmental cycles such as technological trends or regulatory changes? Questions such as these can enable researchers to leverage our entrainment framework to examine questions beyond the scope of our empirical inquiry.

## Conclusion

While OSS communities are typically self-organizing at the developer level, our research suggests that community-wide processes such as release cycles entrain with individual-level task allocation mechanisms to shape OSS activity. Hence, we initiate a stream of inquiry into how multifaceted processes become entrained to each other within online communities and how such entrainment affects salient outcomes such as productivity, conflict, and artifact quality.
