Keywords: Decision making teams Biased information exchange Team social structure Applied linear control GDSS technology

## a b s t r a c t

Ill-structured decision-making by teams is conceptualized in terms of information exchange. A linear system for the dynamics of information types is used to formulate “optimal” information exchange over time as a control problem. The control problem highlights issues in managing the exchange of evaluations across stage transitions in heuristic decision-making. The managerial problem is then extended to represent social structure in teams as a regulator of the amount and type of information that members exchange. A non-linear system in information exchange as mediated by the distribution of status among team members is de<sup>fi</sup>ned and used in numerical exercises to examine parameter sensitivities. Finally, the technology of managing information exchange to follow a de<sup>fi</sup>ned quality maximizing path is reviewed and the capabilities of recent GDSSs to implement the control variables in the conceptualization are described in detail.

## 1. Introduction

Decisions in complex organizations are increasingly being made by teams [5,10,15,36,44]. This is most often the case when decisions are ill-structured [33,55]. The designation of ill-structured describes decisions for which there are no well de<sup>fi</sup>ned algorithmic or heuristic solution procedures. Ill-structured decision making can be framed in information exchange. In such a framework, interaction is operationalized in terms of the amount and type of information that members exchange. A relevant typology includes ideas, data/facts, positive and negative evaluations, and interrogatives.

Since there is no algorithmic or heuristic solution to an ill-structured decision, ideas are a key information type in de<sup>fi</sup>ning an objectivemaximizing decision. However, ideas require <sup>fi</sup>ltering on objective related criteria. Consequently, negative evaluations are a requisite information type in maximizing the quality of an ill-structured decision. Moreover, the objective-maximizing exchange of information can be expected to differ between ideas and negative evaluations. While quality can be expected to monotonically be increasing in the number of ideas exchanged, the relationship of negative evaluations to quality is likely to be approximate by quadratic. Too few a number can under-evaluate ideas; too many can inhibit the initiation of ideas. [27,46,47].

Finally, in considering managing for quality objective, it should be recognized that teams have emergent social structures [6]. One of the consequences of these structures is a tendency to oversend negative evaluation to lower status members and under-send this information type to higher status members [49,51]. As will be argued, quality in decision making is increased when negative evaluations are sent to members in proportion to the number of ideas they initiate rather than their status in team. From the above, a decision-making team's quality maximizing objective when decisions are ill-structured can be qualitatively de<sup>fi</sup>ned as (1) maximizing the number of ideas generated by the team, (2) maintaining the exchange of negative evaluations in bounds of a de<sup>fi</sup>ned ratio to the number of ideas and (3) sending evaluations to individuals in proportion to the number of ideas they have initiated.

One of the conditions that increase the number of ideas exchanged is in heterogeneity in team membership as, for example, in their demographic and background variables [41]. However, the diversity in demographic variables that can contribute to team objectives through increased heterogeneity in the idea pools of team members can simultaneously be dysfunctional for team objectives. This is because the variation in demographic variables introduced by member heterogeneity most often increases the variance in the social structure of a team and the biases that it introduces to the exchange of information in the team. An additional managerial objective would then be to retain contributions of diversity in the initial idea pool while minimizing the biasing effects of status inequality that can arise from diversity. The preceding observations are indicative of the complexity of managing a decision making team toward quality objectives. In the next section, a framework in information exchange will be introduced to address the de<sup>fi</sup>ned managerial objectives.

## 2. Problem motivation

The observations in the previous section indicate the challenges in managing the ill-structured decision making of a team toward a quality objective. Prescriptive procedures clearly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned conceptual framework. This section will elaborate on a framework for ill-structured decision making in the amount and type of information that team members exchange. The <sup>fl</sup>ows of information in the team will then be given explicit form in a linearized dynamic system that will be used to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a control problem for the managerial objective. Procedures to manage information exchange in ill-structured team decision making for objectives de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the system will then be taken up in a subsequent section.

2.1. Managing the information exchange of decision making teams for quality objectives

One tradition in managing social processes in the interaction of team members has focused upon negative evaluations and directly implements rules that order or constrain the initiation of this information type in information exchange. For example, nominal group [7,29] and Delphi procedures [20] either have members generate ideas independently of evaluation or provide rules for the ordering of member opportunities for idea initiations in a phase of the interaction of group members. However, negative evaluations clearly have the function of <sup>fi</sup>ltering ideas on quality criteria and when given interactively can direct idea generation. As such, procedures for removing process losses [53] by proscribing of negative evaluations for part or all of the decision making are also likely to limit or remove process gains that result from the direct evaluation of ideas in information exchange. Even a cursory review of the background in idea generation and decision making by teams suggests that management of the paths of evaluations is likely to be a key control in maintaining quality maximizing information exchange [26]. Unlike ideas, evaluations can be managed by inserting or editing them in computer-mediated information exchange.

As will be taken up in detail, decision support systems (GDSSs), as systems for computer-mediated management of interaction, have the potential to manage information <sup>fl</sup>ows according to concept-de<sup>fi</sup>ned designs. For example, an administrator can use a GDSS to reduce structurally generated biases in information exchange by either anonymity in the exchange or the masking of initial status cues of team members for at least parts of the interaction. Available methods also allow editing and inserting of information to control the path of evaluations for quality maximizing paths. Finally, computer-mediated and face-toface interaction can be mixed in a hybrid system when the objectives of the team are served by different rules for interaction in different phases of decision-making.

A guiding premise in such a design is that process gains are best served when the type and timing of information exchanged in the team are set as a function of a phase sequence in interactions that can be related to decision quality. As has been recognized, there are conditions under which a team may elect to loop back to a previous phase before reaching a decision. Phase-referencing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from a well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned conceptualization of the dynamics of information exchange in team decision making. An essential part of such a conceptualization is in de<sup>fi</sup>ning properties and dynamics of information types and mediators of the exchange such as a team's social structure.

## 2.2. Practical applications

As can be summarized in terms of information exchange in interactive teams for ill-structured decisions, (1) information that is exchanged is frequently biased away from quality optimizing rates of the information types, (2) time is often inef<sup>fi</sup>ciently allocated across decision phases if there is not at least a general de<sup>fi</sup>nition of phases and transition criteria and (3) social processes bias the sources and targets of information in types and rates of initiation. Team members that are likely to be higher in status through age, gender and organizational rank tend to over-send information in comparison to other team members even when capabilities for the decision are recognized not to be unequal across members. These biases are most evident in risky information types of ideas and negative evaluations.

To contextualize this, consider the following. In 2001, a Fortune 500 company decided to further centralize their operations and move their headquarters to a larger campus. While decision making in the company was previously made through a managerial hierarchy, it was decided that a team composed by members of several departments of the company and major contractors would make complex decisions on the design of the facility. Since optimal designs are judgmental and there is no de<sup>fi</sup>nable heuristic for optimality, the decision making was ill-structured. An extensive team building program preceded interaction in decision making. In this training, agreed upon procedures proscribed negative evaluation for most of the interaction. Although members differed in their demographics and background variables, training emphasized social equality in team interaction.

As a basis to assess their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, interaction in team meetings was videotaped. Subsequent analysis of the exchange of information in the team showed in fact that the distribution of information types was closely related to a status distribution de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the demographics and rank in the sponsoring organization. This disparity was cataloged in the information type of ideas for which these was recognized to be no reason for the documented bias. Although explicit negative evaluation was proscribed for most of the decision making, videotapes documented the equivalent in interruptions and contrary statements, most notably by higher status team members. These results were in spite of the agreement on equality in interaction in team building. Interestingly, in a post decision meeting, members were generally unaware of bias in sources of ideas and evaluation by gender, age and rank until documentation was provided.

This background story does not indicate faults of the team. It rather exempli<sup>fi</sup>es what are typically non-optimal conditions in unmanaged interacting teams. An objective of the conceptualization to follow and exercises that support its implications is to further the design of procedures that can guide the management of interaction in a team such as the one described above toward quality objectives. The implementation of procedures can be expected to incorporate capabilities of GDSSs with an informed administrator/facilitator.

In the next sections, ill-structured decision-making by teams will be given an explicit form in information exchange. The basic system will initially be used to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a control problem for quality optimizing paths of negative evaluation. The amount and timing of the exchange of this information type is commonly seen as a key issue in managing for quality objectives. The system will then be extended to include other information types and effects introduced by the social structure of teams in ill-structured decision making. Dynamics and sensitivities implied by the system will be investigated with numerical methods. Finally, managing dysfunctional effects in interactive teams through GDSS capabilities and administrator-GDSS interface will be addressed.

## 3. Maximizing quality objectives in ill-structured decisions as a control problem

Dynamic systems of information exchange in team decision-making for quality objectives can provide a framework for the use of technology to manage the team toward the objectives. To facilitate the design of managerial procedures for the team objective, the initial form of a system in information exchange introduced here will be in an objective-maximizing path of dynamic variables that are modi<sup>fi</sup>able by a managerial program. As has been recognized, while ideas are commonly addressed as objectives in brainstorming and other team applications [38], quality in problem solving and decision-making clearly has more complex dependencies in information exchange. The consideration in the initial system that will be introduced is in terms of maximizing the number of ideas initiated in the team as a key objective in ill-structured decision-making. A coordinate consideration is in keeping the number of the negative evaluations in a quality maximizing ratio to the number of ideas. A well-supported phase organization in illstructured decision-making will be considered to contribute to de<sup>fi</sup>ning quality-increasing dynamic paths of information types.

Control systems [19,30] are useful speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of a dynamic system in which a path of a variable can be designated to optimize objectives. In the application introduced here with a de<sup>fi</sup>ned objective in decision-making, negative evaluations (N) and positive evaluations (P) can be considered as control variables in dynamic idea functions (J). Whereas ideas are generated internally by team members, evaluations can be externally managed in team interaction by the design of GDSSs.

## 3.1. Information flows in the ill-structured decision-making of teams

A system in information exchange can be given a reduced linearized form in dynamic rates of ideas and evaluations.

$$
\mathrm{dJ} / \mathrm{dt} = - \mathrm{K} _ {1 1} \mathrm{J} + \mathrm{K} _ {1 2} \mathrm{P} - \mathrm{K} _ {1 3} \mathrm{N}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\mathrm{dP} / \mathrm{dt} = \mathrm{K} _ {2 1} \mathrm{J} - \mathrm{K} _ {2 2} \mathrm{P} - \mathrm{K} _ {2 3} \mathrm{N}\tag{2}
$$

$$
\mathrm{dN} / \mathrm{dt} = \mathrm{K} _ {3 1} \mathrm{J} - \mathrm{K} _ {3 2} \mathrm{P} - \mathrm{K} _ {3 3} \mathrm{N}\tag{3}
$$

where, J is the rate of idea exchange in the team;

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {P} & \text {is the rate of positive evaluation exchange in the team;} \\ \text {N} & \text {is the rate of negative evaluation exchange in the team;} \\ \text {K} _ {\text {ij}} & \text {is a rate parameter.} \end{array}
$$

The signs of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients in Eqs. (1) to (3) follow fundamental properties of these information types. In Eq. (1), the rate of idea exchange decreases as the number of ideas exchanged in the team increases, ${ \mathrm { K } } _ { 1 1 } < 0 .$ The rate of idea generation increases with the rate of positive evaluation $\mathrm { K } _ { 1 2 } > 0 ;$ decreases with the rate of negative evaluation ${ \mathrm { K } } _ { 1 3 } < 0 .$ . In Eqs. (2) and $( 3 ) , \mathrm { K } _ { 2 1 } , \mathrm { K } _ { 3 1 } > 0$ since the rate of idea generation generally increases the rates of both positive and negative evaluations. Relationships between the rates of positive and negative evaluations are assumed to be negative $K _ { 2 3 } , K _ { 3 2 } < 0$

Most unclear are effects of the rates of positive and negative evaluation exchange on their own subsequent rates. Here, it is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed that $K _ { 2 2 } , K _ { 3 3 } < 0$ since it is assumed that members generally recognize that too high a negative rate can disintegrate the team and too high a positive rate can detract from evaluative requirements of the task. However, observations of “climate effects” [45,46,48] do suggest the possibility that $\mathrm { K } _ { 2 2 }$ and $\mathrm { K } _ { 3 3 }$ are not uniformly negative over the entire range of P and N, respectively, and that a nonlinear form might eventually offer a more accurate representation.

Certain strong prior hypotheses about the magnitude of K coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients can be offered for the linear system in Eqs. (1) to (3). These are summarized in Table 1.

Although Eqs. (1) to (3) can be solved for equilibrium values of a matrix of K coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and tested for stability conditions, much of system design focuses on outcomes of controlled paths of N, and attention in explicit modeling will be given to this control. In discussion to follow, J and P will be considered as partly determined by their interdependencies and initial levels and partly determined by N. The level of N will be considered to be adjustable to a designated optimal level.

## 3.2. Stage transitions in ill-structured decision making

Recent research has indicated that segmenting the decision process into stages such as information gathering and idea generation prior to evaluation of alternatives on quality criteria increases the likelihood that all decision relevant information will be generated and used [21,26]. The transition from the idea generation phase to the idea evaluation phase is clearly a critical juncture in representing a conceptualization of information exchange that increases decision quality. Too early a transition to an evaluation phase can lose the contribution of uncommon ideas that occur late in the generation phase. Correspondingly, the absence of a transition can result in a loss in quality from inadequate sorting of ideas on evaluative criteria.

In Fig. 1, the transition from idea generation to idea evaluation is represented at time r. In practice, it is important to allow r, the time of transition, to be adaptively set as a function of the team's path of information exchange as determined by criteria or judgments that assess ability, task, and properties of the interaction rather than pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned. In this <sup>fi</sup>gure, this transition is assumed to occur when the idea rate falls to J , a proportion, a, of J , the initial rate of idea exchange. At time r, an increase in N serves the functions of evaluating the generated ideas while keeping the subsequent rate of idea generation at a low level so as not to interfere with evaluation and convergence to a decision.

The path of J in the interval r to v is denoted by the solid line extension of the J function from J to J . The dashed line extending from J to J indicates the path which the J function would have taken if not suppressed by an increase in the N function. Ideally, the rate of negative evaluation in the team will be maintained at some low level to allow ideas to be re-de<sup>fi</sup>ned or recombined, and new ideas to be exchanged until the team signals convergence.

The team is considered to start with an initial positive rate of idea exchange that depends on variables that are exogenous to the interaction system (e.g., abilities of team members, heterogeneity in the membership, content domain and type of decision). Uncommon ideas are expected to be key contributors to decision making that is ill-structured. As noted, uncommon, original, or “quality” ideas occur late in the generation of ideas so that the quality of an ill-structured decision can be expected to increase from allowing the rate of idea exchange to have its natural stopping point rather than prede<sup>fi</sup>ned time limits. ## 3.3. The control problem

The control problem that can be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed from the preceding discussion is given in Eq. (4). The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of time interval [0, ∞) in the control problem leaves the upper bound unde<sup>fi</sup>ned since the intention is to deal with an initial case in which the interaction time is determined endogenously by J and N. Eq. (4) de<sup>fi</sup>nes the control system in matrix notation as:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} d J / d t \\ d P / d t \end{array} \right] = K \left[ \begin{array}{c} J \\ P \end{array} \right] + b N (J, P, t)\tag{4}
$$

where,

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \left[ \begin{array}{c} J \\ P \end{array} \right] & \text { is   the   state   vector } \\ K & \text { is   a   coefficient   matrix } \end{array}
$$

b is a vector of constants that de<sup>fi</sup>nes how ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently the control is transferred to the system

$N ( J , P , t )$ is the dynamic path of the rate at which negative evaluations are exchanged in the system.

The problem can be stated as: <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal control $N ( I , P , t )$ such that

$$
\text { Min   w } \int_ {0} ^ {r} J (t) d t - \int_ {r} ^ {v} N (J, P, t) d t\tag{5}
$$

where,

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {w} & > 0 \\ \text {r} & = \min \left\{\mathrm{t} > 0 \mathrm{I} (\mathrm{t}) = \mathrm{I} _ {\mathrm{r}} \right\} \\ \text {v} & = \min \left\{\mathrm{t} > 0 \mathrm{I} (\mathrm{t}) = 0 \right\} \end{array}
$$

and N is continuous in t on $( 0 , \infty )$ and differentiable on $( \Gamma , \infty )$ . Moreover, let $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { ~ N ~ } ( \mathrm { t } ) \leq \mathsf { A } _ { 1 } . \mathrm { d } \mathrm { N } / \mathrm { d } \mathrm { t } \leq \mathsf { A } _ { 2 } , } \end{array}$ where, $\mathsf { A } _ { 1 }$ is a maximum value which N can assume and $\mathsf { A } _ { 2 }$ is a maximum rate of change in N (i.e., dN/dt).

Both ${ \bf A } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathsf { A } _ { 2 }$ keep the behavior of the system more consistent with plausible values for variables under consideration. If the number of negative evaluations exchanged reaches too high a level, the team can lose all cohesion. Similarly, changes in the rate at which negative evaluations are exchanged after point r are bounded to avoid abrupt changes that may threaten credibility.

Eq. (5) is an objective function that minimizes the difference between a scaling factor proportion of the total number of ideas exchanged in the interval $\mathrm { t } = 0 \mathrm { t } 0 \mathrm { t } = \mathrm { r } ,$ , and the total number of negative evaluations exchanged in the interval t = r to t = v. Time r is de<sup>fi</sup>ned, as above, i.e., the time in team interaction where I falls below $\mathrm { J } _ { \mathrm { r } } \left( = \mathsf { a } \mathrm { J } _ { 0 } \right)$ . The stopping time, $\mathbf { V } ,$ is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as occurring when ${ d J } / { d t } = 0$ . Interaction is conceptualized as a continuous exchange of ideas and evaluations. In the latter periods of interaction, it is hypothesized that negative evaluations will be greater than ideas in a quality maximizing sequence. Assessment of ideas and convergence on a decision is a primary task in these periods. When the rate of idea generation in the system goes to zero (or some low rate), interaction is effectively terminated.

The quality-optimizing dynamic path of N is determined by the stage or phase in decision making. In the interval t = 0 to r, a constant level of the rate N that is proportional to the initial rate of idea exchange is expected and de<sup>fi</sup>ned from the solution to Eq. (3). Here, the system designer should choose a minimum N that will provide a <sup>fi</sup>nite r for given initial values of variables and parameters in the system. In the interval $\mathbf { r } < \mathbf { t } \leq \mathbf { v } ,$ , N depends on the total number of ideas exchanged in $0 \leq$ $\mathrm { \Delta t \leq r , }$ the scaling factor w and the levels of J and P. The path of P is also “free” in this system. This information type has not been emphasized in discussions since it is seen as a less critical contributor to decision quality than J and N. However, it is expected that dP/dt N 0 at t = 0 since $\mathrm { J } _ { 0 }$ will be large relative to N and $\mathrm { K } _ { 2 1 } > \mathrm { K } _ { 2 3 }$ . As J decreases and P increases, dP/dt is expected to decrease according to Eq. (3). When dN/dt increases at $\mathrm { { t } > r , }$ dP/dt is expected to decrease with a rate proportional to N and go to zero.

Minimizing Eq. (5) is an approximation to maximizing decision quality under study hypotheses since in the time interval 0 to r, it allows the system to have approximately as many ideas as a “free” system, and in the interval r to v it seeks to match the number of negative evaluations to a proportion of the total number of ideas that have been exchanged. While the ratio of negative evaluations to ideas is an initial benchmark, this can be re<sup>fi</sup>ned by advances in arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence that allow more complex rules for managing the information exchange [31].

While the linear control system represents key relationships in information exchange, a non-linear system offers a basis to more de<sup>fi</sup>nitively give conceptually driven form to systems in information exchange. Although this system is not tractable enough to examine analytically, numerical methods can be implemented to examine its properties. Additionally, there can be bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from disaggregating the model into a more micro version with structural differences among individuals and de<sup>fi</sup>ned mechanisms of aggregation.

## 4. Dynamic information exchange in interactive groups and teams: an integrated system

4.1. Single-source and multiple-source ideas in the information exchange of interactive teams

Ideas generated by team members can be dichotomized into those which are the initial ideas of a single member and those which are combinations of initial ideas that they or other members of the team have already given. The latter category of ideas can provide interactive teams with an important basis for “process gains”, i.e. superiority in the number and quality of ideas over an equal number of individuals acting independently of each other [34].

## 4.1.1. Single source ideas

The current size of the “pool” of relevant ideas that the jth member is initially aware of, $\dot { J } _ { j } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ , is considered to be a function of this member's initial idea “pool” as corrected by the number of ideas already given by this member and the number of ideas either given by all other team members or common ideas in their idea pools. Taken together, ${ j _ { j } } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ and ${ \dot { J } _ { j } } ^ { ( 2 ) }$ represent the dynamic of idea initiation by the jth individual. Equation ${ \dot { J } _ { j } } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ represents this member's rate of generating his or her original ideas which are not combined with ideas of other team members. Equation ${ \dot { J } _ { j } } ^ { ( 2 ) }$ represents the corresponding rates of generating ideas that result from combining the jth members' own ideas with ideas of other team members.

The number of ideas that the member is aware and actually initiates to the team is mediated by social processes that include the expected social cost of being evaluated, especially if there are members who are assumed to be higher status in the team. This can be directly represented in the social mediator of a K function for trust in the meritorious distribution of equations for the dynamics of ideas. Eq. (6) is a form for the initiation of single service ideas by an individual team member.

$$
\dot {J} _ {j} ^ {(1)} = K _ {a, j} \left(A _ {j} (1 - \alpha_ {j}) - \left(J _ {1} ^ {(1)}... J _ {n} ^ {(1)}\right)\right), J _ {j} ^ {(1)} (0) = 0\tag{6}
$$

$J _ { j } ^ { ( 1 ) }$ is the number of single source ideas that the jth member initiates in a de<sup>fi</sup>ned time interval

$$
\begin{array}{l} A _ {j} \qquad \text { is   the   initial   pool   of   ideas   of   the   jth   member } \\ \alpha_ {j} (J _ {1} ^ {(1)},..., J _ {n} ^ {(1)}) \end{array}
$$

is the “overlap” in the initial pool of ideas between the jth and kth members.

## 4.1.2. Multiple source ideas

Multi-source ideas are based on combinations of ideas that can be in terms of (1) an idea by the jth members that has not been given in the team and an idea of another team member, and (2) two ideas already given by team members other than the jth member that are combined by the jth member. Eq. (7) gives a form for multi source ideas.

$$
\begin{array}{l} j _ {j} ^ {(2)} = K _ {a, j} \bigg (\sum_ {k \neq j} J _ {j k} ^ {(1)} \bigg) \Big (A _ {j} - J _ {j} ^ {(1)} - \alpha_ {j} \Big (J _ {1} ^ {(1)}... J _ {n} ^ {(1)} \Big) \Big) \\ \quad + K _ {b, j} \bigg (\bigg (\sum_ {k \neq j} J _ {j k} ^ {(1)} \bigg) ^ {2} - \bigg (c _ {_ 1} \sum_ {k \neq j} J _ {k} ^ {(2)} \bigg) \bigg); J _ {j} ^ {(2)} (0) = 0 \end{array}\tag{7}

4.2. Social mediators of information exchange: equity and trust in the rate mediator

Teams can generate conditions that increase the willingness of its members to send more messages that have social risk (i.e. likelihoods of being negatively evaluated). The extent to which a team member will be willing to risk his or her status to contribute to team objectives depends in part on their trust in other team members to act similarly and manage the exchange “equitably” [2,32,43]. Equity in information exchange can, in turn, be described in terms of a distribution of positive and negative evaluations to members that is proportional to the ideas they initiate in information exchange. ## 4.2.2. Bias in micro-level status judgments: defining the Δ function

Team members typically have dual motives that underlie their participation [49]. While they are motivated to contribute to the team objective, they are simultaneously motivated to maintain their social standing or status in the team and this will partly depend on the evaluations that they receive in interaction. From preceding conjecture, the weighting given to status differences (i.e. the Δ function in Eq. (11)) will be important to the amount and type of information that the number initiates. Literature on personal weighting of gains and losses strongly suggests that this weighting will not linearly correspond to actual status differences.

A basic claim of the present framework is that similar to biases in assessing monetary outcomes [1,23,24], judgments of outcomes that arise from positions in status distributions of teams introduce systematic bias in the valuation of anticipated social gains and losses from initiating information. Team members are generally loss aversive. That is, they will generally “pay” more to avoid a loss with a given likelihood than for a gain of the same likelihood [23,24]. In the case of status judgments, this can be interpreted as meaning that they will overweight the possible status loss from a negative evaluation by a higher status team member in comparison to a negative evaluation from a team member who is lower in status by an equivalent distance.<sup>1</sup>

## 4.3. Dynamics of evaluations

In giving a form to the key information type of evaluations in illstructured decision making; there is a basis to expect that evaluations are different from other information types in their dynamics. Unlike other information types, evaluations are both informational and affective. They are informational, as are other information types such as data and questions, in that they can discriminate the quality of other information types. They are also affective since evaluation is likely to be related to self-esteem. Considered strictly as information, negative evaluations would be expected to decrease with status differentiation, since they have increasing risk of status loss for team members other than the high status member. However, as affect, there is a basis to expect that they have a mimetic or copying property in which increases in negative evaluations by a member can result in increases by other members $[ 2 7 , 4 6 , 4 7 ]$ . As a team increases in its status differentiation, the higher status members are more likely to exceed the team rate in negative evaluations.

## 6. Managing negative evaluations as a control variable in information exchange

Having given form to dynamic systems for information exchange in idea number and decision quality, procedures to manage the information exchange to maximize decision quality will next be considered. From a managerial perspective, ideation is endogenous to team members. That is, managerial procedures can put appropriate conditions in place, but idea generation is an internal process in members of the team. In contrast, negative evaluations can be a managerial control variable. As such, the technology of controlling N in a team's interaction will be emphasized.

Since it is also recognized that the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information in teams with a well de<sup>fi</sup>ned status hierarchy depends on perceived risks to the relative status position of members that are the source or target of speci<sup>fi</sup>c information exchanges, there is a basis to modify, restrict or eliminate source or target identi<sup>fi</sup>cations in information exchange in idea generation phases of the decision-making process. In this phase, neither the sources of an idea nor evaluations of the idea need be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

In the evaluation phase, the source of an N could also be kept public or private depending on judgments of their contributions and their status position. With experience in designing optimizing behavior in real teams, information exchange in virtual teams can be managed to get close to real time face-to-face team processing [9,22,25].

For example, in predecision training that is often part of “team-building”, acceptance of the principle of social equality in participation (i.e., participation that is based on capability rather than position in the teams' social structure) is not assumed to be tantamount to its occurrence in the interaction of the team [e.g., 51]. However, making the objectives and their bases in a quality objective explicit to the team and agreed upon by its members can be expected to move toward the objective if not attain it. Managerial procedures for each of the phases are designed to combine insight into processing with technology to bring about team decisions that have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly greater quality than unaided interactive decision. Clearly, procedures require calibration to account for variation across industries, types of decisions and whether the team is virtual or meets in a combination of face-to-face and computer-mediated interactions. As has been observed, under these conditions, there is a basis to anticipate that aided team decisions will be greater in quality than the results of team members acting independently. This remains to be experimentally established under conditions on the designs that have been outlined in Table 5.

## 7. Summary and discussion

A framework for ill-structured decision making by interactive teams that is based on the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information between team members has been offered as a basis for implementing technology to manage teams toward quality in these decisions. In this framework, the social structure of the team mediates the amount and type of information that team members initiate. The information types of ideas and negative evaluations have been recognized as critical to decision quality. Processing these information types in decision making is organized in sequenced phases in which different types of cognitive operations predominate. To accommodate these observations, a form was proposed for the time dependent relationships between the <sup>fl</sup>ow of ideas and negative evaluations that are hypothesized to maximize decision quality.

The proposed framework recognizes that cognitive operations and social processes are inextricably linked. An understanding of the outcomes of cognitive processing in interactive teams, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from de<sup>fi</sup>ning social bases of information exchange. Towards this end, relationships between negative evaluation, idea initiation and the social structure of status distributions have been explicitly considered. The time dependency in contributions of information types to decision quality was also addressed. Following a conceptualization of decisionmaking phases, it was hypothesized that the contribution of ideas to quality is greatest in an initial phase of the interaction while the contributions of negative evaluations to quality is greatest in the <sup>fi</sup>nal interaction phases. Finally, on structure, it was suggested that when unmanaged, the presence of status differentiation increases the total number of negative evaluations exchanged and the domination of the number of initiated ideas by higher status members.

The linear system used to formulate hypotheses about “optimal” information exchange over time as a control problem provides an initial basis for considering the implementation of the account of quality optimization in ill-structured decisions. In particular, it highlights issues in de<sup>fi</sup>ning the transition between heuristic decision-making phases and the outcome-optimizing amount of negative evaluations to be targeted. An initial formalization of the framework for information exchange, a dynamic linear system was used to give a form to managing for initiation of ideas by team as a control problem.

Next, a non-linear system was proposed to better represent the complex dynamics of ill-structured decision-making. Numerical exercises indicated the dependencies of the number of ideas initiated by team members and of decision quality on system variables and parameters. A more explicit objective function in terms of controllable inputs and procedures to manage toward quality optimizing levels of control variables was then implemented in the non-linear system and exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed in applications. It was recognized that while decision quality clearly depends on both the number of ideas and the number of negative evaluations, the quality increasing paths of these information types differ.

In the information processing of a team, quality in ill-structured decisions is conditioned by a balance between ideas and negative evaluations. The level of negative evaluations has to be of a magnitude suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to <sup>fi</sup>lter ideas on quality criteria but less than the magnitude that will inhibit subsequent idea initiation. A form for a qualitymaximizing decision in terms of the information processing by the team that follows from above premises was then offered.

Finally, the technology of managing information exchange in interactive groups and teams was addressed. Most notably, managing the exchange of negative evaluations was further detailed as a relevant control variable and a policy objective. Consistent with the discussion of social structure in the team as a mediator of information exchange, it was suggested that GDSSs capabilities in adding or removing information about team members as sources or targets of information can contribute to decision quality. The capability of GDSSs to accomplish this and manage other dysfunctional effects reported by investigators [37,39] has also been indicated.

Finally, administrator procedures to manage information exchange in the team toward a quality maximizing decision are integrated with the capability of a Level III GDSS. Taken together, the foregoing exposition offers a conceptual basis for the design and management of interactive teams that can reduce commonly cited process losses while retaining process gains in decision making and problem solving. Technology for control procedures that can move closer to “seamlessly” managing information <sup>fl</sup>ows and minimizing effects social structure in the phase organization of ill-structured decision making has been cited in available GDSSs.

New technology in managing the information exchange of teams and recently reported practical experience with virtual teams provide a basis to expect substantial improvements in the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of interactive teams for relevant objectives in decision making. Given this background, more complete de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of objectives in the quality of ill-structured decision-making and the information inputs for this objective, increase in their importance. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of quality and its generating functions summarized in this exposition provides a basis to initially parametize process in interactive information exchange and social processes that enter into quality objectives. As indicated, the proposed system and numerical results that have been reported can be considered as giving direction to the design of decision support systems in which managerially-imposed conditions on interaction in a decisionmaking team can be maintained. Whatever the results of lab studies, ill-structured decision making by teams is likely to increase in over time for reasons that are important to organizations. These include member satisfaction, representation of diversity and the transfer of team experience to implementation. The conceptualization of process and de<sup>fi</sup>nition of methodology to understand and manage team information exchange toward quality objective therefore remains a priority in the study of organizational decision making.
