# Risks and benefits of business process outsourcing: A study of transaction services in the German banking industry

Heiko Gewald <sup>a,</sup>\*, Jens Dibbern <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> E-Finance Lab, Institute for Information Systems, House of Finance, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe University, Gru¨neburgplatz 1, 60323 Frankfurt am Main, Germany <sup>b</sup> Institute of Information Systems, University of Bern, Engehaldenstrasse 8, CH-3012 Bern, Switzerland

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 21 June 2007 Received in revised form 15 April 2008 Accepted 15 March 2009 Available online 1 April 2009

Keywords: Business process outsourcing Risk–benefit concept Perceived risk theory Path dependence theory Partial least squares Questionnaire surveys Group comparison

## A B S T R A C T

We developed a model of the adoption of business process outsourcing (BPO) based on risk–benefit analysis. The model was tested in the German banking industry in four areas of transaction processing. Our results showed that, in general, perceived BPO benefits have a substantially stronger impact on intention to increase the level of BPO than perceived risks. However, the experience that banks have with the BPO process, [whether already decided on BPO (Pro), still in the pre-decision phase (Neutral), or opted against BPO (Contra)], moderated the impact of factors affecting the perceived risks and benefits of BPO. Banks that opted for BPO were mostly driven by a desire to focus on their core business while considering financial risks. Banks in the neutral position had the most balanced view of risks and benefits, mainly financial and performance ones, and also focusing on the core business and increased business process performance. Those banks that decided against BPO mostly focused on benefits while the perceived risks were mostly formed by strategic and performance risks rather than financial ones. The results point out the path dependencies of the BPO decision.

## 1. Introduction

Business process outsourcing (BPO) involves the delegation of an entire business process to a third party provider, including its supporting services. Business processes in which IT plays an important role have become prime candidates for BPO. One industry whose way of carrying out business processes has dramatically changed is the banking industry. Associated business processes like trade settlement or execution control are now conducted using IT systems. In virtually every BPO engagement, the service provider assumes responsibility for both the business process and the underlying IT services. Thus, the BPO client transmits a set of information input to the service provider who executes the process and passes processed data back to the client. However, in the German marketplace, studies of service providers [8] and market research firms [23,43] indicate that the BPO adoption rate is still unexpectedly low. This raises the question: What are the main factors that hinder as well as drive the intention to increase the level of BPO from the perspective of key decision makers in organizations?

Most prior studies have emphasized the high growth potential of BPO. A study of 31 European banks reported in 2003 that the majority intended to increase their level of outsourcing, particularly of BPO [31]. Based on an in-depth case study about Lloyds London, Willcocks et al. [44] concluded that the market for back office transactions was large enough for new vendors to enter and that it would grow substantially. They further found a rising market for BPO when reanalyzing over 350 case studies on outsourcing. In spite of these promising BPO scenarios, however, there is still scant empirical research on the actual determinants of the BPO decision [40], except for one study that focused on whether business processes should be outsourced domestically or offshore [41].

This motivated us to analyze the determinants that enable or obstruct the BPO decision, developing a model that integrates multiple BPO decision criteria. We focused on two important antecedents of the decision: the risks and benefits that senior management associates with BPO.

## 2. Theoretical foundation and the development of hypotheses

## 2.1. Conceptualizing the dependent variable

While some firms have already adopted and implemented BPO, others are still evaluating it, while some have not yet seriously considered it. Rather than seeking to explain variation in the current degree of BPO across banks, we focused on the variation in their intention to increase the current level of BPO.

## 2.2. Risk–benefit concept

In line with Ajzen’s TRA [1], we argued that management’s intention to change the BPO level depended on its attitude towards BPO, which was influenced by salient beliefs about it. However, we did not wish to test the entire causal chain from salient beliefs to attitude and intentions as an antecedent for BPO behavior. Instead we focused on the more immediate salient beliefs about BPO that may form a decision maker’s attitude towards BPO.

This view is consistent with the risk–benefit concept of decision theory [36,37]. Accordingly, we argue that positive beliefs about BPO increase the manager’s perceived benefits, while negative beliefs result in perceived risks [26]. Risks have been found to influence adoption behavior in various ways. For example, in IS research perceived risk has been shown to influence the adoption of e-services and of new IT systems [21,24]. Taken together, the influential role of perceived risks in adoption processes in general and in outsourcing decisions in particular is widely supported at the individual as well as the organization level.

Perceived BPO benefits aid in reaching an organization’s objectives, achieved by reaching a higher level of efficiency or effectiveness in performing business processes, e.g. by reducing IS costs or closing gaps in different types of IS resources [22,42].

The decision maker assesses the potential risks and benefits that arise from BPO and combines them into overall perceived risks and overall perceived benefits. The result is an overall evaluative appraisal of BPO which influences the intention to change the current level of BPO. Accordingly, the following hypotheses were made:

H1. A high level of perceived risks of BPO negatively influences the intention to increase the level of BPO in the bank.

H2. A high level of perceived benefits of BPO positively influences the intention to increase the level of BPO in the bank.

The resulting risk–benefit BPO adoption model is shown in Fig. 1.

## 2.3. Perceived risks of BPO

We adopted the framework developed by Cunningham [14]. He divided into six dimensions (risk facets): performance, financial, risk/time, psychological, social, and safety. When applying this framework to the BPO context, we reduced it to four facets: performance, financial, strategic, and psychosocial.

Performance risk admits that the BPO engagement may not deliver the expected level of service. Potential losses due to this can be significant in the banking industry. They may severely damage the bank’s reputation and even initiate corrective action by a regulatory body. Therefore, managers must carefully analyze the ability of the service provider. Potential sources of failure are the inability to provide the resources [39], a lack of vendor capabilities, declining service levels over time or the service provider’s lack of experience.

H3. The higher the perceived performance risks of BPO, the higher the overall perceived risk of BPO.

Financial risk assumes that the bank may have to pay more to reach the expected level of service than initially anticipated. Higher than expected costs often occur in IT implementation due to ‘‘creeping’’ requirements, hidden costs, or renegotiating the contract [4–6,18]. Thus:

H4. The higher the perceived financial risk of BPO, the higher the overall perceived risk of BPO.

Strategic risk admits that the bank can lose critical resources and capabilities necessary to stay competitive. The strategic impact of back office transaction processes per se may be limited, but their potential role as complementary assets cannot be neglected. They serve as the backbone for front office services such as investment banking or sales activities.

Essential resources and capabilities may include cross-functional skills as well as technological know-how necessary to facilitate innovation. Moreover, outsourcing may reduce a bank’s flexibility to react quickly to new internal and external forces, leading to a general lack of control and a high dependency on the service provider [19].

H5. The higher the perceived strategic risk of BPO, the higher the overall perceived risk of BPO.

In addition to these risks at the firm level, outsourcing may also affect the personal affairs of the managers responsible for the business process. Psychosocial risk involves the possibility that the personal reputation and career of the manager responsible for the business process will be harmed due to outsourcing. Outsourcing ventures are often associated with negative assertions in the daily press about loss of jobs. This may affect the personal reputation of bank managers amongst peers, clients, and staff, as well as lead to a loss of power due to loss of authority over resources. Thus:

H6. The higher the perceived psychosocial risk of BPO, the higher the overall perceived risk of BPO.

## 2.4. Perceived benefits of BPO

We included four major benefits of BPO from previous studies on IS outsourcing.

Cost advantages assumes that external vendors can provide business functions at lower costs due to specialization and the realization of economies of scale and scope [33]. Thus:

H7. The higher the perceived cost advantages of BPO, the higher the overall perceived benefits of BPO.

Focusing on core competencies frees up resources to be used more productively in areas that create value. While support for this argument has been mixed with regard to IT outsourcing [17], it may play a role in BPO, since business process performance is more directly linked to value creation than that of IT services. Therefore we postulated:

H8. The higher the perceived ability to focus on core competencies through BPO, the higher the overall perceived benefits of BPO.

Access to specialized resources. Outsourcing service providers offer a set of services to their clients. Because of learning curve effects, the vendor develops skills in handling the offered processes [30]. Furthermore, economies of scale allow the service provider to utilize special resources (e.g., tax specialists for processing exotic mutual funds). Access to leading edge IT resources has been shown to be one of the main indicators of IS outsourcing success and an important driver for outsourcing decisions. Thus:

H9. The more BPO is associated with access to specialized resources, the higher the overall perceived benefit of BPO.

Quality improvement is a reason why some corporations choose to outsource. This is often associated with gain in efficiency and effectiveness [9]. Banks expect the service provider to incorporate industry best practices and total quality management procedures. The quality of transaction processing can have a direct impact on customer satisfaction. Thus:

H10. The higher the perceived quality improvements through BPO, the higher the overall perceived benefits of BPO.

## 3. Survey method

We conducted a survey in the German banking industry by using a mailed questionnaire.

## 3.1. Operationalization of the constructs

Every construct in the research framework was measured by a set of survey questions. Wherever possible, existing measures were used, translated into German and adapted to the BPO context. Main contributors of previously tested indicators (translated from German into English) are shown in Table 6 of Appendix A.

The questionnaire was intensively discussed with knowledgeable researchers and pre-tested independently with managers of three different banks not included in the sample. Based on these insights, the questionnaire was modified and finalized.

## 3.2. Instrument administration and survey response

The German banking industry was chosen for three reasons: (1) the share of digital transactions is particularly high in Germany. (2) German banks are facing severe cost pressure which requires them to assess alternative ways of service provisioning and governance. (3) The German supervisory authority of the banking system has established a stable and predictable set of regulations [45]. Accordingly, the banking industry shares a common understanding of outsourcing.

In 2003, 2226 banks were registered for conducting business in Germany. For our research, the 200 largest banks were selected, based on their total assets as reported for the year 2003. The cumulated balance sheets of these banks account for more than 90% of the German banking market [27].

Four types of banking transaction processes were selected: back office/settlement processes for transactions in securities, consumer credits, domestic payments, and foreign exchange/money market. At the time of the study, these processes were most likely to be outsourced [29]. For each of the processes, the responsible manager was chosen as a respondent. Moreover, empirical evidence on outsourcing decision making has shown that the manager responsible for the entire service most often is the final decision maker. In case of the BPO decision, including the responsible process manager in the decision making process is particularly important as regulation requires the bank to remain fully responsible for the business processes after outsourcing [10].

In order to identify the business process owners, each of the selected banks was contacted by phone. Since some banks did not offer all four products to their clients, only 593 questionnaires were mailed. The requested time frame for returning the questionnaires was 6 weeks. Managers who had not returned the questionnaire after 4 weeks received a phone call asking whether they needed assistance. Overall, 218 usable question-

## 4. Model testing

Structural equation modeling (SEM) technique using partial least squares (PLS) was chosen for testing. PLS was preferred due to the small sample size. PLS produces consistent parameter estimates for sample sizes as low as 20, which made PLS appropriate for our analyses [12]. The PLS-calculations were performed with PLSGraph version 3.0.

## 4.1. Measurement reliability and validity

In order to assess the quality of our measurement instrument, tests for convergent validity and discriminant validity were performed.

Convergent validity is represented by indicator and construct reliability. The first was examined by looking at the construct loadings: in our model, all loadings were significant at the 0.001 level and above the recommended 0.7 parameter value (tests were conducted using the bootstrap routine with 500 re-samplings). Construct reliability was tested by examining the composite reliability (CR) and the average variance extracted (AVE). The estimated indices were all above the threshold of 0.6 for CR and 0.5 for AVE (see Table 6).

Discriminant validity was assessed in two ways. First, we examined the cross-loadings, obtained by correlating the PLS component scores of each latent variable with both its respective blocks of indicators and all other items included in the model. As seen in Table 7, the loading of each indicator was higher for its respective construct than for any other. In addition, every construct loaded highest with its own indicators. Our second test for the aggregated data set $( n = 2 1 8 )$ was based on a confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS 6.0. Although our sample size was limited for the use of covariance-based SEM, we used this procedure as an alternative way of assuringdiscriminant validity. Our examination of the correlations between the latent variables revealed that none of the correlations wereabove the threshold of 0.85, suggesting that the constructswere correlationally distinct implying discriminant validity. Thus, both the PLS and AMOS tests supported discriminant validity.

## 4.2. Structural model test for the aggregated data set

Having gained confidence in our measures, the next step was to examine the structural paths, each represented a hypothesis in our model. The validity of the individual hypotheses was examined by looking at the magnitude of the standardized parameter estimates between the constructs together with the corresponding levels of significance (t-values) obtained by running the bootstrap routine.

Our findings for the composite data set including all banks independent of their status quo in the BPO decision are shown in Fig. 2. The path coefficient $( \beta )$ between a manager’s perceived risk of BPO and her/his intention to increase the level of BPO was negative and weakly significant, supporting H1 $( \beta = - 0 . 1 3 7$ $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , while perceived benefits had a positive significant impact on BPO intention, supporting H2 $( \beta = 0 . 5 8 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) .$ Perceived risks were significant and most strongly influenced by financial risks $( \beta = 0 . 4 1 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , followed by strategic risks $( \beta = 0 . 2 3 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and performance risks $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 , p < 0 . 0 5 ) ,$ supporting H3 to H5. The impact of psychosocial risks is not significant $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 )$ . On the other hand, the most significant facet of perceived benefits was the focus on core competencies $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 $ ), followed by quality improvements $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 5$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and cost advantages $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting H7, H8, and H10, while the path coefficient of access to specialized resources was not significant, contradicting H9.

The overall explanatory power of the model was assessed by looking at the squared multiple correlations $( R ^ { 2 } )$ of the dependent variables. $R ^ { 2 } – \mathrm { v a l u e s }$ of 0.67 are normally considered to be substantial, while 0.33 is moderate, and 0.19 is weak. Fig. 2 shows that 44% of the variation in the intention to increase the level of BPO is explained by the model. The explained variance of perceived risks $\left( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 6 \right)$ and perceived benefits is nearly substantial $\left( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 0 \right)$ . The test results also suggested some variation between the independent variables concerning the strength of the impact. For example, the impact of perceived benefits on intention was about four times stronger than that of perceived risks. This discrepancy was further supported when examining the effect size of both independent variables via the calculation of f<sup>2</sup>. The results show a small effect size for perceived risks $( f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 1 5 )$ and a moderate effect size for perceived benefits $( f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 9 2 )$ on the intention to increase the level of BPO.

## 5. Discussion of findings

## 5.1. Commonalities across groups

Overall, the empirical results offered solid support for our risk– benefit model of BPO. Notably, however, the results suggested that perceived risks and benefits of BPO were not factored into the decision to the same extent. Perceived benefits generally tend to have a much stronger impact on the decision than perceived risks. The strongest and most consistent perceived benefit of BPO is the opportunity to refocus the bank on its core competencies. The focus on core competencies was a significant predictor for the intention to increase the level of BPO. Obviously, senior management perceives BPO as a sensible measure to free up attention from non-core business activities and to restructure service offerings and the value proposition of the bank.

No significant differences could be found in terms of the impact of cost advantages or psychosocial risks. Cost advantages are not the primary concern in evaluating BPO. The less prominent role of cost savings in the BPO decision could be traced back to the fact that realizing economies of scale and scope may not differ much between use of in-house facilities and external vendors. Indeed some banks have implemented Shared Service Centers as a form of internal organizational centralization that has allowed them to gain similar economies of scale [25]. Moreover, the degree of standardization through BPO may not be as high as expected: in a follow-up interview with one bank manager who had decided against sharing facilities with another bank, variations in transaction volume and in transaction objects made it too hard to continue its consideration. Another interesting result was the low importance of psychosocial risk.

## 5.2. The moderating role of decision status

In addition to the general findings, a number of significant differences in the path coefficients between constructs occurred when comparing banks that had a different status quo in their BPO decision (i.e. Pro, Neutral, and Contra). Apparently, these different decision histories led to different perceptions of BPO risks and benefits.

The first group difference was the relatively strong recognition of BPO risks by those banks that had not yet considered BPO or were undecided. They showed a more balanced recognition of risks and benefits as opposed to banks that decided against BPO (Contra). These differences were significant at the 0.10 level. ‘‘Neutral’’ Banks also showed a balanced view between efficiency criteria, as reflected by their recognition of financial risks $( \beta = 0 . 4 7 ^ { * * * } )$ , and effectiveness criteria, such as performance risks $( \beta = 0 . 3 3 ^ { * * } )$ and quality improvements $( \beta = 0 . 5 0 ^ { * * * } )$ . Moreover, they are the only ones that consider benefits from access to specialized resources. Contrary to H9, however, there is a negative relationship between perceived access to better resources and overall perceived benefits of BPO $( \beta = - 0 . 2 9 ^ { \ast } ) .$ . Thus, in spite of greater perceived access to specialized resources through BPO, preadopters could still keep transaction processes in-house, while they would outsource processes in spite of a lack of access to specialized resources. Most banks have conducted settlement processes for several years, mostly since their foundation. Therefore, the benefit of gaining advantages in know-how and technical resources through a specialized service provider may be limited. Also, the processes investigated were commonly regarded as commodity processes, indicating that the need for specialized resources may not be high. Thus, access to specialized resources either plays no role in the decision or this factor may be overridden by other factors such as occurs in banks with a neutral decision status.

Interestingly, those banks that already opted for BPO show the most one-sided consideration of BPO decision factors. Besides their strong desire to focus on the core business, they also consider financial risks quite strongly. Past experiences may have let to uncertainty about the pricing mechanism and overall costs of outsourcing, while the potential threat of low performance through BPO may be ignored due to the desire to get rid of a business process (through BPO) which does not belong to the core business.

Banks that decided against BPO show a stronger focus on benefits than risks; cost advantages and quality improvement may later change their opinion about BPO.

## 5.3. Theoretical implications

Ours is the first study that has empirically showed that the risk–benefit concept from decision theory is a viable option for understanding outsourcing behavior. It is important to note that the avoidance of risks through BPO was found to be less important as a decision criterion than the evaluation of potential benefits. This has important implications, because it shows that potential market failure may not be the major factor in evaluating make-or-buy choices of BPO: strategic benefits are very influential.

Our study also shows that the perceived benefits and risks of BPO are significantly moderated by an organization’s BPO history. Adopters strongly focus on efficiency, while non-adopters focus more on effectiveness.

## 5.4. Practical implications

Our study produced a set of risks and benefits to be considered in the BPO decision. Banks that opted either for or against BPO may question the strong focus on either efficiency or effectiveness. For service providers, our study gives important factors to emphasize when offering services to German banks. The assessment of perceived benefits showed that potential clients appear to be shifting their focus from cost savings to increased business process value and quality factors like process improvements and divestment of non-core business processes. This offers a way for rethinking the outsourcing proposal; providers will succeed if they are able to offer additional business value, risk mitigation or risk sharing. In particular, lowering the client’s financial risk seems important for gaining BPO accounts.

## 5.5. Limitations of the study

Our study focused exclusively on the banking industry in Germany, thus the results may not be representative of other industries or across countries. This is of special importance if one takes the inherent reference framework of outsourcing and the associated risks and responsibilities into account, as required by national German regulations.

Although our results were generally independent of the kind of transaction process and bank type, increasing the generalizability of our results [32], more strategic and less commodity like business processes may have affected the results.

## 6. Conclusions

We examined the role of risk and benefit perceptions of senior management as determinants of the decision to outsource business processes. Overall, our study showed that perceived risks and benefits were useful factors for predicting the intention to outsource business processes while perceived benefits outweighed perceived risks. Overall, German banks can be seen to favor business value over cost reductions due to BPO.

The study has also shown that the outsourcing decision history plays an important role in evaluating future outsourcing arrangements. Banks that already opted for BPO had a strong focus on efficiency while mostly ignoring the impact of BPO on the effectiveness of business processes. By contrast, organizations that decided against BPO or that have not yet made a BPO decision were much more cognizant of business process quality and performance issues. Since banks are forced by German regulatory forces to stay responsible for the correct execution of the business processes, the mentality of BPO adopters to focus solely on efficiency and concentrating on core business processes may turn out negatively in the future.
