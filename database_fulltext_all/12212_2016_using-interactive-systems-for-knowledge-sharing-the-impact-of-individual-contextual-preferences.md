## Abstract

Grounded on the communicative ecology framework and theories related to guanxi and communication context in China, we investigate the moderating effects of individual preference for communication context (IPCC) on knowledge sharing via interactive systems (KSIS) behaviour. Drawing on survey data from a hotel chain’s employees, we explore how guanxi elements drive knowledge sharing (KS) behaviour and enhance KS outcomes. Our data demonstrate that IPCC significantly moderates the effect that KSIS has on KS outcomes, but the IPCC has a direct and negative impact on KS outcomes. We explain these seemingly contradictory findings and their implications for research and practice.

## Keywords:

Communicative Ecology Framework (CEF), Guanxi Elements, Communication Context, Knowledge Sharing via Interactive Systems (KSIS), Knowledge Sharing Outcomes

# Using Interactive Systems for Knowledge Sharing: The Impact of Individual Contextual Preferences in China

## Abstract

Grounded on the communicative ecology framework and theories related to guanxi and communication context in China, we investigate the moderating effects of individual preference for communication context (IPCC) on knowledge sharing via interactive systems (KSIS) behaviour. Our data demonstrate that IPCC significantly moderates the effect that KSIS has on KS outcomes, but the IPCC has a direct and negative impact on KS outcomes. We explain these seemingly contradictory findings and their implications for research and practice.

## Introduction

Knowledge Management (KM) is widely-accepted as being crucial for organisations that wish to promote best practices and reduce redundant reinvention efforts (McDermott and O’Dell, 2001). Effective competition in a knowledge intensive industry depends on employees not guarding or hoarding knowledge as personal secrets (Lu et al., 2005). Furthermore, the dynamic nature of competition demands the right knowledge in the right place at the right time. We argue that knowledge sharing is not an isolated behavior as it requires a means/method to realize the behavior, which can be through face-to-face or via technology. In our study, we are interested in technology-facilitated knowledge sharing, more specifically when mediated by interactive systems. Facilitated by IT, the right knowledge can equally be communicated via formal systems like knowledge repositories or informal interactive systems like instant messengers, blogs, wikis, etc. To date, KM research has largely focused on organisational-level or community-level contexts characterised by an emphasis on the IT-supported codification of explicit knowledge; only a few studies have investigated non-codified knowledge sharing and knowledge exchanges via interactive systems. This is perhaps not surprising as codifiable knowledge has long been recognised as a source of strategic and competitive advantage (Kogut and Zander, 1992), with IT enabling “collaboration among different units and individuals unconstrained by the boundaries of geography and time” (Lu et al., 2005). More importantly, we argue that regardless of the existence of an organizational level knowledge repository or a company-wide knowledge community, informal knowledge sharing using interactive systems prevails in companies and facilitates the business operations and innovations, largely due to its highly dynamic and individualized conversations. Nevertheless, the use of interactive systems for informal knowledge sharing is significantly increasing in organizations and beneficially complements formal KM practices, even as this domain is theoretically under-explored (cf., Davison et al., 2013).

Apart from the reliance on company norms to drive KM behaviour, individual employees may also choose to engage in knowledge exchange for their own reasons even though it may not lead to higher remuneration. In fact, both individual preferences and cultural aspects influence KS practice. This is notably the case in China. The prevailing Chinese culture has an embedded preference for informal and tacit forms of information (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997; Davison et al., 2013). Furthermore, the focus on KM in China parallels China’s increasing importance in the global economy. Unfortunately most prior knowledgefocused research in China has not examined indigenous cultural practices but instead has focused either on comparisons with other countries (Chow et al., 2000) and/or on the inward transfer of knowledge (Li and Scullion, 2006). Such studies are commonly informed by Western theories, assumptions and priorities, and thus look into China through an etic (externally informed) lens, rather than studying China emically (from the inside). Important exceptions are Young et al. (2012) and Davison et al. (2013). Examining KS from the emic perspective by including indigenous, culturally-related constructs can provide richer contextual information and also more insightful theoretical implications, as explained below.

Comparative research is valuable for cross-cultural purposes, but it typically does not permit the identification of the full richness of knowledge exchange behaviour in the Chinese context. Key components of the Chinese context that have received less attention in the research literature, yet which are central to the way Chinese employees share knowledge, are guanxi and context. These two constructs have received much less attention from prior KM studies, the majority of which were conducted in Western companies. Nevertheless, Davison et al. (2013) explored the role of indigenous Chinese variables, including guanxi, in a qualitative study of knowledge exchange behaviour in two Public Relations firms in China. However, we have not seen any evidence of a larger-scale survey of employee attitudes towards knowledge sharing in China that explicitly considers indigenous Chinese variables (cf. Tsui, 2006). As we explain in more detail later, guanxi refers to the reciprocally obligatory relationships that Chinese employees maintain with selected others: they leverage these relationships as they communicate, solve problems and help others. Accordingly, we suggest that the guanxi elements exert an important influence in the Chinese context of knowledge sharing.

Meanwhile, context refers to an individual’s preference for communications to be implicit or explicit (Hall, 1976). In a high-context culture, much of the meaning in communications can be inferred from the context itself. It is unnecessary to communicate in precise detail as interlocutors share sufficient common knowledge to ensure that a few words, or even a nod or a wink, can convey precise information. In a low-context culture, on the other hand, little common knowledge can be assumed between interlocutors and so it is necessary to write or speak meanings explicitly in words. In this study we explore the technology-based knowledge sharing (KS) behaviour of Chinese employees at a major international hotel chain (code-named Ravine). We focus on the influence of guanxi elements on employees knowledge sharing practices with their network members mediated by interactive systems, and the moderating effects of context (high or low) on both guanxi elements and the ultimate outcomes of KS: individual work performance and collective network efficacy. The inclusion of collective network efficacy as one of the outcome variables is due to the focus of this study on relationship networks. Furthermore, this choice is also consistent with the perspective of communicative ecology, as explained below.

In terms of research foundation, we employ the communicative ecology framework (CEF) (Altheide, 1994; Foth and Hearn, 2007) as the overarching theoretical lens to conceptualize the intertwinement of technology, discourse and communication context. The concept of an “ecology of communication” was first put forward by Altheide (1994) and later on developed into the CEF, applied in sociology and communication/media studies (Foth and Hearn, 2007; Hearn et al., 2009). The CEF has only received minimal attention in the information systems (IS) discipline (Davison et al., 2014). However, the CEF provides us with a legitimate and logical basis to identify theoretical constructs and link them into a homological model about communication context and IT-enabled knowledge sharing in companies where both individual and network performance are at stake.

Specifically, this theoretical lens enables us to answer the following question: What are the impacts of indigenous cultural traits, including guanxi and the individual preference for communication context, on the technology-based knowledge sharing behaviour of professional employees and their work-related outcomes? Following this introduction, we review the relevant literature before proceeding to the theoretical development and hypotheses. We then explain the research context and introduce our data collection and analytical techniques. The results of the study follow, together with a discussion of the findings. Finally we conclude the paper with contributions, limitations and suggestions for future research.

## Literature Review

There are several areas of literature that are relevant to this research. These include: the communicative ecology framework (CEF), guanxi, context and knowledge sharing via interactive systems (KSIS). We briefly review each of them in turn, providing sufficient detail to permit the development of hypotheses in the following section.

## Communicative Ecology Framework (CEF)

The concept of communication ecology (Altheide, 1994; 1995) was developed in the field of communications and media research to analyse and interpret the intertwinement of social interactions, discourse and communication media and technology among individuals, collectives and networks in physical and digital environments. Accordingly, a communication ecology that refers to the communication process in a context includes three dimensions: “(1) an information technology (IT); (2) a communication format; (3) a social activity” (Altheide, 1994, p. 667). These three dimensions are also often referred to as the technological, discursive and social layers (Foth and Hearn, 2007) in a communicative ecology.

Specifically, IT in Altheide’s study simply refers to “those external devices, procedures that are used in helping create, organize, transmit, store, and retrieve information” (Altheide, 1994, p. 668). The technology here covers all the communication, not only involving a form of technology (any mechanical devices external to the people engaged in a conversation), but also including the traditional oral format of conversations. So the technological layer comprises communication media and technologies (Foth and Hearn, 2007) While format refers to “the selection, organization, and presentation of experience and information” (Altheide, 1994, p. 668). This discursive layer (Foth and Hearn, 2007) means the communication is organized through certain patterns, shapes, looks which Altheide refers to as formats. The social layer refers to people and the various social structures with which they identify themselves. Such structures can range from informal personal networks to formal institutions or organizations, for example, including groups of friends, formal community, organisations and companies (Altheide, 1994; Foth and Hearn, 2007).

The fundamental premise of the CEF is that changes in communication media have mediated and also transformed social relationships, activities and processes (see also Hearn and Foth, 2007). As new media and communication technology have the ability to change the flow of information, the social interaction between and among individuals, social actors, organizations and governments has been influenced, with consequent changes to the way in which power is constructed, maintained and challenged (Notley, 2009). Although the CEF is derived from the concept of communication ecology, there is no single agreed definition of a CEF and various approaches/methodologies have been used to understand and apply this framework in various contexts, as explained below.

Making use of the three dimensions (in some studies also referred to as the three layers) of communicative ecology (Altheide, 1994; 1995), researchers have investigated and interpreted communicative ecologies at different levels, including individuals, organizations, communities, the urban context, the domestic sphere, governments and social activities of protest as show-cases in a special issue of the Electronic Journal of Communication (Hearn and Foth, 2007). Similarly, the CEF was proposed as a template to analyse the interactions of social context, IT, communication formats and demonstrate how these interactions can influence social activities (Altheide, 2013). More recently, in responding to the call by Foth and Hearn (2007) to explore “the context in which communication processes occur” (p. 9), Davison et al. (2014) drew on the CEF to study how the social interaction and work-related communication in the organizational context were mediated by IT with subsequent impacts on individual work performance.

Although the CEF (Altheide, 1994; 1995) is an influential framework in the field of media and communications, it has rarely been applied in the IS discipline, perhaps due to its broad conceptual coverage and the difficulty associated with measuring the three dimensions of a communicative ecology. However, we contend that although the CEF is particularly useful in investigating social activities, its tenets are also valuable for research at both the individual and the organizational levels in the domain of social and work-related communication. We argue that currently, the most pervasive communication technologies include interactive systems like live chat, social media and wikis that can facilitate and mediate the interaction between interlocutors. The communication context, and especially the concept of guanxi in China, reflects the dimension of the social layer in a communication ecology. In organizational studies, knowledge sharing constitutes the fundamental basis of discourse and cannot be ignored. Considering that the CEF suggests the existence of dynamic relationships among the social layer, discourse and communication technology, it is logical to put these theoretical constructs, viz., guanxi, communication context, interactive systems and knowledge sharing together, and to examine their influence on individual work performance and collective network efficacy as a whole. In the following subsections, we review each of these theoretical constructs in detail.

## Guanxi

Guanxi is a Chinese concept that can be loosely defined as “a close and pervasive interpersonal relationship [that] is based on high quality social interactions and the reciprocal exchange of mutual benefits” (Ou et al., 2014). Guanxi elements include interpersonal trust, face and reciprocal obligation (Lee and Dawes, 2005). Over the last twenty years, guanxi has been operationalized in a wide variety of different ways depending on the context wherein it appears. For instance, guanxi can be seen as involving a network of social ties (Luo et al., 2008) that emphasizes the reciprocally obligatory nature of interpersonal favours (Lee and Dawes, 2005) in a long-term relationship (Leung et al., 2005). Guanxi is both ubiquitous and critical in Chinese society (Fu et al., 2006): Chinese seek to develop and maintain guanxi throughout their working lives (Xin and Pearce, 1996). In the present context, guanxi is important because its presence facilitates the access by individuals to knowledge held by others who belong to the same relationship network (Davison et al., 2013). Therefore, in this study we focus on the importance of those guanxi elements (interpersonal trust, face and reciprocal obligation) in the Chinese context of knowledge sharing.

## Context

Hall (1976) defines context as the “information that surrounds an event” arguing that context is critical to meaning. Conventionally, context is described as being high or low. High context cultures are characterised by communication styles in which individuals prefer to draw inferences from implicit information. In contrast, individuals in low context cultures prefer information to be stated explicitly and also exhibit a preference for quantifiable detail. As Hall and Hall (1990) note: “In low-context communication, the listener knows very little and must be told practically everything. In high-context communication, the listener is already ‘contextualised’ and so does not need to be given much background information”. Hall (1976) suggested that all societal cultures occupy spaces along the low-high context continuum, with no culture exclusively occupying a single space. Thus it would be misleading to assert that a single nation state, such as China or Finland, has a single societal culture that occupies a single space on the high-low context continuum. Instead, different contextual points on the spectrum will be appropriate in different circumstances, of people, topic and sub-culture. However, although high-context and low-context communication takes place in every society and culture, cultures do differ in the degree of context considered normal and necessary in every kind of discourse. In this respect, it is notable that Chinese society has been traditionally considered to be one where high context communication predominates (Gudykunst, 1983).

The high context aspects of Chinese culture are in part related to the Chinese preference for implicit communications (Martinsons and Westwood, 1997; Gao and Ting-Toomey, 1998), where meanings are “inferred from contextual clues or internalised in interlocutors themselves” (Chang, 2001). However, human values, such as contextual preferences, are not immune to changes due to the passing of time (Naylor 1996) and economic modernization in particular (Gold et al., 2002). China’s recent economic growth has been paralleled by social and cultural changes that may have exerted an impact on both a propensity for high context communication and indeed guanxi-based interactions (cf. Inglehart and Baker, 2000; Ingelhart and Welzel, 2005). A strong sense of individualism has now emerged in Chinese society (Yan, 2010), even as individuals continue to rely on guanxi networks for resource management (Yuan, 2012). In order to understand contemporary Chinese communications behaviours, an examination of “the interplay between … contemporary social forces and traditional values and beliefs” (Leung, 2008) is necessary. As Fang and Faure (2011) observe, Chinese communication culture is a “socially constructed changing phenomenon which embraces diversified and even paradoxical mental switching and value orientations”. Indeed, the same person can exhibit a preference for both explicit and implicit, high and low context communication, at different times and in differing circumstances (Faure and Fang, 2008). This may happen due to changes in the communication and working surroundings, as well as in the education environment. However, few empirical studies have been conducted into both the contextual preferences of Chinese people and how their communication style and behavior have changed as a result of the increasing popularity of social networking tools in the workplace.

## Knowledge Sharing via Interactive Systems

Intense relationships are believed to open doors for new knowledge creation “via interactive knowledge sharing with people who trust each other” (Lechner and Dowling, 2003, p.14). In the process of joint new knowledge creation, socialization processes and therefore strong ties are often required (Johannisson 1998). Similarly, Tang (2008) proposed a typology of knowledge networks including transactional and interactive forms. She contended that an interactive knowledge network is based on long-term communication and interaction in which the creation of new knowledge is enabled between organizations. In a comparative sense, codified knowledge (such as patent licensing, material transfer, and data transfer) is shared in the transactional knowledge network while the relationships between organizations that participate in such a network are temporary and oriented towards short-term goals.

As already noted, knowledge sharing in the Chinese context tends to take place between people who share guanxi. These closely connected people form networks based on interactions that may include both face-to-face and online forms. However, in the digital era, we note that this form of knowledge sharing usually involves informal IT applications not formal corporate systems or face-to-face interactions (Davison et al., 2013): employees were observed to prefer online live chats via MSN or QQ (a Chinese online messaging system) even when they were physically located in close proximity in the same office. Such empirical studies echo Wagner’s (2004) contention that KM and group collaboration can be facilitated by interactive and conversational systems. Consistently, Hong et al. (2011) pointed out that we are now experiencing a paradigm shift from conventional KM to conversational KM as a result of the evolving interactive communication technology. Specially focusing on instant messaging, the interactivity of a technology was conceptualized as synchronicity, two-way communication and control (Ou and Davison, 2011). We argue that following the same conceptualization in this research domain, interactive systems can be defined as the class of systems whose operations involve a significant degree of user interaction. In this study, we focus on knowledge sharing that is mediated by such interactive systems as instant messengers, wikis, blogs, microblogs, discussion forums and collaborative editing tools. Unlike the traditional KM tools such as knowledge repository, intranet and expert systems that focus on static knowledge, these interactive systems facilitate the direct and dynamic conversations among network participants.

## Theoretical Model and Hypotheses Development

As explained in the Introduction and Literature Review, we employ the tenets of the CEF (Altheide, 1994; 1995) to establish our theoretical model. Specifically, in the IT dimension, we focus on the currently most prominent interactive systems/technology for communication. Then, following Davison et al.’s (2013) call to focus on informal KS via interactive tools, we refer to our key construct as knowledge sharing via interactive systems (KSIS) as the discourse dimension of the CEF. Specifically, KSIS in this study refers to an employee’s selfreported behaviour when engaging in a broad scope of knowledge sharing activities at work including seeking, sharing, retrieving and communicating about knowledge via interactive systems. In the social dimension of the CEF, we suggest that guanxi elements is the key factor given the significance of this construct in studies about China. In the discursive/format dimension, we include the individual preference of the communication context as the communication pattern referred to in the CEF. Following the CEF and the above literature review, we develop a set of formal hypotheses around KSIS based on an integrated theoretical conceptualization of guanxi, the communication context, KS outcomes and the KS activities in which employees engage.

## Antecedents of KSIS

The CEF (Altheide, 1994; 1995) suggests that the use of communication technology cannot be separated from the social context. In China, guanxi is one of the most significant components of the social context (Davison et al., 2014; Ou et al., 2014). Consistent with Lee and Dawes (2005), we conceptualise guanxi elements as including interpersonal trust, face maintenance and reciprocal obligation, primarily focusing on employees’ value and appreciation of these guanxi elements at work. More specifically in the context of knowledge sharing, trust refers to the belief in the good intent, competence and reliability of employees with respect to the exchange and reuse of knowledge (Kankanhalli et al., 2005). Face maintenance is defined as the image that one cultivates and manages in order to gain acceptance and recognition from others (Huang et al., 2011). Reciprocal obligation refers to the degree to which one believes one can improve mutual obligations and relationships with others (Bock et al., 2005). Researchers (Huang et al., 2011; Davison et al., 2013) have suggested the importance of guanxi as a whole for knowledge sharing. We argue that employees who share interpersonal trust with each other, know how to develop and maintain their own and guanxi-linked others’ faces, and appreciate the value of reciprocal obligation are more willing to share knowledge in general. When an employee believes in the good intent, competence and reliability of other people with respect to contributing and reusing knowledge, it is more likely that this employee would like to make use of interactive tools to contact other people in order to seek and share knowledge. It is also likely that the same employee will attempt to create lasting and binding ties that help to guarantee the future exchange of knowledge. In today’s working environment, IT applications that facilitate interactive communication between people are readily available. The need to develop and maintain guanxi can increase an employee’s involvement in using networks to seek and share knowledge. In addition to trust and reciprocal obligation, face may also play a role in determining whether an employee engages in KSIS or not. Employees who value their guanxi-based KS relationships will very likely do their best to help others if they request knowledge. Meanwhile, by demonstrating their skills, expertise and helpfulness, these employees will benefit by virtue of the opportunity to burnish their reputation and so earn the respect of others in the professional network. These efforts can be largely facilitated by using interactive systems for knowledge sharing. Integrating the above arguments, we propose:

H1: An employee’s appreciation of guanxi elements, including reciprocal obligation, trust and face maintenance, positively affects his/her knowledge sharing via interactive systems.

## Outcomes of KSIS

According to the CEF (Altheide, 1994; 1995), new media has the power to change the flow of information and thus transform the communication process. At the individual level, we argue that using interactive systems for communication and knowledge exchange can significantly improve individual work performance as well as collective network performance. In fact, the CEF has clarified the impact of ICT on both individuals, by fulfilling participants’ needs and interests (Notley, 2009) and network society, by reconfiguring the relationships of interlocutors (Altheide, 2013). Although the literature has demonstrated that the outcomes of knowledge sharing are diverse (e.g., Newell et al., 2009; Scarborough & Swan, 2001), we argue that the effective use of interactive systems for sharing can bring positive effects to both individual work performance and also collective network efficacy. In this study, work performance refers to an employee’s self-evaluation of his/her work performance in terms of confidence, productivity, decision making, and work quality, following Rice (1992). Meanwhile, collective network efficacy is defined as the individual network member’s confidence that his/her work-related network can produce desirable outcomes (Hirschfeld and Bernerth, 2008). This is particularly apparent in contemporary work environments, where different domains of knowledge are involved and employees are distributed across time and space (Maznevski and Chudoba, 2000): such interactions can gradually influence the composition of the whole social network, i.e. the ecological system (cf. Altheide, 1994; 1995). Employees who share knowledge in near real-time with their network members via interactive tools are able to locate answers to questions and solutions to problems. For instance, Voelpel and Han (2005) demonstrate how knowledge contributed by Chinese employees in an online forum was leveraged by Italian employees to achieve a successful project outcome for the organization (Siemens) as a whole. Using interactive systems to exchange knowledge means that employees can bounce back quickly from adverse experiences, help other network members, locate appropriate network resources, leverage them effectively and finally facilitate the sustainability of the whole collective network. To summarize, the effective use of interactive systems for exchanging knowledge can help ensure the realisation of desirable outcomes. Accordingly we propose:

H2: An employee’s knowledge sharing via interactive systems can produce positive outcomes including improving his/her individual work performance and collective network efficacy.

## The Effects of Individual Preference for Communication Context in the Research Model

Following the CEF’s (Altheide, 1994; 1995) view of the social context, we identify communication context as one of the most relevant social and cultural factors when investigating the use of IT in organizational settings. According to Hall (1976), the communication context describes the cultural rules that relate to information exchange and, in particular, the degree to which information in a culture is explicit or implicit. Specifically, a personal preference for a low context style of communication suggests that information is vested in words or precise and unambiguous meanings; on the other hand, a personal preference for a high context style of communication suggests that information is communicated in a rather implicit way, vested in shared experiences and assumptions and conveyed through both verbal and non-verbal codes.

In a similar view but more specific to IS, Martinsons and Westwood (1997) contend that little value is perceived to accrue to information that is codified into a standardized form for Chinese, especially when the communicated information largely depends on the situation and the relationship between interlocutors (i.e., whom one communicates with, what is the social status of the interlocutors, what kind of information is involved, etc.). In practice, “key details, ideas and knowledge are selectively passed on to chosen individuals” (ibid., p.222). These analyses powerfully explain why informal one-to-one communication prevails in both personal and business communications in China, considering that information is viewed as a personal resource rather than an organizational asset. The consequent need for media richness and technology favours the use of informal systems. Consistently, it has been highlighted (Hall, 1976) that the personal network is a primary source to obtain information for those people who prefer high context communication, while people with a low-context communication preference seek information and knowledge “from a research base or use information sources such as reports, databases, and the Internet” (Jean et al., 2010, p. 67). Following these theoretical views, we argue that employees with a high context communication preference favour interactive systems for seeking and sharing information, considering that right information can be communicated with the right person in his/her personal network at the right time. We thus hypothesize:

H3: Compared to an employee with a low-context communication preference, an employee with a higher-context communication preference favours knowledge sharing via interactive systems.

Culture has been frequently identified as a moderator in IS research (Schepers, and Wetzels, 2007; Yoon, 2009; Hwang and Lee, 2012). The literature has implied that cultural differences can moderate the development of relationships in business behavior performance. For instance, the Japanese are found to exhibit a stronger positive relationship between commitment and information when compared to their American partners (Griffith et al., 2006). More consistent with the focus of this study, Jean et al. (2010) made use of Hall’s (1976) argument to directly model high/low communication context as a moderator in their research model about organizational learning and its determinants. Therefore, also using Hall’s (1976) theoretical guidance, we explore the moderating effects of communication context of employees in this research.

When employees exchange knowledge with colleagues or team members who share interpersonal trust, reciprocal obligation and a responsibility to maintain each other’s faces, it is more likely that they can understand the implicit meanings hidden in information and knowledge. This is consistent with the view of Martinsons and Westwood (1997) that key information, such as that which concerns new products, new technologies and new market opportunities, is often obtained by socializing and interacting with friends and acquaintances in the personal network. Similarly, employees in a guanxi network will be able to pick up the actual or hidden meaning of information when exchanging experiences and knowledge. It is also much easier for those employees who prefer a high communication context to obtain and understand the implicit meanings of shared knowledge via the direct communication using interactive systems rather than accessing static systems like knowledge repositories or problem-solving databases.

On the other hand, when communicating with strangers who are outsiders to trust networks, more explicit communication is required as less knowledge is held in common. Hall (1976) also highlights how high-context persons prefer relationships that are stable and persistent over an extended period of time. In this case, static systems that document explicit information and codified knowledge are more valuable for those people who appreciate guanxi less. Following this logic, guanxi elements, including trust, obligations and face, significantly influence a high-context person’s sharing behaviour. This suggests that for those employees preferring high communication context, guanxi elements exert a stronger influencing role to motivate the employees to engage in KSIS more. We therefore hypothesize:

H4: An employee’s preference for communication context has a positive moderating effect on the path between guanxi elements and knowledge sharing via interactive systems, where the guanxi elements have a stronger effect on increasing his/her knowledge sharing via interactive systems when the employee has a preference for high context communication.

The existence of interactive knowledge sharing systems, also known as conversational knowledge management systems (Wagner and Bolloju, 2005), reflects how much of the knowledge creation and sharing in these systems is undertaken through a process of discussion with questions and answers (discussion forum), collaborative editing (wikis), or through a process of storytelling (weblogs) (Wagner and Bolloju, 2005). Researchers (e.g., Brown, 2001) value storytelling for the sharing of otherwise implicit knowledge. Furthermore, it has been suggested that “conversational knowledge management systems usually forego formal knowledge representation, as end users usually do not formally structure their knowledge as rules or similar constructs” (Wagner and Bolloju, 2005).

Consequently, we argue that knowledge sharing via interactive systems is more effective for those people who prefer a high-context communication style: these systems can facilitate the sharing of implicit knowledge at work via story telling. From the media perspective, those interactive technologies can be characterized as having both a rich symbol set and a relatively high transmission velocity that enables synchronicity. We argue that employees from a highcontext communication context prefer interactive systems for knowledge sharing, considering that these technologies can mimic real time situations. By using emoticons, flashing icons and video, many social cues can be replicated online emulating the digital presentation and capture of a nod or a wink during the knowledge exchange. Therefore these interactive systems can more effectively solicit real-time help and enable problem solving at the right time. Furthermore, sharing via interactive knowledge systems can match a high-context person’s desire to build relationships with network members. By linking guanxi members in their own interactive systems, a stable network can be formed and maintained. Updates about individuals and necessary knowledge can be immediately transferred at the right time in the network. Therefore we propose:

H5: An employee’s preference for communication context has a positive moderating effect on the path between knowledge network sharing behaviour and its outcome variables including individual work performance and collective network efficacy, where knowledge sharing via interactive systems has a stronger effect on work performance and collective network efficiency when the employee has a preference for high context communication.

## Methodology

We established our measures based on the literature. We followed the conceptualization of guanxi elements from Lee and Dawes (2005) covering three dimensions: trust, reciprocal obligation and face maintenance. Specifically, we adapted existing measures for the context of knowledge sharing from Bock et al. (2005) for reciprocal obligation, Kankanhalli et al. (2005) for trust and Huang et al. (2011) for face maintenance. In addition, the measure of guanxi (Lee and Dawes, 2005) also includes affect (ganqing or emotions) and interaction. Recalling the literature review, we explained the reason for not including interaction as the operationalization of guanxi in this study. In terms of affect, the guanxi that we observed in our qualitative interviews with hotel employees in the research context was highly functional and directed towards work contexts: it did not include emotional components. Reciprocal obligation and the creation/maintenance of face were often cited as key components of guanxi, but no interviewee mentioned any emotionally-linked facets of guanxi. As a result, we decided to exclude interaction and affect/ganqing from our guanxi elements and instead focused exclusively on trust, reciprocal obligation and face maintenance. Considering “multidimensional constructs provide the ability to increase granularity and detail on different aspects of a construct” and “parsimonious models can provide abstractions that generate insightful explanations about a complex phenomenon” (Petter et al. 2007, p. 628), we operationalize guanxi elements as a second-order construct, following Petter et al. (2007). Considering that trust, reciprocal obligation and face maintenance are the three core dimensions of guanxi, we operationalize them as formative measures of their second-order construct, guanxi elements, capturing employees’ perceived value/importance of these three guanxi elements in the context of knowledge sharing. Grounded on the work of Morris et al. (2009), we established new items about KSIS. The moderator, individual preference of communication context, is based on the conceptualization and scales from Hall (1976) and Kim et al. (1998) from low to high. For the same reason, to achieve a parsimonious model (Petter et al. 2007), the dependent variable, KS outcomes, is operationalized as a secondorder construct covering individual work performance and collective network efficacy. The purpose of operationalizing the KS outcomes as a second-order construct instead of two separate constructs is to achieve a parsimonious model, following Petter et al. (2007). Work performance is measured using the scales from Rice (1992) and collective network efficacy is measured by the scales from Hirschfeld and Bernerth (2008). Considering that individual performance is not conceptually equivalent to collective network performance measures like efficacy, we operationalize them as formative measures of their second-order construct, knowledge sharing outcomes, following the guidelines for formative measures suggested by Petter et al. (2007).

We used the survey method to collect the data. This survey instrument was initially developed and validated in English. Before putting the survey items into the actual data collection process, we examined their reliability and validity with two experienced researchers in the IS discipline and one working professional. We explained our research objectives and sent them the soft copy of the survey questionnaire (in English) for independent comments. With their suggestions, we improved the questionnaire by revising ambiguous words. After confirming the face validity of the survey items in English, we then translated them into Chinese and back translated to English to ensure equivalence of meaning across the two language versions. The survey instrument was operationalized on web-based survey software (Qualtrics: Version 0.749s). We list the final survey questions for data collection in Appendix I.

The context for this research is the China-based operations of a global hotel chain, which we code name Ravine. The second author gained access to Ravine as a result of his frequent stays at and personal connections with the General Manager (GM) of one Ravine property. This GM introduced him to a Vice-President for China who agreed to support the data collection effort. Ravine operates hotel properties at all levels from simple to luxury. Ravine does not operate formal KM Systems internally. However, employees are free to contact external parties for knowledge exchange activities, so long as they do not disclose corporate secrets. In this study, we don’t attempt to distinguish between internal or external knowledge sharing because in reality both practices are equally important and occur simultaneously, according to our interviews in various hotels of Ravine. Our interviews with employees indicated that the typical knowledge shared by employees in this hotel chain covers a wide range of topics including: digital marketing, external relations with suppliers, public relations, relationship management with key partners and stakeholders such as travel agents, government departments, journalists and meeting organizers, the organization of functions and banquets, the effective methods to retain customer loyalty and the handling of customer complaints.

We emailed a survey link to the GMs of 54 Ravine hotels in the Greater China region (including Hong Kong and Taiwan) requesting them to ask their employees to complete the survey. 19 of these GMs replied to confirm they would ask their colleagues to complete the survey. A limitation of the study is the fact that it is hard to estimate how many employees received the invitation because the GMs may have decided only to invite certain employees. Nevertheless, a total of 301 valid responses were received over a ten-week period, from 15 cities. Although 5 respondents did not indicate the demographic details and 71 respondents refused to disclose their job titles, we still include them in the data analysis considering the validity of their responses to the survey questions about the research model. ## Data Analysis

We used SPSS and Smart Partial Least Squares (SPLS) to calculate construct validity and reliability. We first examined the convergent and discriminant validity with factor analysis. The factor loading scores on their expected factors are all above 0.54, with the factor loading scores much higher on their expected factors than on other factors. Meanwhile all eigenvalues of the constructs exceed 1.0. The communality scores all exceed 0.54. These results confirmed adequate reliability of the measures.

Second, since ‘KS outcomes’ is an endogenous second-order construct represented in the research model, we validated this construct with two steps. For its two first-order constructs, work performance and collective network efficacy, the reliability of the measures were both above 0.90. For the second-order construct of KS outcomes, the factor scores of two firstorder constructs as the latent variables from the SPLS analysis were taken as the composite dimensions of KS outcomes in the SPLS analysis, following the method of handling secondorder constructs in SPLS suggested by Petter et al. (2007). Using the same procedures, we also confirmed the validity and reliability of the other second-order construct, guanxi elements, in this study.

Furthermore, construct reliability for all principal constructs was assessed by identifying the composite reliability scores, all of which are above 0.70 (Table 2), suggesting acceptable internal consistency. The square roots of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) are all above 0.73, which is greater than all other cross correlations. This shows that all constructs capture more construct-related variance than error variance. In addition, we conducted collinearity diagnostics to examine multicollinearity among constructs. The results show that maximum value of variance inflation factor (VIF) came to 2.99, which is lower than the suggested maximum of 5 (Hair et al. 2011), ruling out the risk of multicollinearity.

## Discussion

According to the data analysis above, this research presents two broad areas of findings. Firstly, the strongly significant link from ‘guanxi elements’, which includes reciprocal obligation, trust and face maintenance, to KSIS and then the equally strong link from KSIS to KS outcomes are remarkable for their consistency as well as the extent of variance explained. Clearly when knowledge is shared in the Chinese workplace, guanxi elements play a key motivating role.

Equally, this KSIS process leads to enhanced individual work performance and collective network efficacy, suggesting a supporting effect since by enhancing the efficacy of the network, so that same network will facilitate better knowledge sharing in future. Whether the same findings will hold outside the Chinese workspace remains to be investigated, but it is plausible to assume that in other societies where guanxi-type structures prevail, then a similar set of relationships is likely to exist.

Moreover, our data indicate that the respondents have little concern for the negative impacts and only a moderate degree of concern for the positive impacts of face maintenance when performing KSIS activities. Specifically, only about 17% and 22% of the respondents agree (scale rating greater than 4) with the statements “Seeking knowledge will cause others in my network to look down on me” and “I feel a loss of face when others in my network don't answer my requests for knowledge” respectively. Meanwhile, about 45% and 36% of the respondents agree with the statements “Sharing knowledge with my network members will help me to gain face” and “I want to share my knowledge with my network members, because it will help me to gain face” respectively.

The second interesting finding concerns the preference for communication context. Contradicting the perception that the Chinese are high-context people (Hall, 1976), our data suggest that our respondents prefer direct and explicit communication (Mean=2.99 on a scale from 1=lowest to 7=highest; STD =1.33); this is normally indicative of a lower-context communication preference. This suggests that the Chinese may be changing and can be explained in two ways. Our sample consisted of employees at a global (non-Chinese) hotel group. Although the employees themselves are all Chinese, it is very likely that they were influenced by Western styles of communication and work practices. Furthermore, a considerable portion of the younger generation of Chinese has received Western-style education and has adopted a Western philosophy of life in their thinking and working. These people may be developing a preference for communication that is direct, specific and explicit. This reasoning explains the negative direct effect of individual preference of high communication context on KS outcomes (b=-0.35, p<0.01) and suggests that the current work environment in China may be becoming more characterised by precision and a reduction of ambiguity.

Furthermore, our data supported the significant positive moderating effect of individual preference for communication context on the path between KSIS and KS outcomes (b=0.19, p<0.01). This suggests that for high-context people, KSIS can yield a much better effect on both work performance and collective network efficacy. Interactive systems can be fine-tuned for these people in order to optimize the benefits of KSIS. The significant effect of gender on KS outcome indicates that female respondents are relatively more conservative than the male respondents in reporting their performance outcomes.

Interestingly, our data indicate the insignificant moderating effect of communication preference on the path from guanxi elements to KSIS. This may be due to the overwhelming effect of guanxi elements in Chinese people’s lives in general, regardless of communication preference. This reasoning implies trust, reciprocal obligations and face are still significant elements for knowledge sharing behaviour, at least in China, and are applicable to both high and low-context persons.

## Implications and Future Research

Grounded on the findings above, this research has implications for both theory and practice. Firstly, grounded on the theoretical lens of the CEF, we identified and investigated the key factors that correspond to each CEF dimension in the context of knowledge sharing in China: guanxi and communication context in the social dimension; interactive systems in the IT dimension; and knowledge sharing in the discourse dimension. We then empirically examined the impacts of these factors on individual performance and collective network efficacy. Considering that the CEF does not provide or conceptualize particular constructs, this study, especially the theoretical model, can serve as a platform for later CEF research on the media preferences of diverse individuals and organizations and how these choices influence their performance. Future studies can build upon the current research models to explore other key factors in each dimension in order to assess the formation and evolution of a communicative ecology.

Furthermore, we suggest that the investigation of knowledge sharing or management arrangements should examine the efficacy of the whole network rather than merely focusing on individual work performance. This is important because individuals in a network and the network itself can mutually inform and influence each other. Also the sustainability of a knowledge network determines the engagement of individuals. The communicative ecology perspective employed in this study can serve as a starting point to further investigate the collective network efficacy in both formal and informal knowledge sharing communities.

Furthermore, in this study we attempted to research a gap that we identified with respect to informal knowledge sharing mediated by the interactive systems. This phenomenon is now prevalent in companies even as it is largely under-explored in research (Davison et al., 2013). Various studies have provided rich insights into the individual influence of motivation, human characteristics, company and organizational culture. We investigate interactive-system based informal knowledge sharing by integrating the indigenous, culturally-related constructs, i.e., guanxi and communication context in this study. This study is thus among the first to provide an integrative lens to examine the competing influence of IT and social culture in informal knowledge sharing practices.

A central implication of our findings is that a theoretical study should not ignore indigenous constructs in the research context. Indigenous constructs offer an inside view of what really matters to local actors and thus play a significant role in revealing human behaviour. Merely testing a model developed elsewhere is insufficient: one should also aim to enhance these models with indigenous factors in order to achieve a stronger degree of cultural sensitivity and thus relevance to local norms. In the current study, which focuses on the Chinese context, we have incorporated guanxi, which we suggest is the most significant indigenous construct. Furthermore, by integrating the preference for a high or low communication context in the research model, we have elicited a richer picture on how culture and individual preferences influence the use of interactive tools for knowledge sharing as well as the consequent impacts on work performance. This study thus provides a foundation to include rich contextual factors in future research about knowledge management.

Our findings about the little attention paid to face maintenance in the knowledge sharing process also highlight some potentially interesting implications. Compared to the other two guanxi elements i.e., trust and reciprocal obligations, face may be less important especially when the right knowledge can be accessed at the right time. In addition, we suspect that when employees trust each other and also anticipate reciprocal obligations in a network, face is unlikely to be a concern for seeking knowledge. From a broader perspective, the inclusion of the traditional Chinese concept of face maintenance as the guanxi element in the KM context in this study can serve as a starting point for more theoretical and empirical studies.

Practically, this is among the first empirical research studies investigating how the nature of a social context may affect the choice of IT and facilitate communication seen through the lens of the CEF. This study provides clear evidence for the effectiveness of interactive systems that support knowledge sharing due to the ability of the CEF to link the technology to the social and discourse dimensions. Managers in companies can make use of this study to adapt their KS tools according to the communication context preference of the organization and employees.

Furthermore, this study suggests the importance of establishing and maintaining guanxi networks in an organization, considering the critical role of guanxi in motivating knowledge exchange for work. China has been evolving in the past few decades, but the Chinese appreciation of guanxi appears to be undiminished (Economist, 2000; Martinsons, 2008). Companies can recognise the importance of developing and maintaining good guanxi networks in the company in order to improve individual employees’ work performance. Our study suggests that this effort can also be rewarded by high network efficacy. This is especially important for knowledge-intensive companies in China.

Finally, this study has empirically verified the important role of communication context in determining work performance in organizations. Our data offered an interesting indication of the current (low) communication context preference of Chinese that is the opposite of the stereotypical perception. This finding echoes the statement by Faure and Fang (2008) that individual preference for communication context can change at different times. We suggest that the communication style and preference for communication context (i.e., low, high, or in between) is changing. At least in our study, the respondents currently prefer direct, specific and explicit communication in their work context. Managers can make use of such lowcontext communication to design information systems and also establish corporate norms and policies to facilitate such communication.

In addition to the above theoretical and practical contributions, this study has limitations that open up several research opportunities. First, this study was conducted in China. The research model should be extended to and validated in other countries. Second, we measured KSIS and the KS outcomes with subjective data. Specifically, performance data were self-reported in the survey and thus the potential bias may exist. Inclusion of objective data in the measures can address the potential common method bias issues. Third, this study and similar research can also make use of longitudinal research methods to observe and investigate how guanxi elements, KSIS and communication preference evolve and influence employees’ work performance and organizational performance over time. Finally, we acknowledge that the IPCC scale needs further work so as to improve its internal consistency.

## Conclusion

The significant influence of guanxi elements on knowledge sharing practices, identified in prior work, is supported by this study of hotel employees in China. However, the preference for a specific communication context emerges as a new and significant moderator of why knowledge sharing via interactive systems makes a difference at work. While guanxi is embedded in the social consciousness of all Chinese employees, we detect changes in the way some employees view communication, with an intriguing preference for a direct and explicit style that is more akin to low context communication. This is remarkable for its juxtaposition to the more usual view of China as a high context society. Although our findings are restricted to the Chinese context, they may be extended to cultures with similar cultural makeup. We encourage researchers to explore the issue of context more carefully especially when dealing with transitional economies shaken by major social and cultural change.
