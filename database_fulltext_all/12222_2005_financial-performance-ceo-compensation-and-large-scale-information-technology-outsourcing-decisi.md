# Financial Performance, CEO Compensation, and Large-Scale Information Technology Outsourcing Decisions

JAMES A. HALL AND STEPHEN L. LIEDTKA

JAMES A. HALL is an Associate Professor of Accounting at Lehigh University. He earned his Ph.D. from Oklahoma State University. Dr. Hall has worked in industry in the fields of systems analysis and computer auditing and has served as consultant in these areas to numerous organizations. Professor Hall has published articles in the Journal of Accounting, Auditing & Finance, Management Accounting, Journal of Computer Information Systems, Journal of Accounting Education, Review of Accounting Information Systems, and other academic journals. He is the author of Accounting Information Systems, fourth edition, and Information Systems Auditing and Assurance, both published by South-Western College Publishing. His research interests include computer controls, database design, and IT outsourcing.

STEPHEN L. LIEDTKA is an Assistant Professor of Accounting at Lehigh University. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Maryland at College Park, where he received the Allan N. Nash Outstanding Doctoral Student award. His research has appeared in the Journal of Business Finance and Accounting and Cost Management. His research interests include corporate performance measurement and executive compensation.

ABSTRACT: The inherent riskiness of large-scale information technology (IT) outsourcing led us to investigate what motivates large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. We employed fixed-effects logistical regressions to examine publicly available data for 51 firms that announced their decisions to outsource all or a large portion of their IT function during the 1993–2001 period. Results suggest that incentives created by CEO stock options and overall compensation mix significantly influence decisions to outsource. We thus provide the first evidence of a relationship between managerial self-interest and IT outsourcing. Additional results suggest that poor overall firm performance, poor cost control, and short-term cash needs also drive large-scale IT outsourcing, but provide no evidence that firms outsource IT to reduce leverage. Overall, we conclude that CEOs consider several personal and firm-level financial factors, including factors unrelated to IT cost and performance, when making largescale IT outsourcing decisions. Our conclusion has numerous implications regarding long-term IT performance, long-term firm performance, CEO contracting and financial reporting.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: CEO compensation, incentive compensation, information systems outsourcing, information technology outsourcing, stock options.

INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) OUTSOURCING is the practice of transferring responsibility to third-party vendors for the management of IT assets and staff, and for delivery of IT services such as data entry, data center operations, applications development, applications maintenance, and network management.

Since Kodak’s landmark decision in 1989 to outsource its IT function to three providers for \$250 million over 10 years, the practice has grown significantly. Indeed, the worldwide outsourcing market is expected to reach \$150 billion by the end of 2004 [61]. Examples of the many companies that have engaged in large-scale IT outsourcing include AT&T, Boeing, Continental Bank, Delta Airlines, DuPont, Monsanto, Solectron, Sun Microsystems, and Xerox.<sup>1</sup>

Successful large-scale IT outsourcing can improve firm performance in several respects. By delegating complicated, time-consuming IT tasks to vendors, IT outsourcing customers can focus completely on those core competencies that create competitive advantage and thus firm value [62, 67]. In addition, IT vendors can improve IT service by employing highly qualified, experienced staff as well as “state of the art” services and equipment [21]. Furthermore, due to vendor efficiencies and economies of scale, IT outsourcing customers can receive the benefits of improved core and IT performance while reducing IT costs. Vendors, for instance, commonly reduce customer costs by operating data centers in low-cost geographic areas [67]. Moreover, vendors can eliminate unnecessary redundancies by combining the work of many customers. Clients, for instance, may be able to transfer online operations to a vendor’s large data center at no extra cost to the vendor. [67].

By definition, large-scale IT outsourcing involves the divestiture of all or most IT assets including commodity assets and those specific to the organization’s business strategy. Commodity IT assets are easily obtained in the marketplace. When firms outsource IT assets of this class, such as network management, systems operations, and some aspects of the computing infrastructure, the associated risk is low. Should the outsourcing vendor fail to perform in accordance with the terms of the contract, the client organization can readily turn to other vendors in the marketplace for service.

In contrast, specific IT assets exist to meet the unique strategic needs of a particular organization, and thus have little value outside of their current use [98]. Firms employ specific assets for such purposes as business systems development, application maintenance, and data warehousing. Companies such as Wal-Mart, for instance, design most of their business process applications internally because of specific functionality requirements that are intrinsic to their strategic business objectives [68]. Specific assets may be tangible (computer equipment), intellectual (computer programs), or human. For example, highly skilled employees trained to use idiosyncratic software systems fit this definition.

The logic underlying large-scale IT outsourcing argues that an organization should focus exclusively on its core business competencies, while allowing the outsourcing vendor to efficiently manage the noncore IT functions. This premise, however, ignores the concept of asset specificity with respect to noncore business functions. In contrast, transaction cost economics (TCE) theory suggests that while commodity IT assets are good candidates for outsourcing, firms should retain specific IT assets in house, even though they are not core to the organization [40, 98]. Naturally, a CEO’s perception of what constitutes a commodity IT asset plays an important role in IT outsourcing decisions. Often, this comes down to a matter of definition and interpretation. For example, most CEOs would define their IT function to be a noncore commodity, unless they are in the business of developing and selling IT applications. Consequently, a belief that all IT can, and should, be managed by large service organizations tends to prevail [59]. Such misperception reflects, in part, both lack of executive education and dissemination of faulty information regarding the virtues and limitations of IT outsourcing.<sup>2</sup>

## Risks Inherent to Large-Scale IT Outsourcing

BOTH LARGE-SCALE AND SELECTIVE IT OUTSOURCING have been criticized because benefits may never materialize [59] and numerous unexpected costs can arise. IT outsourcing customers, for instance, often fail to anticipate the costs of vendor selection, contracting, and the transitioning of IT operations to the vendors [10]. The potential for unanticipated costs mushrooms once the vendor takes control of IT assets. Two years into an outsourcing contract, for instance, a U.S. tire manufacturer was spending up to 35 percent of the yearly contract amount to manage the relationship with its IT vendor and monitor the vendor’s performance [10]. Furthermore, serious concerns about the ability of information-age businesses to self-regulate regarding the protection of sensitive personal data [23, 24] are exacerbated when that information is forwarded to third-party IT vendors. Medical industry observers, for instance, express concerns regarding the ability of offshore IT vendors to comply with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) privacy rules [92]. Indeed, some states are considering legislation that prohibits the outsourcing of sensitive financial and medical data to offshore vendors [19]. Overall, consistent with the occurrence of unanticipated problems, 47 percent of 66 survey respondents reported that the costs of IT outsourcing exceeded outsourcing benefits [20].

Beyond the general IT outsourcing concerns noted above, large-scale IT outsourcing raises unique issues due to the aforementioned transfer of both commodity and specific IT assets to third-party vendors. Outsourcing highly specific assets is more costly and, hence, more risky than outsourcing commodity assets. Because specific assets are unique to the client, the vendor must recover all associated setup costs from that client rather than spreading the costs across many client organizations with similar or standardized processes. Because the vendor cannot achieve economies of scale in the transaction, it will charge the client organization a premium.

Adding to this risk, the vendor can exploit client dependency once the client has divested itself of its specific IT assets. Recognizing that the client cannot easily replace these essential strategic resources, the vendor can raise service rates to an exorbitant level. Similarly, as the client’s IT needs change or grow beyond the contract terms, the vendor can negotiate new or incremental services at a premium. This dependency may threaten the client firm’s long-term flexibility, agility, and competitiveness. Firms that retain their strategic IT capability in-house maintain an understanding of technology, internal control, and their IT future. These abilities erode over time within firms that pursue a large-scale outsourcing approach, further increasing dependency upon the vendor [97].

Large-scale IT outsourcing arrangements also make client success completely dependent upon the vendor’s ability to perform. At the time of this writing, Electronic Data Systems Corporation (EDS) presents an interesting case in point. The price of EDS stock has dropped roughly 70 percent since the end of 2001 due largely to a weak economy, the revelation of aggressive accounting practices, and the bankruptcies of two of its largest customers: WorldCom and U.S. Airways. As a direct result, EDS stockholders have filed a class-action lawsuit, and the company has had to terminate 7,000 employees. At this point, we can only speculate on the fate of EDS and the implications for its IT outsourcing clients. Certainly, however, being completely dependent on a vendor experiencing serious financial and legal problems must be disconcerting for the management of client firms, who cannot be assured that essential IT services will be performed at all, let alone be of reasonable quality.

Perhaps foremost among large-scale IT outsourcing risks is the potential loss of strategic advantage and innovative ability due to incongruence between a firm’s IT strategic planning and its business planning [33]. Previous research has reported that the IT resource must support a firm’s business strategy, and that misalignment between business strategy and IT management strategy results in decreased business performance [35]. Organizations, therefore, need to develop IT strategies concurrently with business strategies [16, 32, 35, 73]. Goldsmith, for instance, argues that firms should not separately conduct IT planning and business planning and then subsequently attempt to link the two [38]. Such separation of IT and business planning is, however, a natural consequence of large-scale IT outsourcing.

Prior research also concludes that a close working relationship between top executives and IT management is important to strategic success [91]. Responses from 213 IT managers in the financial services industry, for instance, indicate that the role played by a firm’s IT leadership needs to be closely aligned with the firm’s competitive strategy [57]. The business competence of IT executives is of particular importance in facilitating congruence between business and IT strategy [94]. CEOs thus need IT management with a strong working knowledge of their firms’ overall business strategies [91]. Large-scale IT outsourcing, however, increases the likelihood that important IT decisions will be made off-site by IT specialists who do not fully understand their clients’ businesses.

Large-scale IT outsourcing transfers an organization’s top IT management and IT infrastructure to a third-party vendor. In conveying these human and physical resources, an outsourcing client also relinquishes its strategic decision-making related to business applications and infrastructure planning and development. This may result in misalignment between a client firm’s business strategy and its outsourced IT strategy, which could negatively affect long-term firm competitiveness [4, 22, 39, 42]. Adding to this misalignment, the outsourcing vendor may gravitate to a one-sizefits-all strategy for its many competing client organizations rather than providing integrative solutions tailored to the needs of the individual user. Outsourcing decisions that lead to such predictable consequences seem at odds with research touting the importance of achieving business and IT strategy congruence.

## The Research Question

IN LARGE-SCALE IT OUTSOURCING, “vendors receive the ‘keys to the kingdom,’ actually operating, managing, and controlling [IT] functions” [59, p. 74]. Consistent with the significant risks discussed in this section, research documents that large-scale IT outsourcing decisions fail at a much higher rate than selective IT outsourcing decisions [60]. As a result of large-scale IT outsourcing failures, many organizations are incurring considerable costs to return to in-house governance of their IT functions. In other cases, outsourcing clients have become overly dependent on vendors and have no choice but to continue unsatisfactory relationships [10].

The inherent riskiness of large-scale IT outsourcing led us to investigate what motivates large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. In some cases, the decision simply may reflect a failure to understand the risks discussed in this section. Given the large body of available research on the topic, however, we do not believe this explanation alone accounts for the continued prevalence of the practice. Rather, we hypothesize that CEOs rationally engage in large-scale IT outsourcing because expected benefits exist that exceed the significant risks involved. While reduction in operating costs is the potential benefit most cited in the IT literature, we argue that CEOs also consider other personal and firm-specific financial benefits that may have little to do with IT strategy or competency. We therefore draw from accounting and finance literature to address the following research question:

Research Question: Do CEOs consider personal and firm-level financial factors, “including factors unrelated to IT cost and IT performance,” when making largescale IT outsourcing decisions?

Specifically, we examine objective, publicly available data for 51 firms that announced their decisions to outsource all or a large portion of their IT function during the 1993–2001 period. In comparing large-scale outsourcing clients to other firms in their industries, we extend prior empirical research in two ways.

First, we use published financial information to examine whether firm financial characteristics drive large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. Previous examination of published financial statements has identified common financial attributes of largescale IT outsourcing clients and thus paints a “financial picture” of those firms [85]. In contrast, our research controls for known interrelationships among financial variables and seeks to determine the nature and extent to which different financial factors actually influence subsequent IT outsourcing decisions.

Second, we introduce the concept of managerial self-interest into the IT outsourcing literature by arguing that CEO compensation contracts can influence IT outsourcing behavior. This conjecture is consistent with theory [47, 53] as well as growing empirical evidence that compensation influences other important investment decisions such as corporate acquisitions [1, 26] and sell-offs [1, 90].

## The Role of Firm Financial Characteristics

IN THIS SECTION, WE MOTIVATE HYPOTHESES relating four firm financial characteristics to large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. In doing so, we emphasize that decision makers do not necessarily have lower expenses or improved IT performance in mind when making IT sourcing choices.

## Poor Overall Firm Performance

In general, poor overall firm performance motivates organizational change [25]. This effect is amplified by the fact that shareholders can liquidate the firm if changes are unsuccessful and losses persist or increase [13, 70, 78]. Since firm value cannot drop below the fair market value of assets, poorly performing firms face limited downside risk from large-scale firm changes. Empirical evidence, for instance, suggests that poorly performing savings and loans are more likely than their competitors to expand into high-risk markets [48]. Similarly, mutual funds that earn below-median returns have been shown to increase the risk of their portfolios relative to better performing funds [17, 58]. In short, poorly performing firms have little to lose and thus are more inclined than their competition to make significant, aggressive firm changes such as large-scale IT outsourcing.

The prospect of illusory improvements in short-term reported performance might further amplify the incentive of poorly performing firms to outsource. Analytic [36] and empirical [11, 28, 52, 77] evidence suggest that CEOs of poorly performing firms manipulate or “manage” transactions and accounting reports to increase shortterm reported profits. Such manipulations may be in the interest of shareholders. Corporate loan contracts, for instance, usually contain covenants that impose strict limits on dividend payments, borrowing, investments, and other financial activities of the borrowing corporations whenever earnings-based ratios fall below specified minimums. CEOs of poorly performing firms, therefore, have incentive to manipulate earnings to ensure that the restrictions will not apply [11, 52]. As another example, poor earnings increase both the cost of borrowing and the likelihood of negative attention from third parties. Not surprisingly, therefore, empirical evidence finds that banks (whose business is especially influenced by borrowing costs and regulator scrutiny) use optimistic estimates of expected future loan losses to manipulate earnings upward in years when earnings are below the industry average [56].

Analytical research finds that CEOs of poorly performing firms will also manipulate earnings to increase personal job security [36]. Consistent with this finding, empirical evidence suggests that CEO turnover is negatively related to firm performance [96] and that CEOs of poorly performing firms attempt to increase their tenure by making accounting choices that exchange higher current reported earnings for lower future reported earnings [28, 77]. Regardless of the motive, large-scale IT outsourcing would appear to be an especially attractive vehicle for profit management, because it can increase short-term reported profitability significantly in at least two ways. First, IT vendors commonly charge less for services in the short-term as well as offer low-interest loans in return for charging considerably larger amounts for IT services provided toward the end of the contract period [54, 59]. Put differently, typical contracts allow IT outsourcing customers to trade increased current reported profitability for decreased future reported profitability. Indeed, even those contracts that charge the same fixed amount over the course of the agreement have an income-shifting effect, because the cost of providing technology services decreases over time. A typical fixed-price deal, for instance, might reduce IT costs by 20 percent during the initial contract year, and yet result in above-market prices by as early as the third contract year [61]. Three years into a contract, for instance, a U.S. metals manufacturer was paying \$100 per processed form, even though the vendor was charging \$50 per form to other customers [61].

Second, prior empirical research suggests that poorly performing firms use asset sales to increase reported earnings [11]. Applied to the current analysis, sale of an IT department to an outsourcing vendor generally results in significant reported profits, since the sales price usually exceeds the accounting book values for the relevant IT assets (human and machine). Specifically, accounting conventions preclude assigning any book value to human assets, accumulated IT expertise, or the synergies created when firms organize assets for a specific purpose. Further, to the extent that physical (machine) assets appear on the accounting books, they often are understated, since firms must make downward adjustments to book values for depreciation and impairment of assets, but cannot make upward adjustments for inflation or asset appreciation. Adding to the incentive to outsource IT, accounting conventions allow firms to hide gains from the sale of IT assets in operating income, which investors consider to be the foundation for predicting future firm performance [9]. This is in contrast to gains from the sale of firm subsidiaries or major operating divisions, which are reported separately as nonoperating “gains from discontinued operations.”

The preceding discussion leads to the following hypothesis:

H1: Poor overall firm performance drives large-scale IT outsourcing decisions.

## High Operating Expenses

A successful IT function contributes significantly to the operating efficiency of a firm, facilitating and integrating essential activities such as materials purchasing, manufacturing, inventory control, sales, customer service, and logistics. Through IT, companies can streamline delivery processes and reduce both administrative and product costs, while improving customer service. As one of many examples, DaimlerChrysler Corporation’s Mopar Parts Group manages more than 280,000 original equipment service parts, and processes more than 200,000 dealer orders per day. The successful implementation of SeeChain<sup>©</sup> applications improved Mopar’s inventory record accuracy 43 percent within six months, significantly improving Mopar’s production, warehousing, and delivery efficiency [99]. Not surprisingly, therefore, empirical research finds that firms identified as IT leaders have significantly lower ratios of total operating expenses to sales, relative to their industry competitors [14, 81].

Firms with inefficient IT functions, therefore, might outsource IT in the hope that total operating costs will decrease. Indeed, proponents of outsourcing claim that firms average a 9 percent overall reduction in costs by outsourcing IT and other functions [34]. In theory, cost savings from IT outsourcing result from at least two potential improvements to the firm. First, judicious outsourcing of supporting functions can free firm resources to perform core operating functions more effectively [62, 67]. Second, the vendor’s experience and economies of scale can lead to reduced IT costs and more effective IT performance.

Using Porter’s “value chain” approach, firms should not exhaust managerial resources on IT activities that do not contribute significantly to firm profitability [72]. Rather, firm strategy and allocation of resources should center on those activities that create competitive advantage, and thus value for customers. Focus on competitive advantage implies that firms should increasingly consider IT outsourcing to the extent that they do not have unique IT competencies. At the extreme, those most likely to outsource should be those whose lack of IT competencies results in excessive ITrelated costs, and thus the need to use profitable activities to subsidize the IT function.<sup>3</sup>

Literature on value chains also applies to IT vendors who, through economies of scale and strategic focus, may be able to produce IT services more cost effectively than their clients [30]. Intuitively, the potential for operating expense reduction through IT outsourcing should increase as the client’s own ability to successfully manage IT declines. Put differently, firms that cannot efficiently provide “state of the art” IT services internally should be the most willing to purchase such services from outside sources, all else equal.

Although academic literature has yet to conclude on the overall effect of IT outsourcing, research does make it clear that the potential for expense reduction through IT outsourcing is contingent upon numerous firm- and function-specific factors [74]. Survey evidence, for instance, suggests that the need for custom IT application development is negatively associated with customer satisfaction from IT outsourcing [40]. Even poor candidates for IT outsourcing, however, might be swayed by media hype and unrealistic expectations of cost reduction [3, 59]. As one executive observed, “The belief is, if you give the problem away, the third party will be able to magically make it disappear” [61, p. 1]. Furthermore, we argue that CEOs of firms that outsource IT can benefit even if operating expenses are expected to persist or increase, since the vendor can be blamed for any future failures.

Although executives cite cost reduction as the top motivation for IT outsourcing [60], empirical research has yet to verify that high firm expenses precede large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. This leads to our second hypothesis:

H2: Firm operating expenses are positively associated with subsequent largescale IT outsourcing decisions.

## Short-Term Cash Needs

As noted earlier, IT outsourcing generally includes the sale of equipment, facilities, and personnel to the vendor. CEOs therefore can use IT outsourcing as a means for generating significant amounts of cash for their businesses [67, 89]. Prior research, for instance, documents a case in which a vendor paid \$300 million for a client’s IT assets [61].

CEOs of firms with low reserves of cash and cash equivalents (hereafter “cash”) have clear incentives to engage in transactions that increase cash, such as IT outsourcing.<sup>4</sup> A firm simply cannot survive without sufficient cash to pay employees and vendors. Further, firms optimally should maintain enough cash in reserve to withstand the costs of unexpected events, such as labor and production problems. Without adequate cash holdings, firms risk disruptions in production, lost goodwill with customers and suppliers, and qualified audit reports. Although the use of IT outsourcing to satisfy cash needs might be shortsighted, vendors recognize that current needs often dominate long-term considerations and thus appear to use short-term financial incentives to sweeten outsourcing deals [59].

By definition, firms with adequate cash reserves do not have the same pressing need to generate cash. Indeed, prior literature argues that high cash levels actually deter firms from liquidating assets—for at least three reasons. First, there is an opportunity cost to unnecessarily holding excess cash rather than investing in productive assets [79]. Second, firms seeking to eliminate idle cash may waste the funds on lowvalue projects when better projects are not available. Empirical evidence, for instance, shows that high-cash firms make less profitable corporate acquisitions than their peers [31, 46]. Finally, firms with excess cash are attractive takeover targets, since acquiring firms can profit by employing the available cash more efficiently. Capital markets research, for example, demonstrates that average stock returns from the takeover of high-cash targets exceed average returns from other acquisitions [86]. To maintain control of their firms, therefore, CEOs appear to avoid investment choices that exacerbate excess cash problems [37].

Our discussion suggests that the appeal of a cash infusion from IT outsourcing should vary inversely with a firm’s cash holdings. This leads to our third hypothesis:

H3: Firm cash levels are negatively associated with subsequent large-scale IT outsourcing decisions.

## High Financial Leverage

As noted in the previous section, the sale of IT assets can generate significant amounts of cash. Among other options, firms can use such cash infusions to reduce outstanding debt. Indeed, annual reports have cited debt reduction as a primary IT outsourcing motivation [85].

It is noteworthy that debt reduction via IT outsourcing is somewhat illusory, since the IT outsourcing customer must lease back the IT assets from the vendor and will essentially pay back the cash infusion with interest in the form of high future lease and service payments [61]. For financial reporting purposes, however, the difference between a debt and a lease is significant. Whereas firms must include bank loans and other debt as liabilities on balance sheets, firms with operating leases for IT assets do not report any related liability until the vendors earn their revenues by performing services over the contract period. In this sense, large-scale IT outsourcing can be viewed as a form of “off-balance-sheet financing,” which is the use of leases to avoid owning, and thus avoid reporting, essential assets and the debt associated with those assets. Recently, accounting scandals have given great notoriety to such practices. The collapse of Enron, for instance, led to the discovery of several business relationships that removed debt from Enron’s balance sheets, but appeared to have had no legitimate business purpose.

Prior research suggests that firms actively use investment choices to bring reported debt and equity into balance [51, 65]. Specifically, high-leverage firms can use offbalance-sheet financing to appear less risky and thus reduce future financing costs [87]. Conversely, low-debt firms have an incentive to increase reported leverage; since low-debt firms may appear to be inefficiently relying too heavily on expensive, nontax deductible equity financing. To the extent that IT outsourcing contracts remove debt from balance sheets, therefore, we argue that the practice should be most attractive to firms with high reported debt.

Consistent with the above discussion, prior research documents a positive (bivariate) relationship between leverage and large-scale IT outsourcing [85]. It is not clear, however, whether leverage itself drives outsourcing decisions or if both outsourcing and leverage are driven by additional common factors. In particular, prior research demonstrates that both leverage [51] and large-scale IT outsourcing [85] are positively associated with firm size. Further, there is a well-established inverse relationship between leverage and accounting returns [51, 93]. As noted earlier, we conjecture that large-scale IT outsourcing is also inversely related to accounting returns. We therefore complement the prior research by including control variables and testing for a direct association between leverage and subsequent large-scale IT outsourcing.

H4: Financial leverage is positively associated with subsequent large-scale IT outsourcing decisions.

## The Role of CEO Compensation

AVERAGE CEO COMPENSATION HAS BEEN HIGH and steadily increasing over the period in which large-scale IT outsourcing has come into prominence. During the 1992– 2000 period, for instance, the average value of the various components of CEO compensation grew from a total of \$1,689,000 to \$8,466,000 for firms in the S&P 500, S&P MidCap 400, and S&P SmallCap 600 indices [8]. CEO compensation plans also tend to be quite elaborate, providing a variety of incentives such as annual bonuses, stock options, stock grants, and long-term incentive payouts.

Principal-agent theory explains the existence of large, elaborate CEO compensation contracts and underscores the potential for compensation to influence CEO decisions. The theory begins with the assumptions that CEOs (agents) are effort averse and seek to maximize personal utility rather than that of their firms’ shareholders (principals). To prevent CEOs from shirking or acting wastefully (e.g., overconsuming perquisites), shareholders can use compensation incentives that motivate CEOs to work harder and more responsibly than they would otherwise by tying CEO wealth to firm performance or shareholder wealth [53].<sup>5</sup>

Consistent with the theory, empirical research finds strong evidence that CEO stock options and overall compensation balance drive a variety of accounting [49] and finance [26, 90] choices, as discussed in the following subsections. Further, as one should expect in equilibrium, the average relationship between compensation and CEO performance appears positive [15].

Not all CEO activities motivated by incentive compensation are in a firm’s best interests, however. Noting that CEOs have private information and that perfect monitoring of CEO decisions is prohibitively costly (if not impossible), the principal-agent literature also argues that firms must endure some residual risk that CEOs will act opportunistically [53]. As discussed in the following subsections, for instance, CEOs can make both real and accounting decisions that increase their personal welfare despite the fact that the decisions are not in their firms’ best interests. Given the dramatic increase in CEO compensation, the potential rewards of opportunism have increased as well. Recent accounting scandals have made the general public aware of the potential negative effect of compensation contracts on CEO behavior. Many believe, for instance, that “irresistible” compensation incentives contributed to the poor business decisions and ethical lapses that led to the collapse of Enron, WorldCom, and Tyco [76].

Despite the importance of these insights, research has yet to consider the role of CEO compensation in large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. Such research is essential, however, for several reasons. First, evidence of a role for managerial self-interest in IT outsourcing decisions refines our understanding of the outsourcing decision process. Indeed, we contend that many large-scale IT outsourcing decisions do not make sense unless one considers the self-interest of CEOs. Second, evidence of a role for managerial self-interest in IT outsourcing decisions underscores the importance of proper CEO contracting and highlights the importance of oversight of IT outsourcing decisions by boards of directors, auditors and regulators. Finally, identification of relationships between compensation variables and IT outsourcing decisions is important for econometric reasons, since such factors would otherwise be potentially important omitted correlated variables in both the current and any future analyses. For all these reasons, we now summarize key aspects of the relevant prior research relating compensation to firm decisions and apply that research to the context of largescale IT outsourcing.

## CEO Stock Options

Stock options have become the largest component of compensation for U.S. CEOs [43]. Option grants give CEOs and other executives the right to purchase shares of their firms’ stock at a fixed price over a fixed time period. At the time of issuance, the fixed or “exercise” price of options is greater than or equal to the current market price. If the market price rises above the exercise price before the options expire, the increase in executive wealth can be quite dramatic. If share prices remain stable or fall, executive wealth is unaffected, since the options need not be exercised.

In general, decision makers with a potential for gain but no potential for loss exhibit a preference for variance-increasing decisions [55, 80, 82]. Applied to the compensation setting, the asymmetric payoff of options leads to the prediction that large option grants drive CEOs to make aggressive changes to their firms [47, 83]. Alternatively, “in the absence of stock options, managers may be overly concerned with job security” and thus prefer firm stability [29, p. 618]. Prior empirical research, for instance, shows that stock option grants are directly associated with subsequent firm decisions to acquire unstable, high-growth target firms as measured by the market-to-book ratios of the targets [26].

Changes in any important function will destabilize an organization [44, 45]. This observation holds especially true for large-scale IT outsourcing. Unlike other functions that have distinct and clearly identifiable boundaries, IT permeates an organization. In fact, organizational structure and certain elements of IT often are indistinguishable. Consequently, large-scale IT outsourcing induces change that pervades the firm. Consistent with the prior research noted above, we therefore conjecture that option-based compensation increases CEO willingness to endorse large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. Put differently, because option pay does not penalize CEO failures, it should motivate CEOs to chase large gains through far-reaching changes to the firm such as large-scale IT outsourcing, even if such gains are not assured or perhaps not even likely [80].

H5: CEO stock option grants are positively associated with subsequent largescale IT outsourcing decisions.

## (Nonoption) Balance Between Annual and Long-Term Compensation

CEO contracts typically provide for a mix of both annual and long-term compensation. As discussed below, prior research has shown the balance between annual and long-term pay to be a critical determinant of CEO choices, since a balance toward the former encourages aggressive behavior, whereas a balance toward the latter (except for options) encourages risk aversion.

Long-term compensation incentives reward performance over periods longer than one year (commonly three to five years, but sometimes longer), and thus are intended to focus CEOs and other executives on the long-term health of the firm. Except for stock options, long-term compensation generally imposes downside risk upon CEOs. As the risk exposure from long-term compensation increases, theory suggests that, all else equal, CEOs will be increasingly likely to avoid changes in firm structure [41, 55]. Restricted stock grants, for instance, are the primary nonoption form of longterm compensation. Under a restricted stock plan, the CEO and other executives receive shares of stock, but cannot sell those shares until the restriction period has expired. Assuming firm stock has a positive value, restricted stock grants increase the amount CEOs stand to lose should their firms perform poorly over the restriction period. This, in turn, exacerbates CEO risk aversion [18, 80].

As an alternative to restricted stock grants, many firms award long-term performance bonuses. While long-term bonus schemes vary significantly between firms, plans typically provide at least some reward when performance is average, and thus CEO wealth is negatively affected if changes to the firm lead to below-average performance [2, 7,

63]. As with restricted stock grants, this potential for wealth loss increases the likelihood that CEOs will avoid significant changes to their firms.

As the percentage of compensation tied to long-term incentives decreases in favor of increased annual compensation, the above arguments suggest that CEOs will be relatively less concerned about long-term firm health. Indeed, in the absence of sufficient long-term incentives, analytic research predicts that CEOs may focus excessively on annual reported profitability in order to enhance their short-term personal reputations, maximize salary increases, and garner larger annual bonuses [71]. As noted earlier, CEOs can use IT outsourcing to temporarily lower reported expenses as well as report gains from the sale of IT assets. CEOs primarily concerned with annual compensation should find such an option especially attractive. CEOs with primarily long-term incentives, however, may avoid such outsourcing decisions to the extent that they may have negative long-term ramifications.

Consistent with our arguments in this section, a growing body of empirical evidence finds that a firm’s choice between annual and long-term compensation affects important CEO decisions. Research suggests, for instance, that annual incentives can lead to accounting choices that accelerate income and postpone expenses [49]. Such choices can include the selection of accounting methods, such as the method for depreciating fixed assets, as well as the estimation of certain expenses, such as a firm’s provision for uncollectible debts. Our conjecture is also consistent with evidence that the stock market reacts more positively to sell-offs of operating units and subsidiaries when firms have long-term performance plan incentives in place [90]. Financial markets thus appear to give weight to the possibility that CEOs without long-term incentives make sell-off choices that effectively trade real or superficial short-term gains for decreased long-term firm health.

Based upon the above discussion, we posit that large-scale IT outsourcing appeals most to CEOs who have a high proportion of annual to total compensation, and least to CEOs with a high proportion of long-term to total compensation [84]. In fact, large-scale IT outsourcing might be preferable to accounting manipulations or selloffs for purposes of managing annual profits, since accounting manipulations are subject to auditor oversight [49], and sell-offs generally require the approval of the shareholders of both the selling and acquiring firms. Hence, CEOs may view IT outsourcing as less restricted than these other means for modifying financial statements and firm investment structures to suit personal preferences. Similar to IT outsourcing, for instance, CEOs have great discretion over R&D decisions, which have both real and financial reporting effects on firms. Consistent with our arguments, prior research finds that CEOs with short-term incentives spend less on R&D to reduce reported expenses, whereas long-term incentives mitigate this effect [27]. The leads to our final hypothesis:

H6: Holding option grants as a constant, higher proportions of annual (longterm) CEO compensation to total CEO compensation are associated with increased (reduced) incidence of large-scale IT outsourcing.

## Research Design

## Large-Scale IT Outsourcing Customers

WE LOCATED 172 ANNOUNCEMENTS of IT outsourcing contracts using Lexis/Nexis, searching news wires for the key words “information technology or information systems” and “outsourcing” for the 1993–2001 period. This sample period coincides with explosive growth in both option-based executive pay [26] and IT outsourcing events.

We identified large-scale (defined as total or near-total) IT outsourcing activities by reviewing announcements for statements such as “Client X will outsource all business systems” or “will outsource comprehensive project management services to support long-term IT strategy” or “will transfer all IT employees to the vendor.” Due to an apparent bias in the press toward only reporting major outsourcing contracts, all 172 incidents qualified as large scale. While the identified large-scale IT outsourcing customers differ in the exact nature of their outsourced IT assets, the resulting sample firms are similar in that they all have signed extremely large, comprehensive contracts that include the transfer of both specific and commodity IT assets to vendors.

We eliminated outsourcing incidents from analysis if either (1) the complete set of necessary firm financial and compensation data was not available, or (2) a new CEO took office during the year of the outsourcing announcement, confounding our ability to test for the effects of CEO compensation on the outsourcing decision. This process yielded 51 (29.7 percent of total) large-scale IT outsourcing customers for analysis.<sup>6</sup>

## Industry Control Groups

A strong, documented relationship exists between industry and IT outsourcing behavior [5, 66]. Consequently, we analyze characteristics of each large-scale outsourcing customer relative to the group of nonoutsourcing firms having the same three-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code at time t – 1 (year prior to outsourcing announcement).<sup>7</sup> Firms that announced IT outsourcing decisions at any time were excluded from the industry control groups. On average, there were 24.73 nonoutsourcing industry members per outsourcing firm for a total of $5 1 * 2 4 . 7 3 = 1 , 2 6 1$ control firms.<sup>8</sup>

## Independent Variables

Overall firm performance is measured as operating income over average assets $( P R O F I T S _ { i , t - 1 } )$ . Firm operating expense levels are measured as cost of goods sold plus selling, general, and administrative expenses divided by net sales $( E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - 1 } )$ [14, 81]. Short-term cash availability is measured as cash and cash equivalents over net sales $( C A S H _ { i , t - 1 } ) .$ . Leverage is measured as total liabilities to equity $( L E V E R A G E _ { i , t - 1 } )$ We obtained data for all of these variables from Standard and Poor’s Research Insight database.

We operationalized the incentive created by CEO stock options as the Black-Scholes value of all new options awarded to a firm’s CEO at time t – 1 divided by total CEO compensation $( O P T I O N S _ { i , t - 1 } ) . ^ { 9 }$ We measure the percentage of total compensation coming from annual pay or, equivalently, the percentage of total compensation not coming from long-term compensation as $A N N P A Y \% _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ which equals salary, bonus, and other annual compensation divided by total compensation (excluding option grants).<sup>10</sup> We obtained data for our compensation variables from Standard and Poor’s ExecuComp database.<sup>11</sup>

Finally, we use the natural log of market capitalization at time $t - 1 ( F I R M \_ S I Z E _ { i , t - 1 } )$ to control for firm size. Previous researchers documented a strong, positive relationship between large-scale IT outsourcing and firm size, and noted that their result might reflect a bias in the business press toward reporting IT outsourcing announcements of larger firms [85].

## Results

RESULTS PRESENTED IN THIS SECTION mirror our contention that CEOs consider several financial factors, including factors unrelated to IT cost and IT performance, when endorsing IT outsourcing decisions. Recognition of these factors enriches our current understanding of IT outsourcing and allows us to identify areas of concern for practitioners and future researchers.

## Financial Characteristics

Table 2 reports results from the maximum-likelihood estimation of Equation (1) for our sample of 51 outsourcing and 1,261 nonoutsourcing firms, as well as results from four alternate tests. Results from tests of the main model and two reduced models (Model 2 and Model 3) support three of our four hypotheses regarding the financial characteristics of large-scale IT outsourcing customers (Table 2).<sup>14</sup> First, we find the coefficient on $P R O F I T S _ { i , t - 1 }$ to be significant and negative as predicted.<sup>15</sup> This result suggests that poor firm performance motivates CEOs to reconsider firm structure in general, and IT governance in particular. Put negatively, this finding corresponds with criticism that large-scale IT outsourcing is a “game for losers” in which poorly performing firms sell off critical IT functions and accumulated knowledge to streamline operations [88]. To the extent that poorly performing firms face limited downside risk and need to stimulate a turnaround, however, dramatic changes such as large-scale IT outsourcing might be a logical final option.

Second, we find that $E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - 1 }$ is positively associated with large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. Our comparatively high coefficient estimate for $E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - 1 }$ corresponds with survey data, which cites cost reduction as the top motivation for IT outsourcing [60].

Third, we find the coefficient on $C A S H _ { i , t - 1 }$ to be negative and significant. Further, we provide evidence that short-term cash needs are a relatively important determinant of IT outsourcing, as $C A S H _ { i , t - 1 }$ has the largest standardized coefficient in each of our regressions. The potential for a sizeable cash infusion, therefore, appears to entice firms into surrendering IT governance to third-party suppliers.

Rather, the omission of correlated variables appears to drive the bivariate relationship. ## CEO Compensation

Consistent with our hypotheses, CEO compensation appears to play a significant role in large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. The significant, positive coefficient on $O P T I O N S _ { i , t - 1 }$ reflects theory that, all else equal, stock option grants increase CEO willingness to make significant changes to firm structure. Indeed, an important function of stock options is to reduce the likelihood that CEOs will avoid beneficial changes to their firms by increasing the convexity of the relationship between wealth and performance [83]. Not all decisions motivated by options, however, are desirable from the shareholders’ standpoint. Rather, high proportions of option-based compensation can create an incentive for CEOs to engage in low-value activities to destabilize the firm [47].

The significant, positive coefficient on $A N N P A Y _ { i , t - 1 }$ mirrors our conjecture that the balance between annual and long-term compensation impacts CEO decision horizons. CEOs with a high proportion of long-term to total incentives appear to avoid changes induced by large-scale IT outsourcing to reduce their risk of lost future compensation. As firms substitute annual incentives for long-term incentives, CEOs seem to become increasingly willing to use IT outsourcing in hopes of generating real or illusory improvements in annual reported profitability.

## Sensitivity Analysis: Administrative Versus

## Product-Related Costs

Our use of total operating expenses in calculating $E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - 1 }$ reflects the argument that firms outsource IT to reduce both administrative and product-related costs. Since the motivation for IT outsourcing may be primarily for one purpose or the other, we disaggregated operating expenses into selling, general, and administrative expenses $( A D M I N E X P _ { i , t - 1 } )$ and cost of goods sold $( C G S _ { i , t - 1 } )$ to determine the relative influence of each on the decision to outsource. This procedure resulted in the loss of 13 of our 51 large-scale IT outsourcing firms (25.5 percent) and 332 of our 1,261 matching firms (26.3 percent), since those firms elected not to separately report administrative and product-related costs in their annual income statements.

Whereas the coefficient on $E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - 1 }$ remains significant using the reduced data set (Model 4), separate examination of $A D M I N E X P _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $C G S _ { i , t - }$ <sub>1</sub> (Model 5) provides no convincing evidence that either is primarily responsible for the $E X P E N S E S _ { i , t - }$ <sub>1</sub> result. We caution against placing much reliance on this finding, however, as the lack of significant results may simply reflect noise generated by inconsistencies among firms in the treatment of operating expenses [95]. Firms, for instance, have great flexibility in determining whether to assign administrative IT costs to products or to selling, general, and administrative expenses. We note that $A D M I N E X P _ { i , t - }$ 1 and $C G S _ { i , t - 1 }$ have a highly significant, negative correlation of –0.463 in our subsample, consistent with the notion that reporting less expense in one category results in reporting more in the other.<sup>16</sup>

## Discussion

IN SUMMARY, OUR RESULTS SUGGEST that CEOs make irreversible large-scale IT outsourcing decisions due to factors that include firm financial desperation, firm cash needs, and the desire to maximize personal compensation. These results echo concerns raised by prior research, which surmises that financial arrangements might be more relevant to IT outsourcing clients than actual IT management issues [59].

Due to the size and nature of the transactions, we argue that large-scale IT outsourcing decisions should be subject to careful internal audit and board-of-directors oversight. Effective oversight, in turn, requires insight into two key IT outsourcing issues. First, auditors and board members must understand the risks associated with outsourcing specific assets, as discussed earlier this paper. Second, auditors, board members, and regulators must recognize that CEO interests may diverge from the interests of other stakeholders in the context of IT outsourcing decisions.

## CEO and Stakeholder Interests

Despite the significant risks, the existence of large-scale IT outsourcing demonstrates that many firms outsource specific IT assets and functions. An important insight from our research is that otherwise illogical IT outsourcing decisions make sense when seen from the standpoint of CEOs, who appear to consider factors beyond IT cost and IT performance when making IT outsourcing choices. CEOs, for instance, might accept potential deterioration in IT performance if benefits to the firm such as a needed cash infusion outweigh any expected costs. CEOs, auditors and board members must fully understand, however, the extent to which the enticing short-run financial benefits of large-scale IT outsourcing might come at the expense of long-term competitive advantage. Similarly, outside stakeholders should recognize the risks involved and view large-scale IT outsourcing announcements as a signal of potential firm financial problems.

An additional insight from our research is that CEO and shareholder interests might diverge in regard to IT outsourcing decisions. Boards, therefore, must be aware of the potential for dysfunctional behavior and ensure that compensation incentives do not drive management to sacrifice strategic advantage, operational independence, and information security through IT outsourcing. A large body of agency research suggests that both careful design of compensation contracts and close monitoring of decisions might be necessary to reduce the risk of opportunism to an acceptable level [53].

Finally, our research underscores the fact that IT outsourcing can benefit CEOs, and often their firms, by generating illusory improvements in reported expenses and profits. Board members, auditors, and regulators, therefore, should question the motivation behind large-scale IT outsourcing decisions and must be resolute in their duty to ensure that all stakeholders receive information that completely and accurately portrays the economic substance of IT outsourcing transactions. Further, financial reports should clearly state whether IT outsourcing reflects or creates any internal concern that the firm might not continue as viable entity.

## Directions for Future Research

RESULTS PRESENTED IN THIS PAPER SUGGEST that poor overall firm performance, poor cost control, and short-term cash needs drive large-scale IT outsourcing decisions, but provide no evidence that firms outsource IT to reduce leverage. Moreover, results suggest that incentives created by both CEO stock options and nonoption compensation significantly influence large-scale IT outsourcing decisions. These findings sug gest important areas for future research.

One important implication of our results, for instance, is that CEOs might rationally make IT outsourcing decisions that have a negative effect on IT performance if personal or firm-level financial benefits outweigh any expected costs. Future research can corroborate this inference by using either financial market or survey-based methods to identify successful and unsuccessful IT outsourcing decisions. To the extent that factors other than IT performance appear relevant to the outsourcing decision, the likelihood of poor post-outsourcing IT performance should increase.

Given the recent spate of major accounting scandals, we also believe our results warrant close examination of existing IT outsourcing arrangements to determine whether published financial statements adequately portray the economic substance of the outsourcing transactions. In doing so, future research can identify patterns among those firms whose financial statements are the least informative. We would predict, for instance, that CEOs with incentive to seek illusory improvements in reported performance are also the least likely to completely report details of their IT outsourcing transactions.
