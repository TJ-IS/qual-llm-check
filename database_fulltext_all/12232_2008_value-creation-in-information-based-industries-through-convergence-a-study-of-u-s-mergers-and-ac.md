# Value creation in information-based industries through convergence: A study of U.S. mergers and acquisitions between 1993 and 2005

Louis Rhe´aume <sup>a,</sup>\*, Harjeet S. Bhabra <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Te´le´-Universite´, University of Quebec at Montreal (UQAM), Montreal, Canada

<sup>b</sup> John Molson School of Business at Concordia University, Montreal, Canada

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 5 December 2005 Received in revised form 18 April 2007 Accepted 9 March 2008 Available online 29 May 2008

Keywords: Mergers Acquisitions Information industry Convergence Technology acquisitions Event study

## A B S T R A C T

According to the International Engineering Consortium (IEC), the realignment and consolidations currently underway in information-based industries is likely to result in the five traditional information industries (photography, publishing, computing, telecommunications and entertainment) converging into three new sectors: information content providers, information highways, and information appliances. We investigated the impact of mergers and acquisitions on value creation both within and across these sectors by estimating the stock market’s response to acquiring firms around the announcement date of their merger. Using data on 2421 mergers and acquisitions between 1993 and 2005 and employing the well-established event study methodology, we found that most acquirers received a target within the same sector or industry and that the related acquisition strategies brought synergistic gains resulting in positive wealth change for the acquiring firm’s shareholders. The few firms that pursued an unrelated diversification strategy that lowered operating risk experienced no significant change in shareholder wealth. Our results contrasted with the zero or negative wealth change found for acquiring firms in general (as reported in the finance literature) but were consistent with the positive wealth change for acquirers in the telecommunications industry found in recent studies.

## 1. Introduction

Mergers and acquisitions (M&As) are an important mechanism for external growth. As Jensen [16] notes, mergers and acquisition are ‘‘a response to new technologies or market conditions which require strategic change in a company’s direction or use of resources.’’ They serve an important role in the efficient redeployment of capital as poorly performing enterprises are often removed in the process. An extensive body of research shows that target firm shareholders are the winners while the acquiring firm shareholders gain little or nothing [2,15]. Acquiring firms have also tended to perform poorly in the years following the acquisition compared to their non-acquiring counterparts [20]. Both the short- and long-term performance for the acquiring firm was affected by the prevailing market conditions at the time of the merger and whether the acquiring firm was an early-mover. Market power, synergy, managerial hubris, strategic realignment, among others, were a few of the commonly cited reasons why firms pursued an acquisition strategy. The 1990s represented the fourth important takeover wave of the 20th century and technology was a major factor.

This sector has grown at a phenomenal rate. Its importance stems from its integration with almost every other sector of the economy. Information-based industries today constitute a significant part of all major economies of the world. However, rapid technological innovation, many small new players, easy access to and use of new technologies, intense competition, changing regulations, and shifting standards have created uncertainty among consumers, investors and other stakeholders.

Wilcox et al. [31] argued that, ‘‘[T]he pervasive and ubiquity of the Internet and continued development of common standards provides tremendous opportunities to smaller entrepreneurial firms to contribute value.’’ In addition, firms that previously belonged to distinct industries have become competitors or partners.

Grover and Vaswani [13] identified increased market access, economies of scale and control of key technologies as primary motives behind the pace of consolidation currently underway in the telecommunications industry. The International Engineering Consortium (IEC) [14] stated that the traditional informationbased industries, organized by form, were realigning along sectors defined by function. This realignment, and the consolidation resulting from it, should result in synergistic gains through better utilization of resources and strengths, thereby creating more value for consumers, shareholders, and other stakeholders. It predicted that the realignment of firms in the five traditional information industries (photography, publishing, computing, telecommunications and entertainment) would result in the creation of three new information sectors (information content providers, information appliances and information highways). The mergers of AOL and Time Warne, Seagram and Vivendi, and Ebay and Skype were examples of this trend. For example, the merger of AOL–Time Warner was from two sectors: the information highway and the information content, respectively. Bore´s et al. [4] suggested that such mergers were important, because owning the rights to content could affect the success of a firm, since the delivery technologies would be more competitive and eliminate substitutes.

The primary focus of our study was to investigate the impact of M&As on firm value in the three emerging IEC defined information sectors. We did this by examining the impact of M&A decisions on the wealth of the acquiring firm shareholders. We analyzed the M&As to determine which types of strategies were more appropriate and how the market valued such decisions.

Several recent studies have focused on acquisitions in technology sectors. Using a sample of M&As from the telephone and cable industries, Li [19] found that firms with slower revenue growth were more likely to diversify. Cloodt et al. [8] studied the impact of M&As on the performance of acquiring firms innovation, concluding that they should acquire targets that were neither too similar nor unrelated to their knowledge base. Kohers and Kohers [17] found that acquiring firms in the technology sector underperformed control firms based on industry membership, size, and book-to-market ratio of equity. Several researchers have found that successful integration is the key to post-merger success [12,26,28]. The studies by Wilcox et al. and Grover and Vaswani addressed issues similar to ours; they examined the short-run stock market response to acquiring firm shareholders and found that M&As involving narrow diversification created more value than those with broad diversification. The results were stronger for smaller firms. However, they did not provide any evidence on the effect of the type of alliances on firm value, even though their valuation impact varied. Furthermore, the sample sizes in these studies was small.

We build on existing evidence by having further investigated the valuation impact of M&As both within and across the three new information sectors and the five traditional information industries using a sample of 2421 acquirers during the period 1993–2005. We examined the stock returns around the announcement date for the shareholders of the firms in the information sectors defined by IEC.

Specifically, we addressed the following questions: (1) Do acquiring firms create more value for their shareholders if they acquire a target firm in the same IEC defined information sector rather than a different sector? (2) What impact does the similarity of the firms have, if the M&A is within the same IEC information sector? (3) Does the valuation impact of M&As in the information sectors vary across the three sectors?

While our focus was on the new IEC information sectors, we also provided evidence on M&As for the five traditional information industries.

## 2. The new paradigm in information industries according to IEC

The information communication industries have an enormous impact on the economy. They have rapidly evolved; for example, from narrow band information transmission to broadband communications. According to IEC, traditional information industries that are clustered around image, text, voice, data and audio and visual devices will no longer exist in their present form; they will be reorganized by digitized content, multimedia devices, and convergent networks. ## 3. Extant work and hypothesis development

Despite the empirical evidence on M&As in general, very little is known on how they have performed in the information-based industries. Most M&As result in some degree of diversification. In our study, we considered an M&A deal to be one of related diversification if the two firms belonged to the same information sector and unrelated if the two firms belonged to two different sectors. Researchers have carefully examined why firms diversify [25], the mode of diversification [18] and the choice of direction of diversification. Park [23] examined the impact of industry and firm profitability, and found that related acquirers belonged to more profitable industries, and were more profitable than unrelated acquirers. Wade and Gravill [30] found that related subsidiaries outperformed unrelated subsidiaries. Overall, the evidence on related versus unrelated diversification is mixed.

A new qualitative approach to identifying related and unrelated diversification was proposed by Rumelt [27], based on a firm’s specialization ratio (revenues of the dominant business), related ratio (revenues from the single largest business among related business) and vertical ratio (revenues from products along the firm’s vertically integrated businesses). Rumelt found that related diversified firms performed better than other firms. Christensen and Montgomery [7] suggested that firms operating in markets that constrained their growth or profitability were more likely to pursue unrelated diversification strategies. Busija et al. [6] showed that firms that use a combination of internal development and external acquisitions tended to engage in related diversification compared to firms that adopted a growth strategy based on acquisitions that favored unrelated diversification. While technology related diversification could be valuable as it results in economies of scope, Berger and Ofek [3] found that diversified firms in general suffered a 13–15% loss in value from diversification. The fact that the value loss was smaller when segments of the diversified firm were in the same two-digit SIC code suggested that closely related diversification was less value destructive than unrelated diversification. Using return on assets as a performance measure, Bettis [5] found that related diversified firms outperformed unrelated diversified firms by about 1–3 percentage points. These findings suggested that while diversification itself results in a loss in firm value; firms adopting an unrelated diversification strategy were likely to be viewed as ‘‘spreading their resources thin’’ and thus suffering a value loss over those who focused on their core competencies.

However, all unrelated diversifications are not necessarily value destroying. Michel and Shaked [22] concluded that firms diversifying into unrelated areas ‘‘have been able to generate superior performance . . .’’ According to these authors, related diversification was likely to yield potential synergy, but had little reduction in total risk: the earnings streams for the two firms were likely to be highly correlated. Galbraith et al. [11] presented empirical evidence suggesting that unrelated diversification could provide a hedging strategy against technological surprises in intensive research and development industries with high degrees of uncertainty.

The differences in the two sets of findings for related diversification suggested that benefit to a firm by pursuing a diversification strategy appeared to be influenced by the uncertainty of its industry. In an information-based industry firms are vulnerable to technological uncertainty because of the rapid rate of technological obsolesce, low barriers to entry, competitive environment, and shifting standards and regulations.

We determined the valuation effect of M&As in the information-based industries by estimating the change in market value of the bidding firm’s equity around the time of the announcement of a merger or acquisition. We considered a merger or acquisition within a specific traditional information industry (for e.g. publish ing) or a new information sector (for e.g. information content providers) as one of related diversification and classified it as ‘within’. Similarly, a merger or acquisition across two different traditional information industries (for e.g. publishing and computing) or two different information sectors (for e.g. information content providers and information highways) as an example of unrelated diversification, thus classified as ‘across’. We, thus, state our first two hypotheses as:

H1. The change in firm value for bidders in M&As across two traditional information industries will be positive. The change in firm value for bidders in M&As within a specific traditional information industry will be zero or positive.

H2. The change in firm value for bidders in M&As across two information sectors (as defined by the IEC) will be positive. The change in firm value for bidders in M&As within a specific information sector will be zero or positive.

We investigated the M&As in the three new information sectors further on the basis of their degree of relatedness. We broke down all M&As within a specific information sector into two groups: highly related (HR) and less related (LR). The HR group consisted of observations where the two firms have the same two-digit SIC code while the LR group consisted of observations where the two firms had different ones. Similarly, all M&As across two information sectors were divided into two groups: highly unrelated (HU) and less unrelated (LU). The LU group consisted of observations where both firms had the same two-digit SIC code while the HU had different ones. This classification scheme is similar to that employed by Vardarajan and Ramanujam [29]. Table 2 summarizes the four categories and provides specific examples for each of the four groups.

Drawing on the existing evidence on related diversification, we predicted that, within a specific information sector the HR group would be valued higher than the LR group. Similarly, based on the evidence on unrelated diversification, we predicted that the HU group would be valued higher compared to the LU group. Our third hypothesis was, therefore:

H3. For M&As within a specific IEC defined information sector, the change in firm value for the bidder will be positive for the HR group and zero or positive for the LR group. Likewise, for M&As across two IEC defined information sectors, the change in firm value for the bidder will be positive for the HU group and zero or positive for the LU group.

Even though common technological factors affect all three information sectors, the degree of technological uncertainty in each depended on the intensity of competition and innovation in a specific sector. We employed a proxy for technological uncertainty and computed the spending on R&D as a proportion of total assets. The mean proportional spending on R&D by firms that were information content providers in our sample was 14.58%. The corresponding figures were 9.53% for firms in the information highway sector and 8.91% for firms in the information appliances sector. Spending on R&D by firms was significantly different for firms in the information content providers than by firms in the other two at the less than 1% level. However, spending on R&D was not significantly different between the information highway and information appliances sectors. An unrelated diversification strategy should be more value enhancing for a bidder from the information content sector. Thus, those from the information content sector that acquired targets from one of the other two sectors should display a more positive stock price response than bidders that make acquisitions within the sector itself and this result was likely to be stronger for the unrelated sub-sample. Conversely, since the technological uncertainty was lower for the information appliances and information highway sectors, highly related M&As should be more value enhancing for bidding firms in these two sectors. Our fourth hypothesis was therefore:

H4. For bidding firms in the information content sector that acquire targets in one of the other two sectors, the change in wealth will be positive for highly unrelated (HU) M&As and zero or positive for less unrelated (LU) M&As. Conversely, for bidding firms in the information appliances and information highway sectors that acquire targets within their sector, the change in wealth will be positive for the highly related (HR) M&As and zero or positive for the less related (LR) M&As.

We computed the cumulative average abnormal stock returns (CAARs) around the announcement date of the merger or acquisition using the event study methodology as our measure for the change in value of the bidding firm.

## 4. Data and methodology

## 4.1. Sample description

Our initial sample consisted of all completed M&As in the U.S. market (NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ) from January 1993 to December 2005, inclusively. The initial list was compiled from the Securities Data Corporation Worldwide Mergers and Acquisitions database. Since most target firms were private enterprises, we limited our analyses to acquiring firms only. Daily stock returns data was obtained from the CRSP (Centre for Research in Security Prices) database. Our final sample of data consisted of material on 2421 firms.

There was no evidence that the sample firms were drawn from one specific time period, though the number of transactions increased in the later years. Computing firms comprised nearly 57% of the sample, followed by electronics (24%), and telecommunications (12%). When broken down by the IEC defined information sectors, both information content and information appliance sectors consisted of approximately 38% of the sample. ## 4.2. Event study methodology

An event study measures the impact of a specific event on the market value of a firm. Under the assumption of investor rationality and a semi-strong form of market efficiency, the impact of an unanticipated event is expected to be incorporated immediately into the firm’s market value. Controlling for other contemporaneous events, the change in market value of the firm from its expected value should provide an estimate of the value of the new information. The economic impact is usually measured by computing the change in price of the firm’s securities over a relatively short-time period.

Researchers from several disciplines have recently employed this technique to study the impact of various events. For example, studies have been made on the impact on firm value of new product announcements [10] and E-business outsourcing [1]. Using results from a series of event studies on information technology investments, Dehninga et al. [9] have showed the linkage between the factors that affect the stock price response at the announcement and their impact on firm value.

We used the event study methodology described by Patell [24]. The market model parameters were estimated using a 60-day estimation window t = -90 to -30, where t = 0 is the announcement date. The CRSP value-weighted index is the proxy for the market portfolio when estimating the market model parameters. MacKinlay [21] provides an excellent review of recent advances in the methodology.

## 5. Empirical results

We focused on the announcement effects in the short-run only and examined the cumulative average abnormal returns (CAARs) for the event windows (0), (-1, 0), (0, 1) and (-1, 1) where day 0 is the announcement date. Many firms often choose to make a major announcement that might affect stock prices only after the financial markets have closed. Since it was often not obvious when the announcement was made, we examined the announcement effect over a 2–3-day window.

M&As across two traditional information industries or new information sectors were expected to result in a greater stock price response than M&As within the same industry or sector.

Results for H1 and H2 are shown in Panels A and B of Table 5. Unrelated or diversifying type acquisitions did not result in any wealth gains for acquiring firm shareholders. For four of the five traditional information industries, the stock price response was not statistically different from zero for M&As from the ‘across’ samples. Contrary to our expectations, for the entertainment industry, the abnormal return for the announcement day was -1.14% (significant at the 0.1% level). Similar insignificant results for the ‘across’ samples were also obtained as seen in the three information sectors of Panel B.

Though we find partial support for both H1 and H2, the conjecture that acquirers in information-based industries would seek to acquire unrelated targets to diversify their line of business because of the greater degree of uncertainty is not supported.

It is also worth noting that, collectively across all traditional information industries, we found that 1986 firms (82% of the sample) acquire targets within the industry and that ‘within sample was greater than ‘across’ sample for each industry. Similarly, for all three information sectors, 1776 firms (73% of the sample) made acquisitions within the same sector. Thus, more bidders sought targets in their M&As that were closely related to their line of business. Shareholder value accrued from synergistic gains in the related acquisitions, a result consistent with prior work.

Finally, our fourth hypothesis predicted that the information sector, which had the greatest level of technological uncertainty among the three sectors, would display more unrelated acquisitions as an unrelated diversification strategy was most suited when uncertainty increased. Contrary to our expectation, we found that 91% of the firms in the information content sector made acquisitions within the same sector compared to 62% for the information appliances sector and 64% for the information highways sector.

The HU sample for the information content sector did not display any significant abnormal returns. For the other two sectors, the hypothesis predicted that the wealth change should be positive for the HR samples. The information highways sector displayed significant positive abnormal returns for all four-event windows and was marginally significant for the (-1, 0) window for the information appliances sector. H4 was, thus partially supported.

Overall, our results showed that most acquirers pursued a growth strategy that preserved focus by acquiring closely related targets. Our evidence within the same traditional information industry or sector showed a related diversification strategy created more value than an unrelated diversification strategy when both firms belonged to two different industries or sectors. Furthermore, for the information sectors, we found that highly related acquisitions within the same sector maximized the value. However, our results suggest more effect of gains accruing from synergy than reduction in operating risk through diversification.

## 6. Summary and conclusions

The International Engineering Consortium predicted that firms in the five traditional information industries currently organized along form are realigning and will eventually become three new information sectors. Using a large sample of 2421 bidding firms belonging to the information-based industries that engaged in a merger or an acquisition between 1993 and 2005 we studied the returns to shareholders of these bidding firms using event study methodology. Our study produced several interesting findings.

First, the wealth effects for acquisitions for acquiring firm shareholders were found to be generally either zero or significantly positive. While this result was consistent with the more recent evidence on related diversifying mergers, it was strikingly different from the overwhelming negative wealth effects for bidding firms documented in the finance literature.

Second, contrary to our expectations, we found that most bidding firms in the information industries pursued growth by acquiring related targets rather than by diversifying through an unrelated acquisition. As firms in the information industries operate with a greater degree of technological uncertainty than firms in most other industries, ex ante one would have expected unrelated acquisitions to be more common.

Third, wealth gains for acquiring firm shareholders were generally observed for M&As within the same industry or sector, being strongest for the telecommunications industry and information highways sector. M&As across two different industries or information sectors did not result in gains for acquiring firm shareholders.

Fourth, the highly related acquisitions expected to produce the most synergistic gains, consistently resulted in wealth gains for acquiring firm shareholders in all three information sectors. With the exception of the information highways sector, our evidence suggested that highly unrelated M&As that lowered operating risk were not accompanied by gains for shareholders.

Overall, we found that shareholders of acquiring firms in the three IEC information sectors experienced non-negative wealth changes. Most firms merged with or acquired related targets that resulted in a positive wealth change. Shareholders of firms that acquired unrelated targets realized insignificant change in wealth.
